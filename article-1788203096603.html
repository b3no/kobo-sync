Sorry to Bother You

Freddie deBoer
Aug 31
 
READ IN APP
 

I had this friend - haven’t seen him in at least fifteen years, not for any reason other than that life happens and you drift - I had this friend and he was a recovering addict. He would tell you that his drug of choice was everything, and that wasn’t wrong, but cocaine was a particular challenge for him. And he came close to dying and even closer to jail and finally got diverted into a program and got sober. Last I heard he had stuck with it, but last I heard was a long time ago. When you go through the program, and there are many programs but it’s all the program, when you go through the program, you might try your best to keep it all a secret. Such things are, at least theoretically, some of the last private experiences left in human affairs. Then again, addiction can often be pretty loud, and if everybody knows about the shitty addict parts you probably want them to know about the uplifting recovery part. Well. He told people.

We would sometimes go to this little music shop in my hometown. Pull guitars down off of the racks and noodle around, pretend to have opinions on humbuckers vs single coils. That store is long gone now; my hometown has always exemplified the way that the places where we grow up are forever the same and yet constantly turning over at an implausible speed. Anyway the proprietor of that particular establishment was a guy who had himself been in the program, long before. Who knows his substance of choice. He was nosy and presumptuous, though probably in a good way. There are a lot of people like that, out there in the recovery community, these greybeards who like to ladle out wisdom and get up in your business. In fairness, they usually have in fact seen it all and are usually right in all of the particulars and perhaps the recovering addicts of the world need the more seasoned among them to act like a constant nagging grandma. In those moments I felt for my friend. “You keeping your nose clean?” he’d say, leaning in conspiratorially. “Staying straight?” My friend would stammer out a positive report. “You need to get your ass to a meeting,” the guy would say, regardless of what my buddy had told him. My friend’s addiction was everybody’s business but in particular, in that guy’s store, it was that guy’s business.

I commiserated, once, as we walked out, having bought nothing as usual. That must be annoying, I said. He shrugged.

“He treats me like I’m an addict,” he said. “I am an addict.”

I feel the same way. It’s certainly annoying when your instability is everyone else’s business, but then I made it everyone else’s business; that doing so was itself an expression of that same unchosen instability is just one of those things. When I meet people IRL who only know of me online, they often act a little weird, preemptively anxious. I think it surprises some to find that I’m generally quite a sedate guy. (Then again I’m sedate because I’m sedated.) Either way, being kind of disassociated and a step slow doesn’t counteract whatever assumptions they have going in. That stuff gets added in, in an unpredictable way, and it’s uncomfortable to be handled, to be managed, by people you just met. The point is that you can feel people’s expectations getting factored into some instinctive calculation about how to act around you in a disquieting way. But, well, I can’t really blame anyone. I have said, in various ways, “My disorder is a big deal,” and I therefore can’t turn around and complain when people act like it’s a big deal in ways I don’t like. This is one of the endless reasons I feel alienated from the disability rights movement; so much of that discourse amounts to people saying “I want you to act like my disability is a very big deal, but only at the times and in the ways I choose.” It doesn’t work like that. If you’re asking for the world’s accommodation you’re first asking for the world’s attention and it’s literally inevitable that sometimes this attention will fall on you in uncomfortable ways. Sorry.

There are a lot of people who will use my bipolar disorder in the most cynical and disingenuous way possible; I’ll write a piece criticizing the latest episode of Stranger Things and someone will write me and say “Off your meds again???” Happens allllll the time. And I’m perfectly happy to dismiss that stuff for what it is, just another attempt by a stranger to exert control over me. But I can’t dismiss concerns about my mental health writ large. I have a cyclical psychotic disorder, after all. I’ve managed it very well for a long time, but I would never say that it’s behind me. The history of bipolar disorder is the history of people who thought that they had gotten past it. People are going to interpret me through the lens of a disorder that I myself insist is a big deal. That’s part of my disorder’s unfortunate endowment to me, one small facet of its sad reality. People close to me know to fear the periods where I get too skinny; that this takes agency away from me, denies me ownership of my body, does not change the fact that they have good reason to be so concerned. There’s no use crying over it.

It would be cool, though, if I actually got the accommodation to go along with the “stigma.” I am quite sanguine about everything right now even as it’s increasingly clear that I can’t salvage this writing career of mine. I have been blessed with the experience of genuine challenge in life and this present moment, this coming to terms with how limited my current professional horizons are, does not amount to a genuine challenge. But, look: I have had three professional contexts in my life, academia, media, and publishing; those are three industries that have an ostentatious commitment to social liberalism generally and to disability rights specifically; not one of them has extended me the slightest accommodation for the behavioral elements of a disability that was first documented in the second year of the George W. Bush administration. That’s just true, a reality I’m increasingly unwilling to dismiss for optics purposes. For a long time I really emphasized my responsibility for my actions even when under the influence of my mental illness, and in every important way I feel exactly the same way now. I’m responsible for everything I do. It’s just that responsibility has to be mitigated by context, and there’s a particular political vision of the moral world that pretends to be dedicated to that mitigation but which has extended none of it to me or to other people who have done bad things. The mitigation, it seems, applies only to the minimally-afflicted post-collegiate types who have made disorder into a meme. This is, I would argue, ethically and theoretically less than ideal.

So you could look at my quotidian troubles. In grad school, particularly in the last several years, I was clearly breaking down. My drinking and sleeping around and general instability had become well known; I was increasingly incapable of behaving with even minimal performative professionalism of the type that people on the job market require. My own faculty were kind but the field writ large, the whisper network of chattering tenured radicals who decided who got which job each year - well, they chattered, and not in a way that was good for me. To a person, they would all tell you that they hated ableism and the way the world was built for normative bodies etc etc etc, but I wasn’t a part of their vision of what disability meant, even though they talked constantly about the “invisible wheelchair” of cognitive or behavioral disabilities. I was just some mouthy grad student they didn’t like very much. So they talked, and academia was in bad enough shape that such talk had existential consequences. Then, when I got to Brooklyn College, I was in about as progressive of a workplace as you could possibly find. Certainly almost everyone there had some theoretical commitment to the kind of disability politics that would protect a theoretical employee with a disability. Sadly, I was an actual employee with a disability, and though I had specifically asked for accommodation for the challenges associated with antipsychotic medications, the reasons for my eventual firing were more or less identical to a list of the side effects of said medications. A Disabled Person is an idea, an ideal. I was just a guy, a guy who struggled to pay attention in meetings with enough frequency that disability accommodation was an easy thing to forget.

And then you have media. It’s an industry with an absolutely theatrical commitment to the paper dragon that is stigma. The Cut runs like three essays a week decrying the devastating effect of stigma on the TFMUD (Today’s Fashionable Made Up Disorder) community. They’ll deny it now but in the height of the woke period plenty of people in media really did the real-deal “It’s ableist to call someone crazy” thing. It’s become a matter of holy writ in the industry that we should never question any claims about disability at all, that we should pretend that factitious disorder simply doesn’t exist. But a lot of the self-same people who hold those ostensible commitments are the same exact people who have spent years trying to permanently banish me from the business and who have at this point more or less succeeded in doing so. I know people hate to hear this from me - it’s not a comfortable thing to say publicly when you have a paranoia disorder - but these efforts aren’t passive or incidental; there’s a group of people who directly and explicitly conspire to keep me from professional opportunity. My options have shrunk considerably in the past few years even as I have enjoyed greater personal stability as I ever have and as I’ve written more and more straightforwardly left-wing arguments. So, really: where’s my accommodation? Or is accommodation only for people who laugh at your jokes?

Of course, the available options are not permanent and totalizing condemnation or blanket, thoughtless exoneration. As I’ve said again and again, for years, there’s ample space to both take my condition into account and to still blame me for things I’ve done. But a lot of people refuse to do the former, go on BlueSky and talk about my sins without ever once mentioning the relevant medical facts that they are well aware of, all while telling everyone that they’re allies to The Disabled. If you could ever force any of them to defend this reality, I’m sure they could come up with some tortured justification for why I in particular am not worthy of receiving accommodation. But you couldn’t ever force them to. This is an era of distributed opinion, and this ingroup social policing works because that distribution means nobody ever has to be the one to explain anything; meanwhile the actual professional policing goes on behind closed doors, in the backchannel. So I’m not waiting for any explanations. It would perhaps be easier to say, again, that these commitments are only real commitments when they’re hard to live with, that preference becomes principle only when there’s a cost. Like I said, though, the basic structure of digital community means that no individual human is ever in the position of having to answer these unpleasant questions. And at the tip of the spear it’s simple to just say on the company Slack that I should not be engaged with, let alone published.

Some people have reacted to my upcoming book by saying some version of “These are important topics, I want someone with a serious mental illness to write about them, but not Freddie; he’s so unpleasant and unstable.” To which I would say, how many people with serious mental illnesses are out there, do you think, who aren’t unpleasant or unstable? The desire is always to find and valorize the crazy person who is not crazy; the instinct is always to showcase the person whose behavioral problems don’t actually result in bad behavior. But this is to accommodate those who don’t need accommodation, you guys, to comfor those who aren’t afflicted. This is the bizarre contemporary spectacle: we live in a modern liberal culture that has never been more acutely sensitive to disability as a concept and yet has never been less inclined to actually express that sensitivity when it’s hard to do so. We have a progressive hivemind that’s flamboyantly concerned with disability as a site of identity but which always finds some identity-based reason not to demonstrate concern for those who are unpalatable in some way. The vast acreage of published disability theory is still insufficient to prompt its acolytes to trouble their simplistic judgment of the types of actions that the actually severely sick engage in. If you follow me.

So, they can’t forgive Lindsay Clancy because only a monster kills children.
Can’t forgive Kanye West because he’s antisemitic.
Can’t forgive a guy who kills his wife and kids because that’s misogyny.
Can’t forgive a schizophrenic who mutters racial slurs because that’s racism.
Can’t forgive Robert Card, an angry white man with a gun.
Can’t forgive the Bondi Junction stabber because he was an Islamic terrorist, or at the very least part of the global intifada, but then again he wasn’t, but still he was clearly a bad guy.
Can’t forgive the Paul Pelosi attacker because that’s what MAGA wants.
Can’t forgive the latest celebrity addict who acts the way an addict acts, until they overdose and die, after which we can’t forgive the enablers who were caught up in their addiction.
Can’t forgive Nick Reiner because, hell, who didn’t love Rob Reiner?
Most ludicrously of all, can’t forgive John Davidson for shouting racial slurs at the BAFTAs, can’t forgive a man with Tourette’s for acting like a man with Tourette’s at a ceremony absolutely stuffed with self-regard for its ostensible celebration of people with Tourette’s.

That last bit, the long-roiling controversy about whether Tourette’s can really cause someone to shout a racial slur in a crowded theater…. What we now know as Tourette’s syndrome was described in texts from the 1400s. That Tourette’s outbursts of whichever kind are truly involuntary has been known to medical science for hundreds of years. It’s a neurological disorder; it’s like blaming someone for coughing. And yet for awhile our media class busily contemplated the notion that maybe this one guy with Tourette’s had chosen to behave that way, that he was actually operating under his own volition when he engaged in the most socially-damning, comprehensively embarrassing behavior you can imagine. If you express skepticism about the fact that the number of people taking the LSAT who request a disability accommodation has exploded at an exponential scale in recent years? That’s ableism. Entertaining the idea that Tourette’s symptoms aren’t really involuntary because that’s what’s momentarily socially convenient? Not ableism, I guess. These things are so unpredictable.

Who is left to be forgiven? Paradoxically, those who are said to have nothing to be sorry about. After all, #actuallyautistic autism, we have been informed, is not a disorder at all, and only a monster would act like there’s something bad about being ADHD, ADHD now a thing that you are rather than a thing that you have. They cancel academic panels about treating autism because nothing about autism is bad and that’s eugenics, even as the people who say so demand to be accommodated for the negative effects their disability doesn’t have. I don’t know where we are in the endlessly oscillating neurodevelopmental-disorder-as-superpower cycle, but surely no one needs to be forgiven for having superpowers. And yet as the TikTok tics experience shows, we have ample collective sympathy for the minimally disabled, the dubiously disabled. The lengths that the essaying class went to in order to avoid judging adolescent women who pretended to have Tourette’s for a few months were remarkable. The guy with actual Tourette’s that did the bad thing received no similar sympathy, and the reason is obvious: because he revealed that Tourette’s is actually a disorder, that the condition is bad, that it hurts. Surely no one thinks the severely autistic need to be forgiven for anything - they’re so disabled that it’s hard for them to engage in the forbidden behaviors - but their ongoing erasure grows out of the same soil: we do not see them because their condition fails to be conveniently benign. And though our lives, impairments, and moral agency are different, so does mine.

Disability accommodation is complicated; surely I cannot ask anyone to like me or support my career and call it a request for accommodation. I have a lot of faults, and much of what I say here is self-serving, even self-pitying, but one thing I did very early one was to accept that my life would have limits because of my disorder and accept that this was no one’s fault. I don’t mind living in a world where people stigmatize my condition, given that the stigma is the product of the very same behavior that speaks to the disorder that demands accommodation. But, you know, you’d like at least some consistency, some moral logic, some plan. That some are less disabled than others does not at all mean that they are less deserving of accommodation, though they are less in need. I’m frequently accused of not “believing in” ADHD or ASD-1, which certainly isn’t true; indeed, I’m sensitive to all of the online playacting of those conditions in part because they are so hard on the people who actually have them. I don’t pretend that all of these lines are bright or clean or that it’s always obvious who should receive which forms of support or concern. But I can safely say that there is no need to ask for sympathy for those who are already sympathetic, and the bizarre nature of contemporary disability politics leaves liberals looking like the rich man who gives charity only to those who aren’t truly poor. And at some point it does leave you pondering whether any of it ever meant anything at all.
