The Sex Strike Isn't Going to Happen

Because straight women actually enjoy having sex, too, among other reasons.

∙

READ IN APP
 

Right after the 2024 election, I wrote a piece so anxiety-provoking (for me, anyway) that I wrote three full throat-clearing paragraphs of disclaimers before I got to my thesis, which was that: A.) the results of the election were causing sexual strife between men and women and B.) I did not think that straight women in America would initiate the sex strike they were promising, let alone perform such a protest to “completion,” whatever that may have been. (At the time, lots of young women on social media were claiming that they planned to participate in “4B,” a South Korean radical feminist sex strike movement which wasn’t even that popular in South Korea.)

At the time, people told me I was too quick to dismiss women’s rage at Trump’s election (look, I was mad too, but not mad enough to go monkmode) and therefore too quick to dismiss the idea that straight women would stop having sex with men in droves, for an indeterminate amount of time, possibly forever. I maintain that I was correct, I apologized way too much, and the 4B sex strike obviously never caught on in any mainstream way, nor will it ever catch on in the modern-day US. This is true for many reasons, the main one being that women, even liberal feminist women like myself, also enjoy sex. Also, a “sex strike” with a solution of “just start dating women instead” is not palatable to most women, given that most women are, in fact, straight.

But let’s back up a bit, because sex strikes have worked before. In 2006, girlfriends of gang members in Pereira, Colombia stopped having sex in an effort to force a reduction in violence, working with support from the municipal government. This made a lot of sense because they speculated that men got involved with gang violence specifically to attract women, and by rebranding this behavior as unattractive, fewer men were inclined to partake. The strike successfully reduced the murder rate in the city by 26%. A 2003 sex strike in Liberia also helped put an end to civil war, although the sex strike was part of a larger movement that involved other protests and sit-ins.

But I’m just not buying that a sex strike will ever happen in the US, at least not anytime soon.

Before you “nobody is saying that” me, the 2024 election wasn’t the only time people called for a sex strike in the US, although it would be valid to question the legitimacy of these other calls to action. In 2017, Janelle Monáe suggested in an interview with Marie Claire that women go on a sex strike. In 2019, Alyssa Milano advocated for a sex strike to protest anti-abortion laws (a bit of a horseshoe abstinence-only thing, I guess). And this week on Substack, a piece by a bisexual woman who “decentered men” by simply dating women instead (it’s giving “still having sex”) called for a sex strike, assuming that straight women would stop having sex at all. (I’m not linking this piece, mostly because this woman doesn’t have a big following despite how often I’ve seen this article circulated, and it would be a #problematic power imbalance for me to inadvertently cause her to be brigaded.)

But the problem with these proposed American sex strikes is multifaceted. First of all, straight women also enjoy sex, probably too much to deprive themselves of it for an indeterminate amount of time with no clear endgame.

Second, successful sex strikes (and to be clear, there haven’t been that many) have existed on a relatively smaller scale, where the male partners of the women involved were the same men causing a concrete problem that needed to be fixed. Most of the women in favor of a sex strike are not married to the men who, even by their standards, “deserve” to be punished.

So for argument’s sake, let’s just say 2026’s “problem” is Donald Trump and MAGA. A sex strike would potentially work if most women in the country were liberal (not just liberal, but extremely politically motivated) and most men in the country were Trump voters. While there is a slight gendered skew within the political parties, it’s not nearly as extreme as you may think. According to Pew Research, 59% of married men are Republican or lean Republican, while the same is true of 50% of married women. People living with an unmarried partner are more likely to be Democrats—60% of the men and 64% of the women.

Assuming that the vague objective of a sex strike is “MAGA bad,” then most of the women participating in it would be liberal women. That eliminates well, half of married women in the country. And since under 10% (reported as anywhere between 3-9%) of marriages are between people of opposing political parties, a “sex strike” that intends to change men’s behavior (ie: stop voting for Republicans) would only be levied at a small subset of Republican men married to Democrat women, and that’s assuming all or most of those women would want to do this.

But the vague objective might be an even bigger obstacle. While revolutionary LARPers abound, most of the major things that happen within American politics originate with elections. Presumably, if MAGA is the problem a sex strike intends to solve, then the desired outcome is “if men are punished for voting MAGA, they’ll vote Democrat.” (I have major issues with this logic, first of all, but for argument’s sake let’s say it’s true.)

At what point would the aforementioned sex strike be “over?” Would a Democratic President in 2028 signal the end of the sex strike? Because that’s two years from now! What about a Democratic majority in 2026? What if it’s the House but not the Senate, or vice versa? Either way, most of these issues are pretty long-term, lest we forget that women also want to get a nut off now and again. It’s no coincidence that many of the women advocating for a sex strike, or any kind of radical gender separatism, are also attracted to women—it’s not nearly as much of a sacrifice if you have that alternative!

Some discussions about sex strikes are more about bad male behavior than politics, although the two are obviously intertwined. For example, the aforementioned Substack essay mentions all forms of bad husband-ing, from men dropping the ball with chores to committing domestic violence (she insists no woman who lives with a man could possibly consider herself safe, no matter how many years of good marriage she has behind her, parroting an untrue statistic that half of the men in America were enrolled in an online rape academy.) But also, she readily admits that women who are in abusive relationships might not safely be able to refuse sex, so presumably, the men being punished for their bad behavior would be the men who posed the least threat of violence.

The idea is so ridiculous that Alyssa Milano probably wasn’t even serious when she called for the sex strike, and probably just said it performatively to make an emotional point. Not only did she never partake herself, but she indicated that her husband (to whom she has been married since 2009) was never the intended “target” of such a strike. And certainly, a liberal sex strike that exists to change the behavior of liberal men wouldn’t be very effective, unless the goal was to make them so fed up with being liberal that they just start watching Myron Gaines and listening to the Whatever podcast. But then the question is: how many liberal women are married to men who would be “deserving” of such punishment? Not many!

It’s also worth noting that Milano’s call for a sex strike was rejected by feminists too (such as myself, retroactively) for the reason that women shouldn’t be punished with abstinence. As prominent feminist and abortion rights advocate Jessica Valenti said, “I hate the idea that not having sex is a punishment for men but not for women. I like sex! Why should I deprive myself of it?”

But of course, Valenti shouldn’t impose this deprivation, nor should anyone, and nor did Alyssa Milano. A sex strike with an indeterminate goal, years-long timeline, and very few deserving targets (they’re calling it “turn your libbed-out husband into an incel to own the chuds!”) simply will not work. It’s not only that it’s unpopular—although of course it is—but we will have no way of knowing when it’s over! When it comes to any goal, even just a New Year’s resolution (or all the PIPs on which I was placed during my unsuccessful decade in tech) a measurable outcome is basically a requirement for it to be realistic. When my manager put me on a PIP that included the goal “be more strategic” it was because he just wanted to fire me and knew I could never prove that I had become more strategic. Similarly, if you call for a sex strike but refuse to put a goal on it, then you don’t really want to achieve anything—you’re just mad. Or looking for attention. Or both!

Perhaps it is all about being mad, and nobody should take any of this seriously, and I’m an idiot for assuming any good faith is involved in these calls for action (I’m literally neurodivergent, so this is lowkey ableist to assume.) Maybe this is just me, but I’m not a big fan of splashy, controversial political statements that you don’t really mean (which you expect everyone to know you don’t really mean) just because you need an outlet for rage. At that point, just go full Threads resist lib and create signs that say “Fuckity Fuckity Fascist Cheeto,” because at least then there won’t be any confusion.

Now, presumably, if the problem at hand is not MAGA, but something more individual and domestic, such as a husband who isn’t helping out enough around the house, you could theoretically withhold sex to achieve your aims, and it might work. But to what end? Even if sex strikes have technically worked in the past, nothing is to be said of those relationships or their outcomes. As I mentioned when I wrote about the concept of “choreplay,” or providing sexual incentives for men to do chores, using sex as a bargaining chip sends the message that it’s a favor you do for the other person and not something you enjoy for yourself. And if that’s truly how you feel about sex with your partner, even if your sex strike “works,” the relationship may not.
