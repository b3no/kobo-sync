They should teach patriotism in schools

Public schools have an important civic purpose beyond reading and math.

Matthew Yglesias
Jul 2
∙
Paid
 
READ IN APP
 

I was at a couple of education events in the spring that all featured very smart, very achievement-oriented people talking about how to address the national crisis in basic reading and math attainment. A common lament was that politicians and pundits are simply not focused enough on the question of whether kids are learning the basics, preferring to fire off hot takes about culture war topics.

This is a view that I very much sympathize with and have articulated over the years.

That said, I’ve also been in a bunch of conversations about things like Noah Smith’s essay on the need for a revival of American liberal nationalism or Jerusalem Demsas’s take about how you have to love America in order to save it.

I broadly agree with these takes. But I also find that they’re a little bit lacking in specificity.

I know that Rob Sand, who’s running for governor in Iowa, does things like open town halls by having everyone sing “America the Beautiful.” A friend described Sand as the Daniel Day-Lewis of politics: He understood quite some time ago that trends in Democratic Party politics were going one way and political trends in Iowa were going another, and he fully committed to a hyper-distinctive post-partisan Iowa-as-fuck persona. He never breaks character, and he not only performs the patriotic ritual, he makes it seem natural.

But if liberals want to embrace American patriotism, that has to mean more than just clicking “like” and “share” on columns that express a second-order thesis that it would be desirable to see more liberal patriotism.

You need to actually do something specific, the way Sand does. I think Democrats should actually do some patriotism.

Pledging allegiance

There’s a House bill called the “Promoting American Patriotism In Our Schools Act,” which is sponsored by Dale Strong of Alabama and co-sponsored by a handful of other Republicans. It would require public schools receiving federal Elementary and Secondary Education Act funds to “recite the Pledge of Allegiance to the Flag of the United States of America at the beginning of each school day.” It then also, to ensure conformity with free speech law, specifies that students must be allowed to opt out of pledging without penalty.

My (private) K-8 school opened every day with the Pledge of Allegiance, and I later went to a more progressive high school that did not.

This obviously did not make a huge difference in practice, but it does set a different kind of tone. Of course, as a fussy writer and former philosophy student, I’ve always found the idea of pledging allegiance to a flag a little bit odd. A flag is a symbol of a thing, not a thing. Historically, the design of the flag has changed many times as a result of the accession of new states, and it’s not like people are switching allegiances when the flag changes.

But it is what it is. The text says that we are pledging allegiance not just to the flag but also “to the republic for which it stands,” which seems like the important part.

And I think if you’re going to be patriotic, then you ought to be loyal to the American republic.

We’re currently in a funny place where I think if I were to say “Democrats should promote the idea that people should be loyal to the American republic,” that would be considered an incredibly lukewarm take. But it’s not surprising or confusing to anyone that all the sponsors on the pledge bill are Republicans.

Yet, historically, this pledge to the flag and to the republic for which it stands is the conventional ritual. So isn’t Representative Strong just right about this? Again, I’m not saying students should be hauled into detention for refusing to engage in coerced speech. But most people tend to go along to get along. Defaults matter, and it seems to me that promoting a default of loyalty to the United States of America is a totally reasonable function for a public school system.

I also think that Democrats in particular increasingly need to ask themselves what they think a public school system is for.

Voucherizing the public school system is unpopular, and opposing that is often one of red state Democrats’ best issues. But Democrats struggle to articulate an actual purpose for these public institutions.

One such purpose, of course, should be student achievement. But that really has to be achievement at the low end. I don’t think it’s really credible to deny that affluent educated parents could just take the money and secure good education for our own kids. The people who’d be failed by a privatized solution are the kinds of kids who private schools would just try to screen out.

Lifting up students at the low end is an important cause. But I don’t think you can really talk about public education in a clear way without talking about the civic function of schools, and that should include cultivating a positive national identity.

Sweet land of liberty

Marian Anderson was one of the most famous American singers of the 1930s.

She was also African American, which greatly impacted her ability to tour and perform in the American South. This reached a head in 1939, when her manager tried to get her booked to perform at Constitution Hall run by the Daughters of the American Revolution here in Washington, D.C. They said no, upholding a color bar against Black performers. The D.C. Board of Education also denied permission to use the Central High School auditorium as a venue.

The N.A.A.C.P. decided to use this as an organizing opportunity and started a big campaign to pressure DAR to let Anderson sing. Eleanor Roosevelt resigned from the organization and led thousands of others to join her in doing so. Franklin Roosevelt was legendarily hyper-cautious about embracing civil rights issues, but he was persuaded that this one was a winner and his Interior Secretary Harold Ickes gave permission to organize an open-air concert on the steps of the Lincoln Memorial.

For her first song, Anderson performed “My Country, ‘Tis of Thee,” the one about how America is a sweet land of liberty.

The concert was a huge deal, and it’s not a coincidence that 24 years later, Martin Luther King Jr., also speaking from the steps of the Lincoln Memorial, closed his “I Have a Dream” speech with the same song:

This will be the day when all of God’s children will be able to sing with new meaning. “My country, ‘tis of thee, sweet land of liberty, of thee I sing. Land where my fathers died, land of the pilgrim’s pride, from every mountainside, let freedom ring.” And if America is to be a great nation, this must become true. So let freedom ring from the prodigious hilltops of New Hampshire. Let freedom ring from the mighty mountains of New York. Let freedom ring from the heightening Alleghenies of Pennsylvania. Let freedom ring from the snow-capped Rockies of Colorado. Let freedom ring from the curvaceous slopes of California. But not only that. Let freedom ring from Stone Mountain of Georgia. Let freedom ring from Lookout Mountain of Tennessee. Let freedom ring from every hill and molehill of Mississippi, from every mountainside. Let freedom ring!

There’s a conventional progressive point here that true patriots have always worked to improve their country rather than just be complacent about it.

But I also think it’s worth taking this whole sequence of events seriously.

Anderson is singing the song somewhat ironically. Her point was that to a considerable extent, America was in fact not a land of liberty.

Yet the performance worked, because people all across the country were taught to sing it non-ironically. Some people probably noticed the mismatch between the stated ideal and the reality; lots of people probably didn’t and just took it at face value. Yet it’s precisely because people grew up sincerely believing that America was a sweet land of liberty that gave the performance its punch.

If everyone had been raised to think of the United States as a country built on stolen land, enslaved labor, and genocide, then there’s actually no dissonance to resolve — a white person would simply think he had a pretty good deal in life.

There’s clearly a view that’s had a lot of influence over American educational practice that if you tell everyone how terrible everything is, that will inspire them to be activists for change. But most people just don’t care that much about politics and never will — you’re just fostering cynicism, fatalism, and selfishness in a way that’s totally at odds with the practice of Lincoln or King or anyone else who’s ever accomplished anything.

America the beautiful

But to return from the realm of instrumentality, it’s worth saying that America is a pretty awesome country.

The notion of the United States as a sweet land of liberty did not fully reflect the reality of 1939. But it wasn’t some kind of crazy cynical lie on a par with North Korea’s claim to be a democratic people’s republic.

The United States really was founded by a lot of religious dissenters and was genuinely a global pioneer in promoting pluralism and electoral democracy. It’s good and correct to say that the United States fell short of its ideals, but I think it’s also important to talk about the part where heroes fought to make the country live up to them and about the ways in which Americans have, through history, lived up to them.

And if you think about the recent history of the United States, there’s something blinkered about viewing the existence of racial tension and ethnic conflict as a distinctive fact of American life rather than a tragic but banal aspect of the human condition.

People from all around the world are constantly trying to move here because the United States is a good place to live. The United States has the highest median living standards in the world outside of a handful of very small countries like Norway. We have probably the freest speech you’ll find anywhere. We have a robust and longstanding civic culture of integration, where the children and grandchildren of immigrants can also lay claim to being the metaphorical children of the founding fathers of the country. We landed humans on the Moon, we split the atom, we have the most Nobel Prize winners, we developed the best Covid vaccines, we created the internet, and we’re at the leading edge of artificial intelligence.

Americans are not the absolute number one most tolerant people with regard to who we are willing to welcome as neighbors, but we’re way up there.

This is basically what schools should teach kids. Not that America is or ever was perfect, but that relative to other actually existing places, the United States has pretty consistently been a beacon of opportunity and freedom without rival. Everyone should sing the songs and pledge the pledges.

Learning from our forebears

When I took the history of early-modern moral philosophy in college, the professor told our class that we should try not to read the authors too skeptically.

She didn’t mean that we should accept everything they said as true and correct — she was prepared to grant that we were all smart people who understood how to read a text critically. Her view was that studying the history of philosophy was a worthwhile undertaking. But the value of the undertaking is not to go through some essays written a long time ago by guys using somewhat unfamiliar language and talking about somewhat unfamiliar questions and just dunk on them.

The value is in trying to understand what these major thinkers of history brought to the table and why they’re interesting.

And I would really encourage us Democrats to build a public culture where we undertake our assessment of the great characters of American history in that spirit.

It’s easy to say that F.D.R.’s internment of Japanese Americans was bad and Ulysses Grant’s blind eye to corruption was bad and Andrew Jackson’s Trail of Tears was bad and Thomas Jefferson owning slaves was bad. Because all that stuff is really bad!

It’s also genuinely important and interesting to understand how F.D.R. pulled the country out of the Great Depression and persuaded a largely unwilling nation to come to Europe’s aid. Jefferson wrote an incredible charter of human freedom. Jackson popularized our modern understanding of democracy. Grant saved the country. There’s nothing wrong with criticizing these figures, but it quickly becomes self-congratulatory and lame to gas yourself up compared to the heroic figures of the past just because you have conventional contemporary ethical views.

Part of constructing a paradigm of patriotic education should be making sure we have an inclusive canon of heroic figures to talk about so it’s not just a procession of white guys with a “too bad about the racism” asterisk.

But it’s genuinely not hard to do this, and I think Donald Trump’s Garden of American Heroes list is a broadly reasonable gesture in that direction.

Patriotism is good. America’s destiny is inextricably tied up with the fate of liberalism around the world, and Democrats shouldn’t just pay lip service to those ideas — we should promote them in concrete ways.
