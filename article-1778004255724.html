Elizabeth Strout’s Portrait of a Good Man in Quiet Crisis

In “The Things We Never Say,” Artie Dam wants candor the world rarely offers.

Ron Charles
May 5
 
READ IN APP
 

The Tuesday Review

Every eight months, someone publishes an essay announcing the rediscovery of Stoner. By now, John Williams’s 1965 novel about an English professor has been lost and found as often as my car keys.

I pray Elizabeth Strout’s new novel, The Things We Never Say, enjoys the same cycle of eternal rediscovery. It’s richer for being less self-consciously polished than Williams’s story. Taking place outside the worlds of Olive Kitteridge and Lucy Barton, it’s a slender classic about a high school teacher almost too tenderhearted for this coarsening world. If you’re a teacher, you must get a copy of The Things We Never Say, and if you love a teacher, you must give them one.

We’re not in Maine anymore. This story opens in Massachusetts in 2024 as the nation is slipping back into Donald Trump’s maw. Artie Dam, once named Teacher of the Year, has taught 11th-grade history for decades, but the atmosphere in the country and in his classroom seems new and ominous. Although the kids still adore him — “Damn-dam, the greatest man,” they call out to him — they seem frightened and anxious for reasons they can’t articulate.

Artie feels the same way, worse even. The nation’s cruel political rhetoric depresses him. He’s tipped into a slough of loneliness he hadn’t realized was pooling within him. And the departure of a good friend makes him recognize how few he has left. (In a wry meta-reference, Artie remembers reading Olive Kitteridge — “some book about a crotchety old woman from Maine” who understood that people can die of loneliness.) Coming back from a party one night, he asks his flinty wife, “I wonder why people never say anything real.”

“Don’t be an idiot,” she says. As a family therapist, she’s already spent the whole day listening to people saying real things. Who wants that at a party?

When Artie persists by asking, “We all think there is free will, but what if there isn’t?” she sighs and walks off to bed.

His wife should be paying closer attention. Strout is. Artie’s bleak, undergraduate questions are a sign of spiritual rot. With her typically matter-of-fact voice, she notes that he’s developed something of a double life. While teaching his classes and joking with students and puttering around the house, he’s been thinking about how to kill himself. “It was a great deal more complicated than he had originally thought,” she writes.

It’s a testament to the depth of her compassion that Artie’s self-destructive plans become increasingly harrowing and funny without sliding into absurdity. Her vision of the human comedy is wide enough to embrace our agony and our delight — and our rapid vibration from one pole to the other. This is, after all, a novel shadowed by real tragedies, including a childhood scarred by mental illness, a deadly accident, and a shattering act of infidelity. And yet, despite that baggage, the story keeps ascending toward a sense of astonishment at the interior complexity of life, what Artie comes to understand as the unspoken “multitudinous aspect of people.” Even the dimensions of his own life, he realizes, are nothing like what he’d long assumed.

Strout includes brief asides about other characters — or jumps unpredictably into the past or the future. We see a student inspired, a colleague cheered or wrecked. These digressions momentarily break the tight focus on Artie Dam, but attention quickly returns to the story of his precarious spirit, a story that accretes memory by memory, incident by incident. As usual, Strout manages to create scenes of intense intimacy in prose that feels as casual and comfortable as your favorite flannel shirt. She’s just so damn good. Every stitch of her artistry is seamlessly hidden. You feel that especially in how well she captures the banality of ordinary conversation in response to the most extraordinary events.

Artie’s affections have that rawness one sometimes detects in great teachers, an element of arrested development or philosophical purity that makes them hypersensitive to the needs of students even while it keeps them slightly out of sync with people their own age, adults who know better than to feel everything so intensely or to question everything so baldly. Artie desperately wants “to be innocent and incorruptible.” It pains him to send these young people out into a culture so mean.

If you were put off by the unlikable Olive Kitteridge — or if, contrariwise, you’re irritated by the debate over characters’ likability — The Things We Never Say makes a fascinating counterexample. Strout reportedly told her editor, “This Artie, I love him.” Such affection would typically be deadly for a serious novel — syrup poured on icing — but Strout’s devotion is the love of a Protestant God who spares us no agony on the path to beatitude. Reading these pages, I was repeatedly awed by her restraint, her willingness to let sentimentality evaporate in the hard light of her prose.

Reflecting its airy plot, Strout has structured The Things We Never Say with appropriate looseness — almost as a collage, a series of short passages, sometimes a few pages, sometimes just three or four lines. The effect is impressionistic while providing the gentlest forward momentum. This is, after all, a story of subtle motions: secrets learned but never acted upon, crimes committed but never prosecuted, suicide contemplated but never attempted.

But it’s also a story of moments more felt than seen — the deepening of Artie’s love for his son, the influence of a dedicated educator’s attention, proof of what Henry Adams meant when he wrote, “A teacher affects eternity; he can never tell where his influence stops.”

Despite being so contradictory himself, Artie is genuinely surprised to finally acknowledge that every single person “held within themselves a vast, unknowable universe.”

True, but that universe feels a little more comprehensible with a novel this good in it.◆

The Things We Never Say, by Elizabeth Strout (Random House, 203 pp., $29)
