The New York Times Engages in Some Extremely Weak Halloween Scaremongering

... why? why? why?

Freddie deBoer
Nov 4
 
READ IN APP
 

Please check out this cool back-and-forth I had with Kelsey Piper on what schools can and can’t do, over at the Argument. You might also be interested in this review of my book from the Wall Street Journal. Streets are saying it’s a great book….

Holy moses, do I hate irrational fearmongering, especially about children, and especially especially about Halloween, a beloved holiday activity that is increasingly threatened by unjustifiable fears of stranger danger, the whole litany of poisoned candy and razor blades in caramel apples. Such senseless panic has directly contributed to the “Trunk or Treat” phenomenon, which has begun eroding participation in trick or treating, one of the purest expressions of childlike joy there is. So you can imagine how deeply unhappy I was about this bizarre piece in the New York Times, which cobbles together a “Halloween is one of the most dangerous days of the year” narrative based on uncorroborated claims, raw counts of murders with no comparison to basal rates, and literally zero reference to relative risk, which is what actually matters. It’s maddening and inexplicable.

Here’s the evidence that the NYT brings to bear for their Halloween-is-scary-but-not-in-the-good-way stance:

  • Counting shootings nationally: The NYT reports that this Halloween saw at least 24 dead and 94 wounded from shootings, based on police statements

  • Geographic spread: the shootings ran from California to Delaware, at nightclubs, a costume store, outside stadiums and near college campuses

  • A causal theory based on unnamed “experts”: costumed revelers plus alcohol, drugs and access to guns leads to violence

  • “Previous studies” showing Halloween spikes: which is actually a single study that analyzed Boston data from 2005–09 and ranked Oct. 31 as the third-most violent day, behind the Fourth of July and New Years

  • The claim about kids, specifically: the assertion that Halloween is the most dangerous day of the year for children; no statistics are given at all, let alone relative risk data, with the only support coming from the researcher’s observation that kids are out at night interacting with strangers

  • Child-victim anecdotes:

    • A 5-year-old in a costume was shot in Detroit

    • A 17-year-old was killed in Jacksonville

    • In an unrelated incident, a 15-year-old was injured at a Jacksonville house party

    • Two teenagers were critically wounded at a shooting at a California costume store

  • The supposed college and university event cluster: “more than two dozen” shot near campuses or at university events

None of this is compelling. Let’s go through each bit.

“24 dead and 94 wounded”

There’s no denominator! There’s no baseline to compare it to! The FBI’s 2025 report put the national murder rate at 4.1 per 100,000 - tied with 1955 for the lowest in the agency’s history. That rate works out to ~38 murders a day nationally. No doubt there were other murders on Saturday which aren’t included but, like… that’s the point. You can’t establish anything without comparing Halloweens generally and this Halloween specifically to average days, or average Saturdays, or average days in October…. The article’s headline number is a roundup of individual shootings pulled from police statements. It isn’t a complete count, and it isn’t compared to anything. And the article concedes this, admitting that its authors don’t know whether this past weekend was unusually violent compared with other recent holiday weekends (or any other baseline). But then, like… why publish? What’s the story?

The geographic spread from California to Delaware

This should really go without saying: shootings happen across the country every weekend, so demonstrating that shootings are happening from the east coast to the west coast is business as usual, not an indicator of unusual amounts of violence. In other words, again, no base rate, no reference to some norm. Meanwhile the effort to manufacture a sense of breadth is pretty transparent; the venues named (nightclubs, stadiums, college campuses) are adult nightlife/event settings - which is to say, places that don’t have much to do with trick or treating.

Costumes + alcohol + drugs + guns = violence

The theory comes from unnamed “experts,” so there’s nothing to actually check, no statistics to consider, no methodology to critique. Of those four ingredients, only costumes are specific to Halloween. Alcohol, drugs, and guns are present at a lot of Saturday-night parties; I’m sorry that that’s true, but it’s true. And! Halloween 2026 fell on a Saturday, and the article never separates a holiday effect from an ordinary weekend effect. You have to have a denominator, guys.

“Previous studies have shown spikes”

Well, only one study is actually named, so the use of the plural isn’t really justified. The research on Halloween risk that does exist debunks fearmongering rather than supports it. For example, take sex crimes against children, an issue of obvious concern. A national study of more than 67,000 non-familial (that is to say, committed by a stranger) sex crimes against children found no significant increase on or around Halloween. This is better, more germane evidence that’s not cited or considered. And of course there’s the strangely durable urban legends about razor blades or poison in candy. Well, Joel Best has spent decades tracking claims of poisoned Halloween candy and has never found a confirmed case of a trick-or-treater killed by random poisoning or adulterated candy. In the only cases where children were seriously harmed by intentional manipulation of Halloween candy the culprits were family members deliberately targeting those children.

The Boston analysis

The referenced study covers one city over four years, is more than 15 years old, appeared (as for as I can tell) as a 2011 blog post on Boston.com, and was never peer-reviewed. So, yikes. And again, the ranking is for mass shootings, which isn’t the same measure as overall violent crime and not what parents are scared about when it comes to Halloween. There’s just no reason an (unproven) spike in shootings should be tied to calling Halloween the most dangerous day for children.

The child victim anecdotes

The 5 year old in Detroit was hit by a stray bullet, inside her home, after trick-or-treating had finished - no connection to the holiday. The 17 year old in Jacksonville was killed around 2PM while waiting in line for a party; the 15 year old was wounded at almost 4AM in the morning while at a house party of more than a hundred people. In other words, they were adolescents engaging in adolescent night life, which carries risks that have nothing to do with Halloween. The costume store shooting is the one incident you might plausibly call “Halloween-linked,” and even then it’s an isolated violent incident at a store - not the kind of thing parents fear when it comes to worrying about the holiday. Unfortunately, that’s likely to be lost on many people who read the piece.

The college and event cluster

Very similar story here. The shooting in Birmingham was tied to a major football rivalry game, which means crowds, tribalism, and alcohol. The Bloomington Indiana shooting happened in a crowd celebrating homecoming. The holiday is entirely incidental to both and each happened in contexts we understand to be more dangerous than the norm - big alcohol-fueled crowds that would have gathered on that Saturday anyway had Halloween not fallen on that day. The Deep Ellum incident is even less appropriate; the NYT piece itself says that the neighborhood has suffered from a crime wave all year and that bars there have been closing because of it. Again, I’m left to ask who exactly thought this piece was a good idea and how it was justified.

“Most dangerous day for minors,” says criminologist

I don’t even know where to begin here. It’s an inherently inflammatory, panic-provoking assertion with no figures behind it, no context, no framing, no comparative rates of anything. What’s the point?

Are there any dangers associated with Halloween? Yes, one in particular: cars. That’s the one and only well-documented elevated risk to children on Halloween, the risk of getting hit by a car, which the article never mentions. A JAMA Pediatrics study found that children aged four to eight were ten times more likely to be killed by cars on Halloween than on comparison nights. This is, obviously, because they’re out walking around at night, going from house to house. But, look. First, that elevated risk is still small in absolute terms, about four extra deaths in an average year in a country with 70 million kids. Secondly, and more importantly, yes - yes, cars are a threat to children on Halloween. But they’re a very different kind of threat than the ones that too many parents fear. Danger from cars are also a problem that can be mitigated with sensible behaviors about where to walk and how to cross the street safely, with reflective or light-up elements in costumes, and with aggressive enforcement of traffic law. And that last bit is really the larger context here: America’s rate of traffic deaths should be a national scandal in general. Other countries (notably Japan) have successfully reduced risks to pedestrians to a dramatic degree, and not just on major holidays. We also have to take a long look at the American addiction to big SUVs and trucks, which kill pedestrians at far higher rates than lower-to-the-ground coups and sedans. None of this has much to do with Halloween.

I don’t know. I’m a bit baffled. A minor surge in shootings may be newsworthy, but the framing of the piece (and I think the headline was updated) is bizarrely fixated on representing Halloween as a uniquely dangerous day. In a context where the beloved, remarkably safe ritual of trick or treating is being increasingly questioned by nervous parents, often replaced with the sad facsimile of “Trunk or Treat,” this kind of loose talk and silly fearmongering is profoundly unhelpful. And, like it or not, the New York Times is the most influential and important newsgathering organization in the world. I find it intrinsically important to call it out when they make mistakes, especially when it seems motivated by ideology - here, the ideology of parental fear. The reality is that American children have never been healthier or safer and we need to continue to highlight that fact.

If nothing else, I hope this post serves as a break from midterms commentary.
