Housing is at the top of everyone’s agenda

New polling, research, investment, and shifting investor behavior all point to housing’s growing influence on voters, markets, states, and lawmakers.

Halina Bennet
Jul 22
∙

Federal law curbing institutional single-family purchases is already reshaping investor behavior, while some states are moving independently on supply and affordability legislation. Meanwhile, new research suggests the affordability problem may be even worse than previously thought, which helps explain why a new poll has housing as voters’ top issue heading into the midterms.

Housing costs rank as top political issue for young voters

Voters aged 18 to 34 now rank housing costs as their top political issue, with 46 percent naming it as one of the one or two most important problems facing the country in CNBC’s All-America Economic Survey of 1,000 registered voters. Food costs were the next-most commonly selected issue among the group, at 33 percent.

That’s not news to anyone who reads this newsletter, and Congress has already identified housing as a source of actionable anxiety for Americans. Both Republicans and Democrats have tried to claim the issue, which led, of course, to the largest bipartisan success of this legislative term in the 21st Century ROAD to Housing Act, which became law nearly two weeks ago. According to the survey, 38 percent of respondents said they trust Democrats to handle the issue versus 32 percent for Republicans.

Housing frustration is in line with other consumer angst, with 60 percent of respondents saying they disapprove of Trump’s handling of the economy and 68 percent disapproving of the administration’s work on inflation and cost of living.

“It was my contention and the contention of some other people that Democrats had a huge miss in 2024 by not talking more about health care,” Jay Campbell, a partner at Hart Research, the Democratic pollster for the survey, told CNBC. “I think housing could be the health care of 2024, given especially the need for Democrats to turn young people out who are inclined to be anti-Trump and pro-democratic.”

A better (lower) estimate of homeownership

A Federal Reserve Bank of Minneapolis analysis argues that the standard census measure of the homeownership rate overstates homeownership relative to a commonsense definition because it uses households rather than people in the denominator.

As conventionally measured, the homeownership rate in the United States is about 65 percent. But this means that about 65 percent of households are headed by an owner-occupant, meaning that a 25-year-old living in his mom’s basement counts as a member of a homeowning household.

The researchers calculate a different metric — the ratio of homeowners to the entire adult population — to measure the share of independent adults who own a home rather than the share of homes that have an owner-occupant. They found that by that measure, only about 53 percent of U.S. adults are homeowners, Axios reports. The new analysis finds that the homeownership situation, which was already understood to be dire, especially for young Americans, is even worse than previously thought.

Just 22 percent of adults under 35 are homeowners under the new measure, compared with 37 percent under the traditional one.

Those who were previously counted and now get dropped by this new measure include adults living with their parents (nearly half of adults under 30), roommates of an owner, and spouses or cohabiting partners of an owner. The new measure acknowledges that even when a household counts as owner-occupied in official statistics, not every adult in that household is building equity — one of the primary ways that Americans accumulate wealth. A household-based measure is a useful way to gauge the housing stock, but an individual-based measure provides a much clearer view of who is benefiting from homeownership.

Wall Street landlords start listing more homes

A provision in the 21st Century ROAD to Housing Act that bans institutional investors from purchasing single-family rental homes is prompting investors to list more homes for sale. According to data from Parcl Labs that was shared with CNBC, the number of institutionally owned homes on the market is now more than double what it was at the start of February. Major institutional landlords have become net sellers of thousands of homes, modestly increasing ownership opportunities for some, but the volume sold so far is still too small to have a material effect on home prices nationally, CNBC reports.

The trend represents a strategy shift. Instead of sitting on scattered single-family rental homes, investors are pivoting toward build-to-rent developments, which are (after much conversation) exempt from the new restrictions.

While fewer Wall Street buyers could reduce competition for some homes, the affordability problem is driven by limited supply, high mortgage rates, and elevated costs from construction to insurance — forces by and large beyond Wall Street’s control. If institutional investors pull out of rental housing, it is possible that the rental supply will begin to tighten and send rents upward. But if those investors really do make good on their build-to-rent intentions, we could see a whole lot of new rental housing become available in the near future.

HUD suspends funding for U.S. Virgin Islands housing authority

The U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development on Monday cut off funding to the Virgin Islands Housing Finance Authority after an investigation revealed alleged fraud in the form of financial mismanagement, false certifications, and improper payments related to $1.9 billion in Hurricane Irma and Maria disaster-recovery funds.

“Organizations riddled with corruption, mismanagement, and crime will no longer be allowed to squander billions,” Secretary Scott Turner wrote of the Trump administration’s crackdown on spending. “Virgin Islands Housing Finance Authority officials cannot be allowed to prioritize kickbacks over helping families recover from disasters.”

Nine years after the territory received nearly $2 billion in disaster-recovery funding, HUD alleges it has spent only $570 million, less than a third of the total, and that the shortfall has deprived islanders of the assistance Congress intended them to have. According to HUD, the authority has completed only two of 95 planned single-family rehabilitation projects and none of the 329 planned single- and multifamily projects. Meanwhile, HUD alleges, the Virgin Islands housing authority has spent more than half of the funds allocated for administrative costs.

State action

Vermont expands low-interest loan program for housing development
State leaders announced on Tuesday that $25 million, or about 12.5 percent of its cash on hand, would go toward the “Invest in Vermont” program, which uses the state’s budget to fund low-interest construction loans. This allocation is an increase in the share of the budget devoted to the program from about 2 percent.

“Right now, there is no investment offering a greater return than housing,” said State Treasurer Mike Pieciak. “More housing will lay the foundation for a stronger Vermont economy. It will grow our workforce, fill our public schools, and make health care more affordable and accessible.”

The program has so far leveraged about $600 million to build or rehabilitate 170 units statewide and has created nearly 200 permanent jobs for Vermonters, WCAX reports.

Texas lawmakers revisit housing reform laws
Housing affordability will almost certainly remain a priority for the Texas Legislature during the 2027 session, just as it was during the previous. The Texas House Committee on Land & Resource Management held an interim hearing this week to evaluate four housing laws passed in the last session that were meant to increase housing supply by reducing regulatory barriers to residential development.

The re-evaluation comes amid concerns from state leaders that some local governments have resisted or found ways around the new laws, which:

  • reduce the ability of neighbors to delay zoning changes (HB 24)

  • allow housing development in commercially zoned areas without requiring zoning changes (SB 840)

  • prevent larger cities from imposing certain restrictions on residential lot density (SB 15), and

  • limit how strictly cities can regulate residential occupancy (SB 1567).

Supporters, including State Senator Paul Bettencourt and local developers, say the measures cut unnecessary bureaucracy and make it easier to build housing, ultimately increasing supply and affordability. Several cities oppose the legislation, arguing that the laws reduce local control and could overwhelm municipal infrastructure like roads, parks, and sewer systems without requiring developers to offset the costs. Before the bills took effect last September, some cities — Irving, Arlington, and Plano among them — had already passed local ordinances, including height and electric vehicle charger requirements, that make dense housing developments expensive to develop, The Texan reports.

California sues five cities over stalled housing plans
The state announced legal action against Calexico, Costa Mesa, Half Moon Bay, Ridgecrest, and Turlock for failing to update state-mandated housing production plans. Those five cities were among 15 that were warned in March that they were out of compliance with California’s Housing Element Law, which requires localities to update housing plans every eight years to meet regional needs.

“California can’t solve the housing crisis while some cities sit on their hands and dare us to do something about it,” Governor Gavin Newsom wrote in the press release announcing the action. “These five jurisdictions had every chance to follow the law and plan for their fair share of housing. They chose not to, so now they’ll answer for it in court. Housing law applies statewide, and no city gets a pass.”

Mandates like this are not unheard of in California, where, just this May, Huntington Beach had to pay $160,000 plus $50,000 for every month it stays out of compliance beginning in June for failing to plan for housing at all income levels. “NIMBYs be warned”: Newsom is coming for you.

Seattle commits $110 million to affordable rental housing
Mayor Katie Wilson announced a Notice of Funding Availability that will support new construction, rehabilitation, and acquisition of affordable rental units. The funding comes primarily from the 2023 Seattle Housing Levy and emphasizes permanent supportive housing and family-sized units, KOMO News reports.

Not in her backyard!
Read San Francisco Chronicle columnist Joe Garofoli’s account of a NIMBY showdown in YIMBY champion and Oakland Assembly Member Buffy Wicks’s backyard. A 415-unit senior-living campus on the site of a Trader Joe’s was proposed, and, as Garofoli writes, “the NIMBY chickens have come home to roost.”

Market conditions

Seattle layoffs weigh on local housing market
D.R. Horton, the nation’s largest homebuilder by volume, identified a wave of Seattle-area layoffs — concentrated primarily in tech — as a drag on regional home sales and just another sign that job loss in one of the country’s most expensive metros is pushing listings onto the market and softening prices, Bloomberg reports.

New England vacancy rates may understate the region’s housing crunch
A Boston Globe analysis of LendingTree vacancy data warns that vacancy figures across New England are misleading for buyers since many of those “vacant” units are seasonal vacation homes. Even if the homes aren’t occupied, they aren’t technically unoccupied, and the housing shortage is not ameliorating as prospective buyers might hope.

Maryland home construction slumps amid shortage
The Baltimore Banner reports a slowdown in new home construction across Maryland, even as the state faces a housing shortage. According to the U.S. Census Bureau, this year “the state is on track to build fewer new housing units than in any year since the Great Recession,” the Banner reports.
