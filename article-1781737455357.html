Congress cuts a deal on the 21st Century ROAD to Housing Act

Meanwhile, housing starts and Houston homeownership dip, even as YIGBY persists.

Halina Bennet
Jun 17
∙
Paid
 
READ IN APP
 

After months of negotiations, lawmakers announced a bicameral agreement Tuesday on a final version of the 21st Century ROAD to Housing Act. If enacted, it will be the largest federal housing package in modern American history.

This agreement caps a long legislative process that began with the Senate’s initial housing bill last summer. And despite plenty of twists along the way, the package has landed in a place that supply-oriented reformers are very happy with.

The most recent version combines the House-passed bill with Senate provisions that were added during negotiations. All told, it covers more than 50 distinct policy areas across housing supply, manufactured housing, mortgage financing, rural housing, veteran housing, and community banking. But in terms of last-minute wrangling, the two most important disputes between the two legislative bodies both were resolved in a pro-supply way: The House accepted the Senate’s Build Now Act proposal, and the Senate agreed to the House’s preferred language to protect institutional investment in build-to-rent housing projects.

This latter piece averts what would have been a ban on large institutional investors buying single-family homes, resolving an issue that received national attention.

Under the new compromise, any entity controlling 350 or more single-family properties would be barred from buying more. But the language features carve-outs for certain build-to-rent, rent-to-own, and renovate-to-rent programs.

The version the Senate originally sent to the House had required large investors to divest from excepted properties within seven years, a rule that homebuilders believe would have essentially killed build-to-rent as an industry. The House stripped that mandate and broadened the build-to-rent exemptions, and this latest bicameral agreement includes the House’s version of that particular provision. In exchange, the House accepted a proposal from Senators Elizabeth Warren and John Kennedy to tweak the Community Development Block Grant program to reward jurisdictions that embrace land use reform and mildly penalize those that block new housing.

President Donald Trump has already acted on this large-investors issue via executive order, directing the Federal Trade Commission to investigate anti-competitive behavior by corporate landlords. As originally written, the Senate legislation was aligned with his stated priorities.

Supply advocates were quick to claim the win. Inclusive Abundance in Action President Derek Kaufman, whose group spent months pushing for provisions that would boost construction in the bill, wrote in a statement to Slow Boring that the deal “delivers across the board.” He credits the bipartisan, bicameral negotiating process — specifically Banking Chair Tim Scott and Ranking Member Elizabeth Warren in the Senate and House Financial Services Chair French Hill and Ranking Member Maxine Waters in the House, who shepherded the bill and led the months-long negotiations.

The Senate is expected to hold a final vote on the bill later this week before returning it to the House for one last vote before it heads to the president’s desk.

Another House bill attempts to accelerate affordable construction

Meanwhile, Representatives Mike Flood (R-NE) and Maggie Goodlander (D-NH) introduced legislation this week targeting a bottleneck in affordable housing construction.

The Build Housing Affordably Act would temporarily exempt certain federally funded affordable housing projects from the requirements of Build America, Buy America, a federal law that encourages the use of domestically produced materials, while HUD studies how those rules are affecting housing production. Rather than eliminate the requirements entirely, the bill would pause them until their effects on development are better understood.

Build America, Buy America was intended to support American manufacturing, but affordable housing developers have been complaining for years that many needed materials are not available from domestic suppliers, resulting in slower construction and hundreds of thousands of dollars in unnecessary costs, the Associated Press reports.

The Build Housing Affordably Act has support from a broad coalition of housing groups, including the National Association of Home Builders, the Affordable Housing Tax Credit Coalition, the Mortgage Bankers Association, the National Apartment Association, the National Association of REALTORS®, the National Low Income Housing Coalition, the National Multifamily Housing Council, and Up for Growth Action.

Housing starts hit a six-year low

The Census Bureau released its May residential construction data on Tuesday, and the numbers are bleak. Total housing starts fell 15.4 percent from April to a seasonally adjusted annual rate of 1.177 million — the lowest reading since April 2020, and well below the 1.43 million that analysts had projected. Year-over-year starts were down 8.7 percent.

The regional picture was uneven. While the figures out of the South and West both fell from a year ago, the Northeast and Midwest were both up. And on the product side, single-family starts declined by a relatively modest 1.9 percent from April (to an 882,000 annualized pace, down 6.7 percent year-over-year), while multifamily starts fell sharply, down 40.2 percent from April and 14.2 percent from a year ago. Builders cited tariff-driven materials inflation and persistently high financing costs as the main culprits behind the pullback, Stateline reports.

HousingWire provides a different read on the situation, arguing that the May drop isn’t a panic — it reflects builders deliberately slowing starts to work down inventory and protect margins, a strategic decision made earlier in the spring selling season rather than a reaction to deteriorating demand. The permits data backs this theory up, as building authorizations were essentially flat at 1.41 million.

Still, the drop in starts to pandemic levels is a bad sign for both state and federal progress on ameliorating the housing shortage.

Houston’s housing affordability crisis continues to deepen

According to the 2026 State of Housing in Harris County and Houston report from Rice University’s Kinder Institute for Urban Research, more than half of Houston-area renters are now considered cost-burdened, meaning they spend more than 30 percent of their income on housing, as rent growth continues to outpace income gains.

Even as home prices have stabilized, rising insurance premiums and other ownership costs are eroding affordability, resulting in more than a quarter of homeowners reporting they are being similarly cost-burdened, according to the report. As a result, homeownership rates declined across Houston and Harris County between 2023 and 2024, with homeownership among Black households in Houston declining 15.6 percent year over year, the largest decrease recorded in more than 15 years.

Researchers warned that the trend could have long-term implications for wealth-building and economic mobility, ABC13 reports.

Houston’s longstanding reputation as an affordable major metro is under increasing pressure, even as the city leads in housing construction, with housing costs consuming a growing share of household budgets and eviction filings remaining above pre-pandemic levels.

Baltimore’s affordable housing is in danger

One of Baltimore’s largest community land trusts is facing mounting financial and legal troubles that could have implications far beyond the organization, according to a Baltimore Banner investigation published this week. The North East Housing Initiative, a nonprofit that has helped create affordable homeownership opportunities in East Baltimore, is under F.B.I. investigation related to roughly $2.2 million in cryptocurrency donations received in 2025 and other financial issues.

The North East Housing Initiative, which has received at least $3.2 million in city support since 2023, owns or manages about 40 homes through a community-land-trust model designed to preserve long-term affordability. C.E.O. Garrick Good said the organization’s financial challenges worsened after federal authorities froze about $1.6 million in cryptocurrency assets, though he denied any wrongdoing and said neither he nor the organization is the target of the investigation.

The nonprofit has also defaulted on multiple lines of credit, faces ongoing litigation from lenders seeking repayment, and recently laid off its entire full-time staff, the Banner reports.

Housing advocates say the organization’s struggles could have ripple effects for Baltimore’s broader community-land-trust movement, which relies on public and philanthropic support to expand affordable homeownership opportunities and prevent displacement.

Spreading the YIGBY word

Religious institutions are an increasingly robust source of affordable housing development as congregations across the country rethink how to use underutilized land and aging buildings, the Washington Post reports. According to Census Bureau data cited by the Post, spending on religious construction rose 17 percent in 2025, making it the fastest-growing category of private nonresidential construction in the country.

A growing share of that investment is tied to housing. A recent Rutgers University report identified 200 faith-based housing projects completed between 2015 and 2025, creating nearly 10,000 affordable homes, with development activity accelerating in recent years. New state laws in Virginia, California, and Florida have also made it easier for houses of worship to build affordable housing on their properties.

One example of a successful project cited by the Post is Central United Methodist Church in Arlington, Va., which recently completed an $84.8 million redevelopment that replaced much of its traditional campus with an eight-story affordable housing building, keeping worship and community space on the ground floor. Similar projects are underway nationwide, as congregations seek new revenue sources, respond to housing shortages, and adapt to declining participation in organized religion.

Nationally, more than 47,000 acres of faith-owned land could potentially be developed for housing, according to 2023 estimates from the Terner Center for Housing Innovation at the University of California, Berkeley.

The “Yes in God’s Backyard” (YIGBY) movement has been picking up policy support across the country. California’s SB 4 (2023) allows faith institutions and nonprofit colleges to build affordable housing on their land with by-right approval, overriding local zoning. Virginia passed similar legislation this spring that will take effect in 2027. New York is working on its own version. At the federal level, the bipartisan Faith in Housing Act from Representatives Scott Peters (D-CA) and Chuck Edwards (R-NC) would create a national framework.
