Where key battleground races stand four months before the midterm elections

There is plenty of time before November, but, halfway through the primaries, conditions are telling.

Halina Bennet
Jul 6
∙
Paid
 
READ IN APP
 

Democrats continue to head into the 2026 midterms with the political winds at their backs, at least according to the early polling.

The generic congressional ballot has held steady in recent weeks, with most averages giving Democrats a mid-single-digit advantage nationally. FiftyPlusOne has Democrats at 49.1 percent to Republicans’ 43.5 percent as of July 3, while Morning Consult’s tracker shows Democrats ahead of Republicans by four points among registered voters, with independents breaking D+12 — a 9-point shift toward Democrats since the start of the term.

At the same time, President Donald Trump’s approval ratings remain deeply underwater, particularly among independents, a dynamic that has historically posed challenges for the president’s party in midterm elections. Silver Bulletin has his net approval at -17.8 as of Sunday, July 5, which is slightly improved from -18.9 the week before, though still worse than Joe Biden’s was at the same point in his term. Other trackers put Trump’s net approval somewhere between the high negative teens and the low negative 20s.

It’s early, and individual races will ultimately be shaped by local dynamics, candidate quality, and turnout. But for now, the national indicators continue to point in Democrats’ favor as the campaign season begins to take shape.

Senate battlegrounds

The New York Times and Siena University did some serious polling during the second half of June. Those numbers (which have limited utility this far out from November), combined with conditions on the ground, give us a sense of where key battleground races stand four months before the midterms:

Maine: The race between Senator Susan Collins and Graham Platner is a real toss-up. A late-June New York Times / Portland Press Herald / Siena University poll of 608 likely voters found Platner up by two points, which is within the margin of error. Another poll taken over nearly the same period by Fox News / Beacon Research / Shaw & Company Research has Collins up by three points. NBC’s politics newsletter asserts that Collins’s vulnerability is that better-educated voters — who tend to support Democrats anyway and are also more likely to actually vote — have drifted away from her, even as non-college voters still like her. Platner continues to deal with fallout from personal-conduct controversies.

Politico reported today that a woman who dated Platner alleged that he sexually assaulted her five years ago. Platner denied her accusations, but his campaign postponed events early in the week.

Alaska: One of the strangest stories of the 2026 cycle is unfolding in Alaska, where a Republican challenger with the same name as incumbent Senator Dan Sullivan has qualified for the ballot. NBC News reports that state and federal authorities are investigating whether the candidate was recruited as part of a conspiracy to confuse voters and help Democrat Mary Peltola, though no charges have been filed. The dispute has already sparked a court fight over whether the challenger should appear on the ballot and how election officials should distinguish between the two candidates. Because Alaska uses a top-four primary, in which all candidates run on the same ballot before the top finishers advance to a ranked-choice general election, even small amounts of voter confusion could have outsized political consequences. The F.B.I., Alaska’s attorney general, and the U.S. attorney’s office are all involved.

The N.Y.T./Siena poll has Sullivan (the incumbent, not the other Sullivan) leading Peltola by two points, 47 to 45 percent. Most polls so far — many of which are by Alaska Survey Research — have Peltola leading by single-digit margins.

Georgia: Senator Jon Ossoff appears to have the edge against Republican Mike Collins, helped by a drawn-out G.O.P. primary that allowed him to build his resources, Politico reports. A Fox News / Beacon Research / Shaw & Company Research poll from the end of June had Ossoff up 13 points among Georgia registered voters.

North Carolina: CNN’s July Senate rankings put this race at the top of the flip list, as former Governor Roy Cooper’s bid puts Democrats in position to win a North Carolina Senate seat for the first time since 2008. The N.Y.T./Siena poll taken during the second half of June found Cooper leading his Republican opponent, Michael Whatley, 50 to 43 percent.

Ohio: Former Senator Sherrod Brown’s bid to return to the Senate has been — and remains — uncertain. N.Y.T./Siena found incumbent Jon Husted at 50 percent and Brown at 47 percent among likely voters, while a Fox poll from the end of May showed Brown leading by a wider margin, 53 to 45 percent.

Iowa: State Representative Josh Turek’s primary win put Iowa in the conversation for Democrats, but the path to the seat is not obvious. The N.Y.T./Siena poll had U.S. Representative Ashley Hinson at 48 percent and Turek at 46 percent in June, while Fox gave the Democrat a four-point edge during the same period. Hinson had a fundraising advantage, ending the first quarter with $6.5 million on hand.

Texas: Democrat James Talarico and Republican Attorney General Ken Paxton are tied at 47 percent each in the N.Y.T./Siena battleground poll.

New Hampshire: The primary is not until September 8, but U.S. Representative Chris Pappas is the clear favorite among Democrats, while Republicans are caught up in a battle between two former senators — each of whom has previously lost to incumbent Senator Jeanne Shaheen, the Democrat who is vacating the seat in question. Trump-endorsed John Sununu, who held the seat until Shaheen bested him in 2008, holds the lead over Scott Brown, who was a U.S. senator representing Massachusetts.

Michigan: With State Senator Mallory McMorrow announcing yesterday that she was suspending her campaign, the Democratic primary race has narrowed down to two candidates: U.S. Representative Haley Stevens and progressive insurgent Abdul El-Sayed. Stevens is Chuck Schumer’s pick, and El-Sayed is Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez’s, setting the primary up to be the clearest test of the establishment-versus-insurgent battle that has been playing out to varying degrees of clarity in New York City, Washington, D.C., Los Angeles, and Colorado.
