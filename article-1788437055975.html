Free Range Parenting Is Illegal

Sometimes, neurotic parenting is the result of neurotic laws and neurotic communities.

Cartoons Hate Her
Sep 3
∙
Paid
 
READ IN APP
 

A little over a year ago, I wrote about the parental safetyism arms race, or the idea that parenting norms are being defined by the most risk-averse parents, eventually extending to “normal” parents who feel pressured to comply with these changing norms, lest a neighbor call CPS on them (or, more realistically, lest they be harshly judged or lectured by other parents.)

Per that article, most parents are less risk-averse than the social media discourse would have you believe. Even most parents who follow this Substack, who tend to be the exact profile you would expect of a helicopter parent (often but not always white, upper middle class, liberal, and millennial) believed the following:

  • 84% felt it was safe for a 6-10 year-old to walk 5-10 minutes to school

  • 86% felt it was safe to bring a baby under 4 months to a crowded indoor family gathering

  • 62% felt it was safe to allow a 10-13 year-old to ride the subway or city bus during the day

  • 78% felt it was safe to allow a 10-13 year-old to fly as an unaccompanied minor to visit a relative who would pick them up at the airport

  • 84% felt it was safe to allow a 9-year-old to sleep over at a friend’s house

  • 97% felt it was safe to allow a 9-year-old to sleep over at a cousin’s house (assuming there was no specific red flag about the cousin.)

And yet, these are topics rife for debate. If you mostly consumed news and social media and had very few real-life conversations, you’d think most parents thought them wildly unsafe. New “helicopter parenting” trends have emerged, such as the end of sleepovers, with parents saying things like: “The possibility that my child might have a good time, or come back with a trauma — what do you think I’m picking?” When a child is playing in his own back yard, CPS is sometimes called, and for some reason they screen in these reports and sometimes open investigations. When a six-year-old boy was spotted playing in his own back yard while his mom was inside, CPS was called, and they proceeded to interview him about whether or not anyone was showing him pornography—something he probably didn’t know existed. A mom of a ten-year-old was arrested when the child was spotted going for a walk outside. And, most recently, a mom was sentenced to six months suspended sentence for letting her five-year-old walk half a mile within a gated community. She will be on the child abuse registry for seven years.

There’s clearly a disconnect between the things most parents feel are fine, and the way society at large overreacts to these things. And yet, for whatever reason, the zeitgeist still relies on blaming “neurotic millennial parents” for adhering to these norms.

Now, I’m a bit of a helicopter parent myself. I also have OCD, which is focused primarily on protecting my loved ones, so I’d be the first to admit that sometimes I take it too far. I am also actively working with a therapist on new “exposures” which are sometimes laughably tame (“Let’s try letting your in-laws babysit your kids while they’re awake!”)

But I know I’m a little nutso and I don’t project this onto everyone else. I am scared of letting my six-year-old walk to school, but I wouldn’t dream on calling the authorities on parents who did this. I don’t even think it’s objectively dangerous! I just know I’m too anxious.

The thing is, I don’t believe the laws have OCD. I don’t think CPS has OCD. Maybe everyone on Reddit does, but I don’t think OCD—which affects a pretty small segment of the population—is behind these ridiculous policies and actions.

Maybe some millennial parents actually are neurotic, and would behave the way they’re behaving even if there were no CPS or law enforcement related risks to allowing their kids some freedom. But many other see the news, they read posts on Reddit asking if CPS should be called on a neighbor for allowing their kids to use water balloons on the porch, and figure they’re better safe than sorry.

Yes, it’s pretty unusual that a neighbor would be nosey or evil enough to call CPS on you for neglect when the “unsupervised” children in question are just playing in a gated back yard, but it happens often enough for reasonable parents to be scared. As one mom from Virginia writes, after CPS was called on her children for playing in their own yard and in the neighbor’s yard, they opened a formal investigation and instructed her that her children could never be left alone, not even in their own bedrooms, until age thirteen. And while this person likely had a vengeful neighbor trying to make life harder for her, that could happen to anyone. Reddit is also full of queries from people who called CPS for the same reason.

Neighbors seem to be leveraging CPS as a last resort when annoying neighborhood kids trespass onto their property. A neighbor’s child creeping into your yard is certainly irritating, and it warrants a talk with the parents, but even if the parents do nothing after you speak with them, CPS and the police should simply not be involved. CPS is not a last resort for revenge.

But despite people like this existing, people still blame the parents for their “irrational” neuroticism. Yes, some parents are truly more risk-averse than necessary, and there’s no objective truth about what is or isn’t safe. But also, some parents just don’t want to deal with CPS (or the police) showing up at their door because their seven-year-old was walking around the neighborhood doing kid things.

This is where we enter a sort of meta-risk-aversion, where parents who don’t believe that playing in a back yard is unsafe will still avoid it because they believe it’s too high of a risk of CPS or police involvement. I’m aware some parents will tell me they let their kids do this, and it’s fine, and they’ve never once worried about it, and nobody they know worries about it. Good for you! Other parents see ordinary moms and dads arrested or harassed by authorities for letting their kids walk to a nearby playground and think, ehh maybe not.

Oh? You think I made that one up? I didn’t. In Florida, a mom was arrested for letting her seven-year-old walk to the park, which was a ten-minute walk. The officer who arrested her justified his decision by explaining that sex offenders lived nearby. (There is unfortunately no neighborhood completely devoid of sex offenders!)

Overzealous or unnecessary CPS involvement is also not rare, and directly impacts poor families the most. A whopping 37% of American children will be the subject of a CPS investigation before the age of eighteen, including more than half of Black kids. The vast majority of CPS allegations (75%) are about neglect, not abuse. Of course, plenty of neglect allegations are true, but some of them are, well, a child playing in his own back yard. And on top of that, CPS is swarmed with calls that don’t even meet the criteria to be screened in. CPS has an important job to do, but is likely forced to deal with too many bogus calls and clearly ridiculous investigations. A two-year-old walking through the neighborhood at night in a soiled diaper is very different from a school-aged child playing in a gated back yard or walking to the park.

People also feel the need to criminalize parents whose children have tragic accidents, because they can usually trace the accident back to one minor mistake or moment of distraction. Whenever I see a headline about a child drowning or dying from some preventable accident, the highest-ranked comment is someone demanding the parents be jailed. Sure, there are situations where parents have been willfully neglectful, but many accidents happen to normal parents who missed one thing. Some folks have brought up that the mom who was arrested for letting her five-year-old walk within the gated community had allowed him to pass by a pond, which was a drowning risk. Yes, that is bad! Young kids should never be allowed near open bodies of water! But must this mistake be…criminalized? With seven years on a child abuse registry? Some decent parents will make a few suboptimal choices, and sure, a word of warning about water safety might help them keep their kids safe, but I’m not convinced the law needs to take further action.

The impacts are twofold: first of all, parents might avoid letting their kids do something perfectly safe in case an anonymous observer decides they disagree. But also, with fewer kids permitted to play outside or walk to school, it genuinely becomes less safe to allow your children to do these things. Safety exists in numbers. Young kids are far safer playing outside when they’re in the company of slightly older kids, or even a group of kids their own age. But if only 11% of kids walk to school, compared with a majority of kids who walked to school in previous decades, then even if you think it’s fine for your kid to do it, they’ll likely be walking alone, not with a group of their friends.

Even helicopter parents like myself are not spared from the safetyism brigade. I really thought that the only thing anyone could criticize me for was being too safety-obsessed. I was wrong! I once took my two kids (at the time, ages five and two) to a children’s museum. While my two-year-old was in her stroller, my five-year-old asked if he could look into the gift shop. Because the stroller was too big to maneuver around the gift shop, I told him to go ahead and I’d keep my eye on him. I was at the entrance of the gift shop and my son was less than ten feet away so I let him look at the toys. Within a couple minutes, the cashier began yelling at my son and asking him “if he had a grownup.” When he pointed to me, the cashier informed me that I had let him “roam unattended.”

Figuring this person was just really unhinged or having an exceptionally bad day at work, I did what any terminally online mom would do: I ranted about it on Twitter. Most people were supportive, but a few sided with the employee. Multiple people said “it only takes a second” for a child to be kidnapped, even with the context that I was blocking the only way out of the store. Multiple people accused me of neglect. Someone else postulated that my son must have been damaging the property of the store (he wasn’t.) People told me that if I wasn’t able to hover over both children simultaneously, I should have avoided any locations where my older child might wander off. I was told I should have unbuckled my two-year-old to follow my older child around the store (I can tell you exactly how that would have gone—chasing after both kids while they ran in different directions.)

We have to share our society with these people, and they’re making it worse!

This is where you might be tempted to bring up the birth rate, everyone’s favorite justification to complain about their least favorite thing—girlbosses, neuroticism, loneliness, capitalism, whatever. Look, I don’t think people are consciously deciding not to have kids in the event that a crotchety store employee yells at you for letting a five-year-old walk around. I think the birth rate issue has more to do with people taking longer (if ever) to find a spouse in the first place, which is downstream from people having fewer social connections in general. (Oh God, not the birth rate being used as justification for my favorite topic!)

But when it comes to the amount of children we consider “reasonable,” part of this equation is how much time and energy we would need to devote to keeping each one safe. If you have more than two children, you are simply outnumbered. As a mom of two with a back yard, there are days when our six-year-old just randomly decides he wants to run into the back yard while my husband and I are taking care of his sister and cooking, and one of us has to drop everything to join him outside, lest someone call the cops—and this is with two WFH parents who are basically always available, which is far from the norm! If you want to have multiple children, you should be able to do so without worrying about law enforcement—or even just harassment and nasty remarks that go nowhere. You have to accept that sometimes, your kids—especially older ones—will be doing their own thing, possibly (gasp) outside!

The type of parenting that flew in the 1950s, or even the 1980s, is just not allowed today. In a best case scenario, you get judged and lectured, and in a worst case scenario, you get arrested.

So, are parents neurotic? Sure. I will gladly wear my “This is What a Neurotic Millennial Mom Looks Like” T-shirt, if only it did not elicit responses of, “Yeah, that’s exactly what I imagined.” But these parents are not alone in their anxiety. They are surrounded by a system of neighbors, anonymous onlookers and other people holding them to this standard.
