Learning from Poland’s economic success story

“European” features like universal health care and a high minimum wage are fine.

Jun 3
∙

A debate has been raging on the internet between Paul Krugman (here, here, and here) and three European economists — Philippe Aghion, Antonin Bergeaud, and Luis Garicano — in which Krugman maintains that the conventional wisdom that America’s productivity has risen faster than Europe’s is mistaken. This all hinges on technical issues about the construction of purchasing power parities, but the debate is being waged with a level of emotionality and vehemence that suggests something more.

Nobody is quite saying it explicitly, but I think that Americans like to argue about “Europe” (a construct that of course flattens major differences between countries) primarily because it’s actually an argument about the United States.

There’s a sense that progressive Americans want to make our country more “like Europe” and that therefore if Europe is bad that says bad things about the progressive project, whereas if Europe is good that says good things about the project. Of course this involves huge handwaving oversimplifications, but I do think it basically captures what’s going on here.

Much of the territory this debate covers is well trod. But I realized it might be interesting to consider it from the perspective of a country that is almost universally seen as an economic success story: Poland.

Poland was poorer than Western Europe dating back to well before World War II, and it then suffered for decades under Communist economic mismanagement and domination by the Soviet Union. But since the fall of the Berlin Wall, Poland has done extremely well. It’s not that Poland is suddenly the richest country in the world, but it’s growing more rapidly than the U.S., catching up to the global frontier, and threatening to soon leave stagnating countries like Italy and the United Kingdom in the dust.

Poland is an interesting development success story in part because it became rich through a model that always seemed like it should work according to a textbook but that in practice people rarely if ever saw. In fact, until recent years, it had become a bit of conventional wisdom that you actually couldn’t grow as rich as Poland has this way.

But Poland is also interesting because it poses the question of what are the stakes in this discourse exactly?

Conservatives are normally happy to acknowledge Poland as a success. The chart above tells an “up from Communism” kind of story, pregnant with implications for how prosperous Cuba might become if it ditched a failed economic model and embraced economic integration with the enormous and very rich country directly to its north.

And yet, insofar as “Europe” means anything in public policy terms, I think Poland definitely counts as part of “Europe.” Not just geographically, but in the sense that the main elements of the social model that American progressives like are present there.

Poland’s growth model

But first, the part of this story that I find most interesting.

Poland’s economic growth has been driven largely by foreign direct investment. That’s a striking contrast to the growth model Joe Studwell and Ha-Joon Chang have touted in their books looking primarily at the success of Japan, Taiwan, South Korea, and mainland China.

The story they told about Asian growth is that a naive belief in establishing sound economic fundamentals isn’t enough. Economic-development success stories, on this view, require you to develop home-grown companies. Openness to investment and general embrace of capitalism are part of the story, but you also need a lot of “industrial policy” bells and whistles to grow your own Samsung, Toyota, TSMC, or BYD.

But while there are obviously Polish companies in Poland, they aren’t really global success stories. The six biggest Polish companies by revenue all have the Polish state as their largest shareholder. Three of them are electric utilities, one is an oil company, and one is a lottery that seems to have expanded into other gambling pursuits. Then there’s KGHM, which seems to be a real mining conglomerate with global operations. The seventh biggest company, Dino, is a supermarket chain.

Which is just to say there is no Polish manufacturing champion out there crushing it in global export markets. But even without big, famous Polish manufacturing companies, the country has a thriving export sector with Polish-made cars and car parts, computers, gas turbines, monitors, and batteries sold on world markets.

So what’s the deal? Well, foreign companies — especially, though not exclusively, German ones — invested a lot of money in setting up factories in Poland.

In turn, fully a quarter of Polish exports go to Germany. A lot of German companies partially offloaded their supply chains to Poland, where labor is cheaper, taking advantage of Polish membership in the European Union to make things seamless. And the Polish government has welcomed foreign investment by setting up special export zones even before E.U. accession and being open to investment from countries other than Germany as well.

Investors seem to have a lot of confidence that Poland is not going to expropriate their stuff or discriminate against foreign companies to advantage politically connected locals, so Poland just keeps converging with richer European countries.

An optimist might have hoped this would work for Mexico in the wake of NAFTA.

But even though Mexico has attracted a lot of American investment and has some successful export sectors, the Mexican economy as a whole has not done very well. The basic story people tell about this is that Mexico has become bifurcated between a relatively small export-oriented formal sector and a much larger economy of locally facing service companies that have very low productivity and little growth and prevent the dynamic export sector from driving broader prosperity. This is all connected to the infamous problem of drug cartels, massive corruption, and high levels of violence.

Even more prosperous places have some version of this dual economy: High tech drives the economy of the Bay Area, but way more people work in restaurants or health care than in tech. Globally traded exports are just not that labor-intensive.

But Poland, unlike Mexico, has a basically functioning state with the rule of law, so the benefits of its growing export sector spread out.

A European social model

Obviously, membership in the E.U. is a big driver of success here.

If you go back to the origins of the Russia-Ukraine war, a big issue is that for all its many flaws, the E.U. has been a growth platform for the countries of Central and Eastern Europe. An independent Ukraine naturally wants in on that, which would mean falling out of the Russian sphere of influence. Russia, by contrast, wants to keep Ukraine in its sphere of influence, which denies the country its best chance at economic development.

But Poland is also “European” for the purposes of American political arguments.

Their minimum wage, for example, is over 50 percent of the national average wage and more like two-thirds of the national median. Going off the average, this would be something like an $18/hour minimum wage in the U.S.

Poland has a national health-insurance fund called NFZ that offers universal coverage, which can be supplemented by paying out of pocket for private clinics or purchasing private add-on insurance. This is not “Medicare for All” as defined by American progressives, who are modeling their proposal on Canada’s Medicare system, which works hard to restrict private health care. It’s more like Medicare, the actual American public health-insurance program for seniors, which has always been supplemented by private care.

In Poland, they have 2.5 guns per hundred people — much lower than France or Germany (19.6 per hundred each) to say nothing of the U.S., where we have more guns than people.

Warsaw has a two-line metro system, with plans in the works to build three more lines. This is in a metro area of about 3.2 million people, similar to Tampa or San Diego.

Kraków has an extensive tram system that’s going to be partially upgraded to “premetro” status with a few tunnels. The Gdańsk / Sopot / Gdynia tri-city area has a S-Bahn system providing mass transit services.

There’s more to life than trains, (lack of) guns, publicly provided health care, and high minimum wages. But along with nice old buildings (which Poland has some of, though fewer than most European countries due to wartime destruction), I think these are the main sources of progressives’ Europe envy.

Actual European public policy is more complicated than this. In France and many other continental countries, for example, there is a requirement for rich people to give a minimum amount of inheritance to their kids when they die. You can’t leave 70 percent of your fortune to charity and divide 30 percent among your two kids — they each get 25 percent minimum. That’s nuts! But it’s not for the sake of such policies that American progressives feel compelled to defend Europe’s honor.

Tech start-ups are good

To be clear, despite 30 years of rapid economic growth, Poland is still poorer than Italy or France, to say nothing of the U.S. It’s not like they’ve solved all their problems.

Poland has prospered thanks primarily to foreign direct investment dragging the country’s manufacturing sector up closer and closer to German standards. But I do think some of its more “European” aspects have been helpful here.

The high minimum wage, for example, helps ensure that even though the export sector is a minority of the workforce, upward pressure on wages from successful export jobs leads to higher pay across the board. You could imagine a world in which the Polish economy was much more segmented, with foreign investment driving higher pay for a few without directly benefiting most Polish people. That could set the stage for backlash against the investment or efforts to expropriate it or other problems.

Still, you can’t get richer than Germany just by having German companies open factories in your country. Eventually, Poland will want to have what the U.S. has: companies operating on the global technological frontier.

That’s hard to pull off.

It’s a genuine achievement of American economic policy that we have so many of these companies. Realistically, we probably also got a bit lucky. The benefits of those companies being present in the U.S. — or, really, in the San Francisco Bay Area and Seattle specifically — have been smaller than they ought to have been thanks to constrained housing supply. But when thinking about what Poland or any other European country could do to foster more high tech, it’s important to avoid hazy generalizations and think specifically.

It’s clearly not the case that better mass transit infrastructure would kill tech start-ups. Would stricter gun regulation? Of course not. How about if we had higher payroll taxes and public sector provision of health insurance? Again, no. If anything, that would make life easier for start-ups. And it would have zero implications whatsoever for founders or investment in high-growth start-ups.

Europe’s problems in this regard almost certainly relate to the nitty-gritty of actually launching a new business quickly and — most of all — the fact that it’s very costly to lay workers off in Europe, which makes it hard to hire or engage in rapid growth. We should not copy those specific aspects of the European social model. Note, though, that California, where most of the successful start-ups are, already has a very high top income-tax rate. For better or worse, that’s not stopping them from having a successful tech sector. But non-tax aspects of the European regulatory system do seem to be making things harder over there.
