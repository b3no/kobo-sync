Cracks in the Republican coalition

Rural and Hispanic voters are souring on Trump and his party, and Democrats are trying to win them over without alienating their own base.

Halina Bennet
Sep 30
∙
Paid
 
READ IN APP
 

The midterm momentum continues to shift left as Democrats post big leads on the generic ballot in new polls, including Quinnipiac’s on control of the U.S. House (September 24-27, 1,032 registered voters) and the Wall Street Journal’s (September 16-21, 1,500 registered voters). Also this week, Cook Political Report moved 15 House races and three Senate races toward Democrats.

As Republicans scramble to hold their seats and their base together, Democrats in swing states are balancing on a tightrope to keep their own coalition intact while figuring out how to appeal to disgruntled G.O.P. voters.

Do I sound like a broken record? Anyway, here’s your weekly midterm update:

Republicans on defense

Trump’s map: President Donald Trump begins his next swing of campaign stops on Thursday with a stop in Durant, Okla., followed by an appearance in Mobile, Ala., on Friday — two states where he won roughly two-thirds of the vote in 2024. Politico reports he will also visit Iowa, Florida, Texas, and Alaska, which are all places Republicans in the recent past counted as safe. Politico’s reporting, which includes interviews with G.O.P. insiders, frames the president’s schedule as a real sign of trouble for the party:

“We are defending more than [Democrats] are,” a person close to the White House told Politico.

The Republican National Committee says that the president is simply energizing the base.

The ad fight: A pro-Trump television ad ending with “Paid for by the U.S. Government” began airing September 23 on Fox News and Newsmax. The Associated Press reports spending of at least $443,000 on the ad by Friday, when a second ad had already begun airing. One such publicly funded ad, which began airing Saturday, is nearly identical to an ad the president ran during his 2024 campaign for re-election, the New York Times reports. While the White House calls the ads “public service announcements,” ethics experts cited by the Times say that the administration is violating federal laws against using taxpayer money for propaganda. The rerun from 2024, in particular, raises legal issues due to its previous use boosting the candidate during a campaign.

Public Citizen has filed a complaint, urging the Federal Communications Commission, Federal Trade Commission, and broadcasters to stop airing the commercials.

The ads have received scrutiny from both sides of the aisle. Among the many Democrats disavowing the commercials, members of the House Appropriations Committee accused the White House of unlawfully using Customs and Border Protection funding for the spots. And as for the G.O.P., Senator Thom Tillis compared the ads to something Hungary’s Viktor Orbán would do, The Hill reports. Senate Majority Leader John Thune told Bloomberg that taxpayers should not be made to foot this bill.

Where the money is moving: The Senate Leadership Fund — the primary super PAC aligned with the Senate G.O.P. — canceled about $630,000 in ad reservations in Georgia over the weekend, the Atlanta Journal Constitution reports, though it also bought $1.7 million in airtime last week. The funding shuffle comes days after Politico reported that Senator Tim Scott, who is chair of the National Republican Senatorial Committee, neglected to include Georgia and North Carolina on his list of spending targets in the final stretch of the midterm, signaling that the Senate G.O.P. may be considering those once-battleground states as lost causes. The N.R.S.C. called the report “completely fabricated” after it was published. Thune said in a news conference on Friday that he continues to support Representative Mike Collins, the Republican nominee for Senate in Georgia.

Democrats on offense

Democrats’ dollars: Meanwhile, the Democratic Congressional Campaign Committee is putting $11 million into ads across 35 districts, including ones that Republicans recently redrew in Ohio, Texas, Florida, and North Carolina with a deliberate eye toward making them uncompetitive, Semafor reports.

Senator Bernie Sanders is making his own investments, putting hundreds of thousands of dollars from his war chest toward Democrats running in battleground states, including Lieutenant Governor Peggy Flanagan in Minnesota and State Representative Angie Nixon in Florida, Politico reports. In addition to money from his campaign, Sanders has raised more than $2.8 million for candidates through fundraising this cycle.

Some candidates in battleground races are not keen to advertise their association with the democratic socialist. Candidate Sam Forstag, a Democrat running for the U.S. House in Montana, removed Sanders’s endorsement from his campaign website, as well as an endorsement from U.S. Representative Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez, with whom he campaigned in May during his primary. Axios reports that other battleground candidates are distancing themselves from Ocasio-Cortez after visible closeness with the progressive during the primary, choosing instead to appear with more moderate 2028 hopefuls such as former Transportation Secretary Pete Buttigieg, Senator Mark Kelly, former Chicago Mayor Rahm Emanuel, and former Vice President Kamala Harris.

In one such case, Dr. Abdul El-Sayed, Michigan’s Democratic nominee for Senate, spent nearly $1 million on ads featuring Ocasio-Cortez and appeared with her on campaign stops over the summer but has not collaborated with her since. His campaign told Axios he would welcome her back soon, but first, he brought Harris to Michigan last week.

Turnout: Democratic primary turnout surged this year, with voters on the left outpacing Republicans in many of the most closely watched states, like Texas and Michigan, indicating a real enthusiasm gap between the two parties, the New York Times reports. In Texas, about 150,000 more people voted in the Democratic primary than in the Republican primary. During the state’s last midterm primary four years ago, there were about 900,000 more G.O.P. primary votes than Democratic votes, and in 2018 — the last blue wave — 500,000 more people voted in the Republican Senate primary than in the Democratic primary, according to the Times.

Primary elections, of course, see lower turnout than general elections, and Democrats have a greater incentive to turn out for them during a midterm vote that’s occurring under a Republican administration. The numbers signal true buy-in from the left on the effort to flip the House and Senate but are not necessarily indicative of who will turn out in the general. Republican voters who sat out during the primaries could very well go to the polls in November, especially as warning signs mount that the G.O.P. could lose its majority in Congress.

Coalition concerns

Rural voters: Historically considered to be a reliable base for the G.O.P., voters in rural parts of America are growing increasingly frustrated with Trump and his party. A poll from the Associated Press and KFF published last week found that while about half of rural voters identify with Make America Healthy Again, about 80 percent of rural voters overall said that the president’s health care policies have not helped — and in many cases have hurt — their communities. About two-thirds of the MAHA supporters agree.

Rural voters are overwhelmingly focused on health care due to high rates of disease — specifically cancer — in their communities that many believe are caused by the prevalence of chemicals, pesticides, and fertilizer nitrates in agricultural runoff, mixed with limited access to care.

The issue is particularly prevalent in Iowa, which has a high and rising rate of cancer. Trump’s leadership, many are disappointed to find, has not helped to ameliorate the situation. Rob Sand, the state auditor who is running as a Democrat for governor, is harnessing that frustration with a campaign on “costs, corruption, and cancer.”

In Michigan, where more than half of the state’s counties are rural, Dr. El-Sayed, who was endorsed by the Detroit Free Press this week, is targeting MAHA voters through a new health platform that supports a right to sue pesticide makers, calls for removing industry lobbyists from regulatory agencies, and targets lead and microplastics in drinking water, CBS News reports.

Beyond health care, the rising cost of everything from fuel to fertilizer paired with policies that are sowing difficulty for farmers’ and ranchers’ livelihoods are making the once-loyal G.O.P. bloc rethink its allegiance, the Wall Street Journal reports. In Alaska, a red, rural state, everything is more scarce and expensive than in the lower 48, and price increases due to the war in Iran are making Republican voters consider former Representative Mary Peltola, the Democratic nominee for Senate, the Washington Post reports.

And whereas the G.O.P. has long been the party considered friendliest to agriculture, Trump’s handling of tariffs has hit the industry hardest.

Of course most rural voters remain Republican, but some in the agriculture industry plan to cross the aisle in November. After consistently voting Republican for nearly his entire adult life, Donald Chase, a peanut farmer and chair of the Georgia Peanut Commission, told the Atlanta Journal Constitution that he plans to vote for incumbent Senator Jon Ossoff in November.

When I spoke with ranchers and farmers last November, many were already disillusioned with the party and feeling angst about the upcoming growing season. Ten months later, the situation has only gotten worse. Read more about how “America’s Farmers Are Rethinking Their Loyalty to the G.O.P.” in the Journal.

Hispanic voters: It has been established that Hispanic voters are shifting to the left after overwhelmingly turning toward Trump in 2024. The New York Times went to Texas border communities to speak with the voters in question, who reported feeling furious and betrayed by a president they believed would fix immigration and the economy. Instead, the Times found, these voters have lived two years of economic struggle, fear, and heartbreak as Trump’s immigration crackdown impacts their communities.

The Wall Street Journal went to Miami to test whether the same discontent exists among Hispanics in Miami-Dade County, which Trump flipped in 2024. It sure does, according to the Journal.

One candidate capitalizing on his community’s anger toward Trump is Tejano singer Bobby Pulido, whose House run as a Democrat is putting Republicans on notice in a district Trump won by 18 percentage points in 2024. Cook Political Report shifted its forecast of the race this week from “leans Republican” to “tossup.” Read the New York Times’s profile of the candidate and his campaign via quinceañeras, weddings, and football games.
