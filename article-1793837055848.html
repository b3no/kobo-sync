9 short takes on the 2026 midterms

What the midterms mean for 2028, AI, the culture wars, and more

Jerusalem Demsas, Jeremiah Johnson, Kelsey Piper, Maibritt Henkel, and Kobe Yank-Jacobs
Nov 4

When The Argument and Split Ticket did our final pre-election update of our 2026 midterm model, we projected an 11-point Democratic advantage among likely voters. That was among the most bullish projections in the business, but Democrats appear to have done even better. They won upset Senate victories in Texas, Kansas, and South Carolina, plus a commanding impending majority in the House of Representatives.

While we’re still analyzing what happened on Tuesday night, the full implications can’t all be captured by vote tallies. Here are The Argument writers’ key takeaways from results around the country:

The party of compassion won

Jerusalem Demsas: There’s been an enormous amount of discourse about how Democrats need to become more combative, more irreverent, and more extremist. But polling The Argument conducted in the weeks before the election suggests that voters viewed last night’s victorious Democrats as “compassionate,” “moderate,” and “caring about people like me.” Meanwhile, Republicans, who were routed across the country last night, were associated with words like “bold,” “strong,” and “extreme.”

Just take a look at the messaging of some of the star Democratic candidates — ideas like love and respect were pervasive in campaigns from James Talarico in Texas to Adam Hamilton in Kansas to Rob Sand in Iowa. Meanwhile, the Republican Party leaned into a politics of grievance, aggression, and dominance.

There’s a lesson here for both parties: The American people are boring and non-ideological. The candidates who just ran the table were mostly normies.

Fear of a blue Texas

Jeremiah Johnson: Republicans have many reasons to be despondent today, but their pain of losing in Texas is hard to overstate. Texas isn’t just another Republican state. It’s the largest red state in the country and a symbol of American conservatism.

Republicans have won every statewide Texas race for the last 30 years. Conservatives celebrated when Texas gained population from blue states like California or when business moguls like Elon Musk based themselves in the state, touting Texas as a model for conservative governance.

Now Democrats appear set to win all seven of the state’s executive offices, as well as the state’s Senate race. They’re going to flip the Texas state House. And the high-profile races weren’t even close.

James Talarico defeated scandal-plagued Ken Paxton by 12 points, and Gina Hinojosa won the gubernatorial race over mostly scandal-free Greg Abbott by 7 points. Conservative social media spaces are in shock at the scale of the loss.

It’s one thing to lose tight races in swing states. It’s another thing entirely to get blown out in a state you’ve spent generations believing was yours by birthright.

Dem gains in Trump country

Jerusalem Demsas: In 2024, Donald Trump made some of his biggest gains in Democrats’ backyard. Economist Jed Kolko found that urban counties swung by about 10 points toward Trump (more than suburbs, small metros, or rural areas). Last night showed that Democrats can make similar incursions.

In Seward County, Kansas, which Trump carried by 39 points in 2024, Democrat Adam Hamilton leads by 13 points. That’s a 52-point swing. Data scientist Lenny Bronner’s early precinct analysis shows that the reddest precincts are where Democrats have done best in terms of exceeding Harris’ 2024 vote totals.

That doesn’t mean all those Trump votes flipped — different people participate in midterms, and different candidates attract different voters. But these results are a useful corrective to the idea that Republican territory is beyond Democrats’ reach.

The first AI election or the last non-AI election?

Kelsey Piper: In our pre-election polls, only 9% of voters had AI as a top-two issue. And in exit polls, more than half of voters say it’s a major issue, but it still ranks behind more famous suspects like inflation, crime, and immigration. But I think this might understate the issue’s significance.

In 46 different referendums around the country on data centers, it looks to me like the anti-data-center side won almost all of them. I don’t know whether this is the first AI election or the last non-AI election, but it’s one of those.

Liberals for the family

Maibritt Henkel: Family, a little like faith or freedom, can seem like a meaningless word in American politics. But if it’s not part of your platform, you’re done for. I was curious how Democratic candidates in highly competitive races were going to handle that, given how much weight the language of “traditional family values” carries among conservative voters.

It looks like the approach that won out last night was an abundance-coded folding of family life into a broader affordability agenda. Roy Cooper, who flipped the North Carolina Senate seat, ran on the slogan: “Make Stuff Cost Less.” He explicitly made childcare part of that project. In New Hampshire, Chris Pappas made a similar move, calling for expanded childcare services in rural areas.

Cooper and Pappas, along with the many other Democrats who campaigned on childcare, seem to have approached the family question with an attitude similar to the one touted by James Talarico in Texas: “You can’t stand for family and vote against paid family leave, affordable child care, or the right of every American to marry who they love.”

The wealth tax that wasn’t

Kelsey Piper: The retroactive wealth tax on billionaires in California failed ignominiously: Not only is it set to be voted down, but Proposition 42, which amends the state constitution to ban similar taxes in the future, is set to pass. A blue wave was not sufficient to salvage this particular progressive proposal.

In general, the left has not really succeeded at persuading voters that higher taxes are worth paying. This is a challenge that will need confronting if progressive policy is to have any staying power.

Voters weigh in on the redistricting wars

Kobe Yank-Jacobs: Yesterday’s result was unequivocal, but there was a time last year when it seemed like a wave of mid-cycle gerrymandering by Republicans — followed by retaliatory measures in Democratic states — could be the deciding factor in the House. First, Texas redrew its lines. Then, California passed a ballot measure to redraw its lines. Other states joined in.

Last night, I was curious to see how three redistricting measures fared on the ballot given this unprecedented, mid-decade tit-for-tat. The results look bad — at least for those, like me, who would prefer to keep House districts fair and neutral regardless of politics.

In Maryland, voters overwhelmingly changed their state constitution to remove the requirement that districts be compact and connected. In Colorado, a measure is losing that would have required mid-cycle redistricting to be reviewed by its independent redistricting commission. The silver lining is Missouri, where voters reviewed a Trump-supported map that would likely have ousted the Democratic representative in Kansas City in 2028. They overwhelmingly rejected the map.

It will be interesting to see how similar measures perform in 2028 as we approach the next redistricting cycle. Voters in Maryland and Colorado are still preparing for retaliation, but hopefully, this cycle’s clash was a one-time affair, not a new norm in American politics.

The fight for 2028 begins

Jeremiah Johnson: There was never much doubt that Jon Ossoff would win his Georgia Senate race, but I still watched closely to see what kind of margins he would get. Ossoff is set to win by 18 points, a stunning margin in a state Trump won in 2024. It’s a fantastic result for Democrats — and the kind of result that leads to rampant speculation about the 2028 presidential race.

Prediction markets now consider Ossoff to be the most likely Democratic nominee for 2028, boosting him ahead of other contenders like Gavin Newsom or Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez. Ossoff says he’s not interested in a presidential run, but the pressure to draft him will be enormous after a performance like this.

Meanwhile, JD Vance, who just a few months ago was trading at 45% to win the 2028 Republican nomination, has fallen to around 30% odds. To the extent this midterm was a repudiation of Trump, it’s hard to be the one politician in America least able to divorce himself from Trump.

Not Kamala’s culture war

Maibritt Henkel: In Arizona and Nebraska, voters approved bans on trans students participating on school sports teams based on their gender identity. These kids will now have to compete according to their biological sex. Similar measures are projected to pass in Colorado and Washington state.

And yet, around 9 p.m. last night, the Woke 3.0 memes started flooding my Twitter feed. The message of this satirical but undeniably euphoric content is clear: The dark days of MAGA bigotry are over, and the girls, the gays, and the theys can come out of hiding.

But whatever liberal renaissance is upon us, it’s not going to be a rerun of the progressivism we saw circa 2022.

Consider Gina Hinojosa, who won the gubernatorial race in Texas last night in a stunning upset. Her response to incumbent Republican Greg Abbott calling her “gender-bender Gina” was an ad that stated her pronouns “are Mom, Texan, and soon-to-be Governor.” Annie Andrews, the first woman elected to the Senate from South Carolina, is a pro-choice pediatrician, yet the Democrat denounced “biological men playing in women’s sports” and ran a campaign laser-focused on affordability.

For better or for worse, we are entering an era when liberal candidates, having learned from their unpopular predecessors, are walking some version of the woke walk but without nearly as much of the woke talk. They also don’t appear to have the same willingness to go to bat for the LGBTQ issues that they had just a few years ago.
