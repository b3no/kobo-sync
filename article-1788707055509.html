Palliative Patsy

Lance Corporal Scott Bukkake
Sep 6
 
READ IN APP
 

Working in hospital linen meant I spent a lot of time talking to nurses. In clinics, nurses are usually tasked with the job of managing the linen levels and most of what I worked directly with were, in fact clinics.

If you have to deal with a lot of nurses, very quickly, the illusion that nurses are inherently smart or capable kind of goes out the window. A physical therapy nurse will probably be the dumbest broad you meet. There are levels to this shit.

In my high school biology class, when we were dissecting frogs, there was a girl who put dead frog organs in her mouth as a goof. She’s a nurse now.

In March of 2025, my mother called me at 10 PM. This was significant, because usually she’s in bed by 7 PM, so when “MOMMEIGH” flashed up on the caller ID that late, I kind of already knew there was a problem.

My dad was in the hospital. He was sedated. He was on a ventilator. And it didn’t look good. They had been watching Waking Ned Devine (one of Dad’s favorites), when my dad very suddenly complained that he couldn’t breathe. He had what I can only refer to as a SHITHOUSE of Pneumonia, and a giant mass in his lungs. And while they were piping 100% fresh, pure oxygen into those lungs, he was only absorbing about 30% of it.

My mom, my sister and I, for the first time, would be dealing with palliative care nurses. Everyone was pretty sure this was it for my dear, old dad. This led to our first conference call with Palliative Patsy.

Patsy was essentially there to determine whether we were going to push for end-of-life care and could offer little guidance on anything else, which I don’t think was a great call. If you have the Death Nurse, you should also have a nurse who at least knows things about the person who (might be) dying. You see, it’s all very complicated and no one can tell you what to do when this happens to you or a loved one, but also things don’t look very goooooood… But, my sister asked a very important question:

“How much oxygen is dad getting now?”

Patsy explained how a ventilator worked. Which wasn’t what my sister was asking. But when you’re emotional and unfamiliar with these situations, it’s hard to phrase things correctly?

She and Patsy went back and forth on the matter of the ventilator in a matter not dissimilar to a Who’s on First bit while I watched my sister become progressively more and more angry in the recliner beside me. We understood the first time how the ventilator worked really well. Like, we got it. So I interjected:


“Okay! Okay! You know how when like, a doctor measures the oxygen in your bloodstream, and they put that little clippy thing on your finger? Sooo, the night dad came in we were given this 30% number, right? Since he’s got one of those things on his finger right now, measuring the blood oxygen level… What would that say right now if someone went and looked?”

Patsy got very quiet. She didn’t have that number. She’d have to ask about that.

“Alright! Let’s find this out first, because I don’t think we can make a decision if we don’t have an idea of what’s improving or not improving.”

Our father’s ability to absorb oxygen was, in fact, improving. Fuck you Patsy. Don’t soft-launch slowly letting my dad die without pertinent information… By the way, how does a ventilator work, again?

The second battle was simply getting my dad off sedation. But we had a pretty heavy history of having to deal with my dad after he’d been sedated. Dad was no stranger to the hospital and had a lot of abdominal surgeries over his lifetime. The most recent had been only about a decade prior.

When dad would come out of surgery he was guaranteed to freak the fuck out. He’d hallucinate bugs and snakes all over him. Whenever the doctors would try and pull him out, this is what would happen and he’d freak the fuck out so badly they’d have to put him right back under, so his little oxygen-deprived heart wouldn’t explode.

“Hey, you know, he sees like… snakes and spiders and demons when he comes out of sedation… Can we try some other drugs or something?”

You’d be surprised at how many times you have to bring this up before they actually do anything with the drugs. You literally must bully your doctors about some things, and I’m not even joking. Getting the drugs adjusted involved my sister skulking around his room like a psychopath and never shutting up about it when a doctor or nurse was in earshot… And when they finally adjusted them? Surprise, surprise! Our dad finally managed to wake up and got let off the ventilator.

I was just getting home when my sister called me to let me know Dad was awake. She put it on speakerphone and the entire ride to the hospital we listened to my little drugged up dad repeat: “I’M STRONG LIKE A BULL…” and “I’M SMALL BUT I’M TOUGH.” He was 5’6 and about 115 Lbs at the time.

The first thing he said when he saw me? “NICE GLASSES!” (I have big ones) and when I asked him how his time on Grateful Dead Island was, he spent a good portion of time telling us how much he hated Jerry Garcia, but also how much he loves us all.

Unfortunately, we did have to spend time with Palliative Patsy again (that stupid bitch). My dad had been very close to finishing chemotherapy, but he caught pneumonia and that was basically that. He didn’t get better and passed away in hospice care a few days before Christmas.
