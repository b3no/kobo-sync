The Q&A: “We need debate. Governments are at their best when challenged.”

Bob Rae and Alex Himelfarb on their new books about Canada in Trump times

Paul Wells
Sep 18
 
READ IN APP
 

I knew I wanted to talk to Bob Rae and Alex Himelfarb when Lorimer published books by them, on similar topics with similar cover treatments, on the same day a couple of weeks ago. Rae you know: Ontario’s only NDP premier, interim federal Liberal leader from 2011-13, Canada’s ambassador to the UN from 2020-25. Himelfarb you may not: Career civil servant, Clerk of the Privy Council under three prime ministers — Jean Chrétien, Paul Martin and briefly Stephen Harper — and then Canada’s ambassador to Italy.

Himelfarb’s book Breaking Free of Neoliberalism in Trump Times is a thorough update of a book he wrote two years ago, when it was possible to imagine there might be no more Trump times. It concentrates on Canada’s domestic politics and positions itself firmly to the left of a lot of current political debate. Rae’s Reclaiming Canadian Sovereignty in a Ruptured World is an edited essay collection with a bunch of contributors (Carol Off, Margaret Atwood, Armine Yalnizyan, Vass Bednar) and a long introduction by Rae. It’s more about Canada in the world.

This isn’t an interview transcript, but rather interwoven excerpts from email exchanges I had with both authors. It’s been edited for length and flow. Himelfarb wrote: “It would be useful and I think entirely appropriate if, in your introduction, you say that my book is so much better than Bob’s.”

Paul Wells: How do you know each other? What does everyone know about the other that’s important, and what do too few people know?

Bob Rae: I first met Alex during the process of restructuring the Toronto Symphony (in 2000). He was Deputy Minister of Heritage, and I was impressed by his quickness, his willingness to find solutions, and his sense of humour. He figured out a way to help, and then we worked together to persuade Ontario to do the same. After that we became closer when he went to work for Mr. Chrétien, and stayed on under Paul Martin. We swapped ideas, and helped co-ordinate the response to my work on Air India. Same qualities came out — interest in finding answers, and then getting to work. Down to earth, funny, enormously inventive, and for me, always supportive.

At a personal level our friendship deepened, and when he went to Italy as Ambassador he went out of his way to help me relax before I had a heart operation — Rome was a great respite!

Alex Himelfarb: If you were in public life you would inevitably bump into Bob in one guise or another. I doubt that any Canadian matches the breadth and diversity of Bob’s contributions to public life, in academia, diplomacy, international affairs, Indigenous justice, philanthropy, and the arts.

He is probably the only politician who can boast that he was a federal MP for the NDP (and was instrumental in bringing down Joe Clark’s Progressive Conservative government), the NDP Premier of Ontario (and ended up writing a book on the challenges of governing from the left) and then leader of the federal Liberals. Whether as a politician, a public intellectual or Ambassador to the UN he did every assignment in the Bob Rae way — driven by values of peace, democracy, and human rights and always with his unique and powerful voice.

What people may not know is that when he plays — for example tennis with his brother — he plays to win. He is great to have dinner with, always very funny, his humour usually — though not always — directed against himself. And he is very lucky in his family life.

PW: Bob, your book discusses the return of Trump to power, but it devotes more attention to his cuts to foreign aid than some do. Why shouldn’t readers forget those cuts? If America is retreating from development assistance, can Canada reasonably hope to take up the slack?

Rae: I come at things with the rest of the world in mind — that was true before my time in New York, and even truer as a result of that. That’s always been me, and even though I know it isn’t always popular or front of mind, I need to express that thought now, not to be ornery but to give what I think is the better way to look at things.

No, Canada can’t make up for the sheer size of US contributions and their own disastrous cuts, but we can stop cutting ourselves. Again, that doesn’t make me popular these days in some circles, but at this stage of life that’s ok.

PW: Alex, your book seems to politely part company with the groundswell of support for Mark Carney against the Trump administration. You seem to be saying: “Sovereignty, sure, but sovereignty to do what?” That’s also a theme taken up by a lot of authors in your collection, Bob. What do you worry is getting lost in a conversation so focused on cross-border confrontation?

Himelfarb: Let me start by saying I am fully part of the chorus congratulating the Prime Minister for doing what no other western leader has done, saying no to the Trump demands, walking away from the negotiating table despite the risks. Yes, I was worried about the concessions we were making to Trump leading up to the negotiations — for example, reversing the small steps we had taken towards digital sovereignty, for which we got nothing, and seeing reports and rumours that we were giving away more of our policy autonomy and locking in unsupportable and likely illegal tariffs. I was relieved when the Prime Minister did the right thing and walked away.

My own view, as I wrote in the book, was that this was never a trade negotiation. This was always about power, about subjugation. We were saved from a bad deal — in part, at least, by the chaotic aggression of the Trump regime and the surge of patriotism among the majority of Canadians. No reasonable deal nor one that could be trusted was possible. As the PM concluded we could not sign on to a deal written in pencil.

As for your larger question — sovereignty for what? — I do believe we have not had the conversation we need on what kind of Canada we are building and what we stand for in the world.

No doubt these times call for an ambitious economic agenda and our PM has won much goodwill not only from the corporate sector but from the majority of Canadians for what he has done in his first year. But sovereignty is not simply about where things get built, or even only about our ability to grow the economy with less dependence on the United States, as crucial as that is.

It is also about who decides what gets built, who owns it once built, and who shares in the benefits and bears the costs. It is about the extent to which Canadians are able to shape our future and place in the world, to exert democratic control over our energy and natural resources, protect our water and land, control critical infrastructure, defend our cultural and digital spaces, and govern the evolution of AI.

There is always a danger, in times of crisis, that the room for debate or criticism of our leaders — for democracy — narrows. In crisis we often accept decisions we would never otherwise accept. We become vulnerable to progress traps: decisions that seem to solve the problem of the day but create even greater problems down the road or lock us into a path that narrows our options. We need debate. Governments are at their best when challenged.

Rae: My book was pulled together quickly, so the authors are partly there because they said “yes” quickly and also said they could live with a tight deadline. But they weren’t chosen because they all agree or have similar points of view. It’s not intended to be the last word, but rather a contribution to the national conversation.

The “kind of country we want to be” will be defined by what we do, and not by navel gazing. Peggy Atwood is great because she has such a granular sense of where policy has been, and knows where it should go, and she has a wonderful no-nonsense way about her. But yes, your point is right. “Sovereignty” is not an abstract. It’s really about how we want to use the freedom we’re fighting for.

PW: Isn’t it true that Canada wasn’t taking its defence obligations and its own economic development seriously enough? Aren’t some critiques accurate even if Trump is the guy making them?

Rae: Yes, and I have been saying so privately and publicly for some time. Making changes in these areas has long been a good idea, and now they are necessary ideas. The writers in the book deal directly with these points.

PW: Whenever somebody says “neoliberalism,” I look for definitions and an origin story. Alex, how do you define it? And what would you say Brian Mulroney has to do with it?

Himelfarb: I would describe Mulroney as more of an “inheritor” of neoliberalism, though he was the architect of Canada’s version. By the time Mulroney came to power in 1984, the neoliberal counter-revolution was well advanced. Thatcher and Reagan were putting in place what has come to be seen as the neoliberal approach and suite of policies: the view that government’s number-one priority was economic growth — and that could best be achieved if governments created a business-friendly environment, removing the barriers to investment and trade, to profit, by cutting taxes, lightening up on regulations, and privatizing.

The Pierre Trudeau Liberals had, in their last years, launched a commission on Canada’s economic future, headed by former finance Minister Donald Macdonald. The commission finished its report just before Mulroney became Prime Minister and its recommendations — including free trade with the US — were essentially an endorsement of neoliberalism.

Mulroney entered with a determination to cut government down to size, eliminate the deficit, and bring more private sector thinking to Ottawa. He was also determined to reduce federal “intrusion” in areas of provincial responsibility, notably energy and social policy, leading to social fragmentation and inhibiting national climate action. And while he had not campaigned on free trade he became its powerful champion negotiating a deal with President Reagan.

The electric 1988 free trade election was about the tension between imagined economic benefits and fears about lost cultural, democratic and economic sovereignty. The deal that we got, and its successors, never got the access to the US market we had hoped for. In important ways it compromised our policy autonomy, setting us on the course that brought us to Trump’s exploitation of our economic integration.

Mulroney disappointed some of the fiercest advocates of the Thatcher-Reagan turn because he was reluctant to cut as deeply as some had urged and was a moderate or even a progressive on crime and immigration and some aspects of foreign and environmental policy. But he had set us on the neoliberal course.

PW: You write that it can be hard to discuss the Chrétien and Martin governments because you were both Prime Ministers’ Clerk of the Privy Council. But it sounds like you’re saying Jean Chrétien didn’t really deliver the government he’d promised in the 1993 election. How so, and why not?

Himelfarb: That is too stark a characterization of a decade of governing. The Chretien government was perhaps the best example of the third way style of governing, balancing investment in talent and tools for the knowledge economy while scaling back government and breaking down barriers to trade and investment.

Most notably, given our current existential crisis, it was the Chretien government that ratified NAFTA. The path of integration into the continental market had already been set by the Mulroney government. But NAFTA enshrined unprecedented rights for investors and expanded intellectual property rights. Mexico’s inclusion added downward pressure on wages and standards and accelerated the southern migration of manufacturing. Several political economists have argued that these free trade agreements introduced Reagan’s neoliberalism through the back door.

Perhaps the most notable shift from the Red Book promises the Liberals ran on was in fiscal policy. The first Chretien budget was a pretty faithful implementation of the Liberals’ Red Book. That budget was severely criticized by Preston Manning’s Reform Party, but also by Canada’s corporate leadership, the mainstream press and some influential think tanks as having failed to appreciate the urgency of the fiscal situation and the need to eliminate the deficit.

And the government took action. The 1995 austerity budget deeply cut programs and the size of the public service, continued the program of privatization that Mulroney had started, and fundamentally transformed intergovernmental health and social transfers, reshaping federal-provincial relations. Within just a couple of years the government balanced the budget and got our relative debt to GDP on a downward track.

Of course there is no doubt that Canada had a fiscal problem when the Liberals came to power, as about a third of every dollar was going to service the debt. But even within the Liberal cabinet there were heated disputes about the speed and depth of the cuts and about whether tax increases might be part of the fix. Sweden also took on its large deficit successfully but did over fifteen years what Canada did in two, allowing Sweden to preserve its health and social services.

After 2000, however, the government changed course: no more tax or program cuts, increased spending to reduce child poverty, within its self-imposed fiscal restraints, investments in health and in post-secondary education, science and technology, culture and the arts, and aid to Africa. The government limited corporate money in elections, said no to supporting the US misguided and disastrous war on Iraq, and to participation in the missile shield, yes to same sex marriage and to Kyoto, and no to bank mergers or punitive crime policies.

So yes the Chretien government cemented the turn to neoliberalism — but in the third way, Canadian-style.

PW: There was this big investment summit in Toronto, with billions of dollars of Canadian infrastructure and building projects up for bids. Alex, I bet you’re nervous about that?

Himelfarb: As I said, Canadians understand the need for economic ambition and the public have placed a great deal of trust in the PM to drive the economy. The investment summit may test that goodwill. Making the target of the summit a trillion dollars — that is, how much rather than what kind of investment — raises troubling questions. There’s investment and there’s investment.

The business community is asking, will the summit deliver the money. But the diverse protestors that showed up — Indigenous leaders, environmentalists, social justice activists — had very different questions. I won’t try to capture all their diverse concerns but they might be summarized with two questions. First, to what extent are the priorities for what we build determined by investors, by capital, versus determined by Canadians democratically?

And second, does the summit open the door to a new super-round of privatization — selling off critical resources, public capacity, public infrastructure — that would in fact undermine sovereignty, and weaken our democracy? Is any public service or public asset out of bounds? Is our care system fair game?

We do not know the answers yet, but no doubt the announcement regarding the privatization of airport operations confirmed the worst fears of many, especially when the economic case for privatization seems so weak. It will not have escaped notice that investors could even get a hefty tax break for taking over these public assets. To put the concerns starkly, there is more than one way to compromise sovereignty. We could negotiate it away or we could sell it to the highest bidder. Ownership matters.

PW: Bob, for the first time in a while, you’re not working for a Canadian prime minister. How would you say this one is handling the job? What would be your best gentle advice for him?

Bob Rae: I think Mark Carney is the right person in the right place at the right time. For a non-politician, he actually understands politics well. His team, in my view, is too small, and while I admire the disciplined style, I also feel he needs to broaden the field of talent around him.

He’s carrying a ton of stuff on his own back, and shows no signs of wear and tear, but my own experience is that it takes a stronger group of a “team of rivals” to actually have the best chance of long-term success. But I admire and like him, and am not going to spend a lot of time second guessing. And we do talk from time to time.

PW: Alex, who’s advocating for the kind of Canada you’d like to see? Who’s been delivering it? Where’s the cause for optimism?

Himelfarb: I draw through the book on Antonio Gramsci, the leftist political theorist writing mostly from prison during the 1930s amidst the rise of fascism. Gramsci responded that he found optimism and pessimism to be vulgar and banal moods. One does what one can. Gramsci did not believe that history has direction or that the arc of the universe bent in any particular direction. History doesn’t bend arcs, people do. So, you join with others to make things better as you understand it — even if no one is listening.

There is no doubt there is tough sledding ahead for Canada. Many of our corporate leaders (and the Premier of Alberta) will want to go back to how things were whatever the cost to our sovereignty. In any case there is no going back. As Carney has said, nostalgia is not a strategy.

Our immediate priority must be to support workers, families and firms through the tough times — especially the young, inevitably hardest hit. And then turn to the hard work of building a more resilient Canada.

There are many Canadians fighting for elements of the Canada I would like to see — for digital and cultural sovereignty, for tax reform to share our prosperity more equitably and to rein in private power, for investments in our systems of education, health, and care, for Indigenous reconciliation based on Indigenous rights and respect for Indigenous knowledge, for public investment in non-market housing and in transportation, for accelerated climate action and the energy transition and for protecting the environment which sustains us all, for electoral and democratic reform, and for peace.

I think what joins the diverse voices are two core goals that give definition to an ambitious sovereignty — the need for a robust and inclusive democracy based on equality of vote and voice; and a commitment to renewed international cooperation to address global health and environmental challenges, safeguard human rights, reduce global inequality and, above all, work for peace.

Carney’s call for a variable geometry and alliance of middle powers holds real promise, not just as a hedge against the hegemons but for building such a new internationalism. We will have to decide what we stand for if the international alliances we are already forging are to go beyond commerce and defence.

We have the opportunity to recover our sovereignty and strengthen our democracy. We are not alone in our fight for sovereignty and for democracy.
