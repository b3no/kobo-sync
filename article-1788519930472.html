Seeing like an elite

Three findings from our latest poll

Kobe Yank-Jacobs
Sep 4
∙
Paid
 
READ IN APP
 

What do Americans hear when they watch Sen. Bernie Sanders or Vice President JD Vance rail against the elite? Are they thinking of right-wing tech billionaires? Imperious college professors? Liberal magazine writers?

Instead of joining the myriad political commentators in continuing to speculate wildly, we decided to ask the American people. In a poll of 3,000 registered voters fielded nationally from July 20 to 26, voters rejected the MAGA theory of the elite: They “don’t think college professors, journalists, and academics are actually among the elite.” Instead, it’s billionaires, celebrities, and business executives who most commonly earned the designation.

Strong majorities of our respondents also endorsed free trade and gender egalitarianism, defying what they took to be opposing elite views on the matters.

In other words, the narrative that we are a country divided between the hardscrabble, anti-woke masses and the effete, culturally progressive elite is, at best, only a partial description of reality.

Our findings spurred lots of discussion, from the pages of The Wall Street Journal and The Washington Post to the r/Professors subreddit. (“The salary certainly isn’t elite,” lamented one user.)

But, as with all polls, the top lines are just the beginning. As I dove into the back end of the polling data, I encountered findings that complicate three hot-button questions: Does our media diet determine our reality? Are people worried about declining birth rates or overpopulation? And is America actually gripped by an epidemic of loneliness?

Different media sources do not necessarily portray different realities

One might lament the lost days of three TV networks, Walter Cronkite, Leave It to Beaver, and a broad swath of U.S. adults holding a relatively common picture of reality. We live in a balkanized media system, the prevailing narrative runs, and so we are doomed to our splintered views.

Our poll results challenge this pessimism.

When it comes to identifying an American elite, the five most common responses from our poll were the same no matter where respondents got their news. Whether they got their news from newspapers or Twitter or anything in between, the groups most commonly identified as elite were billionaires, celebrities, business executives, and elected officials, and people making over $200,000, in that order.

Not only did different news consumers identify the same elites, but they also identified the top five in the exact same order.

Now, you might notice some diverging patterns between TikTok and Reddit consumers, which seem to undercut the broader consensus. For instance, TikTok watchers are 7 percentage points less likely to think billionaires are elite compared to all respondents, while Reddit users are 8 points more likely to think this. Does this undermine the notion that people share views across news sources?

The problem is that it doesn’t actually seem to be the news source itself that is driving this divergence. When you dig into the numbers, you see that low-income respondents are 13 points more likely to use TikTok than Reddit. In other words, this two-channel difference is not primarily driven by news consumption, but by income.

In general, despite relatively strong consensus among all groups that billionaires, celebrities, and business executives are elites, socioeconomic status was the stronger dividing line on this definitional question.

Wealthy people more likely to ascribe the

Poorer people and those without degrees were less likely to label any group elite. Looking first at income, respondents earning over $100,000 per year were more likely to think billionaires (by 12 points), business executives (7 points), and celebrities (9 points) are elite compared to those who earn under $50,000.

That effect was then amplified by college degree status. For instance, within each income bracket, degree-holders considered business executives elite at levels 10 to 15 points higher than non-degree peers in the same income bracket.

In short: If you earn more or have a degree, you are significantly more likely to ascribe the label “elite” to a group that we asked about. Conversely, if you earn less or lack a degree, you’re more likely to be ambivalent about labeling any given group elite.

We also saw divergence, naturally, along partisan lines: Trump 2024 voters were more likely to think college professors were elite by 12 points (18.5% compared to 6.4% for Harris voters), while Harris voters were more likely to think billionaires were elite by 16 points (85.6% compared to 70% of Trump voters saying the same). Both groups selected elected officials at roughly the same 43% rate.

That said, whether you’re raging against the woke mind virus on Twitter or flipping open The New York Times over your morning coffee, you probably count Elon Musk — a billionaire, celebrity, and business executive — among the elite, whoever they are, and can rest assured that most of your fellow Americans would do the same.

Are there too many people or too few?

For several sets of questions in our poll — some relating to government influence on the economy, some on gender relations — we asked respondents what they assumed elite opinion to be.

Respondents by and large did not feel that American elites shared their views.

On average, the gap between people’s own views and what they ascribed to elites was a full 24 points. The elites are out of touch, or at least many Americans see it that way across various issues.

But there were two questions where this gap shrank dramatically: overpopulation and declining birth rates, where there was just a 7-point gap and a 5-point gap, respectively.

Perceived gap with elites shrinks on population questions
The average gap between personal and perceived elite views is 24 points across the poll. That gap shrinks when people are asked to assess whether overpopulation and declining birth rates are big problems, respectively.

These unusually small gaps on population issues can be deceptive. Different groups imagine elites differently, and their opposing perceptions cancel out when aggregated — particularly when you look at partisanship.

When it comes to overpopulation, 55% of Harris 2024 voters are concerned, but just 41% think that elites share that view. Among Trump voters, the two aggregate figures happen to be identical: Forty-five percent personally express concern and 45% think elites are concerned. That does not mean every Trump voter feels represented by elites. When you look at respondent-level data, only 44% of Trump voters selected the same answer for themselves and elites on the issue.

When it comes to birth rates, both Trump voters and Harris voters diverge a lot from their perceptions of elites but in completely different directions.

Just 33% of Harris voters said they are concerned about the birth rate, but 45% of them believe elites are worried about it — possibly the result of Elon Musk’s increasing identification with the issue.

Fifty-four percent of Trump voters, on the other hand, are worried about declining births, but just 31% of them think elites are concerned — a whopping 23-point gap.

But what’s really curious about these findings is that almost 1 in 5 respondents were both worried about low birth rates and overpopulation. They seemed to want the population to go up and down simultaneously.

If you’re thinking about these issues in the way that many political commentators do, then being worried about too many people and too few at the same time doesn’t make any sense. They tend to see issues systemically: Declining birth rates will stress the social safety net in a few decades, and you probably wouldn’t want the population to go down if you’re trying to get birth rates up for this reason.

But people might see issues more practically: If overpopulation isn’t just “too many people” but is instead “too much traffic,” and if declining birth rates aren’t a fiscal catastrophe but an intensely personal question of whether childbearing is intrinsically good, you can be worried about both issues.

Looking at who these 18% of people are doesn’t provide a very clean story. They are a mixed bag of Trump voters, Harris voters, men, and women who defy easy categorization. But hey, that’s the messiness of public opinion polling.

There’s a loneliness epidemic everywhere but in the data

In 2023, when then-Surgeon General Vivek Murthy announced an epidemic of loneliness, he equated the health effects to smoking 15 cigarettes a day. According to his report, approximately half of Americans were considered lonely — and developing an increased risk of heart disease, stroke, and dementia as a result.

Our July poll painted a very different picture.

When we asked people whether they would describe themselves as a lonely person, just 7% answered that they definitely would. Another 23% said, “yes, to some extent.” Even if we lump these two groups together, the result (30%) is around 20 points below the number cited in the former surgeon general’s report.

What explains this difference?

Well, the Cigna study Murthy cited is based on a survey that did not actually ask respondents whether or not they were lonely. Instead, it asked them a range of other questions and then created an index of loneliness. Experts judged what loneliness was based on things they thought were related to the subjective experience of loneliness.¹

There is, ostensibly, a good reason to do this: social desirability bias. People might be disinclined to tell a stranger polling them that they are lonely. But giving researchers more leeway to come up with their own idiosyncratic criteria for loneliness comes with its own pitfalls.²

Personally, I think there are good reasons to lend credence to the claim that there are declining rates of social connection, as well as good reasons to worry about it. But that is different from trying to measure the experience of loneliness itself, which is clearly a tricky business. I don’t think that fully half of Americans are chronically lonely, but that doesn’t mean we shouldn’t take social disconnection seriously.
