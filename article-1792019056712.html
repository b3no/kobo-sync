Paying Whole Foods prices for a losing basket

Republicans have more cash than Democrats, and they’re burning it faster — from a Kansas rescue mission to a Maine ad that ran in Oregon.

Halina Bennet
Oct 14
∙
Paid
 
READ IN APP
 

With Election Day three weeks away, the polls and vibes continue to move in essentially the same direction: leftward with enthusiasm. Politico reports that five senior Republican lawmakers granted anonymity to speak candidly believe the House is all but gone and that the party’s focus is now on limiting the extent of the damage and saving the Senate.

As prices continue to rise in conjunction with the drawn-out war in Iran, an anonymous House Republican running for re-election told CNN that “Trump is dragging the party down,” resulting in an all-hands-on-deck effort to keep the most viable candidates above water. Some Republican candidates are taking steps to distance themselves from the president, most recently in the case of Senator Mike Rounds, who is running for another term in South Dakota and has scrubbed references to Trump from his campaign website. The New York Times reports that the president’s unpopularity is a particular threat to Senator Susan Collins, the only Republican senator representing a blue state.

Pollster Larry Sabato told CNN on Saturday that as of then, the Senate was the Democrats’ to lose. But of course, the races are close, polling is uncertain, and nobody really knows what’s going to happen.

Triage and investment

Republicans are shifting gears — and money — away from some races. The New York Times reports that two major G.O.P. super PACs are pulling money from incumbents Thomas Kean Jr. (who faces Democrat Rebecca Bennett in New Jersey’s traditionally red Seventh Congressional District) and Mariannette Miller-Meeks (who faces Democrat ⁠Christina Bohannan for the third time to represent Iowa’s First Congressional District) as both their paths to re-election narrow. The PACs are also withdrawing all funds aimed at targeting two Democrats running for re-election: Greg Landsman in Ohio and Kristen McDonald Rivet in Michigan. The Times reports that the Congressional Leadership Fund alone is redirecting $10 million from those seats to 16 other districts.

In Senate races, Republicans are spending big to shore up red-state seats that have become unexpectedly competitive. Axios reports that a top Trump operative, Chris Grant, has been hired to revive Senator Roger Marshall’s campaign in Kansas as the candidate faces a formidable challenge in Adam Hamilton. The Senate Leadership Fund is making its first investment in the state this cycle, pulling funds from North Carolina (which Republicans are apparently now prepared to write off) and redirecting them to Kansas.

Republicans are also spending millions in Nebraska — funded, in part, by a “cryptocurrency-focused group” — to retain a Republican-held seat that independent candidate Dan Osborn is vying for. And in its effort to boost one key candidate, the G.O.P. fumbled: an ad buyer mistakenly ran a spot to support Maine Senator Susan Collins in Portland, Ore., rather than Portland, Maine. A Suffolk University poll published this week has the close race going the Democrat Troy Jackson’s way by two percentage points.

The Republican Governors Association has likewise reduced its television ad spending for Michigan gubernatorial candidate John James. James faces Democrat Jocelyn Benson, the current secretary of state, who has a 23-to-1 financial advantage over the Republican, the Detroit News reports.

Democratic groups are expanding their spending to match Republicans in the races both parties believe are competitive. Democrat-linked Senate Majority PAC is pulling money from North Carolina’s Senate race, where former Governor Roy Cooper is the comfortable favorite, to instead spend $7 million in Ohio and $2.5 million in Iowa on television ads, Bloomberg reports. Trump carried both states by double digits in 2024. The PAC has also pulled ad funding from Georgia, in a vote of confidence for Senator Jon Ossoff’s re-election bid. In Kansas, a state where a Democrat has not won a Senate seat in nearly a century, Democratic groups are spending at least $9 million to support Adam Hamilton, the New York Times reports. Hamilton, whose candidacy has come out of the woodwork in the final stretch of the campaign, has emerged as a true sign of hope for Democrats and some Republicans. Two former Kansas G.O.P. governors have endorsed the centrist Democrat, who is the founding pastor of the country’s largest United Methodist Church congregation across its multiple campuses.

As both parties turn their attention — and their money — from North Carolina, David Plouffe, the Democratic strategist who managed former President Barack Obama’s narrow 2008 victory in the state, warned Democrats not to take it for granted. In audio from a call with Cooper’s finance team obtained by Politico, Plouffe said that even if North Carolina looks like the best opportunity for Democrats to flip a Senate seat, the purple state has fixed partisanship and swaying voters will be difficult. The last time a Democrat won a Senate race in North Carolina was in 2008, and that victory was slim.

“We have to win the ones we have to win,” Plouffe said of the race.

Even as the overall picture favors Democrats, the G.O.P. is not broke and continues to have the cash advantage. Elon Musk has spent more than $276 million on Republican efforts since July, contributing to the party’s already swollen wallet. But the G.O.P. is also spending more than Democrats and is stretched thin by unanticipated battles in increasingly expensive races. As CNN put it, “They’re effectively paying premium prices at Whole Foods while Democrats are shopping deals at Costco.”

Everything’s bigger in Texas

The Senate race in Texas has topped half a billion dollars in combined ad spending and reservations, CNN reports, and the polling is tight thanks to slipping approval ratings for both Trump and the state’s Attorney General Ken Paxton, the Republican candidate. A poll taken by ReconMR/Siena found that Paxton’s favorability net was -15 overall.

State Representative James Talarico — the Democratic nominee — is polling ahead of Paxton in four surveys released over the last week from the University of Texas (Talarico +3), the Republican Trafalgar Group (Talarico +4), ReconMR (Talarico +4), and most recently Marist (Talarico +2). Paxton leads by less than one point in a recent poll from Quantus Insights; even when the conservative pollster has the Republican in the lead, it’s by a hair.

The vibes are as positive for Talarico as the polls. A Dallas Morning News columnist wrote that in the Park Cities, one of Dallas’s most Republican areas, Talarico yard signs far outnumber Paxton’s in the writer’s daily count.

“If Paxton can’t win the Park Cities, he has no chance to win the race,” the columnist, Dallas Cothrum — the C.E.O. of a land use and zoning firm — wrote. “I know, I know — signs don’t vote. They do, however, demonstrate momentum and, more importantly, a sense that people want to be on the winning team.”

Coverage of Paxton and his movements over the last week is unlikely to help his case. The Wall Street Journal reported that in 2024 the attorney general (accompanied by a woman who was not his wife) was in Dubai partying with two fugitives indicted on federal health-care-fraud charges. One of the fugitives, health care executive Anosh Ahmed, donated $190,000 to Paxton, $25,000 of which was given days after Ahmed was indicted. The Washington Post reported yesterday that Paxton received campaign donations from the staff or political arms of five organizations whose legal interests his office had weighed in on. The sums varied, and in some cases, Paxton wrote legal opinions that benefited those donors.

It is to be determined whether the conspiracy furthered by his campaign that Talarico had checked in to an Ohio mental hospital will make the Republican even less credible or if the rumor spread enough to make the Democrat appear unfit.

As Paxton fails to gain momentum, Politico reports that the donors who were once reluctant to spend in his race are now fully behind the candidate.

“In this difficult environment, only money can save some Republicans,” former U.S. Representative Carlos Curbelo told Politico. “Whatever bullets they have left they should use. Otherwise they might get blamed for any razor-thin defeats where more resources could have been the difference.”

Even as donors get in line, Paxton’s team claims that his primary opponent, the incumbent Senator John Cornyn, and the political consultant Karl Rove are out to get him, with one Republican working on Paxton’s race implying to NBC that the duo is behind leaked, damaging private conversations. Cornyn, for his part, has been clear that he has not and will not fundraise in support of the nominee.

Senate map update

  • In Ohio, early voting data suggest Democrats are gaining momentum even in the counties friendliest to Trump. The G.O.P.’s early vote lead has shrunk from 30 points to five since 2024, CNN reports. The race between incumbent Republican Jon Husted and former Senator Sherrod Brown is close. The Columbus Dispatch reports that the Senate and governor’s races could both very likely come down to independent or third-party voters — and which major party loses more votes to third-party (primarily Libertarian) candidates.

  • In Nebraska, amid heavy Republican spending, Dan Osborn, the independent candidate, is facing an exodus of many of his senior staff, Punchbowl reports, signaling trouble for a candidate who has already been running against leaked tapes in which he spoke down on Trump supporters — the voters he is trying to court.

  • Alaska’s Democratic nominee for Senate, former Representative Mary Peltola, had a haul of $17 million in the third quarter, her biggest fundraising quarter yet against Senator Dan Sullivan. It appears that allegations that she used slurs and mishandled staff by making them feed her pet lizard have not dented her support.

  • Montana’s Senate race has not gained much national attention compared with those in key swing states. Republican attorney Kurt Alme is running ahead of the independent candidate Seth Bodnar, who has been sinking under the continued presence of Alani Bankhead, the Democratic nominee whom many Democrats have asked to step aside. This week, Shannon O’Brien resigned as Montana Democratic Party chair to endorse Bodnar, writing in an op-ed for MS Now that Bodnar is the only and best candidate with a shot at beating Alme.


    “Bankhead does not have the momentum, resources or will to win this race,” O’Brien wrote. “For no apparent reason, she came out of the gate mudslinging at Bodnar, a decent human being, seemingly ignoring the Republican and her ideological opposite.”

  • In Michigan, candidates for the Senate are wading into contentious territory. The Democratic nominee, Dr. Abdul El-Sayed, released an artificial intelligence platform that calls for a pause on A.I. development, which he called “the existential crisis of our time.” Mike Rogers, the Republican nominee, has previously warned that A.I. development poses a national security threat and has, in breaking with the president and other G.O.P. candidates, vowed to prioritize regulation if elected, the New York Times reports.


    Rogers, who once allegedly asked AIPAC to withhold support for his race after it became a key sticking point in the Democratic primary, did not rule out accepting AIPAC funding when he took part in a debate on Thursday. AIPAC spent more than $30 million trying to foil El-Sayed’s campaign during the primary to no avail. El-Sayed is leading Rogers in nearly every public poll published since the beginning of August.

Don’t forget the House

Fox News shifted its predictions for 18 House tossup races this week. Every move slid toward Democrats.

Or governors

  • Iowa’s largest and most influential newspaper, the Des Moines Register, endorsed Democratic candidate Rob Sand for governor over the Republican Zach Lahn, publishing a glowing editorial in support of the candidate.


    “Sand should be the inexhaustible engine who rejects excuses and sets Iowa on a path to water safe for playing and drinking, a place where cancer crushes fewer families, where public education, cities of all sizes, innovation and justice can thrive,” the editorial board wrote. “And he should be the public face of a state where natives want to stay or return to raise a family and where domestic and international migrants see opportunity.”

  • Florida’s Democratic nominee for governor, David Jolly, has his sights set on wooing Black voters, who he believes could decide the race between him and his Republican opponent, Byron Donalds, the Miami Herald reports.

  • In Oregon, Republicans see a rare opportunity to flip a governorship, as Governor Tina Kotek’s overwhelming unpopularity in the state gives the Republican candidate, Christine Drazan, an opening, the New York Times reports.

Not so fast

With all pre-election predictions, polls, and feelings, there are caveats that could easily produce a different result than anticipated. Axios reports that polls widely underestimated Trump in 2016, 2020, and 2024, so even if all polls are gesturing in the same direction, the end result could, in theory, swing the other way, especially as many of them are producing outcomes within the margin of error.

And even if Democrats do take the House or the Senate or both, then come the issues of governing and proving to voters that the party has earned the chance to represent the country again in 2028 and beyond.

“The Democrats owe a positive, affirmative agenda to the public, and a midterm tends to be kind of a protest vote against the leader in power,” the Washington Post reports Senator Elissa Slotkin said. “Things change very quickly now, and the country is just looking for new and different and bold.”
