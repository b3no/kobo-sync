The Memory Distortion Field of American Cultural Power

Your past may belong to you - but can you keep it?

Conor Fitzgerald
Nov 23
∙
Preview
 
READ IN APP
 

This article is going to be about Ireland as an example of the way American cultural power can change the self-perception of people in smaller countries (and indeed in all countries) in profound ways, but as an entry point, I want to start off talking about films.

I watched a lot of American high school movies as a kid—off the top of my head, I must have seen The Breakfast Club, Clueless, and The Lost Boys half a dozen times each during my teens. One of the forgotten movies of that era that stuck with me was Gleaming the Cube—a very silly cash-in on the skateboarding trend starring Christian Slater as a high school student living and hanging out in a fallout shelter in his parents’ garden. What a dream! A teenager, it was communicated to me, was a person with the autonomy of a post-college adult but the accepted lack of responsibility of a child. For an intensely solitary and private person, I certainly found his living situation intoxicating; I probably only saw that movie once, yet I still remember it 36 years later.

This wasn’t limited to movies specifically about teenage rebellion or the problems of adolescence. Even young adults in family films seemed free to do all kinds of things that would have been impossible for me. I’m still amazed at how, in Back to the Future, Marty McFly can come and go from his shabby but respectable lower-middle-class home as he pleases; no one cares if he enters or leaves at 3 a.m., or if he gets to school on time. If a person in our house so much as stirred at 3 a.m., my parents would have instantly been on the scene—either my father in a vest telling me to cop myself on and get back to bed, or my mother wondering what the crisis was and whether she should call an ambulance. No wonder I pined for the lack of surveillance the movies suggested was normal: but I digress...
