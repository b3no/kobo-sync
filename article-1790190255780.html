No School Will Decide Najya Hannah-Jones’s Future or Fate

nothing in our educational debates will ever get better until we accept as a society that schools cannot change an individual’s nature

Freddie deBoer
Sep 23
 
READ IN APP
 

There’s the perennial debate: what’s the value of the humanities, in the 21st century? The answer from the administrative class, generally, is “not much,” which in turn provokes a lot of arguments about the inherent value of truth and beauty, how the humanities teach the things that make life living, that kind of jazz. And indeed, I believe in all of it. But it’s also the case that the humanities impart far more practical advantages too, and the most obvious is the ability to live in history, to better understand the present by locating it in the context of the past. For example: our modern educational debates are hampered terribly by our sheer indifference to history, by our refusal to locate current ideas in the flow of time. This Nikole Hannah Jones controversy shows exactly why.

If you’re unaware, Hannah-Jones recently published an essay in the NYT magazine reflecting on her decision, a decade ago, to enroll her daughter in predominantly Black public schools in Brooklyn, rather than to prefer “selective alternatives,” a decision she and her husband made in the pursuit of equity. Jones talks about various systemic issues at the school - a lack of “accountability” and high standards is, of course, central to the narrative - and details a subsequent confrontation with her now-teenaged daughter, who can’t do fractions. Hannah-Jones expresses deep regret for placing ideological activism ahead of her child's educational needs. This has, predictably, led to tons of crowing from the usual suspects, especially the pro-private school vouchers, pro-school “choice” people. “Look at this deluded liberal, mugged by reality!” The lesson is clear: parents should abandon their progressive guilt and do what’s best for their children, which means moving them out of our failing public school system and into superior charter and private alternatives. The biggest hit on public schools and teacher unions in years, and all thanks to a notoriously woke journalist!

Of course, none of this makes any sense. Most obviously and importantly, this story is the definition of anecdote - it is, at best, a single data point about one particular child in one school system in one city. Drawing any conclusions from one individual student will never be a sensible thing to do. And in fact that school system could hardly be less representative of the median American district: the NYC school system is absolutely enormous, the largest in the country by far, orders of magnitude larger than the median; it’s a large urban district in a country where ~45% are classified rural; about 16% of NYC public school students are white, compared to about 45% nationally; English language learner/ESL students are about 60% higher prevalence in NYC public schools than the national average; students with disabilities, 50% higher prevalence; NYC’s school district has twice the share of “public” charters compared to the national average; the per-pupil cost of public school in NYC is twice that of the national average; about 75% of NYC students are economically disadvantaged compared to a national average of 53% free lunch eligible; the homeless student population in NYC is enormous compared to the national average. I could go on. Looking at New York public schools and drawing broad conclusions about public school writ large is simply not a sound way to understand anything.

This is especially important because we know, for a fact, that the average American public school is doing just fine. Our highest performing students are the best in the world, our median students are doing OK and in fact quite well for one of the world’s most diverse school systems, our problems are overwhelmingly concentrated in a small number of outlier schools with truly terrible outcomes, which are universally found in areas of economic disadvantage and social hardship. (Go find me a public school district in a top quarter income municipality that’s in the bottom quartile in performance. You can’t find one.) Unfortunately, political necessity has compelled many people to avoid this reality; whether your desire to reform the education system comes from a noble desire to help or from an ideological hatred of public institutions and unions, you are likely to find it to your benefit to exaggerate the scope of public school’s problems. But the reality is that our schooling problems are problems that afflict our poorest students in our most dysfunctional communities. If you are a middle class parent in a middle class city with schools filled with middle class kids, the odds are very good that your kid’s school is fine, and fine is all you need.

But I was talking about history.

Why would the kind of engagement with the past that the humanities encourage help us to understand a very 21st century story of racial guilt, clumsy progressive instincts, and modern education? Because every particular about the debate over Hannah-Jones’s essay stems from a set of assumptions about what school is and does and is for, a set of assumptions that is profoundly modern and would largely appear foreign to most human beings for most of history. Those assumptions include

  • The academic outcomes of any individual student or groups of students are highly mutable and largely determined by schools and teachers

  • Students have more-or-less equal ability to perform academically; there may be some tendency towards better or worse performance, but these tendencies can be overcome by dedicated teachers and quality schools

  • Additionally/alternatively, the job of schooling is to create equality within or among students to some degree or in some fashion; we may accept that there will always be some degree of variation, some better students and some worse, but the purpose of schooling is to bring students closer together in performance and a high-quality school will not see wide performance gaps between students

  • Student outcomes are highly sensitive to standards, however defined - if schools and teachers hold students to high standards, students will meet those standards

  • The proximate purpose of school is to give students economic power; schooling increases a student’s attractiveness in the labor market and thus sets them up for a life of greater financial success and personal security, and ultimately the moral value of schooling flows from this reality

  • Concomitantly, the ultimate purpose of school is to create social equality and social justice, whether between races or between social classes or in a more amorphous sense; we fund and support schools and demand excellence in schools ultimately because schools are the only path to social equality.

For most of the history of humanity, and most of the history of education, almost everyone would have found these to be bizarre assumptions; today, they are so deeply written into our ideology that we don’t recognize them as assumptions at all. They are, fundamentally, not subject to debate. And while there are plenty of noxious things to dislike about premodern attitudes about everything, including education, there’s also something to be said about such skepticism. Because few of those assumptions are beneficial and few of them are true.

I know that many readers feel that my education writing has become repetitious; I can only beg forgiveness and point out that nothing ever, ever changes in our educational debates, so I have to keep making the same points over and over again. But I’m determined not to repeat myself too much today. So let’s return again to the subject of history. In particular, let’s contrast the loving, anxious, overwhelming concern we feel from Hannah-Jones and for Hannah-Jones’s daughter, as we read that essay, with a parent’s attitude towards an average high school senior in 1975. Let’s call him Mike, if only because Michael was the most popular boy’s name in the year of his birth, 1957.

Mike’s parents, we can assume, don’t have the same level of anxiety that today’s parents do. The modern educational crisis narrative is often said to begin with the 1983 publication of the Reagan administration tract A Nation at Risk, which set the template for our endless panic about schools and how they fail this proud country. Parents mostly cared about grades, which were distributed twice or four times a year in a report card; not all high schools of the period would have a formal class rank of grade outcomes, and many would not even roll grades up into a GPA. In fact, it’s remarkable how little information Mike’s parents had access to. Forty-two states had some kind of standardized testing instruments for K-12 students at that time, true, but a significant majority of these were sampled tests rather than census-style tests. (That is, they tested representative groups of students rather than all students - which is a wise practice, but I digress.) What’s more, a small minority of those states actually required local districts to administer those tests; they were largely optional, subject to the whims of a given district. Read all about it here. (God bless ERIC.) The upshot is that Mike’s parents know a tiny fraction of what a motivated helicopter parent in 2026 NYC might know now. Mike’s parents have no way to answer questions like

  • Is Mike meeting the state’s expectations for grade-level mathematics?

  • Is Mike proficient in reading according to some common external standard?

  • Did he show academic growth comparable to peers over the last year?

  • How does his performance compare with students elsewhere in the state? Students from his age group in other states?

  • How well is his school doing compared with neighboring schools?

  • What percentage of students at his school can read proficiently? What is the standard by which proficiency is defined?

  • Are students like him doing unusually well or unusually poorly at his school?

Mike’s parents have information about Mike as judged by his school and his teachers, according to materials and rubrics and standards largely developed at the school, by those teachers. What they don’t have is information about Mike and his school that’s been compared to any external standard. They have very little to go on about how well prepared Mike is to succeed in college admissions or on the job market. When it comes to the notion of school preparing Mike to face his future as an economic and professional actor - the singular obsession of modern American educational discourse - they’re flying blind. And yet my strong impression is that Mike’s parents would be far, far less anxious about his performance than a bourgie Brooklynite couple would be. Why?

For one thing, as this old cartoon suggests, the notion that a given student’s outcomes are the responsibility of their teachers instead of that student themselves is a very modern notion. If Mike did poorly in school, his parents were much more likely to blame it on him and his worth ethic than on his teachers; the kneejerk tendency to blame teachers for student outcomes is largely a product of the contemporary educational “reform” narrative and would be quite foreign to Mike’s parents. (It turns out that this, too, is an old wisdom we should not have forgotten.) Still, regardless of who’s to blame for Mike’s performance, good or bad, we can reasonably ask why there appears to have been so much less ambient panic about how students in schools were doing in decades past. I think there’s a pretty obvious answer, and it’s found in this chart:

Mike’s parents were less likely to fall into a panic over his educational prospects because his educational prospects were fundamentally less essential to his ability to live the good life. Mike and his parents lived in a country where those without college degrees could still expect to earn a decent living that enabled them a middle class lifestyle and its various affordances: a home, a car or two, raising children. The “factory at the edge of town” job, the well-paying unionized job in manufacturing and industry, was still alive and well in their era. What Mike and his parents couldn’t know, at the time, was that this way of life was soon to be destroyed by offshoring and automation. They didn’t know that the Reagan-Thatcher era of neoliberalism was coming. As I’ve long argued, it’s no coincidence that the educational crisis narrative begins in the Reagan administration; the need to educate our citizenry into affluence became acute only after the alternative to education was destroyed. Najya Hannah-Jones probably doesn’t know it, but she’s living in a world remade by globalization, one where her entire generational cohort has little choice but to pursue education as the path to financial security, even in a supposedly post-college world. Her parents would probably expect her to pursue higher education regardless. But everything about the pressure they and she feel for her to excel academically is filtered through a changed labor market, and any big picture take on education that does not reflect that reality is propaganda.

The Public School Doom Loop

  • Public schools teach populations of students that are non-randomly different from the populations in charter and private schools, with public schools systematically more likely to teach students who face social and demographic academic challenges

  • Politicians and the media erroneously ascribe lower performance in public schools to those schools and their teachers rather than to systematic inequalities in how their student populations are assembled

  • Parents who have the means to do so avoid public schools or pull their children out of public schools, and children with parents of means are more likely to succeed in school, which deepens the systematic disadvantage in student populations

  • Educational metrics for public schools fall further thanks to this dynamic, which fuels the narrative that public schools are inferior

But so what!, I hear you say. We do live in a post-industrial economy United States. We do know that most people are going to require some degree of education to succeed in the labor market, AI fever dreams notwithstanding. And contemporary parents, like Nikole Hannah-Jones, do have more data than Mike’s parents did. They’re better equipped to make an informed choice about what school to send their daughter to, and New York already has far more choice than the average American city or town. Surely they have a responsibility to send her to the best school possible? Shouldn’t they choose good schools over bad?

Well, as I’ve pointed out far too many times, we have no coherent idea about what the “best schools” are, and which school you send your kid to has very little influence on their educational and economic outcomes. Again, I’m sensitive to repeating myself, so I will just point you in the direction of my existing arguments to this effect. Here’s 18,000 words and 200 citations arguing that student performance is largely fixed over the course of academic life. Here’s an old piece of my pointing out the ways in which New York’s “school choice” narrative is undertheorized and incoherent. Hannah-Jones references New York’s famed exam schools; here’s a recent piece by me pointing out that the relevant research universally finds that these schools provide no educational or economic benefit compared to traditional public schools. The private school people have been crowing about Hannah-Jones’s essay, but the perceived private school advantage is a product of the fact that the private school population and the public school population in this country are vastly different; the research tells us that it is pre-entry ability that determines which students will flourish and which will fail, not which of them goes to private or public school. She was robbed of a future because she didn’t take phonics!, sayeth the people caught up in the latest education fad. But by 11th grade students who take phonics and those who don’t are statistically indistinguishable from one another. If only she had grown up under NCLB!, but in fact even the optimistic case for NCLB shows no change in reading, a modest change in 4th grade math, and close to no change in 8th grade math, meaning that again, initial promising outcomes faded away in time. Did she go to pre-K??? Doesn’t matter, pre-K has no or even negative effects on outcomes. Etc, and etc, and etc. Longtime readers will tell you that I have much, much, much more rigorous research to share with you.

The overwhelming preponderance of evidence tells us that Najya Hannah-Jones’s academic future will not be determined by the school her parents send her to or the teachers who educate her. Nor will it be meaningfully influenced by whether she gets SAT tutoring, if she takes piano or chess lessons, if she plays volleyball, if she has access to educational games on an iPad, if she wears uniforms at her school, or if she takes a Common Core curriculum. What the evidence tells us will matter is how well she has previously performed in school, how well she performed in kindergarten, the central tendency of her academic life - which is to say, her academic talent. Because there is every reason to believe that such an attribute exists. Both behavioral genetics research and common sense strongly imply that this attribute has a meaningful genetic component. Of course environment also matters, but the top-down picture suggests that the environmental questions that matter are those like “Does the child have a pediatrician?,” “Does the child eat at least three meals a day?,” and “Does the child have at least one parent who is present in their lives who is not violent, an addict, mentally ill, or otherwise incapable?” These are, obviously, questions about conditions that schools are not equipped to change. And when you strip away the propaganda, when you dispense with the streetlight effect, you’re left with an evidentiary body that tells us that we cannot change the distribution of academic talent in the world, not in any meaningful and permanent way; our ability to succeed in school is normally distributed, meaning that there is a -2σ set of people as well as a +2σ as well as an average, and there always will be.

No, genes aren’t all of talent and talent isn’t everything. Student performance is also subject to far larger social and economic forces that operate well above the heads of schools and teachers. Najya Hannah-Jones is living and learning in a period in which academic metrics are absolutely cratering throughout the world. Unfortunately for some, the uniformity and scale of this collapse is simply not congruent with an argument of the type “American public school teachers are lazy and untalented, their unions are corrupt, and they’re teaching the wrong way.” It’s perfectly congruent with the observation that parents and teachers and concerned parties have been making for many years: that modern digital technologies like smartphones and LLMs are almost purpose-built to make learning harder, to make students more distracted, less curious, more dependent, less studious, and more helpless. We can’t prove that case definitively, not yet. But we can look at the remarkable similarity in declines in developed world scores and conclude that whatever’s happening is happening above the level of national policy and procedure, to say nothing of the level of states, districts, and schools. And I just find it bizarre, under these conditions, that people would fixate on the hoary old lines of argument about entitled teachers and entrenched unions. That debate arose in a different world. It’s time to evolve with the evidence.

Since the publication of The Cult of Smart, people have complained that it doesn’t give parents much in the way of advice. Sure, it’s easy to say that society needs to be more redistributive, that we have to give people a more robust safety net so that they can flourish despite the vagaries of talent and fate, all that jazz. But where does that leave a mom or dad who just wants what’s best for their kid?

Well, I know this answer is not what such parents are looking for, but here goes: your role as a parent is to love your child regardless of how good they are at any particular competitive field. Your job is to help them discover what they’re best at and what they enjoy and, within reason and while establishing realistic expectations, to help them build a life of meaning. Some of that will necessarily entail being honest (a gentle version of honest, anyway) about our natural personal limitations. I’m not going to go to a 7-year-old’s Little League game and then tell him he’ll never make it to the majors. But I am going to tell a 14-year-old with unrealizable MLB dreams, look, you can love this game, but it doesn’t have to love you back, so if and when baseball ends, what’s something else you can do that would feel fulfilling to you? How could you survive financially in the world, if not by becoming a millionaire baseball player? These conversations can be uncomfortable, but they are necessary. The reality is that we’re all constrained in our lives, in all manner of ways, some by poverty, some by disability, some by happenstance, some by simply being unfortunate enough to be born without a natural talent in the skills that capitalism happens to value in the early 21st century. I do believe that we as a society can decide to remove some of those barriers to success, to lessen some of those constraints. But I am only faithfully reporting the most sober and careful review of the evidence when I say that we have no reason to believe that schools are among the viable tools we have to do so. Sadly, our society is bizarrely fixated on the idea that they are the only tools.

Najya Hannah-Jones’s parents are affluent, educated, successful professionals. The crude-but-likely-effective extrapolation is that she’ll be fine, maybe not gifted but more than capable of doing the kind of tasks that are necessary to succeed in the modern white collar workplace. Of course, she dreams of more than that, and no doubt so do her parents. But then, her peers at her supposedly failing public schools did too, and many of them were no doubt born with worse odds than she was, in all manner of dimensions. If we want to help them all, we can become the kind of humane society that ensures none of them have to face a future without access to food, clothing, shelter, education, or healthcare. But we can’t make them all equal in ability, no matter how hard we try. Talent is real and talent will out. I don’t for a second begrudge Nikole Hannah-Jones for her worrying over her daughter and what’s best for her. After all, a big part of what I’m telling parents is that your responsibility to their emotional wellbeing should come first, ahead of your responsibility to their academic ambitions, only in part because you can control the former much better than the latter. If your kid is miserable at their local public school, I wouldn’t tell you that you have an obligation to keep them there. But that calculus just has nothing to do with whether they’re going to end up with higher SAT scores if you move them; we know they won’t. And it’s a category error, the conflation of those things, and the response to Hannah-Jones’s essay demonstrates that our media class is simply incapable of keeping them separate. And politics is why: liberalism, broadly defined, has no idea what to do with differences in natural talent, and our thought leaders reject any position that does not allow for a policy fix to social problems.

It’s like I said, nothing about these debates ever changes. This one essay has gone mega-viral and inspired a vast corpus of responses, but the degree to which it all just sings the same old song is striking. And the reason why is because the most natural, obvious observation about human talents and abilities I can imagine - that they are unequally and unfairly distributed - is forbidden knowledge, something that most people know intuitively but feel they can never say. Neither liberals nor conservatives will allow for the slaughtering of that particular sacred calf. As long as that’s true, as long as we have to pretend to believe that all kids have the ability to excel in school, which no one sincerely believes, we will run in these same lazy circles, have these same tired debates, and never emerge with a policy response that can possibly work. We are cursed by our refusal to mutter out loud the simple reality, understood by most children, that different individual human beings can be more or less smart. There’s nothing bigoted or cruel about that observation, but we flee from it all the time, it’s effectively banned in our most prominent publications, and without it, we’re stuck. And I personally am bored and exhausted of the same disingenuous arguments made by people who quietly know what I know but refuse to say what I say because their dying ideological order has no answers for a simple question: how do you help those who are unlucky enough to be born untalented?

I suspect my son, now 18 months old, will be pretty well academically equipped, at least sufficiently to have a good shot at a stable financial life. But who knows? Genetics are complicated, the environment is massively multivariate, and chance is as fickle as always. My own childhood, after all, was a story mostly about the influence of luck. I can’t change the big things, I’m afraid, and neither can Nikole Hannah-Jones. What I can do is raise him to feel loved and valued, give him room to explore what he’s good at and what he likes doing, and constantly remind him that the greatest thing in life is to be a kind person and a loyal friend. That is, simultaneously, not nearly enough and all there is. Aside from that, I will try to be honest with my son: almost all of us have dreams we will never fulfill, adult life is mostly about wanting and not getting, our reach forever exceeds our grasp, and we have no chance but to learn to live lives other than the ones we dreamed of. I will tell him that he needs to dream but also to adapt, that he needs to pursue success but accept failure, that he needs to remember that he can no more guarantee a particular future than he could guarantee the makeup of his own genome. And I would tell him that whether he’s the smartest or the dumbest kid to ever live, I will love him all the same.

What would I tell Najya Hannah-Jones? I guess I would say, “Najya, your part is to be what you’ll be.”
