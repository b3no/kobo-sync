Welcome to The Weekly Scroll!

The Weekly Scroll is a once-a-week roundup of the most interesting, strangest, and funniest stories from around the social internet.

This week, we’re looking at Anakin memes, the downfall of MAGA’s cultural influence, and why the vibes are so bad at Netflix.

Anakin Skywalker and the end of MAGA

The biggest news story this week has obviously been the midterm elections and the enormous blue wave that saw Democrats retake control of both the House and the Senate. Most polls and forecasts predicted a bad night for Republicans, but almost none predicted a wipeout on this level (although polling by The Argument and modeling we built with Split Ticket came about as close as anyone).

So how did the online right spend their last few days before they were wiped out? Posting memes about Anakin Skywalker, obviously.

Why Anakin? Many young conservatives identify with the wayward teen who would later become Darth Vader — he’s a lonely, misunderstood, angry young man. He feels betrayed by a Jedi bureaucracy that’s unfairly keeping him from his rightful place in society. He’s convinced that a corrupt system has denied him opportunities that he deserves, and he turns to a dark alternative to get what he wants.

Share

If you don’t swim in the murky waters of the online right, you might not believe how seriously this simple meme was taken. This was not just a joke: MAGA influencer Kangmin Lee called Anakin the “archetypal disenfranchised chud” and “perhaps the most profound piece of right-wing media in sci-fi form.” Another MAGA influencer claimed, “It cannot be overstated how culturally significant Anakin poasting is. I dont think you could engineer a better anti-progressive memetic virus in lab if you tried. [sic]”

This bled directly into politics and into the midterms. Right-wing shitposting account “Beff Jezos” suggested that the Anakin edits might turn the tides of the midterms. Katie Miller (the former spokesperson for DOGE and current wife of newly appointed White House chief of staff and aspiring Nosferatu cosplayer Stephen Miller) dressed up as Padme for Halloween and posted “Find the Anakin in your life.” The Department of Education started Anakin-posting. Elon Musk posted an AI-generated image of Anakin-as-imperial-patriarch with the caption, “This is what they stole from us.”

It’s obviously absurd that this was the right’s online obsession in the days leading up to a crucial election. But, hey, the Anakin story does actually illustrate modern right-wing politics pretty well.

In the Star Wars prequel films, Anakin is an impressionable young man who is tricked by a cackling, elderly tyrant into undermining democracy, after which he has children who hate him. Then, in the original trilogy, he loses and dies.

So, you know, not the worst analogy.

While no one has ever accused MAGA adherents of being culturally literate, it is shocking how poorly they understand storytelling. There were Anakin meme posters sincerely trying to both-sides Emperor Palpatine. Look, Star Wars is not the kind of story where you can make a contrarian argument that the bad guy is actually the good guy. Anakin kills a room full of children! The empire literally blows up planets!

And this flailing, I think, tells a deeper story about the decline of the weird online right’s cultural influence.

The story begins pre-MAGA, when Gamergate exploded and Steve Bannon realized that young men’s anger about video games and feminism could be harnessed by political opportunists. Taking advantage of 4chan meme culture, the online right swept into the zeitgeist and has spent the last 10-plus years steadily expanding its influence.

Personalities like Milo Yiannopoulos and Mike Cernovich made their reputations on Gamergate content. What started as an army of shitposters eventually merged with the conservative media ecosystem to create a network of podcasters, influencers, streamers, and online celebrities capable of controlling the national conversation.

This is a group of people that thought, both at an individual and institutional level, that posting AI-altered based memes of weeping women being arrested was a winning strategy. The movement peaked with Charlie Kirk’s assassination last year; for about 72 hours after Kirk’s death, it felt like the right had the power to cancel nearly anyone it wanted to, and that it might be able to canonize him while making cultural dissent impossible.

But that effort failed. Rather than being canonized as a saint, Kirk is now the butt of a million jokes, and there are children who only recognize him as a meme and don’t even realize he was a real person.

There’s a reason that after the midterms, JD Vance suddenly started talking like a Yale Law graduate again instead of an irony-poisoned groyper. Remember that this is the same man who, when MAGA conspiracists were inventing fake stories about Haitians eating cats, posted “Keep the cat memes flowing.” Now he’s embracing his original plan to be a studious, thoughtful commentator! Vance’s singular political talent is shifting with the winds, and he can see that there’s no more juice in the online right.

To put it another way, the Cruel Kids’ Table turned out to not be so popular.

That is what I take away from the fact that the big conservative memes in the month before the election were gender-bent Draco Malfoy and Anakin Skywalker. The online right’s cultural influence is on life support, and they’re grasping at increasingly bizarre straws, memes that nobody outside their echo chamber can understand or relate to. Perhaps they should have paid more attention to Anakin Skywalker’s story. Like Anakin, these folks thought they were an unstoppable force, destined to sweep away the old order. But Anakin loses everything that matters to him, gets maimed, betrays every principle he has, and still ends up regretting what he’s become.

Netflix is losing the war it already won

Netflix is an objectively successful company. It’s been the clear winner in the streaming wars: It has more users, lower churn, and higher profits than rivals like Disney+ and HBO Max. It made almost $11 billion in profits last year from a user base of more than 300 million subscribers. It is the 800-pound gorilla of the industry.

But despite all that success, the company has had an incredibly rough year.

Netflix’s stock is down more than 50% since its all-time high 16 months ago.. The company just laid off approximately 1,000 employees, and its head of film and chief content officer were both given pink slips in that process. Its effort to buy Warner Bros. failed. Its total viewing hours grew just 2% in the first half of the year, and Bloomberg reported that the average customer is opening Netflix less frequently and spending less time watching than they did a few years ago.

In other words, Netflix is still on top, but it’s slipping. I think there are two stories to tell about the streaming behemoth’s troubles.

The first is a story about Netflix’s particular decisions over the past few years. It has taken far, far too long to produce follow-up seasons to breakout shows like Wednesday and Stranger Things, leading many viewers to lose interest in those shows. And though it has developed big, buzzy hits like The Queen’s Gambit, The Witcher, and Bridgerton, you’ll notice that none of those titles debuted in the last five years. Instead of finding new breakout hits, the company has leaned into increasingly desperate attempts to hijack the attention economy. Short-form video! Influencer boxing! Video podcasts! Uh, video games, I guess?

This is why Netflix, despite being so much bigger than its rivals, increasingly feels disposable. It’s focusing on all the wrong areas and not churning out culturally relevant hits.

That’s one story. But the second story has nothing to do with Netflix’s decisions and everything to do with overall trends in entertainment. I’ve somewhat hidden the ball here by not yet mentioning Netflix’s real competition: YouTube.

According to Nielsen’s latest viewing data, YouTube now accounts for 15.8% of all television viewing in America. Netflix, by comparison, accounts for just 7.7%. And that gap has only grown over time. Short-form video continues to eat everything in our culture, and YouTube is simply in a much better position than Netflix to capitalize on that trend.

In the broad war for entertainment, it may simply be easier for YouTube to move from lower-quality user-generated content to higher-quality prestige content than it is for Netflix to move in the reverse direction. You see other tech platforms attempting this same pivot: Instagram is now experimenting with “Instagram for TV” and horizontal videos up to 90 minutes in length.

An even scarier possibility is that people simply don’t want prestige content as much as they want slop. Maybe vertical video will continue to devour everything, including all the other streaming services. Maybe Fruit Love Island is the future of entertainment, and we’re all just in denial about it.
