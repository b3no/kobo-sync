Everything is country

How a once-loathed genre became the sound of America

Halina Bennet
Aug 29
∙
Paid
 
READ IN APP
 

“Anything but country” used to be a common, glib response to questions about musical taste. It reflected a widely held view among non-fans that country was the one genre of popular music that could be written off entirely, even by people who generally considered themselves broadminded.

The attitude could be chalked up to snobbishness toward music that originated in the rural American West and South, or to a willingness to reject a genre that itself seemed very limited in the demographic diversity of its artists. Or maybe it was basic antipathy to a style of music known for a simple, predictable, and unsophisticated structure. Regardless of why, until recently, country was understood to be a niche taste — a large niche, to be sure, but one that many felt comfortable rejecting.

Today, though, I can’t remember the last time I heard that line. Country is no longer a genre to be tolerated or avoided; it’s become ubiquitous in America on the charts, the radio, and of course online, the place where music is most widely circulated today.

This week, Ella Langley’s “Choosin’ Texas” hit 19 weeks at number one on Billboard’s Hot 100, tying the record with two other country hits, Lil Nas X’s “Old Town Road,” featuring Billy Ray Cyrus, and Shaboozey’s “A Bar Song (Tipsy),” for the most time spent at the top excluding holiday songs.

Over the past seven years, we’ve gone from anything but country to everything is country, as pop, rap, and hip hop have gotten twangier.

One easy explanation for this shift in tune — both musical and attitudinal — is politics.

If the government is Republican and the country widely votes Republican, it makes sense that the music of choice would widely be the one most closely associated with Republicanism. While this works on the level of vibes, it doesn’t really match the specific facts of country’s ascendance very well. If this were a rightward cultural retrenchment, the sound driving it would belong to the people whom country music has traditionally centered. But many of today’s biggest country stars are not closely aligned with the beer-drinking, truck-driving, wife-loving motif that has long-stereotyped the genre.

Instead, many of the artists who brought the genre to new heights over the past decade are the kinds of people who were once locked out of country music. A theory about conservative revival can’t explain a genre that is thriving after having been remade by the people it long excluded.

Welcome to the era of Big Tent Country.

2018: The year country broke

To get to the Big Tent Country we see today, we have to look back at the moment that forced such an exclusive genre to crack. In December of 2018, 19-year-old Montero Lamar Hill, working under the name Lil Nas X, released a song on SoundCloud that he had made that month for just $50, including $20 for time at an Atlanta recording studio. It was the early days of TikTok, and the song went viral. By March, it had made its way into the Billboard Hot 100 and just as it was about to top the Country charts, Billboard removed it from the category because, as Variety put it, the song had “too much trap and not enough twang.”

The episode launched a conversation about race and country music’s exclusivity, gatekeeping in the industry, and the boundaries of genre as a whole. Nas tore those boundaries down, not only as a rapper making country, but also as a Black man and a gay man in the genre.

“A cowboy lives by his own rules,” he told Variety.

His remix of “Old Town Road” featuring Billy Ray Cyrus broke the record for most weeks at number one on the Hot 100, and the record held for five years. Nas’s embrace of country and his fight to be included broke down the boundaries around what counted as country, paving the way for the genre-blurring that has allowed country music to balloon in popularity.

Beyoncé’s album “Cowboy Carter” was similarly explosive when it was released in 2024, but the album quickly overcame pushback from both the public and the industry and won both Album of the Year and Best Country Album at the Grammy Awards. She was the first Black woman to win the latter category. Immediately after she did so, the Grammys announced a rule change. Starting in 2026, there would be two separate categories: Best Contemporary Country Album and Best Traditional Country Album.

The gatekeepers are still trying to gatekeep, but despite their best efforts, they cannot stop the genre’s dissolution as it is absorbed into everything else.

Everything is country, and nothing is country

At the same time as Beyoncé made the case that country music and culture came from Black foundations that were erased by those gatekeepers, Shaboozey, an eight-time Grammy-nominated musician who blends country and hip-hop, released the year’s biggest hit. “A Bar Song (Tipsy)” tied Nas’s Billboard record, and, while some questioned whether or not the song is truly country, the song was nominated for both Best Country Solo Performance and Best Country Song at the Grammys.

As Ella Langley tied both Nas and Shaboozey this week for time spent at number one, she did so without any controversy. This almost certainly has more to do with the fact that she, unlike her peers, is a conventional artist for the genre. She is both white and considered, at least by Google and Spotify, to be purely a country artist. Regardless, “Choosin’ Texas” is sharing room at the top with two other country hits. The song has brought even more people to country and cemented the genre’s success: It shows that country no longer needs to rely on fusion with more popular genres to achieve widespread acceptance.

Most of America’s most popular musicians are doing country. If the genre can now be used to describe Taylor Swift’s pop instincts, Morgan Wallen and Shaboozey’s hip-hop-inflected hits, Sabrina Carpenter’s honky-tonk pop, and “Cowboy Carter,” it’s barely describing a category anymore. The genre has breached the boundaries of its siloed fanbase and been embraced as the sound of America.

Arguably, this is a return to an order that is much older. In the 1940s, certain artists would top the country and R&B charts simultaneously — it wasn’t until Elvis did so consistently that rock music began to be conceived of as an entirely separate genre. But now, there is a rejection of such a tendency to create strict categorizations for genre-bending artists and we are back where we were decades ago.

Big Tent Country

Perhaps, then, if everything is country, nothing is country — a contradiction and metamorphosis that were always inevitable. At least, Dolly Parton, a pioneer of post-1940s Big Tent Country, seemed to think so.

Parton broke, nay burst, into the boy’s club in the late 1960s, becoming a successful singer-songwriter in a genre where women doing so was a rarity. She defied the genre’s rules immediately, refusing to be kept out by Nashville’s idea of who or what belonged or what country sounded like. An early genre-bender, she mixed pop into songs like her 1977 Grammy-nominated hit, “Here You Come Again.” Parton, according to her producer Gary Klein, was clear about her intention to mix genres, not simply to make a foray into pop.

“She wanted people to be able to hear the steel guitar, so if someone said it isn’t country, she could say it and prove it,” Klein told journalist Tom Roland for his 1991 book, “The Billboard Book of Number One Country Hits.”

Once Parton secured her place in the genre and her influence as a whole, she spent the following decades actively bringing the walls down further. She embraced opportunities to bring unconventional-for-country artists into the fold, collaborating with Pitbull, Post Malone, Paul McCartney, Queen Latifah, Norah Jones, Pink, Sabrina Carpenter, and Beyoncé.

Parton, who died on Tuesday, was celebrated by nearly everyone. In April, a poll from the University of Massachusetts found that Parton, by a long shot, was the most beloved person in America. With a net-favorability of 65 percent, Parton led both former President Barack Obama and Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy by 50 points. After her death, she received tributes from the White House, the Democratic Socialists of America, conservative Christians, and the queer community. She was perhaps the most coherent and stable personification of American values and morality.

Parton’s genius, then, was not really about crossing genres. It was her refusal to conform to one version of what country or a person was supposed to be. She sang gospel and Grammy-nominated pop and collaborated with Sting and headlined the Grand Ole Opry. Her refusal to conform is the same attitude that Nas embodied when he broke the genre open.

I’m not surprised that country is having a moment. But I think that rather than evidence of the rise of a single political party, its ubiquity is resistance to and a fight against division.

Parton never let one side claim her. And country music, which finally belongs to everyone, has followed her example.
