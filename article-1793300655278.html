The Q&A: “There are not that many institutions left to speak truth to power.”

The rector of the Université de Montréal on reaching a billion-dollar fundraising goal

Paul Wells
Oct 29
 
READ IN APP
 

I’m on vacation this week, then doing some work overseas next week, but when I heard that the Université de Montréal has reached its $1-billion fundraising target, I was happy to speak to the big university’s rector (the equivalent of a principal or president), Daniel Jutras, about the accomplishment.

I’ve written about the sense — my sense, anyway, or my brooding worry — that universities are growing estranged from broader communities and from our politics and governments. One way to remind everybody of universities’ persistent relevance is to ask citizens to contribute — which requires, in turn, that universities demonstrate they’re worth contributing to. The UdeM’s campaign needed, and received, widespread support in Quebec and outside. Some stats:

  • 31,212 UdeM alumni and 5,942 employees contributed to the campaign

  • 27,039 donors made their first gift to UdeM as part of the drive

  • While some donations from private family foundations were huge, the large majority of donors (40,010) gave less than $1,000.

I’m happy to help Daniel Jutras spread the word. This conversation has been edited for length and clarity.

Paul Wells: You’ve raised a billion dollars. Why did you need the money, and how did you get it?

Daniel Jutras: We always need money to fund activities that are not fully funded, or even funded at all, by our primary sources of funding, which are government funding and tuition fees. There are a lot of activities, initiatives, research projects and support for students that would not be funded otherwise. I think that’s the primary reason for universities to engage in philanthropy.

The second reason is that I think it’s appropriate now, in all campaigns that engage universities, to have a dimension that calls for greater participation of alumni: to bring them back into the fold, if you like, to get them to think more about their university. And the two are connected. The more alumni are connected to the university, the more likely they are to contribute to its ongoing success.

Part of our campaign was to count the number of acts or gestures of engagement by our alumni. Not just giving, but participation in activities on campus. We had a target of a billion dollars for the monetary campaign, and a target of 200,000 acts of engagement by our alumni.

We’ve achieved a billion dollars, and we’re at 178,000-odd actions that we can identify as manifestations of people’s commitment to the university.

How did we get the money? Dollar by dollar, I think, is the short answer. It’s an enormous endeavour. It requires — and people don’t always know this — the application of some professional practices. It’s a specialization, if you like, something that requires a bit of expertise.

We slowly built that expertise. My predecessors had already begun to develop philanthropy and alumni relations well before I came, but we really accelerated this over the last five or six years, and even more so since the campaign went public three years ago.

We invested more in personnel who connect us with alumni and seek giving from both alumni and non-alumni. About a third of our giving comes from people who are not graduates of the university.

PW: The official launch of the campaign was the beginning of 2024. Did you expect to be done by now? Did you have a deadline?

Jutras: No, we didn’t have a deadline. I would say the natural deadline would be the end of my own term. I’m in the second five-year term of my mandate as rector of the Université de Montréal, and so we thought we had to be done by 2030. I didn’t want to burden my successor with a promise of this nature. But we’re well ahead of what we’d anticipated. I think this is a very audacious effort on our part. It’s the largest capital campaign that any francophone university in the world, as far as we can see, has ever launched.

Setting the target at a billion dollars was quite audacious. Unnerving, I would say. Stressful. Our expectation was that we’d be done by 2028. So we’re a bit ahead of schedule.

PW: You’re a legal scholar. You’re a former executive legal officer of the Supreme Court of Canada. Did you expect that you would be spending this much of your job essentially as a salesman? Do you think you got to be good at it?

Jutras: I absolutely expected that I’d have to do that. That’s what all university presidents do. We spend a lot of our time not just raising money, but interacting with others to explain what our university is doing for our communities, for research, for the advancement of knowledge.

The bulk of the work that I do is just that: speaking to a variety of stakeholders and explaining what we can do for them, what we’ve done in the past and what we hope to do in the future. So I definitely expected that. It was also written in my mandate letter. The board of the university that appointed me was very clear.

Before that, I’d been dean of law at McGill University. A legal scholar, yes, but also an administrator. And as you know, that faculty has a long tradition of philanthropy. As dean, I developed a bit of expertise, or at least experience, in raising money for the university. So I had a sense I’d be able to do it.

But the truth is that there are a lot of other people engaged in this effort. Our deans are also engaged in it. Obviously, you know some of them very well, and many have been very successful in raising extraordinary sums of money, developing on their own great relationships with philanthropists and alumni. So I do a lot of it, but there are a lot of other people around the university who do this.

PW: Having spent time at McGill and now leading at the Université de Montréal, this raises an old piece of cultural shorthand in Quebec — that fundraising and philanthropic giving are something that the English do, and something that francophones have less of a tradition in.

Your release announcing the billion-dollar target being hit calls that a myth. Was that an obstacle that needed overcoming? The sense that this is something that the others do?

Jutras: The candid answer is that there’s always been truth in any myth. There’s always a little bit of information that explains why the myth developed. And it is true that philanthropy is different in Quebec when you compare it to what’s happening elsewhere in Canada or in the United States.

Clearly, it wasn’t an obstacle. I mean, the target was ambitious, but it’s smaller than the philanthropic targets of American universities or even Anglo-Canadian universities. It’s certainly, as I said, the largest of any French-speaking university in the world. But I do think we needed to account for a little bit of truth behind that particular statement.

It’s not true that Quebec society is not generous. A large number of our largest institutions have been created by philanthropists. Sainte-Justine, various hospitals and so on. Quite clearly, there’s a history of supporting our community through generosity. But it’s different. I said in my speech to the Board of Trade three or four years ago that I thought generosity here is more emotional, more spontaneous. It’s not as structured. People do give at the grocery store, in significant amounts when you add them up. But not many people have a planned gift in their will, which I think is more common in the English-speaking community.

There’s also — and here we get into deeper elements of the psyche of Quebec society — perhaps a larger faith in government to be the vehicle for solidarity and welfare measures. I think it’s also connected to the history of Quebec, where the Church was responsible for much of what we now see being funded by philanthropy: schools, social services, healthcare and so on.

But nonetheless, I think the result demonstrates that the myth is busted now. We received gifts from 46,000 different donors, which is a large number of people. Two-thirds of those are graduates of the university. Twenty-six thousand of the 46,000 are making their first gift, which I think is notable.

And there are some very, very sizable gifts. Most obviously, the gifts from the Fondation Courtois, which supports the Institut Courtois that you’re familiar with, and that I think is now going to be a jewel in fundamental research in physics, new materials, quantum and AI. It’s extraordinary. Hundreds of millions of dollars from a single foundation was unexpected in some ways, but certainly extraordinarily welcome. And the $40 million from the Fondation Chopin-Péladeau in Quebec to support the development of entrepreneurship.

So there have been quite a few manifestations of support from the French-speaking community. But not just that community. The friends of the university have been with us for many years. The Fondation Jean Coutu, the Desmarais, the Goodmans, the Azrielis. I can name a number of people who’ve been longstanding supporters of the university and who again manifested their support in this campaign.

PW: As you know, a lot of my readers live outside Quebec. Some of them are surprised to hear about a leading Canadian university doing almost all of its work in French.

Jutras: We’re finding that if we can frankly convey the significance of what our university does — if you look at the results from Research Infosource, for instance, which measures volume and funding for research, we’re number two now in Canada. I think perhaps that simply comes as a surprise to a number of your readers.

But this university is extraordinarily active in research. It’s certainly a leading player in a number of important fields, ranging from AI, obviously, which is well known now.

The key players that people keep reading about in the newspapers, many of them come from my university, starting with Yoshua Bengio and then moving well past Yoshua Bengio.

The most recent recipient of the Turing Medal is a colleague of mine, Gilles Brassard, who works on quantum informatics or computer science.

We have remarkable expertise in healthcare. Obviously, a very large and dynamic network of hospitals and research centres in Montreal. Sainte-Justine, the Montreal Heart Institute, significant expertise in some of our affiliated hospitals in cancer research and clinical care. Sacré-Coeur is a world-class centre on trauma. So there’s quite a bit of weight here around human health.

We have longstanding expertise in animal health, with a faculty of veterinary medicine. There aren’t many in the country. It’s the only one in French in the country.

And then the expertise ranges towards the humanities and social sciences as well. We succeed at an extraordinarily high level in SSHRC funding for humanities and social sciences. Research centres that you’re familiar with, the CÉRIUM, for instance, and now the MAPI, the Maison des affaires publiques et internationales, which are developing, I think, an extraordinary profile.

I’m surprised, but not that surprised, that this is not well known across the country, because I think news does not circulate easily across that cultural and linguistic divide.

I think people should be happy to know that there is a very successful, large-scale, world-class university working in French in this country.

PW: All of this campaign, and much of your time as rector has taken place at a time when the general relationship between North American universities and the broader society has often seemed strained.

There’s a kind of progressive estrangement between universities and governments, between universities and people who don’t go to university. A sense that they are woke encampments where things happen that have nothing to do with everyday life, and that are to some extent almost antagonistic to everyday life.

We certainly see that in the Trump administration’s campaign against American Ivy League universities, but also to some extent in the Legault government’s attitude towards university funding, international students and things like that.

Do you still have some convincing to do, or some explaining to do, to the broader society and to governments?

Jutras: I think we do, most definitely. And I’ll get back to that in a moment. But I would say the context here is a little different from what people have experienced outside Quebec, for a number of reasons.

French-speaking universities, and mine in particular, have not been in the limelight for what you just described as wokeness that infuriates some governments on the right and some politicians on the right. We didn’t have an encampment during the early days of the Israel-Gaza conflict, and the university, I think, addressed those interactions between people on both sides of that debate quite well.

We are committed to academic freedom in ways that I think have been very explicit from the outset of my mandate. We had a mission to develop principles and guidelines on academic freedom and freedom of expression on our campus, and the vivre-ensemble, if you like, the ways in which we interact on this, has been quite positive. So I don’t think people think first of the Université de Montréal when they think of embattled campuses with fights between the left and the right.

But it’s true that there’s a crisis of confidence in the country that is affecting universities. What’s interesting in this respect is that, if you look at the surveys and the stats, I was with Jean-Marc Léger last week, preparing for an interview with him as one of our alumni, and we were talking about that.

I asked him about the most recent surveys that he did. He did one for the Canadian Association of University Teachers, and it shows that in Quebec, confidence in science and confidence in universities is actually quite high, 70 per cent or so, higher than what you would see in the rest of the country. I’m not quite sure how to explain that, but we’ve been able to maintain that confidence.

One possible explanation is that it costs much less to come to a Quebec university than it does elsewhere in North America. So the cost-benefit analysis that people engage in yields different results in terms of the value of time spent in our university and the money spent to study at our university.

I think we’re still in a pretty good position in that respect to convince Canadian citizens and Quebec citizens and our communities that we play a significant role in their welfare, and in the welfare of the country and its sovereignty and resilience.

We’ve invested a lot of energy on this over the past few years because there are genuine concerns about the capacity of universities to transfer the knowledge that is developed within the university to actual applied science that creates innovation and improves the lives of citizens.

We also have to explain that there’s an intrinsic value to universities, independently of the ways in which knowledge is transferred and used by society. I do think universities are critical institutions, and there are not that many left to speak truth to power. There are not that many organizations that have the distance from power, sufficient critical foundation, sufficient resources and mobility to continue the search for truth, unimpeded by all kinds of obstacles and constraints. That needs to be maintained, even if we can’t always see what the next invention will produce for Canadian citizens.
