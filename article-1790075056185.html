Why I send my kid to low-income urban public schools

Not every school with unfavorable demographics is a dysfunctional mess — policy matters!

Sep 22

I disagree with Nikole Hannah-Jones about a lot of issues, but I thought her recent piece about her regrets over sending her daughter to her poor-performing local public school rather than acting like most parents and deploying her financial means to maximize school quality was extremely well-written, emotionally affecting, and honest.

I’m also a little sad to say that among acquaintances of mine, especially non-parents, the main takeaway from the story seems to be that the threads of idealism were completely misguided and every parent has an absolute obligation to do everything in their power to optimize for their own kid.

Reading the story, I had some uncomfortable moments because Kate and I have made structurally similar choices with our son’s education. From pre-K (which starts at age three in D.C.) through fifth grade, he went to our local public elementary school, a Title I school that is 47 percent Black, 20 percent Hispanic, and 22 percent English language learners; it was even less white when he started.

He now goes to our locally zoned middle school, which has similar demographics.

Since he’s only been at the middle school for a few weeks, I can’t speak to the experience in detail. But our experience being the affluent family at a mostly low-income majority-minority elementary school was fine — good, even. Not without difficulty or downside, of course, but even from a purely selfish viewpoint, I’d say the difficulties and downsides were minor compared with the price of private school tuition or the annoyance of moving to the suburbs.

What’s more, not only do I think it’s been a fine decision on a selfish family level, I think that it has accomplished some of our broader goals. The school primarily serves low-income families and many of the kids do have difficult home lives and discipline problems at school. But the school was still a well-run, happy place for our child, who learned to navigate a much broader range of social interactions than if we’d sent him to school only with other affluent children, and the presence of a critical mass of affluent, educated, engaged parents I think made a real difference in the lives of the teachers and the students.

I’m not here to quibble with Hannah-Jones’s take on her experience, and our situations are certainly not identical.

Instead, I’m writing to those who reacted to her piece with blanket skepticism about inner-city schools or with hostility to the idea of considering the needs of other children when deciding what to do for your own, because it would be a shame if every affluent urban parent of a toddler decides they need to flee to the suburbs.

Maybe you do and maybe you don’t. But the actual details of schools that serve high-need populations vary quite a lot, and it’s worth both engaging with the specifics of your local situation and thinking about the general policy questions.

D.C.P.S. has been on an upward trajectory

One key difference between our situation and Hannah-Jones’s is that while Washington, D.C. Public Schools (D.C.P.S.) was notoriously bad for a very long time, it’s been on a broadly positive overall trajectory.

Some of the gains at D.C.P.S. reflect the fact that more affluent parents have been enrolling their kids in public school, which has mechanically pushed the averages up. But we’re not looking at a pure compositional effect — D.C. has been showing stronger results across all the major demographic subgroups (I really recommend Josh Boots’s article for Capital Commonsense if you want a detailed rundown of all the good news).

Why have the schools in D.C. gotten better?

I think it’s broadly because under ex-Mayor Adrian Fenty a contentious new “reform” regime came into power under the stewardship of D.C.P.S. Chancellor Michelle Rhee, who became a national media celebrity thanks to her battles with the teachers’ union.

Fenty failed to secure re-election after one term, but his successor, Vincent Gray, replaced Rhee with her deputy, Kaya Henderson, who did not seek national media attention in the same way but essentially stayed the course.

This Fenty-to-Gray transition stabilized and consolidated the reforms, and then while current Mayor Muriel Bowser’s 11 years in office have been fairly uncreative on K-12 education, she hasn’t really done much to alter the positive trajectory.

One particularly effective component of these reforms is a well-designed performance pay system for D.C.P.S. teachers that ensures that the most effective teachers are very generously compensated by national standards. That has improved retention of the teachers you would want to retain and made D.C.P.S. a good place to teach if you’re the kind of ambitious educator who wants to take on big challenges and deliver results.

D.C.P.S. has also been a national leader, albeit not necessarily on the Mississippi scale, in implementing a rigorous phonics-based early-literacy program.

These changes have not miraculously solved every problem in every school. But they have improved things. And they’ve made D.C.P.S. an appealing place for families like ours who believe in public education but aren’t naive about it.

Our son’s former elementary school has been at the forefront of these changes. It’s not the top-testing school in the city (as in any city, the highest-testing schools all have very high shares of rich, highly educated parents), but it performs incredibly well on value-add and demographically adjusted measures. It’s led by an energetic principal who works hard to recruit strong teachers and it was written up in the New York Times as an exemplar of sound early-literacy instruction.

But these things aren’t automatic, and parents can play a big role in creating a virtuous cycle.

For several years, Kate and one of her friends ran the school’s Parent Teacher Organization. They raised a lot of money, primarily from the minority of affluent families at the school, and used that money to support the teachers and make their lives easier so the principal’s work of recruiting and retaining them would be easier.

But there’s more to it than just donating to the P.T.O. It’s also the parents with more resources who’ve been disproportionately involved in volunteering for the school’s fall and spring celebrations, which are widely attended by the entire school community. Kate organized and ran, with the help of a coach we hired, a soccer club for fourth-grade boys when there was no room for them on the school team. Another dad in the class taught a chess club when the school needed inexpensive after-school activities. Other parents chipped in and handled the administrative tasks of registration so that kids whose families couldn’t afford extracurricular activities weren’t left out. The presence of more affluent families doesn’t make schools better by magic; it’s being actually involved in things that helps lift other families up.

The background policy conditions are important too, though. They include the teacher pay stuff I mentioned earlier, but the school also ran a pretty tight ship on discipline. Our time there wasn’t without difficulty on this front (as Hannah-Jones mentioned in her piece, out-of-class times like lunch and recess can be particularly challenging), but the school made a strong effort to communicate rules clearly and enforce them consistently. And I think everyone who cares about equality and integration needs to understand that if rules aren’t enforced — whether that’s in public schools or public transit or whatever else — people are going to opt out. There’s a ton you can do to help your kid outside of school, but if the discipline piece doesn’t work, there’s no salvaging that. And at our school it worked pretty well.

Outside options

For all that I’m positive about the education offered at my child’s elementary school, there was no math tracking (everyone is lumped together in the same class regardless of ability); the main educational focus has been on improving outcomes at the low end. To the point of Boots’s Capital Commonsense article I mentioned earlier, D.C.P.S. has actually achieved improvements at the low end, which is important to me. A lot of insane stuff has been done in American school systems in the name of equity, and what D.C. did was actually get better results for the most struggling students. It’s one reason we feel so strongly about continuing to participate in and support D.C.P.S.

Still, that focus doesn’t necessarily create the best environment for the brightest kids.

Our solution has been outside math tutoring. We’re certainly not unique in this respect — lots of parents across America use programs like Kumon or Russian Math. We’ve opted for individual tutors rather than big national chains because that was more convenient, but this sort of supplemental work has been key to keeping our kid happy and engaged at his public school.

What I would say, though, is that lots of parents all across America end up using some kind of supplemental instruction. If you send your kid to a diverse urban public school, you probably will, but moving to the suburbs is no guarantee that you won’t. And while it costs money, it really is a lot cheaper than private school or moving to the fanciest school district.

Our middle school doesn’t have enough kids to do within-grade tracking (a “regular” sixth-grade math class and an “advanced” sixth-grade math class, for example), so they instead do a form of math tracking where if you place strongly at the beginning of sixth grade, they just send you over to the seventh-grade math class. When our son learned that option existed, he was eager to qualify, so he worked hard over this past summer to make sure he did well on his entrance test. He’s always liked math, but he was more motivated and excited about math at school than he’d been in years!

The English classes at his school aren’t tracked at all, but he’s always been an avid reader who spends plenty of time with books of his own accord. We were disappointed, though, by how unambitious the curriculum was in terms of assigning books. What we’d ideally like the school to do — and what I think a private school or a better public school would, in fact, do — is have recommendations on hand to push a precocious reader forward with material that will challenge him but not be so dense as to make him miserable. Instead, for our kid, it fell to Kate to come up with some more ambitious books for him to read this year.

We’ll see how it goes.

One distinction I want to draw here, though, is between a school that is genuinely bad or dysfunctional in the sense that the teachers and the staff can’t or won’t do their jobs properly and a school that simply isn’t really oriented to our child.

Our elementary school is genuinely a very well-run school, a safe and nurturing environment full of talented staff who work tirelessly to care for the students. But it’s also true that the staff’s main job is trying to get the bottom third of students up to an acceptable level of competence. And they’re really good at this! But at a diverse public school like this one, if you want a lot of enrichment for a kid who finds school pretty easy, you need to put in some effort and money to find supplemental options outside of the school.

If you end up way further out on the effort spectrum with homeschooling or on the money spectrum with private school, you can almost certainly get a more tailored experience.

On the other hand, as long as your kid is happy, I think there’s a fair amount of value in learning to get along with different kinds of people, to deal with a certain amount of being a little bored, and to not constantly be the center of attention.

Some common sense

I feel that I have pretty banal views about all of this, and I’m a little wary of the hotter takes I see flying around on social media:

  • Obviously don’t send your child to a totally dysfunctional school or one where they’re miserable. I don’t think K-12 schools have a huge impact on the objective life trajectory of the children of affluent professionals (I’m pretty sure Nikole Hannah-Jones’s daughter is going to be just fine), but your child’s quality of life in the moment still matters.

  • On the other hand, it’s valid and correct to give some consideration to the impact of your choices on other people’s children. A country full of people who care about their community is going to be a much better country than one where parents are all gassing each other up into amoral familism.

  • Most of all, don’t just assume that your local public school is dysfunctional if it serves a low-income population or has below-average test scores. Make an effort to actually look into demographically adjusted measurements of school performance. There are plenty of well-run schools with so-so or below-average results due to challenging demographics. A school that’s doing well with the students it has will probably also do well with your student.

One thing that’s frustrating to me in almost all conversations that I have with parents about our children’s collective schooling is how little interest there is in education policy.

People toss off casual remarks about school budgets that aren’t true. Or they have concerns about the disciplinary environment or level of academic rigor, but they don’t pay attention to the Obama-Trump-Biden-Trump ping pong on discipline (Trump is right) or the pernicious influence of the “grading for equity” movement. I’ve felt good about the management of D.C.P.S. during my son’s entire school career, but there’s a new mayor incoming who is allied with people who I think have unsound views about public education.

I have no particular desire to leave D.C.P.S., and neither does our son, but there are some downsides to sending your kid to even a well-run diverse public school (there are also upsides like “it’s free” and “I can live in a neighborhood I like”). But if it stops being well-run, that’s a totally different question.

Left-of-center professional circles tend to be unfortunately torn between people taking refuge in exclusionary suburbs and then hand-waving about underfunded inner-city schools or else asserting that desegregation efforts are like a magic wand that will make all dilemmas and tradeoffs go away. If you want public education to work (and I do), you need to insist on things like reasonable discipline policies, reasonable amounts of ability-grouping, and reasonable levels of academic standards. If you don’t, then the impulse to privatize access to schools either formally with vouchers or informally with zoning becomes hard to resist.
