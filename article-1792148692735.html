Plus school closures, literary adaptations, and Olivia Rodrigo

This is not worth a whole article, but one of the craziest takes I’ve read in a long time was a Jacobin piece arguing that the decline of reading isn’t about streaming video but rather the nefarious influence of “the consolidated corporate publishing industry that made books pricier, more generic, and harder to find.”

I promise you, though, that it has never been cheaper to read classic works of literature. On your smartphone you can download the complete works of Jane Austen for $0.49 or get all of Shakespeare for $0.99. Public libraries still exist. In inflation-adjusted terms, hardcover books are much cheaper than they used to be. It’s true that the specific publishing genre of cheaply produced mass-market paperbacks has gone into decline, but that’s because (a) ebooks have displaced them as the lowest cost option and (b) as reading has declined there is less of a low-end market to serve.

The problem is that the exact same technology that has made it cheaper and easier than ever to access the classic works of literature has also made it easier to access a bottomless pit of content and there’s only so much time in the day.

David E: A few weeks ago you tweeted that you thought the public defender community had “lost the plot sometimes,” and quoted a case in which a public defender was arguing that the Second Amendment protected their client’s right to possess a gun. You have written before about the differing obligations of politicians (who must, by and large, cater to the public will) and activists (who must, when their cause is unpopular, seek to persuade the public that it is right). I understand you’re not an ethics lawyer, but how do you view the political role of public defenders or other actors who have obligations to particular individuals but may also have broader political goals?

My basic read of the public-defender situation is this. One of the core ideas of our criminal justice system is that providing zealous legal representation to each defendant, including the guilty ones, serves to protect the interests of the innocent. This principle extends to the indigent. In some ways it is particularly important for them.

So it is honorable and appropriate that there are people kicking the tires in every criminal prosecution, seeking to expose improperly obtained evidence or to impeach the credibility of law enforcement officials or to reveal the shortcomings of witness testimony or expert analysis. The existence of public defenders doing this work is an annoyance to the law enforcement apparatus and causes some problems, but it also prevents serious abuses.

My sense from looking at the Public Defender Service for the District of Columbia, and many other major public-defender operations in America, though, is that the ideological basis of the work has shifted.

Rather than seeing zealous defense of the accused as a vital protection of the rights of the innocent, the office is dominated by folks who reject the legitimacy of punishing the guilty. So they advance a lot of arguments that aren’t about the facts of specific cases but are in fact efforts to make sweeping policy changes to decriminalize swaths of anti-social behavior.

Ian Kenley: My wife and I read your article about why you sent your kid to a low-income public school. Our child will begin primary school soon, and we’d love to know more about how you concretely evaluated schools. Specifically, were there any school performance measures or people that you sought out?

In D.C., the best source of information is the EmpowerK12 data dashboard. What’s actually available is going to differ from place to place. But basically what you should be looking for is a data source that includes both information about schools’ results on state assessments and also information about schools’ demographic composition.

MW: I’m starting to think ahead to college for my oldest son, who is the same age as yours. You’ve written previously about the fact that success among kids who go to highly regarded K-12 schools is primarily a selection effect, with the school itself having little causal effect on outcomes. Do you think this extends to the university level as well? On the one hand I feel very privileged to have gone to an elite university, and I do think it opened doors for me professionally. But I was actually pretty unhappy at Harvard and graduated after 6 semesters to get out of there as quickly as possible. I am now a (very happy) physician practicing alongside colleagues who went to much less prestigious (and pretentious) schools but ended up in the same place. I’m personally coming to the view that the main benefit of going to a place like Harvard is the networking effect — important if your child is an aspiring management consultant, investment banker or politician, but probably less so for everyone else. How are you thinking about how to guide your son as he gets closer to deciding about college?

The best evidence on this comes from Raj Chetty, David J. Deming, and John N. Friedman, who have a paper on “The Determinants and Causal Effects of Admission to Highly Selective Private Colleges.”

I would say that the main takeaway from this paper should be that the causal impact of attending an elite college is dramatically smaller than most Americans appear to believe. I’ve also discussed this paper with Deming, who is currently dean of Harvard College as well as being a smart guy who does a ton of interesting research, and that is not his preferred interpretation. The way he sees it, they identify a real causal impact here and also some very genuine admissions biases toward kids from more affluent families and there’s a case here for taking real steps to diversify the social elite with a more meritocratic admission system. What’s sort of missing in this friendly disagreement between the two of us is an index of what the conventional wisdom is — from where I sit, the level of ambient stress about college admissions that professional class parents express suggests a belief in much larger causal impacts than this paper finds. But the question of what people actually believe is an interesting separate research question.

In terms of what those causal impacts are, the authors don’t say it’s all networking but they do find that the causal impact is especially large when it comes to admission to elite graduate-school programs and to getting jobs at a relatively small number of what they call “prestigious firms.” I think that supports your intuition that if your aspiration in life is just to get a normal job — even to become a very successful Main Street Millionaire — then going to Harvard is not necessarily a big deal but that there is a specific niche of institutions where an Ivy pedigree is very valuable.

David: As left wing candidates’ old comments about Obama resurface, I’m curious how you remember that period, when you, like them, were young and on the left. Did you have friends whose enthusiasm for Obama turned to disillusionment? Mamdani apparently worked for Obama’s campaign right after school (he discusses it around the 11 min mark here) but by the time he was 20 yrs old, we know he was sharing Glenn Greenwald’s critiques and calling Obama’s foreign policy “evil.” Why do you think you grew more conservative instead of following that trajectory?

And would a President Sanders have similarly disappointed his supporters? Do you think AOC or Mamdani’s legacy will ultimately satisfy the left, or will they be remembered more like Bill de Blasio?

Like any really influential figure, Obama forged a post-Obama politics that makes it a little bit hard to make sense of what the actual politics of the moment were like. At the time that Obama was running for office, a moderate Democrat was Mark Pryor or Mary Landrieu and a person with my politics was a progressive. Which is to say that a person with Obama’s politics was a progressive. Which is to say that people who self-identified as progressives were really excited about Obama’s political ascension; it was really the first time since F.D.R. or whatever that someone from the progressive wing of the party seized the mantle of leadership and won national office.

But that in turn just means that the “progressive” camp contained an incredibly wide range of views.

And from the very beginning, Obama generated a lot of angst among the more serious left-wing intellectuals. Anti-war types pointed out that while he opposed the Iraq War, he did not really embrace a radical critique of American foreign policy. Economic populists argued that while the Affordable Care Act was a big deal, Obama-style expansion of the welfare state didn’t really challenge the capitalist system. Greens warned that his technocratic approach to emissions reduction didn’t amount to the kind of twilight struggle against the fossil fuel industry that they wanted. And he antagonized a lot of left-wing African American intellectuals with his recourse to “respectability politics.”

I think most older leftists did not really experience the Obama years as a moment of disillusionment — they had low expectations of American electoral politics and felt that Obama more or less met those expectations. But there’s a large and very left-wing cohort of people who are somewhat younger than me and who experienced this more as whiplash, from intense hope to intense disappointment and even at times as a sense of betrayal simply because they lacked context on what pre-Obama politics was like that would have helped them to understand in what sense Obama was a progressive icon and in what sense he was not.

But there was also always a kind of ambiguity around Obama. The Obama-era Democratic Party contained a large and influential moderate wing up until the 2014 midterms. Obama’s official position was always that he was more progressive than the red-state senators from the classes of 2006 and 2008 but that he needed to pass bills and make appointments that they would vote for. That always raised the question of where he “really” stood. My view is, I think, less that I became dramatically more conservative since Obama’s second term than that I was a major defender of him against his critics on the left back in his first term and was not a huge fan of the “Obama unchained” dynamic that we all got once the Old Moderates left the scene.

The thing was that when the Old Moderates were around, it was easy to find them frustrating. Today when we talk about moderate Democrats, we’re talking about people like me. But obviously the median voter in a state like Louisiana or Arkansas or Montana or North Dakota is way more conservative than I am and so were the Democratic Party senators who could win those states. I used to always take for granted that since every state has a median voter, every state’s local Democratic Party would make fitful efforts to win Senate elections by running nominees who were more conservative than I was. Then I could sit at my desk in D.C. and alternate between complaining about them and defending the need to work with them pragmatically.

Since they’ve been gone, though, I’ve really missed those guys.

I think the mainstream view among post-Obama Democrats — not super-radicals but the Progressive Establishment — is that the lesson is that the Old Moderates all ended up losing in 2010 / 2012 / 2014 anyway so they should have been squeezed harder to prog-max at all times. These people are happy to see New Moderates like Josh Turek, Mary Peltola, and James Talarico win pivotal Senate nominations in 2026, but they’re going to be furious if those New Moderates don’t end up betraying their voters. And that’s not how I see it. In retrospect, I wish that more had been done by the national party to defend the interests of the Old Moderates. And while I’m glad that Chuck Schumer and Hakeem Jeffries have backed a smart group of nominees in key races, what I want them to do is articulate a national party agenda that can win those key races. I don’t love the idea of Turek and Peltola and Talarico out there on an island constantly getting squeezed by party leadership and screamed at by progressives.

I think the elite decision to choose polarization has had a dangerously destabilizing impact on America and that we need to really embrace democracy in a more fulsome way and not just try to sneak a fast one past people.

But I know lots of people who I’ve been friends and acquaintances with since the mid-aughts who have the exact opposite take on this. They think we should just be pretty nihilistic about the ebbing and flowing of the national political environment and then push people to achieve as much as is humanly possible any time the opportunity to secure a trifecta presents itself. To those people, Obama’s restraint during his first term was a betrayal. To me, his lack of restraint once he wasn’t on the ballot anymore was more the betrayal. But either way, we are all participating in a post-Obama political dialogue that’s very influenced by what he did.

Rbbb: Have you seen any indication that the Democratic Party has any plan as to what strategy they should pursue in the event that they take control Congress? My suspicion is that they fritter away their opportunities till 2028 comes around.

This is actually very related to what I said above. A rational planning process would say, “If we are lucky enough to win the Senate, our tipping point member is going to be from the red states, so we ought to be crafting a plan that Mary Peltola, Josh Turek, James Talarico, Sherrod Brown, and Roy Cooper are enthusiastic about.” The problem with that is that Chuck Schumer’s median voter in the caucus is someone from a much bluer state who is vaguely worried about a D.S.A. primary challenger and that Schumer’s main practical job is funneling money from donors who are generally very progressive toward those pivotal red-state races.

All those stakeholders he’s managing would be very disappointed by a plan that optimized for Iowa or Texas. So if Schumer made a workable plan, it would be rejected by his caucus. And if he made a plan his caucus was amenable to, it wouldn’t work for the pivotal members. So refusing to discuss the issue seriously is genuinely optimal even though it’s also stupid. I think that ultimately the whole party needs to become much more serious about the question of what it means to win and hold majorities, which starts with recruiting good candidates in the pivotal races but really needs to involve inverting the basic dynamic within the party. It’s fine for the big tent to include the occasional Bernie Sanders or Abdul El-Sayed as gadflies. But if you want to win seats in Iowa and Texas and Alaska and North Carolina and Ohio, the mainstream leaders of the party should be articulating ideas that are acceptable in those states. And the only way for that to work is for the bulk of the members of the caucus to be okay with those ideas.

Sean: I’m a software engineer, and for the last couple years, I’ve been giving a workshop on how to use Python for data analysis to university professors who don’t have a computer science background but are looking to use data more in their research. In the past, I’ve focused on more on language syntax, but now I feel like the much more realistic workflow is doing the analysis using AI and the problem shifts to verifying it did the right thing.

You’ve written about how you now use AI (I think mostly Claude?) on doing some analysis and visualizations for Slow Boring. I’m curious from your perspective, as someone who is smart but without a CS background, what have you learned about doing data analysis with AI, and how to verify it?

Two things I do:

  • Human spot check individual data points — just pick one item (Iowa or whatever) and manually check if it’s done that one correctly

  • Spin up a new instance of Claude and ask it to interrogate the analysis

lwdlyndale: There seems to be an emerging progressive position that public schools just shouldn’t be closed for reasons of declining enrollment or poor performance (here’s an example from Minneapolis of this consensus.

Given demographic trends this just doesn’t seem sustainable. Any idea for what to do about this?

This is not even remotely new. A huge part of the backstory to Brandon Johnson’s rise in Chicago is that Rahm Emanuel was pretty aggressive about closing schools that had both low enrollment and weak academic results and there was tremendous public backlash to that. Then all the way at the other end of the sociological spectrum, various good government types have been trying to get towns in rural Maine to consolidate their schools throughout the 21st century and there’s always tremendous resistance. Towns will persist in operating schools that are clearly below any kind of efficient scale for as long as they possibly can. People just really don’t like the idea of their local school closing.

When I had Cleveland Mayor Justin Bibb on a panel I moderated a while back, he brought this up as an example of how when you hold an executive office, you can’t just say things people want to hear. He said he was obviously not excited about the school closure element of the Building Brighter Futures plan and knew there would be backlash but concluded that all his other options were also unpleasant and would also generate backlash, so he might as well do the right thing. And God bless. But I think we waste a lot of time on the internet having dumb arguments about easy questions like “does it help to win elections to take popular positions on issues” and spend too little time debating these governance dilemmas. People don’t like school closures, but they also don’t like tax increases or teacher layoffs or cutting the police budget, and ultimately you have to make choices within a budget constraint and it’s just a hard job.

Shreeharsh Kelkar: I teach in a college where students are by and large progressives, and some are especially political. When these political students present, I often hear, even in presentations that are about other topics, ideas about crime and policing that strike me as wrong-headed. For e.g., policing was an extension of slave patrols, prisons are just another form of enslaved labor (thus the American prison system is an extension of slavery and Jim Crow), etc.

How exactly does one push back on this, especially if they are delivered as parenthetical asides? Probably one answer is not to push back when these are parenthetical asides (which is my answer) but I’m curious if there are other possibilities here.

I’m not sure what you teach or what context this arises in, but I think you should consider pushing back. After all, these things aren’t true and people shouldn’t say untrue things. The pushback might even be as gentle as, “I don’t think it’s a great idea to casually drop inflammatory claims that you’re not backing up with evidence into your presentation.” The slave patrol thing, in particular, I think is really very straightforwardly wrong and not up for debate or interpretation, and anytime I hear it, I’m inclined to say we ought to be asking why there are police everywhere from Finland to the Bahamas. This just can’t possibly be the institutional legacy of America’s slavery history.

John NH: I love and endorse the Moby-Dick idea. Why do movie versions of literary masterpieces always diverge so much from the novel? Outside of Shakespeare, where the dialogue is the story, which seems to keep writers and directors in check? As great as Ray Bradbury is, where does he get off telling Melville that Fedallah was excess bloat?

Nowadays, a lot of thriller writers will deliberately craft novels that are designed to be adapted into movies or TV shows, but most books can’t be faithfully adapted for the screen because they weren’t written that way. A lot of the best literary adaptations (“Blade Runner,” for example) take an extremely free hand with the adaptive choices. And a lot of great novels are much too sprawling to do literally at feature film length and just require compressions and cuts.

Even when you take something like Denis Villeneuve’s “Dune” movies, he stretched the story out across two long films and ended up cutting out a lot of stuff from Frank Herbert’s book. I find myself very torn on the whole project because I really miss some of the stuff that was cut but also find the movies to be brilliant but maybe a bit too long. What I really want is for the one novel to be stretched across three films. But that’s a lot of “Dune.” I just really like “Dune.”

You also get into some interesting questions. We’re all going to get a new “Sense and Sensibility” movie soon. They’ve cast Daisy Edgar-Jones, who is 28, as Elinor Dashwood and Esmé Creed-Miles, who is 26, as her sister Marianne. These characters are 19 and 16 in the novel. This is a pretty significant change. But it’s done in part because to contemporary sensibilities, Marianne marrying (spoilers for the 1811 novel “Sense and Sensibility”) Colonel Brandon at the end would play as wildly inappropriate. By aging everyone up, you make the plot resonate more with a contemporary audience. But you are also doing some violence to the author’s intentions. Marianne is genuinely very silly at times, but that’s because she’s an impulsive teenager, not because she’s the world’s biggest idiot. When you age her up to 26, the marriage to Colonel Brandon becomes more palatable but I think it’s going to make her earlier behavior seem bizarre. Given that we got aged-up characters in a very well-regarded 1995 movie, I wish the new one tried to stick to the text and give us the teen drama that Jane Austen wrote, but I do understand why movie producers are reluctant to go that route.

Sam Curry: A genie tells you that you can create a ten-minute video that every American will watch. They will provide their full and undivided attention when watching this video. The video will be slickly produced, visually appealing, and well-written. You can cover any topic (public policy proposal, explanation of a topic, history lesson, etc.) What is your video going to be about?

It’s going to be on housing. I think there is a lot of evidence that many voters sincerely do not understand the relationship between housing supply and affordability and also that they can be persuaded by evidence. If you could actually force everyone to watch a highly persuasive, evidence-rich video about this, it would probably make a big difference.

Chad Holtz: When you bring up a hypothetical Gavin Newson administration, are you trolling us or do you truly believe he is the likeliest 2028 nominee? If you are messing with us, it’s pretty funny. If not, gross.

I’m not saying it’s super-likely, but I think he’s got top-tier odds and is also an excellent stand-in for “totally generic mainstream progressive Democrat.”

If the next president is a left-factionalist or an avowed moderate or even Kamala Harris (who, after all, would be making history as the first woman president), then the specifics of the situation are going to be important. Newsom is just the vague forces at work in Democratic Party politics personified.

President Camacho: Your Pixies reference in Friday’s mailbag got me excited about rediscovering 80s and 90s indie rock. What music (new or old) have you been listening to lately and what is your medium for enjoying music in the digital age?

Because I’m old, I mostly listen to now-old music from when I was young. But also an interesting thing I’ve noticed is that as I’ve become the kind of old person who doesn’t pay much attention to new music, it tends to be very mainstream pop stuff that does get me to tune in. I don’t really know anything about what’s happening in indie rock these days, but I am very familiar with Taylor Swift’s catalogue, and I like Dua Lipa. I loved “Brat” and was disappointed by Charli XCX’s newer album.

In that spirit, though, my favorite of the contemporary pop stars by far is Olivia Rodrigo because her music so clearly references and is influenced by the alt-rock I grew up on. When I first heard “The Cure,” I could have sworn she was sampling a Smashing Pumpkins guitar riff.

But even though I know she sometimes gets accused of ripping off other songs (most notably “Good 4 U” and Paramore’s “Misery Business”), this is pretty clearly original. It’s just kind of got a Smashing Pumpkins vibe. And at her shows, she’s played songs with Weezer and Alanis Morissette and other giants of the ’90s. To hear a young person talking about “Pinkerton” just warms my middle-aged heart. To bring it full loop, she booked the Breeders as an opening act and the first time I heard her music was a friend excitedly texting me a link to “All American Bitch” to tell me Gen Z had discovered the Pixies’s quiet-loud-quiet formula.
