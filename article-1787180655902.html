The have-and-have-not housing market

Wealthy buyers are buying houses a million over asking with cash while everyone else watches starts fall and rates stay stubborn. Localities are experimenting with solutions to close the yawning gap.

Housing starts declined 12.4 percent in July compared with the month before for single- and multi-family construction, falling 13.5 percent from the same time last year, according to Commerce Department data released this week. Residential permits, though, rose 5 percent — an indicator that new housing could be coming down the pipeline to offset a near-term slump.

Mortgage rates offered little relief. Freddie Mac’s weekly survey put the 30-year fixed rate at 6.67 percent for the week of August 13, which is down slightly from the previous week but still up from the same week last year, which had the rate at 6.58 percent. Meanwhile, the 15-year fixed rate was at 5.96 percent, down from last week’s 6.01 percent but up from last year’s 5.71 percent.

Day to day, mortgage rates have been jumpy amid economic uncertainty driven in large part by the war in Iran and a lack of clarity around whether or not the Fed will move in September.

Builders, for their part, are not quite ready to throw in the towel despite a soft month. The National Association of Home Builders/Wells Fargo Housing Market Index reported Monday that builder confidence in the market for new single-family homes rose one point in August. While the increase was essentially negligible, it surpassed economists’ predictions of a slight decline from 34 to 33, Reuters reports.

Still, this month, at least a third (35 percent) of builders cut prices and nearly two-thirds (63 percent) used sales incentives, roughly consistent with trends across the summer. The Midwest had the strongest regional builder confidence, the National Association of Home Builders reports.

The state of affairs might not be what it seems. Even as current housing data looks decent, Redfin makes the case that wealthy buyers, for whom the market truly is strong, are propping up national figures while everyone else gets squeezed out.

“Many everyday buyers are constrained by affordability challenges, while wealthy buyers have the means to keep competing for desirable homes,” Chen Zhao, Redfin’s head of economics research, said. “That upper-end strength is helping prop up prices even as the broader market cools, giving buyers some bargaining power.”

This is perhaps most apparent in San Francisco, where the median price for a home is $1.7 million. CNN reports that A.I.-driven wealth is continuing to fuel bidding wars that are pushing some homes a million dollars over asking. With all the competition, the Bay Area market is truly strong — Redfin found that about one in three home sales were all cash from April through June, according to CNN. The pressure extends to renters, who are experiencing rates for two-bedroom apartments exceeding those in New York City. Still, active listings are down 30 percent from this time last year.

For those who have not become newly minted tech millionaires, Business Insider reports that Capital Economics, a macroeconomic research firm, predicts 2026 will be the worst year for the housing market since 2011. According to Capital Economics, homebuying has slowed due to high borrowing costs and mortgage rates, which the firm does not expect to budge, and home prices (outside of frenzied markets like the Bay Area) are on track to remain flat amid low demand.

The market, then, is neither uniformly weak nor uniformly strong. It has diverged into a hypercompetitive race among the ultrawealthy and an inaccessible, stagnant rut for everyone else.

Building the way out

On the production side, there is light at the end of the tunnel. Factory-built housing is having its moment in the spotlight as a provision in the 21st Century ROAD to Housing Act is cutting friction and costs that have historically acted as barriers to building supply. The Wall Street Journal published an article on Tuesday that gets into the weeds on this particular element of the federal housing package, identifying one reform — removing a requirement that manufactured homes retain a permanent steel transport frame — that could save up to $10,000 per home. It’s a longtime Slow Boring cause.

For modular housing, the legislation requires the Department of Housing and Urban Development to examine financing barriers and determine whether a standardized building code could work. Unlike manufactured homes, modular housing does not have a federal construction standard and different jurisdictions have different codes, forcing factories to customize their designs to fit certain cities and states and preventing large-scale production. Additionally, financing presents another hurdle, as modular factories need money upfront for materials and construction, while most traditional construction loans release funds incrementally as a project progresses. Banks are also less familiar with modular homes and find them to be risky, the Wall Street Journal reports.

Easing those barriers could unlock the potential for more housing, in this case through methods that reduce labor costs, construction waste, delays, and variability. But some members of the modular housing industry, according to the Journal, are urging a slowdown in the movement to create a national code, advocating instead for a review of potential new rules since building requirements do, for good reason, require regional specifications.

Meanwhile, localities search for a fix

Washington, D.C., renters facing high housing costs are getting creative — including some young women who are renting rooms in convents. NPR reports Catholic-run homes are charging residents between $500 and $700 a month to live in the district.

St. George, Utah, has decided to prioritize housing even as the federal government attempts to move in the other direction. The southwestern city opened its first permanent supportive housing project for seniors, veterans, and homeless residents last week, debuting 110 units in a converted and expanded former motel. Switchpoint, the nonprofit developer behind the project, used a mix of federal low-income housing tax credits, a state housing tax credit, and $1 million from Intermountain Health — a large nonprofit health system — to finance the development, which aims to address homelessness directly, rather than through shelters alone, the Salt Lake Tribune reports.

And here’s another idea out of Utah: Senators John Curtis and Chris Van Hollen, of Utah and Maryland, respectively, are pushing for a bill that would allow localities to lease surplus U.S. Postal Service properties to construct housing, the Salt Lake Tribune reports. In May, the Brookings Institution released an analysis that found U.S.P.S. land to be a promising site for new housing, since post offices are already integrated into communities and served by existing infrastructure. Brookings determined that the underutilized sites could accommodate up to 237,000 units of housing.

In Los Angeles, a $2 billion project is underway to construct a complex of four skyscrapers in Warner Center, a master-planned mixed-use commercial and residential neighborhood. The complex will include 3,192 affordable units for seniors, who will have private rooms and bathrooms but share communal kitchens and living spaces, the Los Angeles Times reports. As the country’s population ages, the project attempts to solve the issue of housing the growing share of seniors amid high land costs.

New York City set a target this week to build 700,000 new homes over the next 10 years, a plan the New York Times writes is at the core of the city’s fix for its housing shortage. The first half of the new goal — 350,000 units — is expected by city planners to be finished in the next five years, the Times reports. The Fair Housing Growth Strategy released Wednesday comes three months after Mayor Zohran Mamdani published his Block by Block plan to spend $22 billion on 200,000 new units of subsidized housing.
