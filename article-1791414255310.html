A blue wave is building as the G.O.P.’s numbers sink

With the war in Iran unpopular and prices rising, every Republican Senate candidate the Times polled is underwater. A Democratic majority is within reach but far from secure.

Halina Bennet
Oct 7
∙
Paid
 
READ IN APP
 

Democrats’ odds of walking the narrow path to a Senate majority have never looked better. But nobody should (or does) feel very secure about the outcome, as polling for most of the key races has Democrats leading relatively narrowly — often within the margin of error. The party, then, could win one, all, or none of these races essentially on a coin flip — or, more realistically, on the known uncertainties around turnout. Still, the fact that so many Senate races that were not considered remotely winnable for Democrats at this time last year are now tight marks a meaningful shift in the political environment.

The key factor is a G.O.P. undertow that is dragging Republican candidates out to sea, leaving behind a blue wave, as President Donald Trump’s approval rating continues to slip, disapproval of the Iran war grows, and frustration over gas prices and the cost of living mount.

The president says he isn’t worried that a Democratic majority will bring about a third impeachment, telling a reporter he’s “getting good” at it.

An ever-widening Senate map

To gain a majority, Democrats must flip four seats while holding all their own frontline seats. A batch of battleground state polling taken in September and released this week from the New York Times/Siena has Democrats leading in Alaska (Peltola +7), Ohio (Brown +3), and Texas (Talarico +6). Iowa leans slightly right (Hinson +1), and Kansas — the black sheep of the midterm’s final stretch — is tied.

Marist also released new numbers, and they are even more positive for Democrats: Iowa (+8), Ohio (+8), Michigan (+7), Texas (+6), and Maine (+4). The numbers shift poll to poll and day to day, so taking them at face value is ill-advised, but it is a bad sign for Republicans if their hope of maintaining a majority depends on polling errors.

Siena found that Democratic candidates lead among independent voters in most key races, which are primarily in swing and red states with large independent blocs. Democrats lead among these voters in Alaska, Kansas, Texas, Iowa, and Ohio. The split among independents voting for either candidate in Maine and Michigan is even. The Hill reports that some G.O.P. senators are privately warning that Trump is toxic with these voters and urging him to stay away from key races, and even some Republican senators in safe red territory are breaking with Trump.

“I’m not going to tell the voters, ‘Oh, it’s President Biden’s fault, and it’s not that bad, you shouldn’t believe your checkbooks,’” Senator John Kennedy of Louisiana told the Washington Sun of rising prices tied to the war in Iran.

The president made campaign stops in friendly territory last week, appearing at rallies in Alabama and Oklahoma, but on Monday he said he would be paying Michigan a visit in “a week and a half” and had plans to make a stop in Maine. In a debate against Democratic Senate nominee Troy Jackson on Tuesday, Susan Collins said Trump would not, in fact, be visiting Maine, drawing a clear line between herself and the president.

Meanwhile, Dr. Abdul El-Sayed, the Democratic nominee for Senate in Michigan, is encouraging Trump to visit his state. “Your candidate can’t speak for himself,” El-Sayed said, referencing his opponent, Mike Rogers, on MS NOW. “Clearly, you are the answer, and you should tell everybody why they should imagine you on the ballot.” The Democrat has been working hard to build a broad Democratic coalition after a divisive primary had Black, Jewish, and moderate voters concerned about his candidacy. The New York Times reports his efforts appear to be paying off.

Trump isn’t the only member of his party with poor favorability numbers. Every Senate candidate polled by the Times is underwater, while every Democratic candidate has a positive net rating.

  • Kansas: Adam Hamilton +12 vs. Roger Marshall -5

  • Iowa: Josh Turek +10 vs. Ashley Hinson -4

  • Texas: James Talarico +9 vs. Ken Paxton -12

  • Alaska: Mary Peltola +8 vs. Dan Sullivan -11

  • Ohio: Sherrod Brown +5 vs. Jon Husted -10

In those races, Republicans either control state government or hold the Senate seat, making quite obvious voters’ dissatisfaction with the party across the country.

Senate races to watch:

Maine: Susan Collins and Troy Jackson faced off Tuesday night in the first of four planned debates over the next month before the November 3 election. The candidates clashed over Trump; rising prices of fuel, energy, housing, and tariffs; and abortion votes.

Jackson questioned whether Collins regretted her 2018 vote to confirm Brett Kavanaugh to the Supreme Court, but she stood by her decision, saying she has always judged nominees on their qualifications and prior voting history — not on how they might hypothetically vote in the future. She said she did not feel that Kavanaugh lied to her during a private conversation before the confirmation vote when he said he considered Roe v. Wade to be settled law. The justice later joined an opinion overturning Roe v. Wade’s abortion protections in 2022. Collins rebutted Jackson by citing his record of supporting anti-abortion measures in the Maine Senate but then said she was the only one on the stage who has “always been pro-choice.” Jackson, who met with Planned Parenthood Maine on Monday and said he had been wrong to hold anti-abortion beliefs, did not directly address Collins’s attack on his voting record.

On costs, each candidate blamed the other for failing to use the powers of their office effectively. Jackson linked high fuel and energy prices to the war in Iran and tariffs, pointing a finger at Collins for her support of Trump and failure to check him. Collins blamed housing, energy, and property-tax costs on state policies enacted under Jackson’s Maine Senate leadership. Collins cited her effectiveness in the Senate, pointing to the $1.5 billion she has secured for Maine from the federal government this term, and questioned the difference Jackson will be able to make as a freshman senator. Jackson dismissed her tariff exemption for road salt: “We can’t eat road salt,” he quipped.

The race is a toss-up, and Collins’s near-30-year tenure in the Senate has made her a mainstay in both state and federal leadership. The Washington Post Pollwatcher reports that the New York Times/Siena poll found 49 percent of likely voters supporting Collins compared with 32 percent who approved of Trump’s job performance — a 17-point gap. In 2018, Republican Senate candidates consistently ran behind Trump on favorability.

Collins is trying to keep her distance, saying in the debate that she is not exactly the president’s favorite senator.

Texas: Perhaps the state undergoing the most dramatic transformation is Texas, where the large Hispanic and Latino voting population has taken a decisive swing left from 2024. The New York Times/Siena poll has State Representative James Talarico up 32 points among those voters.

This lead is big for a Texas Democrat but not unprecedented. In 2018, former U.S. Representative Beto O’Rourke led polls of Latino voters by 10 to 27 points, depending on the survey, eventually winning 69 percent of their vote but losing to incumbent U.S. Senator Ted Cruz. In 2024, Colin Allred also led among Latino voters in polls, but 52 percent of Hispanic and Latino voters ultimately said they voted for Cruz during an election in which the group also went for Trump.

Meanwhile, Texas Attorney General Ken Paxton, the Republican nominee, and his boosters seem panicked.

“I’ve never been in a race where I felt less control over the campaign and, like, what issues are going to matter because it’s all coming — you know — from D.C.,” Paxton said during a small fundraiser last month, Politico reports. “I don’t control message or what we’re doing.”

The state has become a bottomless pit for the G.O.P.’s money, pulling a tremendous amount of funds from other key races that are increasingly slipping out of the party’s comfortable reach.

“This is a slow-moving car crash that we’re witnessing right now,” a state Republican strategist told Politico. “And there’s a 75 percent chance that the ultimate result here will be catastrophic from a G.O.P. Senate majority standpoint.”

Trump is visiting San Antonio Wednesday night to rally for Paxton. Governor Greg Abbott, whose own race is tightening, will skip the event.

Following the money

With less than a month before Election Day, both parties are making triage decisions about their spending on Senate campaigns.

WinSenate, a super PAC connected to Senate Democrats, is pulling money from North Carolina, where former Governor Roy Cooper — the Democratic nominee — has a comfortable and consistent lead in the polls, The Hill reports. Meanwhile, the National Republican Senatorial Committee is running coordinated television ads in Ohio, Maine, Alaska, Iowa, Michigan, and New Hampshire, foregoing spots in North Carolina and Georgia, Punchbowl News reports.

Despite the triage, both parties are still reporting unprecedented fundraising numbers. The Democratic-aligned Senate Majority PAC raised a record $151 million in the third quarter, entering October with nearly $112 million on hand. Combined with its nonprofit arm, the PAC brought in a total of $202 million, the New York Times reports. ActBlue hauled in a record $833 million for Democratic candidates and causes in the third quarter, passing the previous midterm record of $586 million in the second quarter and narrowing the gap between its party and the G.O.P., Bloomberg reports.

Still, Democrats are being outspent by Republicans in top Senate races, and the gap for gubernatorial races yawns even wider, Jonathan Martin writes in his column for Politico.

Gubernatorial races are closer than they once appeared

Democrats are competitive in several contests for governor where the state executive position is currently held by the G.O.P.

Marist has Democrats ahead in Iowa (Rob Sand +12), Ohio (Amy Acton +6), and Texas (Gina Hinojosa +3), as well as in Michigan (Jocelyn Benson +12) and Maine (Hannah Pingree +12), where the offices are currently held by Democrats. New York Times/Siena has Sand up 11 percentage points, Acton up four, Hinojosa up three, and Alaska’s Democratic nominee Jonathan Kreiss-Tomkins up four in the final round of ranked-choice tabulation.

Republicans have their own bright spots. New York Times/Siena has Kansas State Senator Ty Masterson leading by five percentage points in his race to flip the governor’s seat and Governor Kelly Ayotte up 11 points in her New Hampshire re-election bid. But the party is also facing some unexpected trouble in Florida, where the MAGA nominee for governor, Byron Donalds, is trying to distance himself from Trump without alienating the president as he faces an unexpectedly formidable candidate in Democrat David Jolly, calling the race “a battle,” the Wall Street Journal reports.
