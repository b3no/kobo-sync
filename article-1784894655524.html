How I Became Episcopalian...ish

As I’ve said many times before, I was raised secular. My parents were not Ricky Gervais tier atheists, but they were both more or less agnostic and felt strongly about not pushing any particular religion on me. They came from different faiths themselves (my dad was raised Jewish, my mom was raised in what she calls “Nearist,” because her family just went to the church that was nearest their house) and despite these different upbringings, they were aligned on raising me with a clear code of morality and ethics, but no particular faith. They wanted my religion or lack thereof to be my choice.

I discovered I was half-Jewish when I was about four or five. I’m not sure what this really meant to me at the time, because my dad’s form of Judaism was very light on the religion and heavy on the jokes and fun Hanukkah presents. I knew, probably from kids at school, that this was more of a religious thing for other people and I was curious about that. One day (and I actually remember this) I asked my mom, “Daddy is Jewish, so I’m half-Jewish, but what’s my other religion? What are you?” to which she replied, “English.”

And being Jewish did feel like being English. For me, it was a culture, like any other that one might reference on Heritage Food Day in elementary school. Just as I did not attend church, I did not attend temple. I never learned Hebrew beyond what was necessary for an annual game of dreidel. Not a single word of Yiddish was spoken in my house except by my gentile mother who, by marrying my dad, had finally earned a pass to say things like “kvetch” for no reason. (I still recall her calling my second-grade nemesis a mieskeit, ie: Yiddish for chopped, kinda deep cut honestly.) We celebrated Hanukkah, but it was a very fun and secular Hanukkah where my dad did goofy voices for half the prayer to make us laugh, not unlike our very fun and secular Christmas, which contained more references to Burgermeister Meisterburger than to Jesus.

But I had some vague yearning for religion, in a way I couldn’t quite explain. I remember “praying” as a very young child, with no real clue of which religion’s God was hearing my prayer. Also, I’m pretty sure my prayers were mostly about unicorns and Barbies.

By grade school, I had discovered that Christianity was a thing, and that it was a thing my mother had experienced in a mostly negative way.

I don’t think she had any particular trauma associated with it, but she did have negative associations with particular types of Christians, who a 2014 Redditor might refer to as “fundies.” This made Christianity a bit taboo and therefore exciting. My mother did not forbid it, but I got the sense that if she could have chosen my faith, she would have picked Judaism despite not being Jewish herself (she had kind of a Rachel Dolezal thing going on.) Church began to feel like an intriguing secret club. I used to pretend to be a priest sometimes and deliver sermons to my American Girl Dolls, but because I had never been in a church before I had no idea what priests talked about. I just kinda wilded out and did standup comedy for a while, mused about my life and going on tangents, occasionally bringing Molly or Samantha up as audience volunteers for magic tricks.

Middle school changed things for me. As you can well imagine, I related very hard to Are You There God? It’s Me Margaret. The prescient 1970 young adult novel follows a half-Jewish girl (Jewish dad, once-Christian mom, neither of whom are religious) who is not only obsessed with religion but with getting bigger boobs. It was basically written for me. I finally had a friend group, and all of those girls were Catholic. They all went to Sunday school together, which they hated, but it was a shared group hatred and I wanted in.

But I couldn’t tell if I wanted religion for a community (even if that community was a bunch of twelve-year-old girls making fun of it) or because I desperately needed rules and structure. From my regular viewings of The Simpsons, I noticed that Christianity involved many rules and forbidden activities. (Like my early viewings of Austin Powers, I didn’t get that it was a joke, and I was unaware that they were poking fun at prudish churchgoers.) I suddenly became terrified that I had committed sins, and needed some kind of framework to identify and avoid them. This was all made worse when my only non-Catholic friend (she was a Jehovah’s Witness) gifted me an “advice book for teens” which was almost entirely about the sins of masturbation and premarital sex, with catchy word bubbles with groovy twirly letters that said things like, “If it feels good, it’s fine, right? God says: WRONG.” Although I had never done either of these things before, I was terrified that I eventually would (they did sound kind of fun) and then I’d go to Hell.

My obsession with going to Hell was almost definitely the beginnings of my OCD symptoms becoming more severe and taking the common form of religious scrupulosity. (I had been suffering from OCD as early as five years old, but I didn’t get a diagnosis until I was sixteen or so.) At thirteen, all I knew was that I had engaged in plenty of depravity already: selfishness, lying, jealousy, and once accidentally stealing an NSync snap bracelet one time at the Limited Too when I snapped it onto my wrist, forgot about it, and walked out of the store. I quite liked the dichotomy of rigid Christian rules and an ever-loving, all-forgiving God who would never be mad at me as long as I was really sorry. I needed explicit commands and forgiveness, so I would always have reassurance that I was a good person and that someone loved me. Christianity seemed like an obvious choice.

At this point, you might be wondering why I didn’t just lean into Judaism. I always enjoyed Judaism as a cultural or ethnic identity, akin to being Italian or Chinese, but I never felt strongly about the religious side. It might have been as simple as not having many Jewish friends, or the fact that Jews are not eager to convert anyone, but a lot of my lack of religious interest had to do with the fact that Judaism’s relatively loose structure around sins and the afterlife left me with a lot of anxiety. I wanted a religion to tell me exactly what to do, and it seemed even the most conservative versions of Judaism didn’t do that because I failed to understand exactly what would happen if I disobeyed. I understood why someone might keep kosher to avoid eternal damnation, but I couldn’t summon the motivation to eschew a good Big Mac without the threat of punishment. (Even the consequence of clogged arteries did not deter me.)

Christianity (denomination unclear) gave me a much more comfortable framework for my particular brand of weirdness—relatively straightforward rules about what I wasn’t supposed to do, very compelling threats to keep me in line, but the lovely and appealing concept that I’d be forgiven if I felt really guilty (and this one was easy for me, because I felt guilty about everything, including some very victimless crimes, like noticing that one of my teachers had bad teeth.) Moreover, as I later learned from a Catholic beginner’s manual handed to me by one of my friend’s moms, all I would have to do to become a Christian would be to…say I was one?! Was she for real? Throughout the manual she had gifted me, I was assured that to receive eternal salvation love from God, all I’d have to do would be to let Jesus into my heart as if I were “opening the door for a friend.” I would not have to learn a new language, get certified by anyone, or do any particular thing, other than just say yes in my head. Was it really that easy?

This was also a really rough time in my life, in a way that I would later learn was very typical for middle school girls. But like many preteen girls, I felt like the first and only person in the world who experienced social isolation, and fashioned myself the Omelas child of my middle school. I had no friends for most of eighth grade—my friend group rejected me in a very humiliating way at the end of the previous school year—and I just recall feeling hopeless and unwanted all the time in a way that could only be resolved by slamming my bedroom door shut and listening to the lyrical genius of Nickelback. But the idea that, even if nobody else liked me, God loved me, was extremely appealing. Faith in God (even if it was based on a very poor understanding of Christianity with a hefty dose of OCD) gave me what felt like secret armor when I was being bullied at school. Luckily, I had the good sense not to say “Well, at least Jesus loves me!” when the popular girls were making fun of me for wearing knee socks.

At this point, despite my “devout” faith expressed mostly in diary entries and private prayer, I had still never gone to church. One summer when vacationing with my family by the Jersey shore, I expressed interest in attending the tiny, wooden Episcopal church near the house where we were staying, in the tradition of my maternal grandparents. My parents, true to their promise that I could choose whatever religion I wanted, agreed to take me. I had no church clothes, nor did I understand what people wore to church, so I showed up at a church mostly filled with eighty-year-olds in flip flops, a low-rise denim mini skirt, and a pastel yellow Juicy Couture T-shirt.

For reasons I still can’t explain, I wanted to cry as soon as I entered the church. Something about the ethereal music and universally welcoming smiles of the elderly parishioners affected me deeply. This has happened to me almost every time I’ve been to a new church, and I still don’t know why, because it’s an uncontrollable urge to cry that feels unrelated to sadness or happiness. But in any case, I held back tears, sang the hymns, listened to the sermon, and concluded that maybe church wasn’t as boring as all my friends insisted. I was having (at least what I believed to be) a transformative spiritual experience that transcended my complete lack of religious education. It felt comforting and warm—the first place where I could go, knowing absolutely nothing, and not feel like a moron for it. The people wanted me there, even with my ridiculous clothes and the fact that I was confused about why I was supposed to shake everyone’s hand all of a sudden.

At one point in the church service, everyone took communion. The priest announced that “all baptized Christians” were welcome to take it, and at this point my mother reminded me that I was not baptized. I felt a deep pit in my stomach, realizing that perhaps there was more to becoming a Christian than simply saying I was one. I would have to be baptized, which felt like a bigger step than I wanted to take, especially given that it would probably involve some degree of participation or cooperation from my parents. They did not say anything judgmental about this journey of mine—after all, they wanted my religion to be my choice—but I got the sense that they believed this new obsession was akin to a Goth phase (something I experienced just the previous year, actually, including a regrettable attempt to change my name to “Volcanica.”) As a result, I was a bit embarrassed to involve them in all this, especially given that I knew there was a possibility I’d lose interest and never live it down. It would be just like the running family joke about how I was obsessed with horses in second grade because I wanted to emulate a specific girl who liked them. (I recently argued with my mother, at the age of thirty-six, about the fact that in spite of this girl inspiring me to like horses, I actually liked them.)

It’s entirely possible that I became an Episcopalian purely by chance. The Episcopal church was the first church I ever attended, and perhaps today I’d be Catholic if I had happened upon a Catholic church. But I liked it enough not to want to try anything else. They focused more on “loving thy neighbor” than on Hell, but still had a concept of rules, and they were conventional enough that I felt this was a real church and not a hippie drum circle.

I attended Episcopal churches spottily each summer. I went to a summer camp that enabled campers to go off-campus with designated counselors to religious services, and there was an Episcopalian option. I didn’t attend church during the school year because there wasn’t one close to me. In hindsight, I’m sure my parents would have cooperated if I had begged them to take me to church every Sunday, but I can’t say I ever begged. My plan was to continue being nominally Christian until I was an adult and wouldn’t have to suffer the indignity of asking my secular parents to get me baptized. (Again, I’m pretty sure they would have agreed to it, but something about having them drive me to my baptism just felt embarrassing.)

I did not broadcast my faith journey in high school. I went to a very liberal private school where being Christian was basically the most embarrassing thing you could be. It was genuinely much cooler to impersonate an anime character or to wear a wizard cloak to all your classes. One year, we had a field trip to several different houses of worship, and the kids took pleasure in grilling the Catholic priest about gay marriage. Meanwhile, they had absolutely nothing to say when the Muslim imam, unprompted, declared, “You cannot be gay or lesbian or any of that.” My takeaway: keep this stuff secret until I was out of there.

I prayed nightly as a teenager, but my prayers had also taken on the form of an OCD ritual. I didn’t actually know the Lord’s prayer, so a lot of my prayers involved numerical repetitions, “lucky” words and other kinds of rituals that ran the risk of nullifying the prayer if not done correctly. When bad things happened, I often blamed myself for not having prayed correctly or for betraying God in some way. When my boyfriend dumped me in ninth grade, I assumed God was punishing me because I had failed to get him to stop smoking pot (in fact, I had not even tried.) Sometimes, I worried that God would take my prayers too literally in a Monkey’s Paw kind of way. I would pray “Dear God, I wish I could stop worrying about whether Dylan likes me” and then pivot to, “But NOT because I simply started worrying about something else, like catching HIV from a toilet.”

I also found myself regularly breaking the religious rules I had promised to follow and performing a DIY confession every night. For a while, I convinced myself that premarital fingering did not fall under the umbrella of sins, and I suspected I would wait until marriage to have intercourse, or at the very least, until I was engaged. But at fifteen, I was dating an eighteen-year-old who promised to marry me someday, and I, credulous moron I was, immediately lost my virginity to him before he promptly cheated on me. I had so much guilt about this experience that I took a “vow of celibacy” for the next two years, although realistically speaking, this celibacy also did not include fingering.

I also worried that I was sinning by being, on some level, agnostic. To those who were raised with faith and have always been religious, I cannot overstate how difficult it is for people raised secular to truly believe any of this stuff. To this day, I’m not sure how much of the Bible most Christians actually believe, or how much I believe for that matter. How many people really believe that the story of Creation is literal? I assume not all, because plenty of Christians believe in evolution. But then, what else must be taken literally versus figuratively? These are questions I still ask myself, and most of the time my answer is, “I guess it really do be like that sometimes,” because I don’t think I’ll ever know, and I don’t know how anyone can be sure. To this day, I’m a bit jealous of lifelong religious people who seem (at least to me) 100% confident that their religion is objectively correct, that they are going to Heaven, and that everything in the Bible is true despite billions of people around the world being equally confident in their own different faiths. I would have loved that kind of certainty, and if I could have taken a pill and gotten it, I would have. Despite getting into religion in the quest for security and reassurance, it left me with more questions and concerns. Arguably, I held myself to a much more rigid standard than any Episcopalian priest actually would of a teenage girl, but I figured I was better safe than sorry.

In college, I struggled reconciling my faith (shaky already) with the type of life I was leading, which involved quite a bit of parties, Bebe bodycon dresses, and Keystone Lite. I joined the Christian youth group at my college (I have to be honest: a cute Christian boy who played guitar was part of my motivation, although he turned out to be taken and the rest of the group was mostly female) but I found it hard to relate to these people. It wasn’t because they were judgmental church ladies—hardly! Plenty of them still partied and drank, and none of them judged me (at least not openly) for still being a bit unsure of my faith. If anything, the fact that they took their faith so seriously prevented them from excluding me for being weird. What would Jesus do? He would invite the weird girl to His parties and forgive her for being annoying!

But we didn’t quite click the way I had hoped. When matters of faith were discussed, these women just seemed far more confident in theirs than I was in mine, and this wasn’t their fault at all, but it made me feel like an imposter. For example, many of them were able to describe having heard God speaking to them through their dreams, or even experiencing physical sensations and visual manifestations of what they believed to be the Holy Spirit. When they attempted to assure me that Jesus loved me, I wanted to be like, “Okay, but what if none of this is even real?” None of them seemed to have those thoughts at all, or to even be familiar with them, although to their credit, they weren’t remotely judgy about my doubt. If anything, they saw it as a sign from God that they needed to fortify the faith of their sister in Christ. Toward the end of freshman year, they tried very hard to get me to sign up for some kind of adult summer camp where they promised I would “literally meet Jesus.” I was very confused about what this entailed, had suspicions that it might involve a man in a Jesus costume, and asked them if this was actually literal or more of a figurative thing. They insisted again that it was literal but wouldn’t explain how. I passed.

At nineteen, I met my husband Nick, who like me, was also raised half-Jewish and secular (funny enough, his other side—the Greek side—had attended Episcopalian church once they arrived in America, but I wouldn’t know this for a few years). In Nick’s case, there had been a couple of childhood attempts at temple, which had gone very badly, in part because the rabbi had gotten furious at him for refusing to wear a yarmulke when he was six and apparently tried to physically force it onto his head. I believe there was also one trip to a Unitarian church which he thought was lame. Unlike me, Nick had zero interest in religion, so I underplayed my faith so he wouldn’t think I was a square. I remember telling my Christian youth group friends about him, and one of the girls interrupted me and said, “All that matters is that he loves Jesus. Does he love Jesus?” I realized that framing him as Jewish might look better than being honest about the fact that he was just completely bored with the entire topic of religion—agnostic as opposed to atheist purely because he didn’t care to think about it that much—so I told them he was a practicing Jew, which was more palatable. (Of course, I later apologized to God for lying.)

My attitude towards basically everything, as it is for many Episcopalians, is to live and let live, so I did not have any desire to convert Nick, or anyone else. But for some reason, around the time we got married six years later, I felt an overwhelming urge to join a church. Nick, as you can imagine, was confused, since this wasn’t a big part of who I was when we met. I became overwhelmed with a fear that Nick was going to Hell (not a typical Episcopalian belief) and that maybe I would go to Hell too because we had quite a bit of premarital sex, plus some before each other (also not a typical belief for Episcopalians). I hoped that this fear would resolve after we got married, but given that our wedding was secular, I was worried that God somehow hadn’t “seen” it. (It’s giving OCD!) I began looking for churches in San Francisco (where we were living at the time), preferably ones that seemed accessible and non-stuffy enough that Nick might agree to go with me. I had a sliver of hope that Nick might start going to church regularly if he was able to find a church that he considered “fun.” I put on my cool youth pastor hat and searched for the most fun church in town.

Eventually, I found a modern, nondenominational “church” which actually met in some kind of conference auditorium with a film projector. I convinced Nick to come with me because their groovy fliers (which involved copious memes and were plastered all over our apartment door) promised that they would provide spiritual peace to “stressed out young people.” Nick, believing this would be some kind of free meditation session with a few Jesus references, agreed to go.

The auditorium was mostly empty—I’d say about fifteen people were in attendance, but you could tell the sermon was intended for hundreds. A great deal of their parishioners were strung-out homeless people looking for free coffee, which was odd, but I liked that the pastor welcomed them in. Certainly, if someone tweaking on meth was allowed to participate in this service, a relatively inexperienced and unbaptized Christian who was still mostly agnostic would be welcome. But quickly, I got weird vibes. Perhaps it was a simple lack of familiarity. This church was decidedly not Episcopalian. The pastor wore jeans and a button-down with rolled up sleeves. There were no candles. We did not have hymns, only Christian rock. There was a projector which played clips of sitcoms. The entire sermon was about how if we gave more money to the church, God would pay us back in “untold riches.” When money was not the topic, it was the importance of evangelizing and bringing my coworkers into the church—yes, really, at least twenty straight minutes were spent on the fact that it was my duty to serve God by converting all the employees at my tech company, hopefully dodging HR in the process. Moreover, they had their own version of the Bible which was annotated with modern language, including several paragraphs about the dangers of “alternative lifestyles.” This was the first church I had ever been to where I did not cry upon entering.

I will try not to judge, given that I entered that church hoping I wouldn’t be judged, but it was just not for me. Nick, meanwhile, expressed disappointment with how much emphasis was placed on “the Jesus stuff.” I realized that Nick was just not going to be into church and no amount of laundering was going to get him interested, so I set off to join an Episcopal church on my own.

I found an Episcopal church—a classic one, with high stained glass windows, incense, a marble altar and green vestments with gold embroidery. As soon as I arrived, you guessed it, I burst into tears. This was San Francisco, so there were very few young people or families in attendance, but there were a few. The priest was a kindly middle-aged woman with four adult daughters. She had a Martha Stewart haircut, wire framed glasses, and a round-cheeked smile that made it easy to imagine what she might have looked like as a child. As soon as I could meet her, I essentially trauma dumped my entire twenty-year history with religion and expressed interest in finally finding a church where I would belong. She told me that she took baptism very seriously, and that if I wanted to be baptized at her church, I would need to meet with her for religious education, one on one, every week.

And I did. Once a week, I woke up early to meet my priest at the church. She had me read some books and some scripture, which I did, but I also just asked her lots of questions, including all the reassurance-seeking questions over which I had previously been ruminating, such as, “Is it a sin if I get drunk but don’t do anything bad when I’m drinking?” or “Is it an affront to God to dye my hair?" or “How old do I look?” I know this metaphor is a bit on the nose, but this woman had the patience of a saint.

I had to have a godmother and godfather for my baptism, who also functioned as spiritual mentors. Keep in mind that normally, if this scenario had gone the way it typically does, I would have been a baby, and my godparents might have been family friends of my parents. However, because my parents were not involved in this and I was a fully-grown adult who was already using tretinoin, my godparents were the priest’s husband John and their daughter Fiona, who was actually my age.

Fiona and I became incredibly close friends. She had been raised in the Episcopal church most of her life, and although she felt more comfortable in her faith than I did, she seemed very much like a regular person who completely understood my occasional agnosticism. Reassuringly, she did not seem terribly worried about going to Hell or committing sins even though she lived a very typical life of a twenty-something in San Francisco. Perhaps this was because the Episcopal church wasn’t big on the fire and brimstone, or perhaps she was blissfully devoid of OCD. Meanwhile, her parents became family to me as well. I obviously was nowhere near as close with them as I was with my own parents, but I felt comfortable seeking them out for guidance in a way that was very alien to me, given that I was not close with any aunts or uncles who might typically fill this kind of role. My godfather attempted to get Nick to attend the church men’s group, assuring him that they barely talked about Jesus, didn’t care if he was agnostic, and it was mostly “a bunch of guys grilling and talking about this crazy thing called life,” but I fear this description alienated him more than Bible talk would have.

Nick sometimes attended church with me, mostly to be supportive. If he was unable to stay awake in the auditorium church that played clips from The Office, there was no way he was going to tolerate a high Episcopalian mass. One time, I brought him for the Easter service, and after the welcome hymn he stood up, confident that the entire service was over. I had to break it to him that he needed to sit down for another two hours. I believe this was the last time he attended church.

While this church was by far my best religious experience, a couple issues troubled me, and they were not the fault of the church. For one, I struggled to understand exactly how much to tithe. I had read that 10% of my income was necessary, but that felt significant to me given that, at the time, we were barely breaking even after paying rent. Could I have spent 10% of my income on the church? Yes, but—and I know this does not make me look good—I didn’t want to. The guilt I felt about not tithing enough made me want to stop going to church entirely, even though nobody said anything about it. I also had difficulties with the rising number of obligations. Given that Nick didn’t want to participate in the church community—something that I eventually accepted—I didn’t want to spend all my weekends away from him, even if I was fine occasionally volunteering or attending events alone. Again, nobody gave me a hard time about being unable to attend the youth group meetings (and, quite frankly, they were playing it pretty fast and loose with the word “youth”) but I felt guilty having to continually say no. I figured I would contribute and be part of the community in my own way, but I couldn’t help but notice that between my full-time job and wanting to spend time with my husband and secular friends, I was spread pretty thin.

As for the aforementioned secular friends, I kept this part of my life secret from them. My friends (and I didn’t have many, but I had some) were not just secular, but atheists who thought religion was stupid. Not uncommon in San Francisco. My best friend was raised Jewish, hated it, and therefore hated all religion, or at least thought it was nonsensical. For unrelated reasons, our friendship was wavering at the time, and perhaps in an attempt to deal it a final blow, I told her that I had been baptized and had been going to church every Sunday. She reacted just as I thought she would—by assuming I was playing a prank and then getting weirdly quiet for the rest of our hangout when she realized I wasn’t joking. After that day, I discovered that she had gossiped to our entire social circle about it, including people who I barely knew. I had been attempting to befriend a new woman—also Jewish but not “into it” and definitely not a big Episcopal church enthusiast—who asked me if it was true that I had gotten baptized that year, because she suspected this was an outlandish rumor. I considered changing the subject, but decided against it and told her it was true. I’m sure I also nervously hedged by promising that I wasn’t going to try and drag her to church, and I wouldn’t judge what she believed. We are still friends to this day.

Eventually, Nick and I left San Francisco and as a result, I left that particular church. I never found another one. I was afraid that once I started going to another church, I would have to deal with more obligations, more concerns about tithing, and more time spent away from Nick and my hobbies, and I was so afraid of disappointing people that I thought it would be simpler never to go. I know none of that sounds like the thoughts of a devout or even halfway decent Christian, and that’s why I feel reluctant to even use that label. When I was being baptized, my priest had assured me that this could not be undone, and no matter what happened I would always be a Christian. But I’d be lying if I said I always felt like one.

My husband and I went on to have two children. True to my parents’ tradition, I did not baptize them, although when God has come up via other exposures, I’ve been honest with them about what I believe (or at least what I’d like to believe) versus what their father believes (or doesn’t believe.) We celebrate a very secular Hanukkah and Christmas. We watch a lot of Burgermeister. Perhaps I’m making a mistake by leaving their faiths up to them, and I know the most likely scenario will be that they find it all boring. I’m raising my kids the way my parents did, because even though at times I felt frustrated that they hadn’t raised me with a religion, it’s entirely possible that if they had, I’d have found it annoying and tiresome and then never embarked on this journey at all. At times, I feel like an imposter in the world of Christianity since I am still not completely convinced, but does it not mean something that I searched for it myself?

Can I continue or claim my faith if most of the time, I doubt any of it is real? Is it enough, in God’s eyes, to just say and hope it’s real? This is one of the biggest questions that plagues me today. Assuming that God specifically grants eternal life (or even just avoidance of Hell) to people who truly believe, doesn’t that unjustly advantage people like my college youth group friends, who were, by pure chance, raised in religious families? (I’m fully aware I’m not the only one to ask this question, by the way—no need to inform me that this is a “midwit take.”) Moreover, I cannot control what I believe! I can only control what I say and do. What if I can’t really force myself to believe in God, but a part of me really wants to, because I wouldn’t care this much if I truly believed God wasn’t real? Do I get an A for effort, or at the very least, a B minus? Maybe the question isn’t even whether God exists or not, and just whether this was ever a religion for me, or just a twenty-year-long manifestation of my OCD. That might actually be a more disturbing thought than God’s existence or lack thereof.

I have no major regrets about my religious journey. Even if I turned out to be incorrect, or doing it for the wrong reasons, I had interesting experiences, met great people, and thought about things that might not have otherwise crossed my mind. But I do wish I could go back in time and tell my younger self one thing: none of this will provide you with any more certainty than you had before. The questions will only keep coming. And most of them—if not all of them—will never be answered.
