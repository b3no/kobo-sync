Beating Wreckage Into Relics

How steel from the World Trade Center became memorials of 9/11

Ron Charles
Sep 11
 
READ IN APP
 

Outside the city hall in Elko, Nev., steel doves fly above a short I-beam taken from the ruins of the World Trade Center some 2,400 miles away.

A pair of architects who work out of their bar-slash-gallery designed this soaring 9/11 memorial, then turned the plans over to a local welder. It’s just one of more than 1,500 monuments built around the country — and the world — from twisted pieces of the Twin Towers.

How beams and bits of scrap metal from America’s most horrific terrorist attack came to anchor these reverential sites is the subject of a new book by Samuel Holleran, an artist, and Max Holleran, a sociologist.

In Scattered Steel: The Afterlife of the World Trade Center, the Holleran brothers focus on a seemingly logistical aspect of Sept. 11 that yields surprisingly profound implications. What, they ask, happened to the 200,000 tons of metal that collapsed in the flames?

Their book, an act of dogged investigation and thoughtful analysis, raises a host of provocative questions about how we remember the dead, how we create hallowed spaces, and how we sacralize material objects.

But first, there’s the strange matter of disposing of so much matter. Even while Ground Zero was still burning, government officials and civil engineers struggled to find safe places to put the debris. Their first choice, the recently closed Fresh Kills Landfill, quickly proved unsuitable for the steel wreckage. That was taken to portside scrapyards in New Jersey.

Around the country, pieces of the Twin Towers eventually arrived draped in flags and were “met by a police escort, a military color guard, or a local motorcycle club.” Religious figures led assembled crowds in prayer. Indeed, given the sanctified nature of the twisted metal I saw at the former Newseum in Washington, I was shocked to learn that 95 percent of the steel taken from the wreckage of the World Trade Center was soon shipped abroad, mostly to India, China, and Turkey, where it was simply recycled into rebar and even pots and pans.

Why that news should be so unsettling goes to the heart of the Hollerans’ book, which examines the peculiar way that “sacred steel” became our primary medium for honoring the victims of Sept. 11. The pieces chosen and retained for transformation into “public talismans” typically show evidence of extraordinary distress, the bending and twisting that evoke the disaster’s unspeakable physical pressure. In that way, these beams communicate “the trauma of the attacks and, simultaneously, the material’s resilience” — and ours.

From 7,000 tons of structural steel kept by the Port Authority, almost 1,900 pieces were eventually given to institutions “as a form of controlled embodiment of memory,” the Hollerans write, “in effect franchising 9/11 memory.” Hundreds of fire stations, city halls, courthouses, and parks could each possess their own relic, their own connection to the national tragedy, and their own proof of belonging.

The hyperlocal, often ad hoc nature of these community memorials has done something that grand national monuments in New York and Washington can’t. “The act of making something has taken on new resonance,” the Hollerans write, “as people feel removed from the world of things, isolated and banished to the digital sphere.” Touching steel becomes the antidote to looping images.

And yet, strong as the 9/11 I-beams are, they’re still malleable to the needs of different audiences, raising tensions in a pluralistic society. Some view the sacred steel as a symbol of masculine perseverance; others have found or cut cross-shaped pieces to represent Christian salvation, and those objects, when paired with America’s military response, can become welded to Islamophobic rhetoric.

How will our regard for these hundreds of memorials evolve over the decades? Already, people under 30 have few or no memories of Sept. 11. Will these rusting sites — many with the abstract appearance of Serra sculptures — fade into the background, no more recognizable than the bronze soldiers sitting on horses around D.C.?

Or can we use every anniversary of 9/11 to reinvest this steel with our sacred hope for a world without violence, without vengeance?◆

Books Go to the Movies

  • No, you’re not imagining things. Practical Magic is back in theaters with the same stars as the original 1998 movie, but the wizardry is new. Sandra Bullock and Nicole Kidman reprise their roles as the Owens sisters in this adaptation of Alice Hoffman’s 2021 novel The Book of Magic, the fourth and final book in her witchy series. (Hoffman is also working on a musical adaptation of Practical Magic with Grammy winners Norah Jones and Gregg Wattenberg.)

  • The sixth season of Slow Horses starts streaming Sept. 16 on Apple TV, with Gary Oldman and his greasy trench coat back in action — or what passes for action from this exasperated old spy (trailer). This season, based on the sixth and seventh books in Mick Herron’s Slough House series, finds the gang of remaindered spooks contending once again with MI5’s Diana Taverner, played with withering condescension by Kristin Scott Thomas, and a plot to kill off the Slow Horses one by one. A seventh season, based on Herron’s Bad Actors, is already in the works.

  • When you don’t have time to read Wuthering Heights, watch the movie, and when you don’t have time to watch the movie, click the microdrama. LoveHit, a new streaming app, serves up classics and original romances in bingeable clips about two minutes long (trailers). Adaptations coming soon include Pride & Prejudice, Jane Eyre, and The Great Gatsby — all boiled down, so far as I can tell, to sweaty soap operas. Emily Brontë’s immortal story appears alongside My Gigolo Alpha, a 74-episode drama about a woman who marries a werewolf to secure her inheritance. But hey, if it brings the kids back to the classics, whatever.◆

A Critic Gets His Due

The Mystery Writers’ Murder Club cuts a little too close to the bone — mine. In 1,000 pieces, this whodunit jigsaw puzzle depicts a meeting of novelists in the library at Murderly Manor.

A literary critic who panned their novels over the years has been found dead in the study. (What took them so long?)

Every writer here — Agatha Christie, Arthur Conan Doyle, Gillian Flynn, Dashiell Hammett, Patricia Highsmith, and many others — has a good motive for poisoning the critic’s whiskey.

“Did Raymond Chandler decide it was time for the critic to take a big sleep?”

It’s your job to piece together the clues from the puzzle and the accompanying poster. (The solution is revealed in a sealed envelope.)

The Mystery Writers’ Murder Club puzzle was created by Max Dalton (Thames & Hudson, ages 10+, forthcoming Sept. 15, which, of course, is Agatha Christie’s birthday).

Speaking of the Queen of Crime, English professor Nancy West has published a curious biography called Agatha Christie: The Mother of the Cozy Mystery. Rather than a typical narrative of the writer’s life, this compact little book is composed of 100 two-page anecdotes about Christie and her work. Historical notes and brief selections from her novels are sprinkled throughout. Tasty for anyone who wants to sample the writer’s life in fun-sized clues.

Meanwhile, Agatha Christie’s Tommy & Tuppence, starring Josh Dylan and Antonia Thomas, comes to BritBox on Sept. 15. This modern-day adaptation of the crime-solving duo, first introduced in The Secret Adversary (1922), finds the couple still flirting — with each other and danger.

Looking further ahead, as any good detective should, you’ll find Cathy Rentzenbrink’s curious memoir, The Agatha Christie Cure: What Reading the Queen of Crime Taught Me About Life (forthcoming Oct. 6). Anxious about the world and stuck in a crumbling marriage, Rentzenbrink wonders how Christie “learned to crack on no matter what? And is there any way that I could learn to do that too?” Those questions inspire her to read all 66 of Christie’s crime novels in order and share what she learns about persistence, about work, about family. She’s a smart, charming writer who moves gracefully between her own travails and Christie’s. “If lonely, tired and in need of comfort and companionship,” she says, “getting in the bath with Agatha Christie is always an excellent idea.”◆

Picture This

As millions of kids and former kids know, The Dot is a sweetly encouraging picture book about Vashti, a little girl who thought she couldn’t draw.

Her teacher tells her, “Just make a mark and see where it takes you.” Not one to be trifled with, Vashti jabs her marker at the blank page and shouts, “There!”

“Hmmm,” her teacher says. “Now sign it.”

Before you know it, Vashti has discovered her inner Kandinsky.

Since Peter H. Reynolds published that story in 2003, The Dot has made its mark around the world.

International Dot Day, observed Sept. 15-ish, was started by a teacher in Iowa who read Reynolds’s book to his class. It’s since swelled into a celebration of creativity involving more than 34 million people in almost 200 countries.

Near me, for instance, Reynolds will be at the Hirshhorn Museum, “the biggest dot on the National Mall,” on Sept. 19 for book signings, “dot light” tours, and art projects.

For Reynolds appearances near you, check here.

No matter where you are, you or a wee budding artist in your life might enjoy the cleverly designed new edition of The Dot published just in time for this year’s celebration. The dot-shaped book in a square slipcase makes an encouraging gift.

re: Libraries Are Not the Problem

In last week’s newsletter, I criticized a study commissioned by the Association of American Publishers and the Authors Guild about the impact of library e-lending on the book economy.

On Wednesday, I received this response from W. Ralph Eubanks, president of the Authors Guild:

What your article misses is that the forces behind the advocacy for state mandates on library e-lending terms are not librarians or their professional association the American Library Association, but scholars who oppose copyright protections. So far, much of the efforts of these groups has been ruled unconstitutional in the courts. In the case of the Maryland law that sought to regulate ebook pricing, the courts declared it “unconstitutional and unenforceable” on grounds that it violated the Supremacy Clause of the U.S. Constitution. States cannot regulate copyright. So, the anti-copyright advocates drafted legislation that would limit what terms libraries can agree to. This continued push to pass similar laws seems to be motivated by a lack of interest in supporting the work of copyright holders, which is the position of the current administration with respect to use of copyrighted material for AI training.

Publishers and authors are not, as you say, lamenting lost purchases. Yet authors are making less and less on the sale of ebooks, since at one time libraries would have purchased physical books to meet patron demand. What we are advocating for is for authors to maintain their level of earnings on all books, particularly ebooks. We want library patrons to have broad access to our work. But at the same time, authors need to be compensated for that access. We are advocating for a fair system not just for authors, but for libraries as well. As many commentators have pointed out, if these bills succeed, then many small publishers will be forced to stop offering ebooks to libraries. They simply will not be able to afford it.

At the Authors Guild, we have fought to gain the best author compensation from publishers for ebook sales from the moment that electronic books became a standard clause in a book contract. In that same spirit, we are now advocating for authors to be compensated for the sale of ebooks in ways that do not undercut their earnings. Authors are earning less, and library budgets are being cut as well. We are advocating for fair compensation and funding for both.◆

This Week’s Poem

I missed my chance to meet Gloria Steinem in 1989. She was set to speak at a small Christian college in the Midwest where I was teaching English. But at the last minute, Steinem couldn’t make it, and we were left listening to a talk by Phyllis Schlafly. (Sometimes the satire writes itself.)

Kim Dower, the author of six poetry collections, had better luck. She sent me this poem, which she started writing on Sept. 2, the day the feminist activist died.

Gloria Steinem, 1972, Emerson College, Boston

She was who we girls wanted to be
All of us scrappy hippies in flannel
Standing shoulder to shoulder in the gym

I can still smell the thick fragrance of excitement
Watching her up there, her gorgeous hair
Aviator glasses we all wanted to wear, her long

Fingers pointing as she and Bill Baird (who cares)
Held up posters of different types of birth control:
Diaphragm, IUD, words I’d never heard

Me, having swallowed “The Pill” since puberty
I was one of the lucky ones unlike Melissa or Lori
Had to scrape up the money and fly to Puerto Rico

I can still remember the joyful heat of emotion, Gloria
Mod Squad tall, giving us Voice, permission to be heard
Holding our hands with words I’ve cherished forever

Our fragile 19-year-old selves changed in an instant
She was the first and only to ever tell us we were not
Pieces of shit in a bloated male world, or words to that

Effect, we could make a difference, we could speak up
We could be feminists, the new word, the word
I wore like over-sized golden hoop earrings

That long weekend visit home, as I swaggered
Into my mother’s kitchen, Gloria’s voice in my head
Saying The Word at dinner while my mother

Looked away, scared to hear, but wanted
With all her heart to join this new world
Her daughter brought home to her.

Kim Dower’s most recent collection, “What She Wants: Poems on Obsession, Desire, Despair, Euphoria,” will be published in paperback in January (Red Hen Press). She’s currently working on a book called “The Party Cake Building” about growing up in the ’60s on the Upper West Side.◆

The Writing Life

The attacks of Sept. 11 eventually inspired thousands of nonfiction books and novels, but for me, three titles remain inseparable from the memory of that day.

  1. “The Pet Goat,” by Siegfried Engelmann, is the story that President George W. Bush was reading with 2nd graders in Sarasota, Fla., when his chief of staff told him a second plane had struck the second tower. In that moment, the frat boy who’d strolled into the presidency on his dad’s coattails became the commander in chief of an endless war “to rid the world of evil.”

  2. Ahmed Rashid’s Taliban: Militant Islam, Oil and Fundamentalism in Central Asia, published in 2000 by Yale University Press, became, overnight, one of the most sought-after books in the world.

  3. The Corrections, by Jonathan Franzen, had been released just before the terrorist attacks of 9/11. My review was set to run the next day in The Christian Science Monitor, but the page had to be redesigned at the last minute because the illustration we’d commissioned — a family of Playskool characters surrounded by flames — suddenly looked in horribly bad taste. Our newsroom, just across the street from the 52-story Prudential Tower in Boston, sent us nonessential staff members home early.◆
