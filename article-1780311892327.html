How Trump put the Senate into play

Could Democrats do the impossible?

Lakshya Jain
Jun 1
∙
Paid
 
READ IN APP
 

The conventional wisdom is that Democrats are unlikely to flip the Senate this year. Even accounting for Donald Trump’s historic unpopularity, doing so requires Democrats to win at least two states that Trump won by 10 points or more in 2024.

Right now, however, they’re on track to do exactly that.

One component of this story is that Donald Trump’s approval rating has rapidly devolved from “poor” to “abysmal.” In the process, he has bled support from every imaginable element of the coalition that powered him to victory in 2024 and from every possible demographic.

On a state-by-state level, the data paints a catastrophic picture for the president. Using our polling data, we can assemble estimates of Trump’s approval on a state-by-state basis among likely 2026 voters (in other words, the ones who will actually vote in the midterms). With these voters, he sits above water in just 15 states, despite winning 31 of them against Kamala Harris just over a year ago.

There is little precedent for a party having a great midterm when they’re facing such strong headwinds,¹ and this cycle is no different. Democrats have made significant gains on the generic ballot as a result of Trump’s immense unpopularity, with our surveys currently pointing to a wave that would actually exceed 2018 in magnitude.

Given the sheer volume of voter discontent with Trump and the corresponding penalty being paid by Republicans, it isn’t exactly surprising that the Senate is in play. Winning Senate seats in Ohio, Alaska, Texas, and Iowa might be tough lifts for Democrats in a presidential cycle. But it becomes much more likely in a midterm like this, especially when Trump is underwater in every one of those states.

A lot has to continue going right for Democrats in order for them to win the Senate. They have to hold every seat they currently control, hope that Graham Platner can hang on to his early polling lead in Maine, and then win three of Ohio, Texas, North Carolina, Alaska, and Iowa.

But this is a viable path to winning a majority. And while I wouldn’t say Democrats are favored to win the Senate, I wouldn’t exactly call them the underdogs either. The environment is blue enough that Democrats only need to post modest overperformances in order to win the five “reach” seats mentioned above, and when you consider the types of candidates they have recruited, you can argue that they’re in position to do exactly that.

In North Carolina, the party has recruited recent two-term Gov. Roy Cooper. In Ohio, former Sen. Sherrod Brown is running again, after a narrow 2024 loss. In Alaska, the party has recruited former congresswoman (and titanic overperformer) Mary Peltola. And in Texas, Republicans ousted incumbent John Cornyn in favor of the scandal-plagued Attorney General Ken Paxton, while Democrats escaped disaster by nominating James Talarico over Jasmine Crockett.

These are the types of circumstances that should induce a sense of panic among Republicans. But it doesn’t appear as if the party has any intention of changing tack, because it can’t. While a sane, rational political organization would probably allow its swing-state candidates to distance themselves from the president, this simply isn’t an option for Republicans due to Trump’s hold on the party.

In 2024, when Donald Trump was winning these states handily, being tied to him wasn’t as big of a problem for Republicans. Back then, suboptimal candidates like Bernie Moreno and Ted Cruz could win simply by letting partisanship do the work for them.

Today, however, Ohio and Texas would be nearly tied if an election were held today, while Alaska and Iowa would also be within striking distance with a bit of luck — and that’s before factoring candidate quality into the picture.

I wouldn’t say it’s the median outcome (in fact, I would personally bet against this if I bet on politics!), but there’s a real universe in which Trump hands Democrats 53 Senate seats through flips in North Carolina, Maine, Ohio, Alaska, Texas, and Iowa. The first two already lean Democratic, the next two are both dead-heats given the candidate matchups at play, and the last two are excellent candidates to flip blue with just a slight improvement in the generic ballot.

This wave looks familiar

The 2026 Democratic wave resembles a somewhat-optimized blend of its recent coalitions. Democrats have essentially eliminated all of Trump’s gains with non-college whites, kept their own gains with college-educated whites, and made significant strides in recapturing many of the nonwhite voters they lost over the last decade.²

There are some lasting coalitional changes visible here. Democratic margins are considerably better than the 2024 levels that Kamala Harris collapsed to, but they’re still below Obama’s 2012 levels. But Democrats have also gained considerably with white voters, and because 70% of the electorate is white, this is a type of trade that is electorally advantageous to make. Rebounding enough with nonwhite voters and keeping the gains with white voters lets Democrats construct a coalition that can stay competitive in majority-minority Texas and overwhelmingly white Iowa at the same time.

For years, Democratic strategists have lamented the shrinking of the Senate map. Now, however, they have been gifted a wave by a Republican president who appears bent on doing everything he can to expand it for them.

Part of this is certainly down to turnout. Though Trump won 2024 by roughly two percentage points, our likely voter screen indicates that the 2026 voters who cast ballots in 2024 were a Harris +2 group, indicating that Democrats are enjoying a four-point turnout advantage.

But on top of that, they are also getting a six-point persuasion advantage. That is very much in line with what we would expect historically — a wave begins with Democrats asymmetrically turning out in record numbers, and it truly takes off through major persuasion shifts.

That’s how the Senate becomes a toss-up.

Should we trust these results?

I think there’s a real and substantive reason that many engaged liberals and pundits alike haven’t calibrated to the new reality yet — and it goes beyond them being white, college-educated voters and partisans (the two groups least likely to swing at all).

The real reason is that polling has actually consistently overshot Democrats’ true support to varying extents in 2016, 2020, and 2024. So when you tell them that a blue wave is coming, they understandably view it with extreme skepticism.

But there are a few reasons this time is different. First, 2026 is a midterm — and Trump-era midterm polling has been considerably more accurate than presidential cycle polling because the Trump voters that don’t respond to polls also don’t show up in midterms. That’s a big reason that surveys just do better in midterm years: It’s easier to correctly model and represent the electorate.

Second, we take extreme methodological care in our surveys to properly calibrate and reach out to Trump voters. Our raw samples with Verasight actually get more Trump voters than Harris voters responding to our polls, and our design effect (how much we have to weight each response by in order to calibrate our raw sample) consistently sits at 1.20 or lower, which is considerably lower than that of many public pollsters.³ That gives us reason to believe that our polls are doing a much better job of reaching the electorate at large.

Methodology

To construct our state-level estimates for the generic ballot and approval numbers among 2026 likely voters, we used our polling estimates and applied them on a demographic level with post-stratification. By comparing the panel-profiled 2024 presidential vote per demographic to the margin with 2026 likely voters of that same subgroup, we were able to calculate the swing at a demographic level. Demographic swings were calculated from our polling data for the national, regional, and state level, where available.

For each state, the three swings were blended together, weighting each component for sample size (and discarding subgroup sample sizes of less than 100 entirely) to ensure the stability of estimates. These demographic-imputed swings were then blended with the full state-level polling estimates, weighted for sample size. Finally, a national post-stratification was applied to the topline national approval and generic ballot numbers to recalibrate back to the full survey dataset figures across 2026.
