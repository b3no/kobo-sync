Young people are rich and miserable

Is Gen Z's Doordash habit to blame for its economic woes?

Jeremiah Johnson
May 22

On Monday, a clip of Canadian businessman Kevin O’Leary went viral after the Shark Tank star railed against Gen Z for buying $28 lunches rather than saving and investing.

Many commentators took the opportunity to engage in boomer-style “avocado toast” politics, bashing the younger generations and blaming all their woes on their lack of responsibility. “This is why you’ll never own a home” was a common refrain.

Others defended Gen Z, sometimes bizarrely: Leftist commentator Sean Padraig McCarthy insisted, with no evidence, that “lunch just costs $28 now,” while social media gadfly Taylor Lorenz claimed that younger people lack the time and capacity to cook for themselves.

The easy thing to do would be to point out how silly some of these defenses are, laugh, and move on. No, lunch does not cost $28. Please be serious. Yes, young people have the ability to cook for themselves, just like every other generation.

But if we look beyond the cheap dunks, the statistics behind the debate are more revealing than the noise emanating from social media blowhards. Gen Z does eat out (broadly defined as any food not prepared at home) more than any other generation — sources as varied as Canadian credit union reports, Bank of America internal data, and the Bureau of Labor Statistics all agree that zoomers spend a higher percentage of their food budget at restaurants. Notably, groceries, despite inflation, are more affordable than ever when compared to wages.¹

Given that Gen Z is the youngest working generation and thus has the lowest current income level and food budget, they’re spending the highest percentage on restaurants. Are the “avocado toast” folks correct? Are zoomers throwing away their chance to own a home and retire because they’re eating out too often?

The real picture is more complicated:

First, America is very rich. In 1933, families spent 25% of their disposable income on food, almost all of it staple groceries eaten at home. Today, food spending (as a share of disposable income) is near an all-time low. Less than 5% of disposable income is spent on food eaten at home, and another 5.5% is spent on food away from home. This decline holds even if you restrict the data to younger households.

Food spending as a share of disposable income is near an all-time low
U.S. food spending as a share of U.S. family disposable income, 1929 to 2024

The idea that food spending has anything to do with housing affordability is pretty ridiculous. It’s not that people can’t afford what their parents could; it’s that homes themselves have gotten much more expensive over time. And that change has nothing to do with the 5% of our budget that goes toward restaurants — housing costs are rapidly increasing because of tight zoning regulations and NIMBYism, not the tikka masala you had for lunch.

Polling indicates that Gen Z are the most pessimistic about their economic future.

So perhaps the zoomers are right? Are they worse off than previous generations, stuck in a system designed to help baby boomers at the expense of the young?

Gen Z is the richest generation so far, but the vibes sure are bad

Gen Z is making more money at its age than any generation before them. And despite inflation and counter to virtually everyone’s expectations, groceries are more affordable than ever before relative to average wages.

There are certainly things in the economy that aren’t perfect. The entry-level job market shows signs of weakness, housing prices have risen dramatically, and fast economic recovery post-COVID came alongside the highest inflation in generations. Moreover, AI threatens to rock the labor market in unforeseeable ways.

But uncertainty is nothing new, and if Gen Z is as rich as I’m saying, well, why are the vibes so bad?

These perceptions of doom and decline come from our media environment rather than from the economic reality on the ground. It’s no secret that the media often fixates on the negative. “If it bleeds, it leads” has been a mantra of local televised news for decades, and the phrase supposedly dates back to the yellow journalism era of William Randolph Hearst.

But if the mass media of a previous era was opium, today’s social media environment is fentanyl, a thousand times more potent and much more dangerous for the unwary. Even the yellowest of journalists from yesteryear can’t approach how shameless modern political influencers are. And the algorithmic nature of our platforms makes it a near certainty that messages prophesying doom will spread further than messages saying things are normal.

Negativity and fearmongering are emotionally resonant in ways that nuanced information simply can’t be.

This doesn’t just apply to the economy:

  • Crime has declined dramatically since the 1990s, but in a recurring Gallup poll asking whether crime has gone up or down in the U.S. over the last year, a majority of respondents believed that crime had gone up in 29 of the last 32 years.²

  • More than half of young people think humanity is doomed by climate change, a perspective not shared by climate scientists.

  • Consumer sentiment is currently at its lowest point in the history of the metric, lower than during ‘70s stagflation, the Great Recession, or the COVID pandemic.

  • Views on race relations and gender equality have both markedly declined in the social media era.

In short, everyone believes everything is broken in every way. This phenomenon is worse the closer you are to politics, especially extremist politics.

Leftists bemoan the state of the world, claiming that racism, capitalism, and climate change have made society unlivable (and so we need a socialist revolution). Right-wingers claim that immigration, crime, and cultural Marxism have made society unlivable (and so we need a fascist strongman). Both groups gain from exaggerating the negative, as the public is likely to turn to extreme ideologies when the world seems to be on fire.

Given that social media has now overtaken television as Americans’ main source of news,³ we shouldn’t be surprised that people are now more likely than ever to believe in narratives that preach doom and despair.

And, to be fair to doom-obsessed zoomers, the last 10 years have given people some legitimate reasons to worry. Gen Z is coming of age in a uniquely turbulent stretch — a pandemic that scrambled key educational and early career years, a generational inflation shock, and now a technological disruption that threatens to remake (or destroy) the entire labor market.

But while uncertainty might be at an all-time high, the current economic reality — when compared to previous generations — is pretty good for Gen Z. The mismatch feels driven by social media.

It’s unclear to me how the cycle of doom can be interrupted — or if it can at all. Society is going to keep shifting away from traditional news sources and toward social media. Algorithms will continue to promote the most extreme and negative viewpoints. The incentives that make this happen aren’t changing. The share of people getting their news from an Instagram or TikTok influencer telling them the world is on fire is currently the lowest it will ever be. Perhaps it’s just a structural part of how our new media works.

But even if we can’t stop it, we should at least be able to notice what’s happening and understand why. We live in an age of doom; even when things are good, they’re the worst they’ve ever been.
