Everyone Wants To Be The Child of Omelas

Men and women describe it differently, but both want to be seen as the unappreciated martyr.

Not long ago, I wrote about a prank my college professor played on our psychology class. He made everyone take a personality test, gave everyone identical results, and then asked how many of us found the results to be accurate. Almost everyone in the class, across gender and other demographic lines, agreed with the results of the test, which mostly said things like “You do so much for other people and nobody appreciates it” and “You frequently sacrifice your own comfort to make sure other people are happy.” Apparently, people (myself included!) were very eager to see themselves as a sacrificial victim.

This is the kind of behavior that often gets pulled into the genderslop ecosystem, where depending on the particular type of labor being discussed, people can dismiss men or women of being obsessed with their victimhood status and having an outsized idea of how much they suffer compared to everyone else. And yes, men and women describe the role of Supreme Victim very differently, in part because men and women often feel unappreciated for different things. But ultimately, the drive is the same: I do everything, nobody else appreciates it, I don’t enjoy any of it, and I don’t even enjoy the outcome. It’s all for everyone else, and I don’t gain anything.

But surely, the entire world cannot be made up of people never reaping the rewards of their own labor and selflessly sacrificing for others while nobody does anything for them, because someone has to be the “others.” And this week, I was lucky enough to witness two completely unrelated, and somehow entirely harmonious pieces of Discourse which show how both men and women fashion themselves the Omelas Child of their own lives—using different words, but with the same underlying and self-serving motivation.

One of these dramas was, admittedly, my own fault. I wrote about how women often choose to have jobs instead of being SAHMs for similar reasons that men do. Namely, they want to make money, and often their spouses also want them to make money because they need to pay the rent and buy food and, if they’re lucky, have savings and a safety net. I know, controversial. Radical, even. In fact, deeply misandrist. You got me! In fact, I wrote this article while using a hardworking male software engineer as a human footstool, and not even in a sexy way.

Why would men be offended by the concept of women wanting to use money to purchase goods and services? Well, it’s complicated.

Some people, both men and women, are lucky to work jobs they love, many more don’t really like the day-to-day of their jobs but like the paycheck and financial freedom that goes with it, and thus, they “like” working because they prefer working to being unemployed. When I ran a survey on this exact thing, men and women expressed pretty similar levels of passion about their work, with men slightly more likely to rate their passion at the extremes, and women a bit more neutral. Even so, small differences aside, most people hovered in the 3-4 range.

If I had to guess, I’d venture that more women than men desire to spend all day at home with young children, but a lot of the women who enjoy this (ie: myself) also enjoy having something else in their lives—an organization, a hobby, whatever—for adult interaction and fulfillment. And, if we’re lucky, a flexible way of making a little money. And many other men and women do not really want to be a stay-at-home parent at all.

Anyway the men of Twitter did not take kindly to this, but not in the way I suspected. My theory was that they’d be mad at me for implying women can enjoy work, because “women aren’t built for it.” But no! They were offended at the concept that men can enjoy work. Apparently, the true natural workaholics—the people who really love to work, who adore their companies, who “make their job their personality”—are women. I was informed that actually, men hate working, derive zero pleasure or personal fulfillment from career accomplishments, but are nevertheless doomed to work because women are forcing them to do it. Men’s jobs are, apparently, nothing more than toil and duty, imposed on them by a requirement that they provide for women, and they gain nothing from their own salary other than keeping their imperious wives happy. If a man is single, he’s only working so a woman will date him, and if a man is married, he’s only working so his wife won’t divorce him. If a man is gay and enjoys his job, well…he’s “basically a woman” so he doesn’t count. Checkmate, libs.

I must say, the way these particular men describe women’s work (enthusiasm, passion, love of the game) versus men’s work (an endless slog of misery, fantasizing about fishing all day) they’d be making a great case for discrimination against men in the workplace! I mean, would you rather your doctor be someone who loathed every minute of their workday, or someone who was joyfully obsessed with being a doctor?

(Of course, I have chosen male doctors, because I don’t actually think this is true. In fact, believe it or not, a male doctor can be just as competent as a female doctor. Men can do anything!)

But, in parallel, another piece of nearly identical Discourse was unraveling: the thankless toil and societally-imposed duties of being a woman. Phrased differently, but actually…not that differently. Because a lot of people, male and female, really love to see themselves as the hardworking yet unappreciated, terminally unhappy victims of society, who only ever do things for the pleasure of others, without the favor ever being returned.

I was too exhausted from the “women are evil workaholics” drama to engage with this next one, but a woman posted that she was radicalized against marriage/the patriarchy/motherhood/whatever, by seeing the women of her family slave away over holiday decorations and festivities while the men sat around on their asses and watched sports.

This felt, oddly, very similar to the whole “men hate working but do it constantly because everyone makes them” thing. There are a lot of women who revel in feeling unappreciated and overworked regarding things that they are mostly choosing to do, either because they secretly enjoy doing these things (but can’t admit to it because it would ruin the illusion of being martyrs) or because they are imposing a standard on themselves which nobody else is imposing. Take, for example, the women who complain that Santa is stealing all the “credit” for their Christmas magic:

(I know this is not a precise 1:1, in that these women would stop decorating for Christmas and nothing would happen, but men who hate their jobs can’t simply stop working without spiraling into poverty. My point is that I don’t believe these people when they insist they derive no value or reward from the outcome that makes the labor worth it.)

Now, I can’t know everyone’s personal circumstances, but most of the time, holiday expectations are driven by the mother of the household. There are very few men sitting around, complaining that their wives haven’t purchased color-coordinating gift wrap, and most children would be perfectly happy to eat KFC for Christmas dinner. And yet, a lot of moms spend way more time on holiday preparations than anyone is asking. The reason for this is probably that on some level, the mom enjoys it and wants these nice things herself. Even if she doesn’t enjoy the labor, she enjoys having a nice Christmas with good food and pretty decorations, possibly more than anyone else in her family enjoys it. I admitted this was true of me—I do work myself pretty hard over the holidays, I acknowledge my husband and kids wouldn’t really notice the difference with less effort, but ultimately I really want a great Christmas, and I want to share that with my family. Knowing this, I have lost my right to complain and play the victim, but I do ask that people notice what I do and say thank you. At one point, I had to sit my husband down and explain this to him because he kept wondering why I “did so much” when nobody was asking it of me. Sometimes, it helps to say, “Because I like it, even if getting to the end result is a pain in the ass.”

This revelation was inspired, at least partially, by how my own mother handled the holidays. Each year, she went all out and expended even more effort than I currently do. Christmas dinners were fancy, multi-course, often inspired by Martha Stewart Living or some French cookbook hiding in the kitchen, and these dinners took her multiple days to arrange. Christmas itself was Ralph Lauren catalog worthy and it was spectacular. But aside from the presents and cookies, my brother and I weren’t asking her to do this. Nobody was holding her to this standard, which somehow included over fifty (yes, really) authentic German nutcrackers placed meticulously around the house. And invariably, every year she would lose it and start yelling at us for being ungrateful, threatening to cancel Christmas the following year. In a way, this became our annual Christmas tradition.

As I got older, I realized we should have offered to help more, and we should have been more appreciative, because we did still benefit from everything she did, but she probably would have done everyone a favor to just say, “You know what? I’m doing a lot of this because I want to do it, I just hope that you appreciate it too.” Instead, she often pretended she didn’t enjoy it, and someone, somewhere, was forcing her to do it. She obviously enjoyed it, or at the very least, even if she didn’t enjoy the work itself, she enjoyed the outcome. This distinction is important, but was not spoken out loud, and was definitely lost on children.

The reality is that yes, there are men whose wives don’t appreciate how much they work, and yes, there are wives whose husbands don’t appreciate what they do around the house, but a whole lot of people seem to be particularly seduced by these narratives, and are especially invested in the idea that actually, they are miserable with all of their life choices, because you see, they never had a choice at all! I would imagine, moreover, that many of these people are married to each other, both resentful that “nobody sees how much I do, but alas, I toil for everyone else.” And the most important part of this belief system seems to be a denial of one’s own pleasures—an insistence that you are actually suffering through all the things you’re doing, because you didn’t want to do any of it, and everyone is making you do it, and there’s no way out.

Despite the fact that these particular complaints are usually unrelated to sex, they feel very similar to the “permission to be horny” phenomenon I covered a while ago. This was probably my favorite-ever essay about sex, because as I was writing it, I realized something fundamental about my sexuality which was apparently relatable to many other straight women: I don’t want to do anything if it feels like I had to ask for it, or if it was my idea. If sex feels like it’s “for me,” I simply don’t enjoy it. My husband agreeing to have sex with me when I’m horny but he’s kinda meh (albeit consenting) is so unappealing that I’d rather just not have sex at all. This is the same mechanism behind women getting annoyed when their husbands take them out for a sweet treat but then decline to get something themselves. It’s not that straight women are allergic to their own pleasure, but we often prefer for our pleasure to be an incidental part of the man’s pleasure, so we don’t have to feel like he’s doing us a favor. The idea of us enjoying something more than the man is, at least for many of us, quite the turnoff. (I’m aware not all straight women are like this, but enough are that the article resonated.)

Of course, men and women both want a consenting, enthusiastic partner, but men seem less bothered by the idea that someone is not really in the mood, but still consenting. When posed with a hypothetical about a sexual partner who would consent 100% of the time, but you would have to be the one to initiate every time, men were far more interested in being the “asker” than women, who were more interested in being the one consenting all the time.

But I think the “I don’t want to be done a favor” phenomenon, or the “permission to be horny” phenomenon, is not so gender-specific when you zoom out beyond the sex world. Most stark gender differences between straight men and straight women are in relation to sex and attraction, and the farther out you get from that space, the more similar the genders are. And I think both genders (or at least large segments of both genders) really hate admitting that they enjoy something, because if they’re enjoying something—nay, not totally miserable doing something—they can’t play the part of the toiling victim. Moreover, it helps if they’re uniquely miserable, and everyone else around them is having a much easier time comparatively.

My husband and I even found ourselves doing this when we tried playing Fair Play, the card game that enables couples to organize and delegate household tasks. Because this game literally involves a numerical score of how many tasks each person is doing, it’s tempting to want to “win” by proving that you do more tasks than the other person, and it’s harder to guilt trip the other person about your tasks if they’re all things you enjoy doing. As we played—even though we were mostly playing for fun and to write about it—we found ourselves claiming that certain tasks we enjoyed doing were actually really cumbersome and thankless, to emphasize how much we were contributing. For example, my husband made a big deal over how much work it is to mow the lawn and how much he hates it, even though I know he loves mowing the lawn because he gets to be alone and listen to a podcast undisturbed. (I made this point, he scratched his chin and said, “You…you’re good.”) Similarly, I started complaining about how I organize all the kids’ toys and cleaning the play room, even though tidying toys is weirdly calming and enjoyable for me. It felt like admitting to enjoying any part of the labor made it no longer labor, and therefore, we would lose “credit” for it. Moreover, I enjoy having a clean play room more, and he enjoys having a mowed lawn more. But we couldn’t dare admit such things!

I also notice this phenomenon every time women discuss the “girl tax,” or the monetary cost and backbreaking labor of looking hot as a woman, presumably imposed on us by the patriarchy. Now, if you want to complain about diet culture and unfair beauty standards, complain away, but quite a few of these “tax” items include things men are not actually demanding. Getting a manicure is not for men! If manicures are too expensive and too time-consuming, you can actually stop doing them, and nary a man will care. Women who get manicures generally do so because they enjoy getting manicures, but admitting they’re doing it for their own enjoyment ruins the whole “I do so much for everyone else” narrative. Same thing with keeping up with fashion trends and spending a lot of money on clothes. To be successful in the workplace and/or to find a boyfriend, you actually don’t need many pieces of clothing, and the clothing you need can be cheap and still do the job. If you’re spending tens of thousands of dollars on clothes and shoes every year, it’s fine, but it’s because you enjoy fashion.

Is this an American problem, perhaps stemming from our Protestant work ethic? Do we feel we are less valuable if we enjoy the labor we perform, or at least enjoy the fruits of that labor? Or do our martyr complexes come from something more universal—the fact that we want to be appreciated for what we do, and work always seems more significant when you’re the person doing it? I am still not sure, but it seems to be we should all be honest with ourselves about how much we really are sacrificing for others—and how much others may be sacrificing for us.
