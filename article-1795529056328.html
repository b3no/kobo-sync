Josh Dehaas: The Safe Social Media Act is a threat to free speech

Here’s what we should do instead

Line Editor
Nov 24
 
READ IN APP
 

By: Josh Dehaas

For more than five years, the federal Liberals have pushed to pass legislation to create a new regulator in Ottawa to censor online speech. Now, they may finally get their way. Not only do the Liberals have a majority, they’ve also found an argument to get the public broadly onboard: pass Bill C-34, the Safe Social Media Act, to keep kids safe online. The bill would require social media platforms to either ban kids under age 16 from having accounts or offer options like youth accounts to keep kids safe from harmful content and addictive features. Let kids be kids, they say. Who could oppose that?

In reality, the protections for kids are a small part of the bill, and probably redundant since social media companies are already responding to lawsuits about their failures to mitigate harms like algorithms that harm kids’ mental health. The opposition should either vote against Bill C-34 in its entirety or insist that it be pared back to just those sections aimed at truly protecting kids. They must not support the creation of a new ministry of truth in Ottawa to police adult speech.

In addition to its measures to protect kids, Bill C-34 would create a legal duty on social media and artificial intelligence companies to mitigate adults’ exposure to “harmful” content, enforced by a new Digital Safety Commissioner in Ottawa. Most of the harmful content that this new bureaucracy would oversee is already illegal, raising questions about why a new federal censorship bureau is required. For example, Bill C-16 already made it criminal to share intimate images without consent, including AI-generated deepfakes.

I suspect what C-34 is really about is censoring speech that some people find offensive. Since hate speech is already criminalized, the goal appears to be to browbeat social media companies into deleting more lawful but controversial speech, defined in the bill as “content that foments hatred.” This duty comes with a convoluted definition that I, despite being a free-speech lawyer, would have difficulty applying in most situations.

AI systems will struggle even more. There is no practical way for machines to figure out which words are hateful when humans can’t even agree. Consider the European Union’s experience with the Digital Services Act, which passed in 2022, and which C-34 is modelled upon. After it was implemented in 2023, researchers for The Future of Free Speech at Vanderbilt University employed lawyers to examine the comments that disappeared from 60 Facebook pages and YouTube channels belonging to politicians and news organizations in France, Germany and Sweden. In Germany 99.7% of the expressions that disappeared from Facebook and 98.9% of the expressions that disappeared from YouTube were legal. The figures were almost as high in France and Sweden. When companies face enormous fines for not filtering out enough harmful speech, their incentive is to filter out anything close to the line. The result will be large amounts of controversial but constitutionally-protected speech disappearing from Facebook feeds and ChatGPT responses. We won’t even know what’s filtered out.

Worse still, this duty will be overseen by a Digital Safety Commissioner who will review mandatory Digital Safety Plans, take reports from the public about alleged online harms, and who can create new regulations to enforce the statutory duties. Again, we can look to experience overseas to see where this leads. Since the passage of the Online Safety Act in Australia in 2021, most major social media companies simply rolled over and censored more posts. The exception was Elon Musk’s X, which has fought back, presumably at enormous financial expense. This included when the eSafety Commissioner Julia Inman Grant ordered X to take down an arguably offensive but clearly legal post criticizing a “Queer Club” for eight to 12-year-old children at a Victorian elementary school. The post stated: “Children should not be learning about sexualities at such a young, impressionable age. This is foul. Leave the kids alone.” Inman Grant’s decision was overturned by a court, but not until after the censorship took place.

So what are the Conservatives to do? They risk looking like they don’t care about kids’ mental health if they vote against C-34. They could still oppose it entirely. Alternatively, they could introduce amendments that would preserve only a simple statutory duty for social media companies to protect children under the age of 18 from Internet-based harms, whether that content is pornography or addictive social media. Companies could then assess their liability and make calls about what they need to do to keep kids safe. This would likely include pushing kids under a certain age onto kids’ accounts.

There is precedent for this. Earlier this year, a New Mexico court ordered Meta to pay US$942 million to young people hurt by its platforms. These kinds of awards should provide enough incentive for social media companies to keep kids safe going forward. A federally-imposed duty would make this even clearer. But we don’t need a new adult speech police in Ottawa to keep kids safe online.

Josh Dehaas is Interim Litigation Director with the Canadian Constitution Foundation and co-author of Free Speech in Canada: A beginner’s guide from ancient roots to current controversies.
