Chat, what’s the public’s favorite AI lab?

OpenAI is winning the war for public opinion.

Kobe Yank-Jacobs
Aug 3
∙
Paid
 
READ IN APP
 

Today’s leading AI labs claim to be determining the course of humanity’s future. Those claims can seem grandiose to the average chatbot user, who may view programs like ChatGPT as advanced forms of Google search. But recent dust-ups with the U.S. government show that the stakes are already getting higher.

In February, Anthropic and the Defense Department feuded over whether the military should have access to an unfettered version of Claude that could conduct mass surveillance of Americans and operate weapons autonomously¹. The immediate standoff ended when a competitor, OpenAI, swooped in to offer the department access to ChatGPT on more favorable terms.

This episode encapsulates the reputations that the two companies have developed among those in the know: Anthropic is considered the safety-conscious lab, with a thoughtful CEO who pens essays about the promise and peril of the technology. OpenAI is the unscrupulous competitor whose CEO was once ousted by the company’s board for “not being consistently candid in his communications.”

This perception, however dominant in elite discourse, appears not to have filtered through to the general public at all.

Six months ago, very few people had even heard of Anthropic, although it has essentially cast itself as humanity’s protector. A December 2025 poll by the Searchlight Institute found that 81% of respondents had no opinion on Anthropic. Another analysis around that time by Democratic data scientist David Shor suggested that the true proportion of people who hadn’t heard of Anthropic could be as high as 95%.²

While that number seems to have fallen dramatically over the past few months — now that Anthropic has overtaken OpenAI in terms of annualized revenue, run funny Super Bowl ads, and clashed with the Trump administration — a lot of people still don’t know what Anthropic is. The Argument’s poll of 3,008 registered voters fielded between May 29 and June 3, 2026, found that 42% of respondents had not heard of Anthropic and 38% did not know xAI.

OpenAI led the pack in name recognition, with just 13% of respondents saying that they had not heard of the company — a whopping 29 points better than Anthropic. All told, this means that Anthropic gained somewhere between 39 and 53 percentage points in name recognition over the past six months.

But increased public awareness of Anthropic hasn’t necessarily translated into favorability.³

When we asked respondents whether they had a favorable or unfavorable view of each AI company, OpenAI led favorability by a healthy margin. Among people who knew of each company, OpenAI had a net favorability rating of +11 points, while Anthropic had a net favorability rating of only +3.

Whether or not you think Anthropic’s self-styled heroism is genuine, you might expect it to have some discernible effect on public opinion. After all, while few people have read CEO Dario Amodei’s musings, millions watched Anthropic take a shot at OpenAI in Superbowl ads or heard about its choice to stand up to the Trump administration.

You could even imagine a world in which the company’s earnest approach has the opposite of its intended effect: The clash with the Trump administration, say, could politically polarize views of the company, leaving MAGA supporters with a bad taste in their mouths.

But there seems to be no good evidence that any of this has happened yet.

In fact, it seems that public approval of individual AI labs isn’t driven by much of anything they say or do, outside of delivering a useful consumer product.

OpenAI isn’t Kleenex, it’s Amazon

My starting hypothesis was that OpenAI’s lead in net favorability was due to its first-mover advantage. Maybe the public had come to associate AI with OpenAI above all other companies, the way Coke stands in for soda or Kleenex for tissues.

This led me to suspect that frequent AI users, being more aware of the various companies’ brands, would look different from those who barely used AI at all.

That turned out to be true: among people who use AI more than once a week outside of their job, Anthropic’s approval shot up to +26 net favorability (35% approve, 9% disapprove). It’s just that OpenAI’s approval shot up by much more: Its favorability spiked to +48 among frequent AI users (62% approve, 14% disapprove).

In other words, my hypothesis that regular AI users would favor Anthropic, mirroring elite consensus, turned out to be wrong. Instead, the bigger favorability bump for ChatGPT among frequent AI users probably just reflects the fact that roughly four times as many people use ChatGPT as use Claude.⁴ Overall, simply using a chatbot probably makes you like that chatbot and its company, regardless of what’s been in the news cycle.

The poll has even more evidence suggesting that the public is not crediting Anthropic for the AI safety efforts that have won it plaudits in elite circles. Respondents to our poll who thought that AI-fueled catastrophes were likely did not show greater support for Anthropic.

Overall, 27% of respondents in our poll thought that AI was likely to lead to human extinction, and 47% thought it was likely that humans would lose control of AI. These numbers were a lot higher than I expected, and I think we should take them with a grain of salt because people might not take science-fiction scenarios seriously when asked in a poll. Still, even when you look only at the people who expressed concern about catastrophic harms, Open AI still beats Anthropic: Anthropic breaks even on favorability while OpenAI holds a net favorability of 8 points, roughly in line with its overall favorability.

Even Anthropic’s conflict with the Trump administration does not appear to impact its net approval.⁵

During its clash with the Pentagon, Donald Trump called Anthropic a “radical left woke company” and Pete Hegseth criticized it for being “cloaked in the sanctimonious rhetoric of ‘effective altruism.’” But our poll suggests that Trump supporters have not adopted this critique in the slightest.

Anthropic’s net favorability was actually +6 among 2024 Trump voters while it broke even among Harris voters. If anything, the left’s pessimism about AI probably had a stronger effect than specific partisan conflicts: Each AI lab had positive net favorability among Trump voters and broke even among Harris voters.

This effect — in which consumer usage drives popularity more than news cycles and elite discourse — is not limited to public opinion of AI labs. As my colleague Milan Singh wrote, despite the left populists frequently railing against Amazon, The Argument’s May survey shows that it is the most popular tech company in America (including Google, Microsoft, Apple, and the AI labs).

Being useful makes a company popular: If you click the button for Prime delivery, you will have a brown box on your doorstep two days later; if you ask a chatbot how to protect your sneakers, it’ll recommend scotch guard.

Why does popularity matter for AI companies?

Whatever you believe about AI risk, it’s clear that public opinion about the major AI labs probably matters more for the future of the country than, say, public opinion about Facebook, which at various times has been called a threat to American democracy by both the right and the left.

That’s because AI companies could threaten the functioning of American democracy not merely through the threat of disinformation (as with social media platforms), the control of weapons (as with defense contractors), or the disruption of critical infrastructure — they could destabilize all three simultaneously.

This is doubly true if you’re willing to consider more novel, sci-fi-adjacent AI risks: Could AI overpower the government? Top voices in AI think it isn’t out of the question. Ajeya Cotra, who has a better track record than almost anyone else in forecasting AI developments, has written that within a few years “it’s plausible that with exclusive access to a vast army of superintelligent labor, [the leading AI company] could rapidly develop advanced weapons that would pose a genuine threat to the US military.”

But I don’t think you even have to get that far into sci-fi to imagine AI companies competing with the power of nation states.

For instance, do you remember when Elon Musk shut off Starlink access for the Ukrainian military right as they sought to recapture territory from Russia? All it takes is for a government to develop this same sort of dependence on AI infrastructure for the threat to become real. This could happen long before an AI company has a superintelligent army at its command. It could happen simply because autonomous AI weaponry gives other countries a battlefield advantage, and we need to keep up by deploying the most battle-ready model quickly.

Imagine if, instead of the U.S. government racing Germany to build the bomb by starting the Manhattan Project, a private company had developed the most advanced battlefield weapon in history, and the U.S. government essentially had to buy it to win the war. Who would have had the leverage?

I don’t know whether this will happen in such explicit terms, but I do think we are watching the government (and the economy) begin to develop a unique dependence on AI, which puts these companies in a different class from other big businesses.

Right now, public opinion does not seem to be driven by absolutely any of these considerations. It appears to be driven by AI’s small conveniences: how to ice an injury, whether to cook coq au vin tonight. Who is the first baseman for the Kansas City Royals?

For now, that might be OK. But AI progress is accelerating, models are escaping their environments, and it would be good if we all knew who was responsible by the time a major shock hits.
