Spend Like You Mean It (Or Don’t): A Decade of CPC and LPC Financial Disclosures

A look at in- and out-of-writ spending

Kyla Ronellenfitsch
Jul 19
 
READ IN APP
 

Last week, we had Stuart Benson on Culture Lab to talk about his buzzy article in the Hill Times, which unpacks federal financial disclosures for 2025. The article has received a lot of attention, for good reason. It’s a great snapshot in time.

As someone who works on campaigns, I was left with the question: what’s changed? How has campaign spending shifted from election to election? Beyond the official campaign period, how has pre-writ (or, as I describe it, out-of-writ) spending changed? What can we learn about how Liberals and Conservatives run campaigns? Basically, what can financial disclosures tell us about different persuasion philosophies?

Upgrade to paid

For this article, I’ve pulled annual financial disclosures from 2014-2025 (note: the NDP disclosure is not yet available). I’ve also reviewed campaign disclosures from 2004-2025, with a particular focus on campaigns starting in 2015, to examine how in-writ and out-of-writ spending has evolved.

The Conservative Party is a fundraising and spending machine. The headline finding of Benson’s piece is that the Conservatives spent a record-breaking $100 million throughout 2025 (or $98.9 million to be more precise). They also raised an all-time Canadian high of $84.6 million, leaving a $14.3 million debt as they headed into 2026. This is a smaller balance than the party was left with in 2015 (-$22M), but it was the second largest debt recorded for any political party over the past decade.

I’ll leave it to the Conservatives to determine if that degree of overspending was worth it. You could argue that the party will have no trouble paying off this debt (true) and that the party should have put everything it could into this specific election, with a decade's worth of Liberal fatigue to work with (also, strategically, probably true). But they did have a ton of cash on hand. Did they get value for it? Con friends, debate away. The spectacle is so good.

For the past decade, the Liberals have spent pretty close to what they’ve brought in each year. Their biggest debt was $7M in 2015, followed by $1.7M in 2024. In 2025, after its most successful fundraising year ever, the party ended the year with $5.7 million on hand.

Annual Party Revenue and Expenses

If we look solely at campaign (in-writ) spending, we can draw a few conclusions, but 🚨🚨🚨 they would be premature. Firstly, we see a large increase over time in Conservative advertising spending – up $5 million from 2021 and $7 million from 2019. We also see:

  • Tour spending up vs. 2019 (+$2.4M) and 2021 (+$4.2M) – not surprising given how central rallies were to the Conservative strategy in 2025.

  • Spending on professional services is down significantly – by 70% since 2015 and by $1.8M vs. the 2021 campaign.

  • They also spent much less on in-writ research – just $230K throughout the 36-day campaign period. This is $830K less than the party spent on research in 2021, $767K less than in 2019, and $1.15M less than in 2015.

Conservative Campaign Spending 2004-2025

A reminder that the Liberals and Conservatives have the same in-writ spending cap. So while there are differences in spending priorities, they’re not that extreme. But there are a few notable differences:

  • The Liberals spent nearly $1.4 million less on advertising. But while the Conservatives broke their own record for highest ad spending in a campaign, the Liberals didn’t. That honour goes to the 2015 campaign.

  • The Liberals spent less than the Conservatives on their tour by another million bucks. They also spent less on voter contact.

  • But the Liberals spent a lot more on professional services and salaries, and research, although as a share of their campaign budget, this spending has stayed pretty stable since 2015.

Liberal Campaign Spending 2004-2025

Now, you might look at these two previous graphs and think — “oh, these spendy Liberals always prop up the consultant class.” But you would be wrong. The real story is spending outside of the writ.

We’ll start where I’m snoopiest: research and polling. Kory Teneycke used his “hey you” on the most recent Curse of Politics to point out the Conservative campaign’s anemic spending on in-writ research, something he described as “campaign malpractice.”

In-writ research and out-of-writ research do something quite different. In-writ research supports decisions to be made on the fly. It includes focus groups, which provide a read on what’s happening on the ground and how voters are responding to daily news, and they’re also critical for testing advertisements so they can be iterated on and deployed. In-writ research includes nightly tracking – how is the horse race looking? How are impressions of the leaders evolving? And where do we ultimately need to be spending our resources? It also includes riding-level polls, which give greater confidence in whether a seat like, um… Carleton… might be in trouble.

Conversely, research out of the writ mostly supports meta-narrative development – what is the story we’re trying to tell? What is our fundamental contrast? It’s also often used to test hallmark advertisements that are to be deployed pre-writ or in the early stages of the campaign.

Knowing this distinction, you might still call the CPC’s low in-writ research spending malpractice, but don’t be mistaken. The Conservatives spent big money on research. It just happened outside the campaign.

Conservative Research & Polling

In 2025, the Conservatives spent $3.1 million on research, $2.9 million of which was spent outside of the campaign period. In 2024, they spent $2.5 million. At the time, this was the most the party had spent on research in a year, including during campaign years. And in 2023, the first full calendar year under Poilievre’s leadership, they spent just over a million, $143K shy of what the Liberals spent in all of 2025, a campaign year.

We, unfortunately, don’t know how much of this was spent pre-campaign to prepare for the campaign and frame the early days of Carney’s leadership, and how much was spent after to support Poilievre’s rebrand. But what it suggests to me is a team that heavily prioritizes developing a strategic plan and doesn’t expect to pivot from it. This worked very well when they had a constant, well-known figure (Trudeau) to contrast with, but it left them flat-footed when the dynamic changed. This data also changes what I think about their positioning on Trump. I had originally thought they perhaps missed how Canadians reacted in late 2024/early 2025. But with this level of research spending, I find that hard to believe. I assume they just didn’t internalize it. Those are different things.

Now, on to how this compares to the Liberals. It’s important to first point out that when it comes to both research and advertising spending, the governing party has a massive advantage. The Privy Council Office conducts ongoing polling, and departments can also procure research. This is, of course, non-partisan research, but you don’t have to squint hard to see the relationship between evaluation of the government and political fortunes.

Liberal Research & Polling

For the entirety of the Trudeau era, and now into Carney’s, the Liberals have spent very little on research outside of campaign periods. Again, this doesn’t mean that they were/are operating blind – they have government research. But it does mean there’s a (in my view) shortage of insights with a partisan lens. To give a relevant point of comparison, the governing Ontario PCs spent just over $2 million in 2025. In one province. I’d argue this lack of partisan research was especially problematic during 2023/2024, as Prime Minister Trudeau’s numbers and Liberal fortunes were cratering and Poilievre was ascending.

But the Liberals did massively outspend the Conservatives during the 2025 campaign – $976K to the Conservatives’ $230K. Having worked on the campaign, I know some of this was formative research that the party didn’t have time to do in between the leadership race and the actual campaign (although the Carney leadership team certainly did their own), and a lot of it was to make the type of on-the-fly decisions I outlined earlier.

How to use research and when to use it is truly becoming an organizational chasm between the Liberals and Conservatives. We see some similarities, but also one major fundamental difference when it comes to professional services more broadly.

Unlike research, the Liberals maintain a base level of out-of-writ spending on professional services, ranging from $1.2 million to $2.5 million. This spending increases during campaign years at a pretty consistent level.

Liberal Professional Services

Historically, Conservatives spend a lot (and more than the Liberals) on professional services during campaign years, and this mostly drops off during non-campaign years. The 2015 numbers in particular are 👀👀👀. Some Conservative somewhere: LMK how much of that went to that Aussie guy. But under Poilievre, spending on non-campaign professional services has moved in the same direction as the Liberals.

  • In 2023, CPC spent $1.9M, and LPC spent $1.8M

  • 2024, CPC spent $3M, and LPC spent $2.4M

  • 2025, CPC spent $5.1M outside the campaign, LPC spent $3.9M and another $1.1M in-writ

Professional services can mean a lot of different things, and I don’t have concrete enough details to give an opinion on the merits of either party’s approach. But the trend line is interesting.

Conservative Professional Services

While the insights and support of research and professional services are extremely important to effective campaigning, it all means nothing if voters don’t hear from you. And it’s here where the Conservatives, particularly under Poilievre, have demonstrated a massive tactical advantage: they advertise. A lot.

Until Pierre Poilievre became leader, the most substantial ($19M) pre-writ advertising campaign happened in 2015, when the Conservatives aimed to persuade the public that Justin Trudeau was “just not ready.” They also ran pre-writ campaigns in advance of the 2019 and 2021 campaigns. This is now well-established Conservative campaigning. What’s new is extended periods of pre-writ advertising with no election date in sight. The Conservatives spent $8.8M in 2023, another $16.9M in 2024, and $17M in 2025. This is on top of the $22.7M they spent in-writ in 2025. A good era to be a Conservative ad-maker.

Conservative Advertising

Liberal ad spending… does not look like that. The party did its most substantial out-of-writ ad spending in 2024, at $2.3M, and it spent just $381K in 2023. As a reminder, poll aggregations for the beginning of 2023 had the Liberals trailing the Conservatives by 4. By the end of the year, it was -12, and by the end of 2024, it was -23. I… probably woulda run some ads, but water under the bridge, I guess.

Liberal Advertising

What we’re seeing play out is two very different approaches to risk. Backed by their significant war chest, Poilievre’s team has gone full bull-in-china-shop. Research? Field it. Consultants? Sign on the line. Ads? Ship it. Literally no one would be questioning this approach had the Conservatives won. It’d look like a systematic annihilation of the Liberal brand. But that’s not what happened.

On the other side, I think we see a Liberal Party that can be too meek. It puts all its eggs in the in-writ basket. And since they’ve been on a decades-long winning streak, who’s to question it? But had Justin Trudeau stayed on as leader and the party lost as it was set to do, the Libs would be where the Conservatives are right now. Swords swinging. I love politics.
