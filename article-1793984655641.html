James Daunt does more press than any of the authors he sells

It felt like something fresh, almost four years ago, when SHuSH published a rather longish piece on a gentleman named James Daunt and his successful turnaround of Barnes & Noble. We called it a bookselling miracle, and it was.

As recently as 2018, Barnes & Noble had reported a loss of $125.5 million for the year. It had fired 1,800 employees and was in the process of closing about 150 of more than 700 stores. It had lost its fourth CEO in five years, the last to sexual-harassment allegations. The share price was down 80 percent from its peak. In 2019, Elliott Advisors, a private equity investor with a bare-knuckle profile, bought the near-corpse of Barnes & Noble for $683 million, which is where Daunt entered the picture as the new CEO.

Daunt had a lot of bookselling experience. In his twenties, he opened an eponymous bookstore in London and built it out to several locations. His original Marylebone location may still be the best bookstore in the city. In 2011, he led the rescue of Waterstones, the UK chain, from near-bankruptcy with the assistance of a Russian backer, Alexander Mamut. When things got hot for Mamut (and many other Russians with assets in London), he sold to Elliott Advisors. Elliott kept Daunt at the helm and expanded his remit when it acquired Barnes & Noble.

The Daunt playbook was to reorient the bookselling chains around bookselling, which might sound obvious, but it wasn’t at the time. Barnes & Noble, like Waterstones, had given up on books and was dedicating more of its space to toys, housewares, and general merchandise, virtually conceding book retailing to Amazon. Investors and stock analysts cheered this pivot away from books—selling a candle can be more profitable than selling a book. But it didn’t follow that a store full of candles was a more successful store than a properly functioning bookstore. There are a lot of places where people can buy general merchandise, but Barnes & Noble and Waterstones were dominant bookselling chains. Daunt’s insight was that people would still visit physical bookstores if the bookstores were good enough. He cleared a lot of crap from the shelves and concentrated on improved selection and displays of books. And it worked.

We don’t know to what extent it worked because the finances of Barnes & Noble stores aren’t publicly available. Daunt has routinely claimed improved sales, and Waterstones does report a healthy level of profitability. Elliott has also bought Foyles Bookshops and Blackwell’s, two other UK chains. Barnes & Noble is continuing to open stores and buy existing bookstores in the US. Elliott is planning to take its whole collection of bookselling assets public in the not-too-distant future, which is something you’re not keen to do if you don’t have a viable retail business.

Daunt appeared to be taking something of a victory lap when SHuSH wrote about him on January 13, 2023. He’d already talked to the New York Times and a couple of other outlets about the Barnes & Noble turnaround. In the months that followed, he was everywhere: Publishers Weekly, Fast Company, Axios, The Verge, BBC HARDtalk, The Guardian, CNN, Fox, The Wall Street Journal, CNBC.

Fair enough. Everyone’s allowed a victory lap.

But Daunt never left the media circuit. In 2024, he spoke to Fortune, the Toronto Star, and again to the The Wall Street Journal, CNN, Publishers Weekly, and a bunch of others. In 2025, he hit the Financial Times, PBS NewsHour, Modern Retail, The Telegraph, Bloomberg, The Bookseller, Xinhua, The Times. He did a TED podcast and appeared on CNN again.

This year has been more of the same: Publishers Weekly, The Guardian, Open Book, Livres Hebdo, The Wall Street Journal (again), Axios (again), Business Insider, NBC News, Stella Borzoni, the Masters of Scale podcast and, late last month, a long feature in the New Yorker.

That is a far from complete list of Daunt interviews. The man will talk to anyone. He’s making time not only for the big dogs but for one-man niche webcasts.

That’s highly unusual behaviour for the CEO of a large company. Most CEOs would prefer a colonoscopy to an interview. The last thing they’ll do is pick up the phone and answer a nosy journalist’s wide-ranging questions about their business, their industry, consumer habits, and their personal life. They see little to gain and endless opportunity for failure or embarrassment. Their public relations departments are tasked with keeping them out of the press except in tightly scripted, strategically important moments.

Yet here was Daunt baring it all in the October 26 New Yorker. He spoke for what must have been weeks to the magazine’s reporter and took him on tours of several Daunt-managed bookstores in New Jersey and the UK. The story is mostly flattering to Daunt, like the vast majority of the coverage he receives. He’s positioned as the man who saved bookstores, a champion of the written word, a bulwark against Amazon, etc. Journalists love bookstores and they’re predisposed to treat handsomely someone who appears dedicated to their improvement. But flattery doesn’t make sitting for an interview a comfortable experience.

Daunt had to talk about his childhood, his family, his college days, his accent. He stood still as a journalist scrutinized his facial expressions, his wardrobe, his weight. The reporter called his siblings, childhood friends, business associates, and employees. That’s more than slightly invasive.

We learn that Daunt is a bit of a nob, the son of a diplomat knighted by Queen Elizabeth. He attended Cambridge in the same era as Stephen Fry, Hugh Laurie, and Emma Thompson. He didn’t run with that crowd, settling instead for Hugh Bonneville as his celebrity friend. Daunt tried investment banking before bookselling. He likes to travel and read, and he uses one of those ridiculous folding bikes that a hapless Bonneville character rides for laughs on the BBC mockumentary W1A.

We feel Daunt squirm as he’s grilled on mass firings and an employee pay revolt at Waterstones, as he explains the awkward financial connections of his former Russian partner, as he notices how long it’s been since he last washed his car.

We remember why CEOs prefer colonoscopies.

So what is Daunt up to? Why has he sat for, by my very rough count, seventy interviews since I wrote that SHuSH? He doesn’t come across as an egomaniac or an exhibitionist. It’s not like he doesn’t have other things to do, running book chains on both sides of the Atlantic. Yet he’s more available than Snoop Dogg.

I don’t have the answer. I guess I could have called him and asked. I must be the only person who writes about publishing that Daunt’s never spoken to. His personal wireless number is probably on the corporate website. But it’s late on a Thursday and I’ve only got a couple of hours to spend here. I’m going to speculate instead—the whole gamut of possible reasons from cynical to credulous.

Daunt has already announced that Elliott Investment Management has been exploring an initial public offering (IPO) for its bookselling assets. That would entail shares in the company being sold on stock exchanges. An IPO requires an aura of success—why else would anyone buy the shares? The more hype Daunt generates for his bookstores, the better the reception for his IPO, whenever it happens. CEOs almost always have a personal stake in such outcomes.

Sticking with cynical, Elliott keeps adding to its already dominant position in bricks-and-mortar bookselling in both the US and UK. That’s the kind of thing regulators and competition authorities notice. It’s the kind of thing publishers, who prefer to have a wide range of retailing options, notice. Every admiring Daunt profile that positions him as a friend to literature rather than frontman for a bunch of competition-shy investment bankers is a win.

I don’t put much stock in either theory. Does Daunt think about those things? He must. But there are more targeted means of dealing with them than endless rounds of media interviews if that’s all you’re trying to accomplish.

A more sensible explanation for Daunt’s ubiquity is that the last fifteen years of his life have been consumed by efforts to resurrect failing book chains. Waterstones and Barnes & Noble were bad businesses with awful reputations. People who like books didn’t like those stores. Daunt’s constant chatter about respecting books, his excitement about reading, and his commitment to a better browsing experience invite people to rediscover Waterstones and B&N. He’s selling bookstore visits in all these interviews, coaxing people to put on their shoes rather than one-click on the competition’s site. You start thinking about this angle and it suddenly makes sense why he’s taking the time to drag a New Yorker reporter through a series of bookstores on two continents.

I would think that Daunt’s constant media presence is also a means of speaking to his employees. There are tens of thousands of them across the chains. He repeatedly emphasizes that restoring booksellers’ confidence is essential to turning around the businesses. He’s always talking about empowering his managers to study and serve their local markets. He goes into minute details on the art of hand-selling books. His interviews are a way of sharing knowledge, setting expectations, and recognizing good work.

Related to that, I think Daunt enjoys talking about his craft. He’s spent his whole life thinking about how to do a better job of selling books. He’s really good at it. He’s got strong and well-grounded opinions about what works and what doesn’t. Someone asks him about it, and he can’t help but spout. There’s an evangelical streak to him.

His presence may also be a way of lubricating acquisitions. If you own a bookstore, or a bunch of bookstores, and you care about what you’ve built, you’re more likely to sell your cherished assets to someone who speaks your language and shares your values.

We should acknowledge that another likely reason Daunt does a great number of interviews is that he receives a great number of requests. This may be an instance of an unusually receptive subject meeting unusually enthusiastic demand. Again, his turnaround story and love of reading are catnip to journalists. He’s articulate, engaging, and sitting there waiting for you to call—of course he gets calls.

More speculation?

Let’s go out on a limb and read some of his father into Daunt. He exhibits a diplomat’s appreciation that cultivating relationships and explaining your position are real work. You see him in the New Yorker piece going around and introducing himself to people in stores and allowing them to feel recognized. No person (or news outlet) is too small to deserve the courtesy of his attention. He deftly adjusts his messaging for different constituencies: the literary press, as opposed to the business press. And he clearly appreciates that his book-saviour narrative serves to eclipse potentially negative narratives associated with the businesses (low pay, the mass firings, etc.).

Finally—and, yes, this might be the credulous part—I think Daunt genuinely believes in what he’s doing. The man loves books. He describes his work as a form of public service. He thinks booksellers can actively promote reading and push back against any and all notions of technological inevitability and books as a dead medium. He wants to do his bit for literary and civic life. I’m going on record as saying the man is to some considerable extent putting himself out there with good intentions.

Reading all these speculations over again, I think the primary reason Daunt keeps doing media is that it’s good for business and advances the work of turning around all these stores is incomplete. That said, there’s probably some truth in all of the speculations, which only confirms that his high-visibility strategy is a good one. And God bless him for doing it. The book world is short on champions. The heads of the major publishing companies have no profile and, it would seem, little to say. There are no strong voices in the trade press. If Daunt didn’t exist, we’d have to invent him.

Recent decades have seen an explosion of government-approved casinos, sports betting, and online gaming opportunities. The politicians and officials behind them meticulously track the revenue they receive, but turn a blind eye to its often devastating human costs.

A powerful work of investigative journalism, this book forces a reckoning with the true cost of gambling in Canada.
