The Just-Enough Woman

No matter how much of a girly-girl I am, I will never feel feminine enough.

For as long as I can remember, I’ve had a bizarre relationship with femininity, but not in the “I hate makeup and fashion, I just want to play sports” kind of way. Judging from how my husband reacts when yet another package from TheRealReal shows up on our doorstep (I swear it’s a good deal! That $40 skirt was $500 retail!) he probably wishes that I had a little more of that in me.

But no, I am not a tomboy—I think I’d need to be able to run a mile without passing out, or perhaps correctly throw one singular ball to qualify. I have no gender dysphoria, or any desire to be perceived as “not a woman.” If anything, it’s more of a gender dysmorphia—the opposite scenario—where I’m a cisgender straight woman, I want to be feminine, all my interests and desires and aesthetics are feminine, but I don’t see myself as feminine and I’m always pleasantly surprised when other people describe me as such. I feel locked out of femininity in an intangible way. I am an amorphous, blobby entity doing its best impression of a feminine woman and failing.

I can’t fully articulate why I feel like this. Factually speaking, nothing about me is clearly masculine. I’m average height. I’m thin. When other people hear that I feel this way, they either don’t know what I’m talking about, or they think I’m fishing for compliments because I want people to reassure me that yes, I am just a tiny, fragile, girlish little wilting baby deer with anemia and nobody could ever mistake me for big and tough. I swear, it’s not that—I know that this neurosis is silly. But I also have a feeling I’m not the only woman who experiences femininity this way.

As a child, when this obsession was at its worst, I viewed myself as enormous, hulking and destructive (Taylor Swift’s famous lyric, “Everybody is a sexy baby and I’m a monster on the hill,” comes to mind.) I knew, logically, that I wasn’t huge or imposing. But I did not see myself as delicate, or as a “little girl.”

And yet, I wanted to be! I was constantly checking myself for hints of masculinity, roughness, bigness that I needed to vanquish. When I got angry or jealous, I hated myself for feeling something so ungirlish and unbecoming. I policed my posture, my walk, my voice, everything, for signs of oafishness. I was envious of other girls in my preschool who took up less space, both physically and figuratively than I did—girls with softer voices and fewer disciplinary issues (I had an annoying habit of screaming the word “horse” during naptime). As I got older and we began writing letters, I envied girls with smaller, lighter handwriting than my own, especially when they dotted their i’s with circles.

I felt dread when I grew out of a clothing size or shoe size, worried that I would keep growing exponentially until I was ten feet tall and five hundred pounds. I remember being furious when I discovered my parents had briefly given me a gender-neutral nickname as a baby, and even angrier when a volunteering mom at my school called me by my last name—and my last name isn’t something pretty and girly like “Rosen”—when taking attendance during a field trip. Sometimes, my mom would tell a funny story about something I did when I was little, and I couldn’t help but cringe at the lower voice she affected to impersonate a female toddler. When I auditioned for the school chorus and they checked my vocal range to categorize me as an alto or soprano, I purposefully sang off-key when they tested the lower end of my range, so that I wouldn’t have to suffer the indignity of being put in the “low voice” category.

I am no longer steeped in that degree of nonsense now that I’m a grown woman. Puberty seemed to have ameliorated most of these feelings for me, but some remained—and shifted. Instead of worrying that I was too big, it was my smallness that I feared was masculine. I no longer wanted to be a little girl. I wanted to be a woman, and what makes someone a feminine woman is very different from what makes someone a feminine little girl. I noticed a lack of what I’ll just call “womanly abundance” everywhere. Other teenage girls’ cups of womanliness seemed to runneth over in every direction, while I felt like I was crouched under the faucet of femininity waiting for a drop. With me, nothing overflowed, nothing blossomed, nothing was overgrown. Everything was too small, not enough. It’s hard to write about this without other people assuming I’m just humblebragging about being skinny, but growing up I wasn’t the type of skinny that people envied—I was reminded constantly, against my will, that only a gay man or pedophile would find me attractive.

It seemed I didn’t have enough of anything. Other girls would complain about having too much hair with too much volume, too much male attention, boobs that were too big, butts that were too big, hips that were too wide. I had none of those things—straight thin hair (I told myself it was “fine but dense” even though that probably wasn’t true) narrow hips, and modest boobs and butt frequently maligned as nonexistent. Once, I got onto the school bus wearing a low-cut top, and one boy in the back shouted, “take it off!” while another promptly corrected him, “she has no tits, we want her to keep it on!”

Even though I got male attention and even boyfriends, I never felt overburdened or violated by some large, looming sexualization that appeared to be common for everyone but me. I still don’t know if I actually got less attention than other girls, or if I just responded to it differently. Girls I knew complained about boys being unable to look them in the eye, being “visually undressed,” having every man they encountered gawk at them, and I just couldn’t relate. Not that these things never happened, but that they happened only on occasion, and they didn’t make up the majority of my experiences with boys and men.

All my aesthetic choices from my teen years onward were colored with the burden of believing that “girls like me” couldn’t get away with anything less than hyperfeminine camp. I felt I was one bad styling decision away from being rendered completely undesirable and genderless. A too-short haircut (which, in my case, often meant “above the nipples”) made me feel indistinguishable from a little boy. For years, I was afraid to wear anything that did not enable me to wear a push-up bra. I clung to my long hair even as it frayed and tangled. I refused to wear dresses and tops that didn’t define my waist, and envied the girls who bubbled with so much femininity that they could rock a cute bob or an oversized graphic tee with relaxed jeans and still look girly. Why not me? I wondered. Looking at myself in the mirror, I couldn’t pinpoint a particular feature or flaw that explained why I felt I couldn’t make those choices. It was just me.

Many of my earliest comics hinged on this theme, where I acknowledged I wasn’t hideous, per se, but something about me was just…wrong.

Later on, my fertility became wrapped up in this mess, or really, my lack thereof. After my husband and I tried unsuccessfully to get pregnant for a year, I got some basic bloodwork done. (I was convinced I was infertile after our first failed cycle, but nobody would take me seriously until it had been a year.) When I got my results back, my estrogen was through the roof, and I foolishly saw this as a final confirmation that I was, in fact, exceptionally feminine. For a fleeting moment, I forgot that we were failing to conceive and danced through the aisle of CVS as I imagined the excess estrogen blooming and blossoming in my womb, my body full of pink flowers spraying estrogen like pollen. (As it turned out, the excess estrogen was likely a holdover from a functional cyst in the previous menstrual cycle.) As we continued investigating our difficulties conceiving, I realized that I actually had lower than average egg count for my age, which felt like a punch in the stomach, and a total confirmation of what I had always felt: I am lacking in womanhood, I am not woman enough, I never have enough of anything feminine.

But that wasn’t the reason for our difficulties conceiving, nor was my below-average egg count severe enough to affect my odds at pregnancy anyway. The ultimate irony of this “journey” was that it was my husband, not me, who was infertile. He was born without a vas deferens, a rare and asymptomatic birth defect most commonly seen in men with active cystic fibrosis, but also occasionally in asymptomatic carriers. My husband did not know he harbored any CFTR mutations until he found out he was infertile.

Oddly enough, this discovery was not a hit to my husband’s masculinity. He had not struggled with his gender performance the way I had, and had never once worried that the world did not see him as a real man. Perhaps it helped that he was captain of his high school lacrosse team, was bombarded with female attention from puberty onwards—once on MySpace from a grown woman who clearly had major problems—and had chest hair in middle school (to be fair, he’s Greek, they have chest hair at birth). Whatever the reason, he saw his infertility as an inconvenient hiccup, if not a bit of a relief because the IVF prognosis for this condition is excellent. One of the first things he said upon diagnosis was, “Well, I guess my ex was definitely lying about being pregnant.” A few times, when discussing our IVF process with friends, he jokingly remarked he was “missing a nutsack piece.”

Instead of reacting like he did—with amusement and a strategic plan for overcoming the obstacle—I absorbed his infertility and made it my own. I carried intense pain for him that he didn’t even feel. His infertility—the minor defect that barely caused him a headache—bruised my ego and took over my identity. I began to see myself as an infertile woman, a label I still have a hard time shaking after having two kids and repeatedly reminding myself that I am not actually infertile. I had already felt infertile after a year of negative pregnancy tests, and on some level I “always knew” I wasn’t like other women, that womanly things like conception and pregnancy wouldn’t come naturally to me. When I got pregnant (through IVF, of course) I worried that I was an imposter among pregnant ladies, that the floral decorations on Mother’s Day weren’t really for me, that the big, sparkly pink club of Moms was not truly open to women like me. I knew how stupid this was. I would not speak this way of another woman who needed IVF, even for her own infertility. But I can’t lie about how I felt. Logical or not, I felt like an imposter. When I sent my friend a photo of me holding my son after bringing him home she texted back, “You know you can take his hospital ankle bracelet off, right? It’s not a clothing receipt. You can’t return him.” I hadn’t realized I had even done this, but I still didn’t want to take it off. On some level, I didn’t think they’d let me keep him. I didn’t think a woman like me deserved him.

I also “always knew” that I wouldn’t make enough milk. It just stood to reason. I didn’t have “too much” of anything womanly. Why would I make enough milk? And sure enough, I didn’t. My milk was delayed, despite a white woman with blonde locs who resembled a skinny Viking trying to squeeze my nipples in the hospital, and I had to formula feed my first child for about a week. Eventually, I did produce breastmilk and I exclusively breastfed him, but producing enough felt like a struggle. Every time I pumped, I felt like less of a woman. I would see photographs of chubby babies with rolls obscuring every joint in their bodies and all I could think about was how my own lack of womanly abundance was reflected in my son’s totally average build. Of course those women would magically produce roly-poly babies with only their magical breastmilk and their magnificent, enormous breasts. What a shock that my meager breasts produced meager breastmilk, which I’m still convinced had the caloric makeup of a diet shake in the ‘90s that gave people diarrhea.

By then, my bigger concern was my child’s health, not my own femininity, and it’s unflattering for me to admit that I placed any personal pride in my milk production at all. But I did. Not enough to consume me, not enough to cause feelings of despair or hopelessness, not enough to detract from the joy of finally being a mom, but enough that I thought to myself, “Of course I wouldn’t have a surplus of anything womanly. I’m a ‘just enough’ woman, aren’t I?”

I recently tweeted about this feeling—especially how severe it was when I was a kid—and a few other women said they had the same experience, but couldn’t quite describe it until they saw my tweet. (I know, I know, I’m doing the Trump thing: there were big strong men coming to me with tears in their eyes! Well, I’ll have you know they were women.) I do not blame these women for finding this issue hard to describe. To someone who doesn’t experience it, it sounds completely made-up, if not nonsensical. It’s not tomboyishness. It’s certainly unrelated to actual gender identity, and it’s hard to talk about without sounding like you’re attempting to place yourself on the gender nonconformity spectrum despite being, actually, turbo-cisgender, if such a thing exists.

Some people have speculated that this is a sign of autism, and well, it wouldn’t be the first time I did or felt something and discovered that it’s an autism clue (after writing the draft of this article I came across a good post by an autistic woman describing something very similar.) This is one of the reasons that an adult autism diagnosis can feel so validating—suddenly everything about you that’s just “weird” has an explanation, and it’s an explanation other people should respect, an explanation that can be given in just one word instead of a rambling essay about breastmilk and shoe sizes, interspersed with Trump references.

Dutifully, I explored the autism angle. Given all my other signs that point in that direction—such as the fact that I intentionally taught myself social skills in adulthood because I did not naturally grasp them as a child—I had to be open to the possibility. But most literature about autistic women not feeling feminine is about autistic assigned-female-at-birth people not identifying as women at all, or even just presenting in a more gender-neutral, tomboyish kind of way while maintaining a female identity. Even among autistic cisgender women, I do not commonly see what I experience. I see posts from autistic women being confused about the various “rituals” of femininity, like makeup and fashion, and finding these things weird and cumbersome. But for me, it is the opposite. I love feminine rituals. I obsessively perform them to feel more feminine, and still don’t feel good enough. All of my desires are the most mainstream feminine things ever. Even the way I experience my heterosexuality is so mondo-feminine that it shoehorns around and probably repulses most men (Specifically, I am unable to get aroused if I believe a sex act was my idea or my choice, and cannot do anything that makes me feel “aggressive.” Only a specific type of man enjoys women like this, and I believe most men would consider this tendency extremely tiresome.)

Maybe this is something that happens to a variety of women, autistic or not. Maybe I haven’t heard much about it precisely because it’s so hard to explain, and really makes no sense despite feeling so real. Perhaps all women feel this way a little bit, because our vision of femininity is based on unattainable ideals that are bound to make every woman feels like she falls short in some way. There is a reason that memes juxtaposing our own imperfect selves with etherial, flawless “other girls” are so universally relatable. Where I feel inadequate because I’m not curvy enough, perhaps curvy women feel inadequate because they aren’t slender enough. Maybe all of us are “just enough” women who feel we are lacking and putting on a crappy performance, and maybe I’m not so weird.

Or maybe I’m really, truly, a certified nutter. That possibility is always open.
