Welcome to The Weekly Scroll!

The Weekly Scroll is a once-a-week roundup of the funniest, most interesting, and strangest stories from around the social internet.

The internet is not just a strange, hyperselected web of people hiding from reality; in many ways, the internet is reality. What happens online shapes real life just as real life shapes what happens online.

Every week, I sift through social media so you don’t have to, collecting the most notable trends, memes, and online dramas in one place. Think of it as a guided tour through the more insane parts of the web, with commentary about what it all means.

If you enjoy internet nonsense but don’t have the time to doomscroll for hours every day, you’re in the right place.

This week, we’re looking at why everyone is talking about “Woke 1,” the collapse of the conservative social media ecosystem, Twitter’s new payout scheme, and Anthropic watermarking Claude outputs.

We’re all trying to find the guy who did this

Last week, Rep. Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez appeared on ABC and got caught in a conversation about former Wisconsin gubernatorial candidate Francesca Hong’s controversial old tweets. In an effort to downplay Hong’s past statements, AOC repeated a phrase that’s been gaining traction on social media: “Woke 1 was crazy.”

This made quite a few people angry.

Moderates and conservatives felt that the phrase was a stock dismissal of the very real excesses of “Woke 1.”

Cultural commentator Ryan Broderick insinuated those using the phrase were empowering sexism and bigotry: “Was #MeToo crazy? Was protesting police killing unarmed Black people live on camera crazy?”

The phrase was popularized by New York City Councilman Chi Ossé, who used it in May to downplay his own overbearing 2021 tweet that “a cis white man should not be the next speaker of the Council.”

It’s an implicit acknowledgment from Ossé, AOC, and other left-leaning personalities that during the “peak woke” years, certain things got a little out of hand. Progressive power brokers seem fully aware that they overextended their cultural and political program, and, hey, they’ve learned a valuable lesson, and they’re backing down from things they said back then.

At the risk of being a stick in the mud, I’m not sure that’s good enough. To use a wildly over-the-top analogy:

Woke 1 was not a natural disaster that just sort of unexplainably happened; it was a specific political and cultural project that specific people orchestrated. AOC was one of those people.

Share

Woke 1 erupted out of reasonable concerns — sexual harassment and police brutality weren’t fake issues — but your head would have to be stuck in the sand to ignore that there were also harmful overreactions that did little to advance social justice:

  • Professors were investigated, suspended, and fired for saying a Mandarin word that sounded offensive in English and for showing artwork that depicted Muhammad. David Shor was fired¹ for tweeting research that showed nonviolent protests were more effective than violent protests.

  • Two Black teenagers in Seattle were murdered because the city wanted to let anarchists cosplay at running an “autonomous zone” with no policing.

  • Perhaps most consequentially, we did actually defund the police in many cities, including in AOC’s backyard. Mayor Bill de Blasio cut anywhere from $420 million to $1 billion² and more than 1,100 officers from the NYPD’s budget (as much as one-sixth of police spending) while murder rates were spiking to the highest level in almost a decade.

What makes “Woke 1 is craaaaazy” frustrating is in part what makes it effective: No one knows exactly what you mean. By Woke 1, do we mean prosecuting Harvey Weinstein? Pronoun buttons and land acknowledgments? Firing professors for wrongthink? Defunding the police? Body-positive models on the Victoria’s Secret runway?

It’s all of the above at the same time, which makes talking about it as one movement difficult.

Here’s my recommendation for those tempted to lean into “Woke 1 was crazy.” Instead of doing that, be specific. What specifically was crazy? If things went too far, which things? What did you say back then that you now regret, and do you think you need to apologize?

New York City Mayor Zohran Mamdani, when confronted with his own statements about defunding and dismantling the NYPD while campaigning, directly said his past words were “out of step” with his current thoughts and that “I am not defunding the police. I’m not running to defund the police.” He didn’t dodge the subject and he didn’t crack jokes. He later went on Fox News and, when prodded to apologize, said, “Absolutely, I’ll apologize to police officers right here.”

The larger context, as I mentioned above, is that AOC is a smart enough politician to read the political moment. She knows that her past calls to defund the police — particularly when she slammed de Blasio’s police cuts as an “illusion” and insisted that “Defunding police means defunding police” — don’t look great today. But if she wants to back away from that, she should do so clearly and specifically without relying on cheap social media jokes.

Nobody wants to sit at the Cruel Kids’ Table anymore

The New York Times recently published a fantastic analysis of how the MAGA media machine is breaking apart. Across a range of platforms and a range of prominent MAGA influencers, conservative reach is declining:

Loyal Infinite Scroll readers have, of course, been following the declines in conservative new media for months. And it’s not just the influencers themselves; it’s also right-leaning websites seeing big traffic declines. Here are some of my theories for what’s going on:

  • Donald Trump is deeply unpopular. Trump’s popularity has steadily declined for his entire second term, with no signs of stopping. Defending a president who is so incredibly disliked is hard, especially when he’s currently losing a war to Iran and doing nothing to address the cost-of-living crisis everyone’s angry about. Right-wing outrage is harder to manufacture when you’re in charge, and the public is still angry at the government.

Trump approval is cratering. Republican vote share is not
Despite Trump approval cratering over the last few months, Republicans have mostly avoided collapsing further in the generic ballot.

  • No more foreign money. This is at least a little conspiratorial, but we do know that Russia was funneling money to influencers like Tim Pool and Benny Johnson. And we know that the Hungarian government under Viktor Orban was paying conservative thinkers and influencers. Now that both of those funding sources have dried up, it’s not crazy to speculate whether it’s had an impact. I don’t actually think this is a primary driver for the declines, but it’s worth mentioning.

  • The rise of alternatives. Rather than following purely political voices, fans of right-leaning content might follow creators like Asmongold, Clavicular, or any number of edgy livestreamers. These folks typically mix occasional political commentary with gaming, lifestyle, and other nonpolitical content.

There’s a parallel here between left voices who recognize that “Woke 1” is past its prime and right voices who are going to have to come to the same realization. MAGA had around three months of cultural dominance after Trump’s election — the very brief era of the Cruel Kids’ Table — before it overplayed its hand and sparked a backlash.

Twitter is changing its revenue program

Everyone’s favorite online grievance casino is changing how it pays creators. Twitter announced this month it was ending its current revenue payouts Sept. 7 and introducing a completely new program for which accounts will have to reapply. The new program is aimed at creators who produce “original, high-quality content.”

Twitter’s monetization program incentivized the rise of slop merchants and repost accounts. Some of the biggest and most viral accounts since Elon’s acquisition are those like Kira or Nature is Amazing, which do almost nothing but repost content found elsewhere. The new program lays out very specifically that people reposting stuff from other users or other sites aren’t actually going to qualify for monetization any longer.

It’s not a coincidence that this comes days after Nikita Bier stepped down as Twitter’s head of product. Bier overhauled the revenue system but seemed to recognize by the end that it was benefiting slop merchants and AI bots. In his last months on the job, he spent some of his time personally nuking spammy accounts.

As far as I’m concerned, killing these accounts is a pure win because it replaces lowest-common-denominator engagement slop with more personalized, quality slop. One area to watch — this could have interesting implications for the clipping economy that drives popularity for so many livestreamers, YouTubers, and podcasters these days.

Claude is watermarking its outputs

Anthropic has announced that it will be adding a watermark to text generated by newer Claude models. The watermark won’t be a literal watermark in the sense of an actual image added to outputs, although Anthropic hasn’t been entirely transparent about how it works. Most likely, Claude probabilistically chooses certain words more often than other similar words in a way that would silently mark the text as generated by Claude.

Plenty of people are excited by the tool’s potential to expose AI writing, but others got quite angry: Ben Thompson of Stratechery called it “no different from insisting that a ballpoint pen advertise itself as the author,” which seems like a wildly mismatched comparison for generative AI tools.

The most prominent concern is about misattribution, that is, that text that was only proofread by Claude will be treated as “AI written.” There is also some research that warns watermarking could degrade output quality, and the head of research at GPTZero argued that “Directionally, [watermarking] does reduce the output quality.” The way this happens is that Claude might choose words that best watermark the output rather than best answer the prompt.

What I find most interesting about this is the inherent cat-and-mouse game played between AI-detection methods and AI-hiding tools. Pangram has gained popularity as a way to catch AI content, but there are already new AI tools claiming to be able to “humanize” AI text that will beat Pangram’s detection. Similarly, Claude has introduced this new watermark, but there are already people building tools to remove the watermark.

I think transparency is good: There’s a legitimate interest in being able to figure out if something was produced by humans or AIs. If you’re ashamed of your AI use, perhaps that says something about how you’re using it.
