Can Mark Carney Save the World (Order)?

The first Pope to address the United Nations General Assembly, Paul VI, introduced himself as an “expert on humanity.”

As such an expert, the pontiff said, he is a spokesperson for both the dead and the living. “For the dead who have fallen in the terrible wars of the past, dreaming of world peace and harmony,” he said. “For the living who have survived the wars and who in their hearts condemn in advance those who would try to have them repeated.”

And for youth, who want more than war; for the poor, the disinherited, the unfortunate — those who often fight those wars. For those “who long for justice, a dignified life, liberty, prosperity and progress.”

The United Nations, the Pope said, is a place for such experts. “People turn to the United Nations as if it were their last hope for peace and harmony.”

It was a pivotal moment, he said. And the U.N. is the only body capable of rising to meet it. “You are an association, a bridge between peoples, a network of relations between States,” he said. One could argue, Paul said, that the United Nations is for mortal life what the Catholic Church is for the spiritual realm.

“Nothing loftier can be imagined on the natural level.”

It is easy, now, to look back at this flowery speech and roll your eyes. The Catholic leader’s homily could be dismissed as moral preening. His invective against war, in favor of diplomacy, his call to justice, his admonishment of radical self interest: It all feels rather hollow, given that we know what the U.N. would go on to become.

But at that moment, in 1965, the world was setting up the United Nations for success. Just months before the Pope’s address, changes to the U.N.’s all-powerful Security Council had taken effect: No longer would it just be the big five powers and a small cadre of rotating members. It would be expanded to 15 members, despite opposition from the major powers. The Council would become more representative, more fair, less beholden to the imperial powers and the hegemons.

It was a good start. But Pope Paul VI challenged the U.N. to be more ambitious in living up to the extraordinary task of being a moral core for humankind. “We repeat our wish,” he exclaimed: “Go forward!”

And then, it didn’t.

For six decades, the U.N.’s role as the world’s chief deliberative body has slowly fallen into disrepair. It succeeded, to varying degrees, on many fronts — from its massively-influential-if-generally-unknown International Telecommunication Union to its peacekeeping mandate the world over. And its halls continued to be a place for diplomats to meet, wheel, deal, scheme, and come to understanding.

But as a global parliament, the U.N.’s role began to decline and has never really recovered.

Greetings, from Nova Scotia, where the annual Halifax International Security Forum is wrapping up.

This year’s confab was, in a word, a bit weird.

The Forum — which brings together diplomats, journalists, military brass, spies, aid workers, activists, and a raft of others — has become one of the premier spots for influential people to untangle geopolitics. It became a particularly useful spot for Ukrainian officials to meet their NATO counterparts, for Taiwanese and Kurdish politicians to entreat with their equals in North America and Europe, and it has become a spot to gossip about what the hell is going on in Washington.

Starting last year, the Forum has lacked any official American participation. Gone are the State Department mandarins, ambassadors, Pentagon bigwigs, or White House officials: The Trump administration has forbidden them from attending any and all globalist meet-ups.

It makes for a Forum that is lacking a superpower at the center, but it solidifies the meeting as a linking-up of middle powers. In that way, it is reflecting a new world order that we seem unsure the shape of.

I’ll have some more grand thoughts coming out of Halifax in the coming days and weeks.
