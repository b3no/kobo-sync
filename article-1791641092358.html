I went to the Renaissance fair and got a lesson in belonging

Maybe the least serious places are some of the most important.

Halina Bennet
Oct 10
∙
Paid

By 10, the field was full of people in preparation. A woman braced one hand against the side of her Honda and tugged with the other at the laces of her bodice, one rib at a time, until woman and garment both sighed into place. Nearby, a man shook out a rust-colored cloak from his trunk and, with a flourish, swung it over his shoulders. Hoods went up, belts were cinched. A girl of about five sat in the backseat of a Toyota, her legs sticking straight out into the misty Maryland morning as her father knelt in the mud, helping her into rubber rain boots.

Hundreds of people in gowns and breeches crossed the ruin of ruts the field had become after days of rain, holding onto their hems and each other as they made their way to the front gates.

I have spent the week since I made my first trip to a Renaissance fair working out why it felt so unlike any other place I’ve tried to lose myself in. Immersive experiences are typically evaluated as feats of construction, wherein the spectacle comes from the authenticity of a set or the discipline of a cast. By those standards, the fair is a horrible miss — unless King Henry VIII’s England was indeed full of wizards and fairies and pirates and princesses eating cheesecake on a stick. But if you throw that standard out and think of immersive in its true meaning: characterized by deep absorption, complete involvement, or an environment that surrounds the senses so that a participant feels present inside an artificial, digital, or physical world, the fair was by far the most immersive experience I’ve ever had.

Don’t get me wrong: I did not for a moment think that I had somehow wound up in King Henry’s court — far from it. I was, however, completely taken with how immediately a part of the community I felt.

I’ve been seeking new experiences recently (mostly with The Argument’s Maibritt Henkel). My conclusion, having spent nearly every weekend of the last six months or so chasing new worlds, is that immersion is ultimately about belonging.

Colonial Williamsburg prides itself on its period accuracy and precision. I went with Maibritt in May, and we spent the day wandering around a preserved version of 18th-century America, learning about mantua-making and blacksmithing and watching actors bring history back to life. The craftspeople, actors, and groundskeepers are all very good at their jobs. But it was far from immersive.

There is a clear separation between the people who preserve the 18th century and you, the person visiting a museum. Visitors are not invited to LARP¹ the colonies, and even if it’s momentarily thrilling to chat with someone in character, you experience the place as a voyeur.

Months later, Maibritt and I once again sought novelty, this time in the form of a trip to Richmond, Va., for Labor Day. You might be thinking: Halina, why would you go to Richmond? Surely Virginia Beach would be more holiday appropriate. We went to Richmond because neither of us had been and because both Mikala Jamison and Jerusalem Demsas enthusiastically recommended the city. Boy, did it deliver.

Unlike Colonial Williamsburg, Richmond is a real, unscripted city. There is no fee for admission, and as visitors, we could do many of the same things that people who live there do. We ate at restaurants on residents’ recommendations, saw local bands play at a bar, wandered through museums and vintage stores, and spent a morning strolling on Belle Isle.

Our time in Richmond, like any visit to a place that is not necessarily known for its tourism, gave us a decent impression of what it might feel like to belong there, to feel at ease and part of the community. Still, my involvement with the city and its community was not total. A local knows things a visitor can’t, and that accumulated knowledge is what makes a place a community rather than a destination. It takes years of living in a place to know it, and nothing a visitor does, however sincere, can shortcut real belonging.

The Renaissance fair has no such waiting period. Someone owns the land and operates the festival, vendors operate the booths and shops, and actors joust as squires, but the community that ultimately creates the place asks for a single thing in exchange for your belonging: show up with enthusiasm and preferably in something strange. Being a part of this particular world requires only that you embrace it. I showed up, for my part, in a wholly period-inaccurate collection of billowy linen items and costumy bits from my closet. I was a Ren fair novice in a thrown-together outfit, but I felt no less a part of the community than the people who showed up in elaborate costumes, obviously longtime regulars.

I spent the day feeling unencumbered joy, despite the fact that the Ren fair is actually pretty boring. There are performances, but they’re generally unsophisticated and ultimately for children. Really, the fair is for wandering, shopping, eating, and drinking. It’s essentially a debaucherous farmer’s market.

My joy, then, was coming from the all-too-rare feeling of totally belonging in an environment that encourages the weird, the wacky, and the whimsical, where the only social convention is that one leans into all three and where the community only exists when the gates open and ceases once they close. Unlike a real locality, no one has a head start on belonging, and unlike Colonial Williamsburg, visitors are participants.

The relief of total belonging was everywhere, evident on people of every age and body type, nearly all of them dressed ridiculously. Children ate turkey legs in Disney princess costumes, some of the largest, most boisterous, and hairiest men I’ve ever seen wore fur tails and no shirts, and people of all levels of bodaciousness poured out of corsets. It was both the crassest and — due to a total lack of judgment from anyone — least provocative place I’ve been. While everyone outwardly appreciated their fellow visitors’ appearances, there was no feeling of being watched or of being a voyeur.

My takeaway is that immersion depends less on how convincing a place is than on whether you are allowed to belong to it. Colonial Williamsburg lets you visit the past, while Richmond lets you approximate the local experience. The fair makes you a citizen.

Granted, this was my first time at the fair, so I am making my assessment without knowing whether the regulars, who have put years of their lives and real money into their costumes, see a hierarchy I can’t. And the staff and vendors are surely more connected to the place than I was. Perhaps what I took for equality was a newcomer’s illusion. Regardless of whether a hierarchy is real, I didn’t feel it.

A costume is a kind of armor that allows you to express yourself without having to be earnest with your self-expression. And unlike a comic convention — the other great gathering of the costumed — you don’t need any particular background knowledge to participate. You can wear something you feel completely fabulous in and that you can say isn’t really you, while also being more you than a conventional outfit you might wear day to day.

That paradox explains the fair’s generosity. Absolutely nothing there is serious, so nothing, and no one, can be mocked, and people are free to be sincere. Belonging is usually gated by time, expertise, or status. At the fair, the only barrier is your willingness to be ridiculous.

Weekend letter of recommendation:

  • You should visit a Renaissance fair or other equally frivolous, fabulous event.

  • I recently read “Doppelganger: A Trip Into the Mirror World” by Naomi Klein and thought it was an interesting look at polarization and digital identity.

  • I’m reading Ann Patchett’s new novel, “Whistler,” which I’m enjoying so far.
