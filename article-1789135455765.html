Allen Lane, The Bodley Head, and an anonymous diarist

In 1926, Allen Lane was a young publisher at The Bodley Head, a firm founded by his uncle. He was approached about a manuscript entitled The Whispering Gallery—the diaries of an anonymous retired diplomat or, rather, “envoy extraordinary.” Its pages, writes Lane’s biographer, Jeremy Lewis, were “crammed with scandalous and revealing revelations about assorted politicians, tycoons and literary men, including Lenin, Mussolini, Kitchener, Churchill, the late Tsar, Henry James, and Cecil Rhodes.”

Who wouldn’t want to publish that?

Lane learned that the manuscript had been whipped into shape by Hesketh Pearson (below), an actor, writer, and military hero, who was also the representative of the anonymous diplomat. The US rights had been sold to the prestigious firm of Boni & Liveright. Lane rode his Triumph motorcycle to Sussex where he met the vacationing Hesketh Pearson and learned that his anonymous diplomat had been keeping a diary since his university years and throughout his impressive career. With the backing of colleagues at The Bodley Head, Lane offered £250 for the rights to the manuscript, with the provision that the diarist’s identity be revealed to him and one of the firm’s directors. His offer was accepted. Lane was told that the diplomatic diarist was Sir Rennell Rodd.

An aggressive print run of 5,000 copies was ordered. Lane sold the serial rights to the Sunday Chronicle and assured literary editors at other major newspapers that The Whispering Gallery would be “one of the most talked about books of the autumn season.” He was not wrong.

By publication day, all but about 500 copies had been sold into stores. Another print run was ordered. Lane was not especially concerned when the Daily Mail denounced the book on release as a “scandalous fake.” A conservative paper was never going to respond well to a diplomat who described Lenin as a sympathetic character.

Then the Observer piled on: “a reeking compost of garbage,” it wrote. The Times joined the chorus. Important figures disputed facts in the book. Winston Churchill, for instance, said he was actually on the Western Front when the diarist had him dining with Asquith in Downing Street.

Lane came under pressure from the higher-ups at The Bodley Head. They asked him to coax the anonymous diplomat into responding to his critics, or sue those describing his book as a fraud.

“I do not mind if I am believed or not,” responded the diplomat via Pearson.

Attacks on the reputation of The Bodley Head continued. Lane and a director of the firm decided to confront Pearson. They arranged a meeting. Pearson arrived late and flustered. He said he’d had a difficult time with the diarist who claimed, “among other things, that it is [Pearson’s] fault that the book has got all this publicity, and that he did not want it.” Lane and the director were mollified.

But the Daily Mail kept up its attacks on the publisher: “They have lent themselves to a fraud on the public and have debased the name of their firm. It remains to be seen whether reputable authors will be willing to have their works published by a firm which is capable of such disgraceful conduct.”

Increasingly impatient with young Lane, The Bodley Head’s board increased its pressure on him to find a resolution. He met again with Pearson and demanded to meet the diarist. Pearson said that was not possible. Among other reasons, the timing was inconvenient because Sir Rennell Rodd’s daughter was about to be married. Pearson was indignant at suggestions that the diarist might not exist and that he had written the manuscript himself. Lawsuits were threatened both ways.

The Bodley Head’s board insisted Lane reveal the name of the diarist. Lane at first refused, but then took it upon himself to visit Sir Rennell Rodd at his home in Cavendish Square. A well-regarded former ambassador to Rome and delegate to the League of Nations, Sir Rennell said he had nothing at all to do with The Whispering Gallery. “Had Lane taken the trouble to consult the reference books,” writes Lewis, “he would have discovered that [Rodd] had recently written his memoirs in three volumes; the last of these had appeared only the previous year, which made it all the more unlikely that he would have written The Whispering Gallery.”

Lane confessed all this to his board. Word leaked to the Daily Mail, which celebrated its exposure of “the greatest literary scandal of recent times.” The Bodley Head tried to salvage its reputation by claiming to have been the victims of a fraud by a “miscreant,” i.e. Pearson, and by blaming “our junior director,” i.e., Lane, for guaranteeing the authenticity of the book.

Then the fun started.

“The miscreant,” writes Lewis, “was out of London when [the] storm broke, appearing in a play in Cardiff. The theatre was besieged by reporters; Pearson made his escape via the nearest pub, and from there he headed to the railway station and the London train, but was recognized by reporters and had to lock himself in the train’s lavatory for the entire journey to Paddington. His house in Abbey Road was hemmed in by journalists, and a warrant for his arrest was lying on the hall table: his publishers had decided to sue him on the grounds of obtaining money from them under false pretences. He was eventually arrested in his solicitor’s offices…”

An eminent barrister who had agreed to take Pearson’s case told him it was hopeless and he should plead guilty. Pearson seemed to be cooked. George Bernard Shaw said that his only move was to ask the court “for no more consideration than to be treated as a fool rather than a scoundrel.”

Instead, Pearson found another lawyer and used his day in court to argue that he had merely delivered to Lane and The Bodley Head the same sort of crap they were accustomed to publishing. No harm in that. They ought to have known the book was a lark. Sir Rennell’s name, he said to the delight of a packed gallery, had been chosen because anyone with half a brain would realize he could never have written such a book. Nor would an intelligent reader have taken the conversations and comic repartee in the book for genuine. Lane apparently never read the book. Pearson’s lawyer went on to describe, “luridly and in unwholesome detail,” the racy illustrated editions of Ovid’s Amores and Apuleius’s The Golden Ass published by The Bodley Head, as well as an “utterly foul” modern novel. The firm’s directors, who preferred to be known for their serious fiction and their biography of Immanuel Kant, crumbled under attack. The jury declared Pearson not guilty after only twenty minutes’ deliberation.

If that weren’t humiliating enough for The Bodley Head, Pearson sued the firm for damages related to “the pain and suffering” inflicted upon him by their prosecution. He demanded £250 plus £500 for the copyright in the book. The Bodley Head settled with him for £416 8s. 1d., what Lewis describes as “the entire profit from sales before the book was withdrawn.”

The Bodley Head was taunted around London as “The Badly Had.” Now Allen Lane, thrown to the wolves by his directors, seemed to be cooked. Maybe he deserved to be so. There is reason to believe he knew the book was a hoax from the jump and didn’t care so long as it sold well.

And then Lane’s aunt, the widow of John Lane, founder of The Bodley Head, died, just a few weeks after the trial. Allen Lane found himself the twenty-something majority shareholder of The Bodley Head. He was soon experimenting with cheap paperback editions of quality books, and in 1935 published the first ten Penguin books. This was the foundation of what would become one of the great publishing empires of the twentieth century.

The Whispering Gallery debacle was exposed 100 years ago this autumn. We’re accustomed, nowadays, to learning that personal memoirs and diaries don’t always accord with verifiable fact. So were readers in 1926. George Bernard Shaw yawned that Pearson’s fraud couldn’t be “anything worse than the fictitious memoirs and travels that have often been published as genuine.”

Jeremy Lewis’s fantastic book has a weird publishing history. It came out in the UK in 2005 as a Viking (owned by Penguin) hardcover entitled Penguin Special: The Life and Times of Allen Lane. Then Penguin released it as a paperback in 2006, alongside an American edition with a longer subtitle: Penguin Special: The Story of Allen Lane, the Founder of Penguin Books and the Man Who Changed Publishing Forever. In 2025, to mark its ninetieth anniversary, Penguin re-released Lewis’s book with yet another title: The Man Who Changed the Way We Read: The Story of Allen Lane and Penguin Books. Don’t hold any of this against it. It’s a minor masterpiece, thoroughly researched and deftly told. The audiobook is also highly recommended.

Recent decades have seen an explosion of government-approved casinos, sports betting, and online gaming opportunities. The politicians and officials behind them meticulously track the revenue they receive, but turn a blind eye to its often devastating human costs.

Through gripping personal stories and a comprehensive investigation, journalist Rob Csernyik shows how public officials evade accountability for lives shattered by gambling addiction―for instance, rarely recording gambling as a risk factor in suicide. From the rise of video lottery terminals to the explosion of smartphone betting, A Losing Hand shows how modern gambling has become more accessible, more immersive, and more dangerous than ever. The result is a crisis hiding in plain sight, one that policymakers have ignored for decades.

A powerful work of investigative journalism, this book forces a reckoning with the true cost of gambling in Canada.
