The Future Is for Everyone — or Could Be

In “The Rise and Fall of the Artificial State,” Jill Lepore dismantles Silicon Valley’s grand promises about AI, democracy, and the future.

Ron Charles
Aug 14
 
READ IN APP
 

On Monday, Meta CEO Mark Zuckerberg published a 6,500-word marketing pitch tarted up as a manifesto.

“We propose a philosophy based on individual empowerment as the source of prosperity,” Zuckerberg — or his avatar — wrote in a document that smells like Ayn Rand’s eau de toilette.

The message may be suspect, but the timing is superb. On Aug. 25, Jill Lepore will publish a wide-ranging warning about the deleterious effects of Silicon Valley on American democracy.

“The manifestos keep coming,” Lepore writes in The Rise and Fall of the Artificial State, each one promising something “magnificently, impossibly, extravagantly world-changing.” For more than a century, prophets of technology like Zuckerberg have been pumping out such “messianic boosterism.”

But the stakes and the dangers have never been higher.

Lepore takes as her inspiration Hannah Arendt’s concern that someday human beings might “need artificial machines to do our thinking and speaking.”

“That day has now come,” Lepore says.

Her central thesis, first explored in The New Yorker in 2024, is that Western democracies are in thrall to a small handful of billionaires who talk like comic-book superheroes and dream of dissolving the constraints of government, our connections to each other, and even “the prison of their own bodies.”

In grandiose mission statements, these “twenty-first-century merchant princes” envision replacing the functions of government with the wiser, more efficient, more data-driven actions of AI. They promise personal independence and infinite power but consistently deliver political manipulation, algorithmic control, and environmental degradation.

It’s no accident, she suggests, that the rise of social media and its vaunted promises for participatory democracy has coincided with the decline of democracies around the world. And she expresses her concerns in the kind of witty, aphoristic lines you always think of too late at a party:

“Silicon Valley executives took their political philosophy not from Plato and Jefferson but from equal parts Atlas Shrugged and Marvel comics.”

Quoting Zuckerberg, Peter Thiel, and other tech moguls, Lepore shows that the AI hype is a subterfuge, a bait-and-switch — promising utopia but delivering it only for a few, while the rest of us are left on an increasingly ailing planet.

Any attempt to regulate these corporations for the benefit of citizens or the earth is met with apocalyptic warnings that only their complete freedom from constraints can save humanity from total annihilation.

The fundamental absurdity emanating from Silicon Valley goes something like this: Democracy must be circumvented and the natural world sacrificed so that tech companies can develop AI systems powerful enough to preserve democracy and the natural world.

It’s amusing — and infuriating — to see how worn and endlessly recycled today’s hype is. In 1975, a sociologist imagined that the computer revolution would return us to “the form of democracy found in the ancient Greek city-state, the kibbutz, and the New England town meeting, which gave every citizen the opportunity to directly participate in the political process.”

What we got instead is Cambridge Analytica exploiting voters’ outrage and your brother-in-law sending out YouTube videos about George Soros funding Antifa. What’s on the horizon will be infinitely more sophisticated and coercive.

The Rise and Fall of the Artificial State tends toward high-level analysis and broad trends, but if the book has a starring villain, it’s Elon Musk, who claimed that his purchase of Twitter was “important to the function of democracy,” even to the future of civilization, but then used the social media platform “to bend political discourse, politics, and government to his will.”

Lepore also writes damningly about the parallel between Musk’s hyperbolic claims and the unhinged ravings of his racist grandfather.

And Musk comes up for a particularly entertaining lashing in a chapter about the way self-professed tech visionaries have drawn their visions from classic 20th-century science fiction. Trouble is, Musk and his fellow AI enthusiasts have consistently misread those dystopian stories as “assembly instructions.”

“They took more than a century of warnings about building a particular set of tools,” Lepore writes, “and instead of heeding those warnings, they decided to build those tools.” (See this recent excerpt in Wired.)

Not all the essays in Artificial State are equally effective — or well integrated with her larger thesis. There’s a strange section about the early 20th-century craze of electrocuting elephants that’s fascinating (and horrific) but seems, like a few other parts, obliquely tied to Lepore’s argument.

But that’s just fodder for interesting debate — which this book is sure to generate on op-ed pages, podcasts, and newsletters (voilà). Put in your hold now at the library, or you won’t get a copy till next year.

I’d love to tell you that The Rise and Fall of the Artificial State ends with a blueprint for resisting the tyrannical march of government by machine, but Lepore is too clear-eyed. We’re here, she states, “because of the failure of liberal democracy to limit corporate power over politics and government, its failure to heed decades of warnings about the dangers of an automated state.”

The best she can do is remind us — four times on the final page — that public debate about these new tools “can still happen.” On the flimsy modality of that claim, our future rests.◆

Cabin Fever

Nowadays when Dawn and I visit my folks in New Hampshire, we stay in the farmhouse on the hill overlooking the covered bridge. But decades ago, when my grandparents were alive, my family kept to the cabin down by the Blackwater River.

The smell of the place — those pine boards marinated in smoke and mold — wafts through my earliest memories.

There is something about a woodland cabin, something at once nostalgic, romantic, and just a bit terrifying. The idyllic getaway is also the scene of the crime, the residence of writers, hermits, witches.

I was delighted to catch wind of a curious project by the novelist Josh Riedel. While doing research for his next book, Riedel tracked down a collection of 30 building designs from 1918 called Sunset’s Cabin Plan Book. The vacation homes it describes have evocative names like “A Cabin in the Clouds,” “A Club House for Twenty,” “The Biggest Little Cabin.”

But rather than build a little house, Riedel built a little zine called Thirty Cabins. He enlisted contributions from 29 writers for a collection of short stories — flash fiction, really — inspired by these rustic dwellings.

Small images of cabin blueprints adorn the collection’s endpapers.

Ed Park sets an ominous tone in the first story about a man looking for a large stick or rock and then sneaking up on a cabin — his “target.” In Kathleen Boland’s witty story, a woman arrives at a large party in the woods where she knows no one. Gina Chung’s narrator might have seen a bear (or a man?) outside the kitchen window.

Time sometimes shifts unpredictably in these stories, as in Rachel Khong’s piece about a woman remembering the drowning of a friend more than 20 years earlier.

The paranoid husband in Ben Purkert’s story is concerned he shouldn’t fall asleep after suffering a concussion, but he might have bigger things to worry about.

Thirty Cabins is alternately charming and creepy, witty and sad, as diverse as the buildings that inspired it. What’s most striking, though, is how quickly these writers can create a scene, a collection of characters, a mood. In the cramped space of just two or three pages, they conjure up whole worlds.

Like a little house in the woods, each one is a quirky marvel of engineering (purchase).◆

Picture This

The first time I was offered an opportunity to speak with Julie Andrews, I ran around the house and told everybody I was going to speak to Julie Andrews — but never answered the publicist, so that fell through.

The second time I was offered an opportunity to speak with Julie Andrews, I set 18 alarm clocks and had two backup phones at the ready.

For those of you who have not yet received a phone call from Dame Julie, let me reassure you that she sounds just as warm, witty, and charming as you’ve always imagined.

She and her daughter, Emma Walton Hamilton, have just published a picture book called Shy, the latest of several dozen collaborations. The story, delightfully illustrated by Eva Byrne, was inspired by a gray poodle puppy that Dame Julie owned while she was playing Eliza Doolittle in My Fair Lady in the 1950s.

“I called her Shy,” Dame Julie tells me, “and it suited her personality. But after a while, when I did my daily practice at the piano — because when you’re in a big show, you have to warm up ahead of time — she suddenly would put her head back, and stretch her neck, terribly sweet, point her nose to the stars, and sing with me. And by that, I mean, she kind of howled.”

Professor Henry Higgins would be horrified, of course. He complains that Eliza is “crooning like a bilious pigeon.”

Dame Julie was far more sympathetic. “She couldn’t help herself,” she insists. “I thought, well, maybe it’s an unhappy thing. So I put her in the next room, behind a closed door, and then I saw the little snout appearing under the door, and these little whimpers continued, and attempts at singing continued, as I continued.”

“It wasn’t music in general — it was just between my little dog and me!”

That funny anecdote is essentially the plot of Shy, although in these pages, the dog’s owner is a young concert pianist, and Shy makes a fabulously successful concert debut.

Shy finds her confidence quickly, but that wasn’t the case for Dame Julie.

“Believe me,” she says, “it took a lot of time to learn to trust and to give. A lot of my early years, I spent learning how to connect, and I had a hard time with audiences because part of me kind of went out to the front of the house and judged my performance, as though I were part of the audience. I wasn’t doing the giving that is so important. But once I latched onto that and felt grateful for the fact that they paid money to see the show and me and all of that, it changed my life, and then I began to really know my craft.”

But after voice surgery effectively ended her singing career in 1997, she had to find a new outlet.

“I was bemoaning my fate to Emma one day, and she said, ‘You’ve just found a new way of using your voice with this writing.’ A great weight seemed to fall off my shoulders, and that’s exactly what it’s all about. I still can contribute, which I love to do, and the joy of finding a story, and particularly writing it with Emma, is tremendous.”◆

This Week’s Poem

Poetry is always concerned with economy but not always economics.

This wry poem from Bob Hicok’s new collection, Breathe, bristles with the experience of running out of money in a country very concerned about inconveniencing millionaires.

Minimum wage

Try buying a car on $7.25 an hour.
Covering rent or a house payment. Food.
Electric and heating bills. Health and car
and home insurance. Try being one
of over one hundred million Americans
who can’t. Try working
two or three jobs so you can probably
die earlier than you need to. Perhaps
you’re already dead or this poem
is boring you to death. I know
I’d prefer to be in a hot air balloon
looking for clouds or Canada
looking for moose or Detroit
looking across the river at Canada,
where I had my first cannoli
because Italy was too far away.
Try buying something nice for your kid,
a red tricycle or sunny future.
Adjusted for inflation,
1962’s minimum wage of a buck fifteen
would be twelve and a quarter now. Adjusted
for decency, capitalism would share
more of the wealth. At a minimum,
a minimum wage should minimally allow
a comfortable life. It’s common sense,
like not sleeping with knives or running
with sharpened wolves or drowning in debt.
One should drown in water or happiness.
Maybe pudding. Try buying your way out
of poverty, lifting yourself up
by your lack of bootstraps. I mean,
have you looked at how much boots cost
these days, at how expensive it is
to have nothing to your name or reason
to believe anything will ever change?

Whole Cloth

In the mid-1830s, the textile mills in Lowell, Mass., were producing more than 39 million yards of cloth — almost enough to wrap around the Earth — every single year.

My family and I recently visited the Boott Cotton Mills Museum at Lowell National Historical Park.

The highlight is a long room filled with 88 looms from the early 20th century. Our guides paused their presentation, handed out earplugs, and started up the spinning leather belts that set a dozen of the looms clackety-clacking away, weaving towels for the gift shop. (Yes, of course, we bought one.)

The park, which also includes a recreated boardinghouse, lays out a story of astonishing industrial growth. Thousands of people, mostly women, were drawn to work on the looms from farms all over New England. Although the jobs could be dangerous and the hours were always long, in the early decades, at least, the workers enjoyed relatively high wages and cultural enrichment unavailable to them back home.

Of course, workers organized for better working conditions and more humane schedules.

But curiously, none of the three or four tour guides who spoke to us mentioned how more than 12 million pounds of Southern cotton happened to arrive at the mills every year. With such statistical elisions, history is woven into a pretty scrim.◆
