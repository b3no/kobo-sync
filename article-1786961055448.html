Woke 1 is dead. We’ve learned nothing.

Blatant indifference to facts is all too common across ideologies

Matthew Yglesias
Aug 17
∙
Paid
 
READ IN APP
 

I’m of two minds about the “Woke 1 was crazy” discourse.

  1. I agree with those who find this to be inadequately apologetic to the individuals whose lives and careers were unjustifiably upended.

  2. No one likes to admit that they were wrong about anything, and if this evasive formulation allows people to shift to new, better views, that’s good.

I’ll offer this Idrees Kahloon article in The Atlantic if you want a taste of (1), but there are lots of articles along those lines and, rather than chime in with one of my own, I want to make a different observation: Whatever you think Woke 1 was, it arose under structural conditions that are very much still with us and that extend well beyond the specific politics of the left.

Many of the themes that dominated Woke 1 discourse have died down. And institutional leaders, to their credit, have gotten a lot better at not reacting in a dysfunctional way to social-media outrage mobs.

But it’s still very much the case that in a world without strong gatekeepers, false or absurd ideas can spread like wildfire and become a kind of instant conventional wisdom.

And what I personally remember most bitterly about Woke 1 is not the behavior of the most hysterical people; it’s the behavior of the people who knew perfectly well that others were behaving hysterically and who carefully avoided saying false or insane things (that’s how you can know that they knew) but who also avoided actively contesting them.

This was, for the most part, savvy behavior.

Those who actually said nutty things have suffered for it to an extent, which is exactly why there’s now this effort to slink away from them. But those who ended up on the wrong side of Woke 1 fads also suffered. The winners were those who ducked the storm — budget experts who knew it wasn’t true that cities spend more money on police than schools but didn’t say so — and emerged with their reputations unscathed.

That incentive structure to avoid being the “well, actually” guy is still very much with us and means that any kind of discourse could go wildly off the rails. And unfortunately, while we are now deeply into an anti-woke backlash on the right, the backlash isn’t taking the form of resisting the poor epistemic practices of the woke left — it’s resisting the value of caring about the weak and unfortunate while replicating the same toxic dynamics.

It’s what it says about you

When Vox was launching, its first social-media staffer explained to me that the essence of social sharing is that it doesn’t really matter what the article says. What matters is what the act of sharing the article says about the sharer.

In the summer of 2020, sharing an anti-policing article signified “I think racism is bad.” So lots of people who, rightly, thought that racism was bad shared lots of anti-policing articles.

And lots of these articles made striking claims. Anyone in a calm, rational frame of mind would say that the right way to respond to an article making a striking and radical claim — that policing originated in slave patrols, that city budgets are dominated by policing, that there’s no evidence policing reduces crime, etc. — would be with a mix of curiosity and skepticism. Curiosity, because sometimes in life we learn something genuinely new that upsets our previously settled understanding of the world. But also skepticism, because it ought to be a little bit hard to shake off your views.

Twenty-first-century technology has, in theory, made it easier than ever to respond to novel claims with an appropriate mix of curiosity and skepticism. You can just look stuff up on your phone or laptop with incredible ease.

In practice, though, people mostly don’t do this. You just reshare the article because of what the act of resharing says about you.

Anti-policing articles are not currently getting a lot of shares, because in the summer of 2026 sharing anti-policing articles says something like “This guy is stuck in the past.” The progressive cause of the moment is Palestine, not anti-racism, so the specific habit of giving assent to wildly overblown factual claims to show you care has migrated from policing to Israel. That’s less practically harmful because it involves a small foreign country over which American progressives have very little actual influence rather than the governance of large American cities.

But a readily available structural dynamic that can easily shift to new topics can be utilized by all ideological camps.

Currents of right-wing hysteria

The ideological camps in America are never mirror images of each other, and one major contrast is a diffuse, fairly leaderless left against a right that is centered on the personality of Donald Trump.

So the ultimate taboo on the right today is not an emergent social-media phenomenon at all, but rather a top-down line about the 2020 presidential election. To get a presidential appointment, you need to be willing to at least pretend to believe that the 2020 election was stolen, and to participate in conservative politics at all, you need to say that this expectation is an unremarkable development and that anyone bothered by it is afflicted with some kind of irrational “Trump derangement syndrome.”

But this isn’t the only example of diffuse insanity on the right.

Another is the panic around vaccines and/or Tylenol being responsible for the rise in autism in America. This is the kind of “big, if true” claim that a rational person would respond to by looking into it. And I think it’s just really clear, if you bother to look into it, that neither vaccines nor Tylenol is responsible for the rise in autism diagnoses, because the rise in autism diagnoses is fully explained by a shift in the diagnostic criteria. There is no rise to explain. Nothing caused it.

Back in 2024 when Joe Biden was president and crime was falling from its pandemic-era peak, the internet was awash in conservative conspiracy theories about manipulation of the crime stats.

Now that Trump is president and crime has continued to fall, pretending to believe that crime is soaring has lost its instrumental value, so you don’t hear that anymore. You do, however, continue to see conservatives asserting without bothering to look it up that crime rates are lower in states like Texas and Florida than in New York and California.

We also go through these bizarre spasms of conservatives arguing that people were richer in the 1950s when, again, this is not a remotely hard claim to evaluate by just looking stuff up. There’s no need to belabor this endlessly, but we all know that if you tweet something inflammatory about immigrants, crime, sharia, the city of Chicago or ideally some combination of the above, then tons and tons of right-wing people will pay it forward without making the slightest effort to assess whether it’s true.

And we also know that the very people most likely to do this are exactly the people most likely to get mad at progressives for behaving the same way around a different “woke” set of topics.

We have to learn to care about facts

My official analysis is that this is fundamentally a structural, technologically driven phenomenon.

At the same time, I really don’t think it’s good to shrug at poor epistemic practices and just chalk it up to the nature of social media. It is in the nature of social media for people to behave that way. But that just means it’s incumbent on those of us with a high profile and some ability to steer the discourse to take time out of our busy days and try to lean against these trends.

I have incredible respect for Andy Masley’s tireless efforts to serve as a corrective against the tide of hysteria about data centers. What’s particularly impressive about his work on this topic is that he refuses to just flip to the other side and become a hardcore warrior on behalf of data centers. Some people say that the hysteria on this topic is a propaganda op led by the People’s Republic of China, but Masley looked into it and says there’s scant evidence for this. He also says that while most of the panic about data centers is misguided, there is a very real localized noise nuisance problem that’s easily addressable with appropriate setback rules but has not always been addressed effectively.

In terms of what it says about you, unfortunately, “data centers are underregulated in certain ways but not in others and while the companies building them are mostly getting a bad rap they are also spreading a false counter-conspiracy theory to try to get their way” just sort of positions you as an annoying, persnickety person.

Importantly, even though this is kind of my personality, I have learned over the years that this is not the way to behave in real life.

It is rude and annoying to start challenging friends and acquaintances on the casual assertions they make during a dinner party, asking for evidence, drawing fussy distinctions, etc. It can be fun and entertaining to have a back and forth about something, but, in person, you really do have to “read the room” to know whether that would be welcome or would kill the vibe.

That kind of social behavior tends to carry over into social media, which really is social in the sense of being dominated by cliques and in-group/out-group dynamics. But it’s dysfunctional in a social-media context because social media really is media, which ideally would be about helping people understand the world in a truthful way.

True wokeness requires knowledge

The good thing about “woke” behavior is that, in principle, the instinct to try to show that you care about the most vulnerable people is correct and praiseworthy.

The disaster of the anti-woke backlash is that instead of rebelling against the specific bad habits of the left, it has largely proceeded by attacking the value of caring about people. Rightists have started obsessively invoking concepts like “virtue signaling” and “suicidal empathy” to put forward a kind of atavistic selfishness as a personal ethic, often wallowing in cruelty.

But, truly, you ought to care about the suffering of others.

Caring is only worthwhile if you do things that are useful, though. People tend to appreciate this when it comes to matters that are close at hand. If you want to help your kid or your sister or friend, you need to find a way of providing assistance that actually works, not just go on vibes. And the reality is that public policy involves a lot of tedious technical minutiae. It’s totally reasonable that most people, most of the time, mostly don’t want to master the technical minutiae of public policy questions.

But if you don’t want to learn a lot of boring details about crime statistics or immigration trends or utility regulation or the Israel-Palestine conflict, then it would also make sense to avoid broadcasting strongly held opinions about these things.

Conversely, if you’re going to have well-grounded and strongly held views on an issue, ideally you’d do something about it.

Lots of progressives got, rightly, furious at the way DOGE disrupted some incredibly valuable global public-health programs. These programs are, dollar-for-dollar, the most effective ways of helping people. But I wish I saw equal passion for actually raising money for these kinds of causes or acknowledging their objective significance in contexts other than “being mad at Elon Musk.”

But beyond that, we’ve only identified highly effective public-health interventions after a lot of trial and error and failure both in the medical science and in the broader field of development and foreign aid. There’s a common view in left-wing and right-wing populisms that solving problems is very easy intellectually and you just need to defeat and disempower the bad people. This is not a view that would survive serious learning about the history of basically any policy topic.

It’s actually quite hard to learn what works and why and to implement good ideas at scale. The Woke 1 problems of dogmatism, incuriosity, closed-mindedness, and sloppiness about the facts are not solved by switching the focus from identity to economics or by becoming right-wing.

You need to actually behave less dogmatically, more curiously, more open-mindedly, and more rigorously, and I don’t see a lot of evidence that the discourse is moving in that direction.
