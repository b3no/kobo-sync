Those are from people who contributed to last November’s GiveDirectly fundraiser at a tier that entitles them to ask a must-answer Mailbag question. I’ve been a little bit remiss in getting to fulfilling my end of the bargain on that, but those answers should be rolling out over the next few weeks. If you’re feeling jealous, Slow Boring will be doing the fundraiser again this November, so look out for that!

M_McQ: If you were in charge, and weren’t worried about short term political consequences, what would you do about the Iran situation? The 2026 attack was clearly a mistake, but Iran does not want to return to the previous status quo. Is there a “least bad” deal we should be aiming for?

This is a case where I think it’s very important that Donald Trump try to actually fix the mess that he made because if the war somehow lingers for two more years and it becomes President Gavin Newsom’s problem, it’s going to be very challenging to resolve.

To see why, you need to go back to 2015 when the Obama administration was close to finalizing the negotiations on the Joint Comprehensive Plan of Action and Tom Cotton tried to scuttle the deal by writing a letter cosigned by most Senate Republicans warning that the G.O.P. was not going to consider itself bound by anything Barack Obama agreed to, so a deal would be pointless from an Iranian point of view. The White House convinced the Iranians that this was wrong, and if Hillary Clinton had won in 2016, it might have been wrong. Indeed, even after Donald Trump won in 2016, there was some thought that he might find it pragmatic to abide by the terms of the deal after all. There were people inside his administration, in fact, who warned him against tearing it up. But he did tear it up, and the Iranians ended up in exactly the position Cotton had tried to warn them about. The Iranians stayed fairly chill about this, opting to try to wait Trump out and see what happened next.

What happened next was Joe Biden became president and his team essentially proposed going back to the Obama-era deal.

The Iranians, understandably, said there was no point in doing a deal that was just going to be broken if the next administration was Republican. They demanded front-loaded concessions from the Biden administration because they no longer felt that American diplomatic agreements were credible. The Biden administration, also understandably, did not want to make a bunch of politically toxic upfront concessions to a regime that is justifiably held in low regard by the American people. So they back-burnered the whole thing, tried to work on Saudi-Israel normalization, and hoped for the best. This diplomatic situation probably provided some of the impetus for the 10/7 attack, which, whatever else it did, does seem to have scuttled efforts to build a regional anti-Iranian coalition.

Then Trump came back and of course we know what happened there.

The point of all this is that not only do the Iranians not particularly want to return to the pre-war status quo, but I also think they really don’t want to negotiate with Democrats at all. They are aware that a Democratic administration is unlikely to bomb them — Biden did not and his team acted to restrain Israel in this regard as well — but they’re also aware that a Democratic administration can’t conclude agreements that will stick. So the basic shape of Biden-era diplomacy, where it’s objectively hard to reach a deal, will obtain in 2029. Offsetting that is the fact that the war is genuinely very bad for Iran — if you think Americans are mad about expensive diesel, things are much worse over there — so there is an incentive to try to resolve this. But it’s just challenging for Democrats to try to make the kind of commitments that the Iranians would want.

It’s, of course, also hard for Trump to resolve this.

But the Republicans are the ones who can make the commitments the Iranians care most about. And while any deal a Democrat makes would end up getting savaged by Republicans (and the rump of AIPAC-aligned Democrats in Congress), the worst that’s going to happen to Trump if he makes a weak deal is maybe a mean editorial in the Free Press. So in the spirit of “only Nixon could go to China,” Trump’s prospects for diplomacy are a lot more promising than someone else’s prospects after we muddle through for years.

In terms of what a deal should look like, I believe in the “Getting to Yes” view that you want to talk about principles rather than positions.

America’s core interests are:

  • We want Iran to verifiably commit to nuclear non-proliferation.

  • We want to uphold the principle of freedom of the seas, which has been a core value of American foreign policy since the Jefferson administration and the First Barbary War.

  • We want some kind of apology for the hostage-taking and other actions taken during the Iranian Revolution that sent the U.S.-Iran relationship into a tailspin.

If we can achieve credible commitments on those scores, we should be prepared to treat Iran like a normal country where we disapprove of the regime and its conduct and its human rights record but accept its legal legitimacy and are not subjecting it to military or economic coercion. This means accepting that Israel, Saudi Arabia, Egypt, and the United Arab Emirates have a bunch of other major beefs with Iran about missiles and Hezbollah and Yemen and saying clearly that this is not our problem. If these countries want to collaborate with each other to form a rich, populous, and technologically advanced bloc of countries that check Iranian regional ambitions, that seems like a reasonable thing for them to do. But they need to work that out among themselves and reach among themselves some kind of resolution of the Palestinian situation that they find acceptable. They can’t be endlessly relying on the United States of America — a country on the other side of the world that does not even really import Middle Eastern oil — to do this for them.

But again, while I think Democrats can make progress toward this vision of being less involved in the Middle East, I think it is dramatically easier for a Republican administration to achieve this since they are the ones who’ve been insisting that America needs to serve as the pointy end of an Israeli or Saudi spear.

Marc Robbins: What is your greatest hope and fear for the world your son will be living in when he’s at your present age?

I’m going to choose to take this literally as “hopes for the world of 2060 when my son will be 45,” not specifically hopes about things that will impact him. So on the hope side, what I want to see is that two big trends that were present in an earlier part of my life but that have stalled out more recently will re-assert themselves.

  • One is rapid economic progress for the world’s poorest people. This was happening for a long time, driven primarily by China and India, but seems to have ended now that those countries are less poor rather than spreading to the still-poor countries of Africa and elsewhere.

  • The other is democracy and political liberalization. In the relatively recent past — even after Tiananmen and Vladimir Putin’s rise to power — there was a ton of optimism about the Arab Spring and democratization in the Middle East, but very little came of it.

In both cases, at one point we had what seemed like strong positive trends that it was widely believed would continue, and now I think the conventional wisdom has just flipped. I am a big believer in contingency, though, and don’t think we should give up on the possibility of a return to positive trends.

On fear, it’s all about the robot apocalypse.

Mark Zuckerman: I grew up in Arlington County, VA, which remains my home. As you are probably aware, Arlington has (in my lifetime at least) always been reliably blue. In the last 30 years or so, Arlington County has basically only elected Democrats to the County Board (John Vihstadt being the notable exception, which took a variety of unique circumstances that are unlikely to be repeated anytime soon). Even as blue as it is, somewhere in the range of 15%-30% of Arlington voters vote Republican, and their voices basically never make it onto the County Board. I don’t love this, both because I’m more conservative than the average Arlington County Board member, but also because I think Arlington County hasn’t been well served by having basically no ideological disagreement on the Board. So I think that a fairer representation of the electorate would be better for Democrats (as it would blunt some of the echo chamber problem), but also would be good for local Republicans, as they would have an incentive to be more serious when they actually have a chance to hold power. I know that Arlington is experimenting with ranked choice voting, but given that board seats are up on a staggered basis, it doesn’t seem like this will make any difference. Do you see a fairer solution? Do you even view this as a problem? Is it possible that “fair” representation would just end up with Republicans electing a MAGA bomb thrower who will only make things worse?

This has a pretty straightforward solution.

Right now, Arlington elects a five-person board and each of the five members of the board is elected at-large for staggered terms. Because the terms are four years long, that means that three years out of four there is a single county-wide board race and then on the fourth year there are two races. That’s a little bizarre, and I think common sense is that if you’re going to use this system, the terms should be five years.

But also it’s a bad system. Because this is a 70-30 blue county, the Democratic Party nominee always wins. But then because the median Democratic Party primary voter is to the left of the median Arlington resident, this means all five members of the board will be to the left of the median voter in the county.

One potential mitigation would be to drop the at-large elections and have five geographically distinct districts. I don’t think that would create a “Republican seat,” but you would at least introduce some variety into the mix.

A better idea, though, would be to elect all five seats simultaneously using a single transferable vote system. This is, in effect, a form of proportional representation, except without relying on party lists. A whole bunch of candidates would run, and they could identify as Democrats or Republicans or whatever they want. Official party organizations could endorse a specific five-candidate slate. And then each voter would rank the candidates in whatever order they prefer. Then you count up everyone’s first choice votes. Anyone who gets more than 20 percent of the vote wins a seat. You then redistribute excess votes from the winners to the voters’ second choices and see if that gets anyone else over the threshold. If it doesn’t, you start eliminating the candidates who got the fewest votes and going to their supporters’ second choice. Here’s an explainer video if you need the details.

The upshot is that in a place like Arlington, you’d expect to elect one Republican and four Democrats (rather than 5-0), and of those Democrats either one or two would be moderates and either one or zero would be D.S.A. leftist types. Which is just to say the governance of the county would be in line with the views of its voters rather than the current situation where a fairly progressive electorate always gets a board that is even more progressive.

Auros Harman: I am running for local office -- a City Council seat in the Bay Area suburb of San Bruno. I wonder how you think the “Common Sense Democrat” platform should be expressed in local politics, when the old maxim that “all politics is local” has seemingly inverted, at least for the voters who have any kind of partisan identity? For some slice of the electorate, now, the only politics they’re paying attention to is national. […]

The big thing I’m trying to do differently this year is just to get started earlier, raise a bit more money than I did last time, and get translated mailers for the ~8% of the district that requests ballot materials in Spanish. I did very little foreign language outreach last time — a friend who’s a native speaker of Spanish was able to do one round of phonebanking, but that was it. But I’m also going back and forth on how much to lean into Common Sense Dem rhetoric that could perhaps reach the conservative segment of the electorate, versus trying to get some partisan messaging out, targeting households that I can identify as likely anti-Trump, on the fact that Marty’s not a Dem and doesn’t seem like somebody who’d be ready to, e.g., ask our local police, if ICE agents come to the Bay Area and act the way they did in Minnesota, to at minimum refuse to cooperate with anything beyond targeting criminals, and potentially even be ready to investigate criminal violations of rights -- if, G*d forbid, an Alex Pretti case were to happen, I’d want our locals to grab evidence, before Kash Patel’s goons can swoop in and obfuscate things.

I don’t want to give too much advice about running in a place I’ve never been to and don’t know anything about.

But I think the general points from my article “How the Center-Left Can Come Back in 2028” apply. What I’m saying here is that there are some stances that cost Democrats with swing voters but that Democratic Party primary voters really strongly believe in. There are certain kinds of places where I think it’s smart for Democrats to moderate on those issues even though primary voters don’t like it and simply plead political pragmatism. But in a presidential race, electability only takes you so far, and you do have to win the primary. So what a presidential candidate really needs is a list of moderations that do well with swing voters but also with Democratic Party primary voters.

Of course, a local race in San Bruno is not a presidential primary. The dynamic applies, though, because you’re looking to find some opportunities to hit the NIMBY incumbent from the right without sacrificing appeals to partisanship in a town where Democrats outnumber Republicans. From the piece, citing work by David Broockman and Josh Kalla, the two moderations that are relevant to local government and popular with Democratic primary voters are:

  • Punishing people who commit property crimes like shoplifting

  • Merit pay for public school teachers

I don’t know to what extent shoplifting is a practical problem in San Bruno. I can tell you that over the past couple of weeks, the CVS near my house has started leaving most of the secure shelving they installed a couple of years ago unlocked (at least during daytime hours, maybe it’s locked up during the graveyard shift), and it’s a huge quality-of-life upgrade. I would really recommend taking this issue on to anyone who’s in a community where people can’t just grab soap off the shelf.

The merit pay thing, meanwhile, just happens to be the education issue that they polled. I suspect you would see similar results for a range of other “normie” education policy stances, like “kids who misbehave should be disciplined” and “the smart kids should take an advanced math class.” On a rank-and-file level it’s really Democrats, much more than Republicans, who are idealistic about education and really believe in its importance and have sort of normal ideas about what a good school should look like. It’s a strange narrow elite inside graduate schools of education and weird leftist blocs inside teachers’ unions who’ve imposed a lot of weird pedagogical fads that have very little support.

Again, depending on the town this may not apply at all. But those are the two areas where I think there’s likely to be the most juice in a “common sense” approach even in a progressive area.

lwdlyndale: Do you think the Dems should call for a national system of limits for online gambling? If so what should the policy be?

I want to draw a distinction here. My view is that ubiquitous legal gambling is bad for America and that there was no actual problem with the gambling status quo as it existed in, say, 1996. We were not living through some kind of costly “war on gambling” that was diverting resources from more critical law-enforcement tasks, and the prisons weren’t full of people who’d been busted running underground poker games. Enforcement was pretty low-key, people who wanted to bet on sports casually made bets with friends, there was a small black market, and Las Vegas and Atlantic City had lucrative casino industries because legal gambling was rare. On the merits, I would favor doing a ton to roll back legal gambling.

But should “the Dems” come out for this?

I think that depends what you mean. I don’t think this is a top five or top 10 or necessarily even top 20 significant issue in American life. It’s something that I think about a lot because I watch a lot of sports with my son and feel like I need to remind him three times a week not to get sucked into online sports gambling. But I want to hold myself on issues that I am interested in to the same standard I hold progressives to. If Chuck Schumer and Hakeem Jeffries hold a joint press conference tomorrow saying that Democrats want to crack down on online gambling, does that help Josh Turek and Mary Peltola and Bobby Pulido and my other favorite frontline candidates? I’d be open to persuasion on this point, but I’m pretty skeptical. I don’t think this addresses winnable voters’ doubts about the Democratic Party, and I think it might alienate some winnable voters and mobilize even more industry money against the Dems.

Which isn’t to say all Democrats should be gambling enthusiasts either. Say some of these people win. They’re going to have to actually do something in Congress. And a great thing for a frontline member to do is to get involved in some bipartisan bills. I think legal gambling has gone way too far, and I know a non-zero number of conservatives agree with me, so there is probably room for some bipartisan gambling bills. I’d love to see smart legislators get involved in that kind of thing. Then, depending on what the proposal looks like, I’ll be thrilled to consider it on the merits and also to see what the polling looks like.

Long story short, I think the bar for “party leadership takes up a new cause” should be pretty high. I’m serious when I say that Democrats need fewer big ideas not more. The party’s problem right now is that it’s struggling to set priorities and to construct a large enough tent. I’m very skeptical about putting new stuff on the agenda. Artificial intelligence is popping so much this fall that I don’t think it can possibly be evaded, but gambling doesn’t strike me as rising to that tier.

Taylor: After reading Hayek’s Road to Serfdom, do you think FDR’s legacy needs to be re-evaluated for his impact on the Great Depression?

Growing up, my history teachers always talked about him as if he got us out the Great Depression and “saved capitalism” from itself.

I think it’s well-documented that Hoover and the Federal Reserve made significant mistakes to contribute to the severity of the depression, but it seems like FDR’s own failures are often reframed as successes. The depression was far longer than previous ones, and, even from a Keynesian perspective, he didn’t engage in the kind of stimulus needed until WWII.

F.D.R.’s handling of the Depression was pretty flawed, but it was also a lot better than Herbert Hoover’s or than what we saw from Weimar Germany or Third Republic France, so I think his legacy is secure. But we should be clear that this is grading on a curve.

“Muddling through in an okay-but-not-great way” is in fact a lot better than failing catastrophically. And then, of course, F.D.R. followed it up with winning World War II. So I think he deserves his place in the Great Presidents Pantheon. But we shouldn’t understand that to mean “F.D.R.’s economic policies were amazingly good.” They were good compared with most of the other leading ideas on the table at the time, but we could have done a lot better.
