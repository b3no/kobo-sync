Reaction to the Investment Summit, EU Associate Membership & opposition positioning

Kyla Ronellenfitsch
Sep 22
 
READ IN APP
 

On the weekend, I released data showing how the public is reacting to last week’s events: the government’s investment summit and Carney’s move to create closer ties with the EU. I promised more to come… so here is your MORE. (btw if you missed that data, don’t fret, it’s included here as well with more detail).

Last week’s events have broken through. 8 in 10 Canadians heard at least something about attempts to make Canada the first associate member of the EU (branding pending), and 2 in 3 have heard something about the Canada Investment Summit.

As a frame of reference, last November, 28% of Canadians reported hearing “a great deal” about the federal budget, 20% had heard “a great deal” about Chris d’Entremont crossing the floor, and in January, 46% of Canadians heard about Trump’s threats to annex Greenland. So last week’s events are big political stories, not necessarily the biggest imaginable (ty America), but big nonetheless.

Benchmarking: News Story Awareness
Q: How much have you read, seen or heard about each of the following federal government initiatives/news stories?

So far, much of the commentary around last week has been pretty black-and-white. Might I suggest at least a tinge of grey?

Initial reactions to both initiatives have certainly been positive — among those with an opinion. The EU move has net approval of +37, and the investment summit has +42; about 1 in 4 Canadians strongly approve of each initiative, while few strongly disapprove. These are big wins for the government, full stop. But I don’t think we should blow past the 21% of Canadians withholding an opinion on the EU associate membership, or the 26% who aren’t sure about the Investment Summit. These issues, particularly the proposed EU associate membership, are live and should be treated as such. The government will need to keep actively managing the EU file. The details matter (obviously).

Approval of Trade Summit & EU Associate Membership
Q: Do you approve or disapprove of the government’s management of each of the following?

Two major policy announcements came out of the Investment Summit: the Productivity Mega Deduction and opening airports to private investment. Respondents were provided with explanations of each policy, and in both cases, opinion leans positive but is quite soft (many somewhat support/oppose or aren’t sure).

  • 53% of Canadians like the idea of the Productivity Mega Deduction. 21% are opposed.

  • Fewer, but still a large plurality (44%), support opening airports to private investment. 32% of Canadians oppose the idea, while 24% aren’t sure.

The deduction receives broad support across the political spectrum, although a particularly large proportion of NDP voters aren’t sure. While Liberals tend to support opening airports to private investment, this proposition receives considerable opposition from NDP and CPC voters. Among these voters, twice as many of strongly oppose the policy as strongly support it. If there’s an area that should worry the government in this data set, it’s this. Potentially manageable, but they’re starting with hints of a problem.

Reaction to Government Policy Change
Q: The federal government recently announced a few new policies. Do you support or oppose these changes?

I watched the opposition leaders’ reactions to the Canada Investment Summit closely. My personal read was that some of the messaging was good – I could see where some of the critiques would have some public opinion footing. But with the Prime Minister’s sky-high popularity, I was curious whether these messages would fully land, or if voters would be reluctant to hear a message from either Pierre Poilievre, a polarizing figure, or Avi Lewis, who is largely unknown. I set up an experiment: all respondents were shown the same paraphrased version of Pierre Poilievre’s reaction to the Investment Summit. Half were told the statement came from Poilievre and the Conservatives, while the other half were told only that 'critics of the government' had made the argument. I repeated this process with Lewis and the NDP. Both descriptions are included below.

In response to the Canada Investment Summit, [Pierre Poilievre and Conservatives/critics of the government] have argued: Canadians have had 18 months of speeches, summits and symbolic signing ceremonies, with no impact for everyday Canadians. It’s all fine and good for Carney to encourage investment in Canada, but instead of changing the policies that are making life unaffordable, he has only made things worse: Canada has the slowest growth in the G7, we’ve lost a trillion dollars in net investment, no new oil pipelines have been built, and food inflation and household debt have skyrocketed.

In response to the Canada Investment Summit, [Avi Lewis and the NDP/critics of the government] have argued: Carney promised to make Canada more independent, but his strategy relies on Wall Street and Trump-supporting billionaires to buy up Canadian resources while doing nothing for regular Canadians struggling with the cost of living and affordable housing. Instead of doubling down on foreign investment and fossil fuels, Canada should tax excess corporate profits and extreme wealth and invest that money in Canadians — building affordable housing, lowering costs and fighting climate change.

Both the CPC and NDP critiques of the investment summit are reasonably well received. More people agree with each message than disagree, although the Conservative attack is better received than the NDP’s critique.

Both leaders create drag on their message, although the effect is considerably more pronounced for Poilievre. When attributed to Poilievre, 47% of Canadians agree with his position. This increases to 54% without Poilievre. Disagreement is also lower without Poilievre (-9), including a 5-pt decline in those who “strongly disagree.” 49% of Canadians agree with the NDP’s position when not attributed to Lewis and the NDP. Overall agreement is 2 pts less when attributed to Lewis, although strong agreement declines by 4 pts.

One caveat: while these are fairly strong positionings, I didn’t test (regrettably) if anyone had actually heard these arguments outside of the survey. Carney pulls a lot of focus, so I’m not sure if opposition positioning, however strong the message might be, actually landed. If a tree falls in the forest, etc.

Opposition Critique of Investment Summit
Q: Do you agree or disagree?

I didn’t run the same experiment, but I did test Poilievre’s reaction to the government’s attempt to become an associate member of the EU. It follows a similar pattern to his critique of the investment summit: 45% of Canadians agree, 34% disagree, and 20% aren’t sure.

Conservative Critique of EU Partnership
Q: In response to reports that Carney is trying to form closer ties to the European Union, Pierre Poilievre said:

I tested two additional arguments – one similar to comments Poilievre has delivered and the other that’s… the stuff of Twitter. 45% of Canadians share Poilievre’s view that we must block closer ties to the EU because of immigration, while 37% disagree (18% not sure). 41% of Canadians agree “we’re now seeing Carney’s true motive – to allow a globalist takeover of Canada,” including 60% of Conservative voters. I’ll be honest, I’m not sure how much the non-super-online would even know what that means. But the Twittered Right (™) certainly does – 79% of Conservative voters who access Twitter/X agree, including 56% who strongly agree. This compares to the 60% who agree/33% strongly agree among Conservatives not on X.

Immigration & the Globalist Takeover
Q: Do you agree or disagree with the following statements?

The most resonant messages tested are two that I actually haven’t seen any politician make, at least not explicitly. Carney has seemed content to show, not tell, how he contrasts with Poilievre, and… I’d say it’s been working:

  1. 59% of Canadians agree: We’re now seeing why experience matters. Carney is putting Canada at the centre of the world stage because of his connections.

  2. 54% agree: There’s no way Poilievre could build the kind of partnerships Carney has been creating for Canada. He doesn’t have the economic experience or the connections.

The Carney/Poilievre Contrast
Q: Do you agree or disagree with the following statements?

4 in 10 Conservative voters also agree: we’re now seeing why experience matters. Carney is putting Canada at the centre of the world stage because of his connections. If the next election is similar to the last — oof.

But, to Liberals, I also wouldn’t be too quick to disregard Poilievre’s immigration positioning. 1 in 3 Liberal voters agree with him. It’s possible that this is an attack without a target – that the associate membership will have little to do with immigration or worker mobility. But the government has done a lot to communicate a reassertion of control over the immigration system. I wouldn’t let Carney’s popularity give permission to be cavalier about this.

Partisan Interpretation: Carney on the World Stage
Q: Do you agree or disagree with the following statements?

To recap: these are live issues. Roughly 20-25% of Canadians don’t have a view on this issue set. While the public leans supportive of the government, opposition positioning still has salience. The cost of living remains issue #1, and, as I’ve written before, anti-corporate sentiment is real. While it’s far too early for the Liberals to declare an absolute victory, the signs are good, and the Carney-Poilievre contrast is great. Proceed, as always, with care.
