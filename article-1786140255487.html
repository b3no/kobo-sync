Theater is weird, and that’s the point.

In which I come out as a total theater nerd

Halina Bennet
Aug 7
∙
Paid
 
READ IN APP
 

A few weeks ago I saw a student production of “Twelfth Night” in D.C.

Though it was far from my favorite of the versions I’ve seen, I sat happily (albeit uncomfortably — no AMC recliners at the Shakespeare Theatre Company) for three hours, often pulled from the moment back to other stagings I’ve watched or performed in. I was tickled watching Viola deceive Orsino and Olivia for what has to be at least the millionth time in the play’s history, struck by the incredible fact that people have been staging this exact script in some form since it was written more than 420 years ago.

There were people in London in 1602 watching the very same love triangle as I was in 2026.

I was just the latest in the long line of people who’ve seen this play, and the version I saw was also just the latest in the long history of the play’s stagings — a history I hope will continue long after artificial intelligence has taken over everything else and we’re all living on Mars or something.

That’s the thing about live theater: Weird though it may be, it’s magical in a way that movies aren’t. A movie is a finished product that will be the same any time anyone sees it, forever. A theater production is an ephemeral event that happens once, for one particular audience, on one particular night. That impermanence lends the form its wonderful weirdness and is one of the most magical things we get to experience in an otherwise optimized life.

Every night in the theater, the cast and crew attempt to pull off a feat in front of an audience in real time, without cuts or edits or do-overs. Nailing jokes, stunts, and songs is, for the cast and crew, a rush and, for the audience, a thrill.

Sometimes things don’t go as planned.

When Lesli Margherita got a splinter through the leg while landing a split as Mrs. Wormwood in “Matilda” on Broadway, her understudy stepped up for the second act and the episode became an example used by my middle-school theater teacher of when the show must go on. Last fall, I saw the first preview of “Anna Christie” at St. Ann’s Warehouse in Brooklyn, and midway through the first act’s climax, just as Michelle Williams had the audience in rapt attention, the entire set collapsed and the play had to pause.

Being 10 feet from Williams while she did the thing she’s expert at was already astonishing. Sharing the experience of complete and utter dysfunction was exhilarating and a reminder of how delicate — how human — live theater is. During the brief interruption, the show’s director, Thomas Kail (Williams’s husband), answered questions in the lobby. He told us about the rehearsal process and his plans for working out the play’s kinks. When the set was put back together, we went back in and everything continued on, almost as if nothing had happened. There really is nothing quite like live theater.

Film special effects and A.I. can produce out-of-this-world spectacle, but that spectacle, to me, is no match for the adrenaline of watching humans right in front of you pull off something that seems nearly impossible.

A bow and a standing ovation are the collective sigh of relief and celebration that everyone who witnessed that particular production got through the high-wire act together, whether everything went according to plan or collapsed entirely. That collective exhale can’t exist in the same way for film, because nothing is actually at risk. Whatever is on the screen happened months or years before and was shaped by an exacting editing process. It will be the same the next million times it is watched.

There’s comfort in that, but no thrill.

This is why theater feels more connective and more emotional than film for many of the people who love it, and why the people who love it do so passionately. (I’m under no delusion that more people love theater than film — I’m not sure I even love one more than the other, and I know plenty of people who just don’t like theater.) Theatergoers, rather than receiving a finished product after having been excluded from the process entirely, are present for part of the process itself. The audience and the actors feed off each other’s energy and create something together; the actors can feel your presence, and you can feel them feel you.

I can watch the same play dozens of times, with or without the same cast, and not get bored because the point is what happens during that production. Some plays have, essentially, groupies who make a point to see every single revival. Meanwhile, there are few movies I’m willing to sit through twice, even if I enjoyed them the first time. The comfort of sameness has its use, but it’s the opposite of why I return to the theater.

So this is perhaps in line with Matt’s point that proshots — professionally filmed recordings of productions — are confusing. They’re antithetical to the magic of live theater about which I just waxed poetic. These videos capture a thing that is meant to be temporary and make it available forever.

Still, a movie adaptation of “West Side Story” is a completely different thing from a filmed version of the stage musical, and proshots serve an entirely different purpose from movie adaptations: They offer audiences a diluted but undeniable dose of the magic of theater.

I grew up in Denver with plentiful access to live theater, but I was far from Broadway and the West End, where the buzziest new shows were staged. Recordings of those performances were as close as I could get to those shows, and while watching them with my friends on our school’s Chromebooks certainly wasn’t a true substitute for the real thing, it was the next best thing. A proshot is a recording of the event, not a version of a product, and is made for an in-crowd who would die to see “Hamilton” on Broadway but will settle for the chance to see Leslie Odom Jr. in a recording of a performance they didn’t get to experience themselves.

As Matt wrote today, the theater industry clearly has an insularity problem at the moment. I’ve often seen plays that were built for an in-group audience rather than for everyone, and I understand why they’re unappealing to most people — they’re often unappealing to me.

But still, the event is worth preserving, just as a movie’s product is worth watching. The industry may be flawed as a business, but theater remains one of the most vital and alive art forms we have.

Weekend letter of recommendation:

  • I watched the movie “Best in Show” over the weekend for the first time in years, and it’s worth revisiting if you haven’t recently. Catherine O’Hara, Parker Posey, Jane Lynch, and Eugene Levy in particular are dynamite.

  • Also worth revisiting is “13 Going on 30.” Watching it as someone who is not far from 30, I’m struck by how creepy the whole thing is. Also, maybe I’m age-blind, but the characters definitely seem older than 30. Maybe I’m just distracted by their really nice apartments.

  • Go see some live theater. It might be terrible, but the experience will probably be fun anyway. Or you could always watch a proshot.

  • My mom just recommended that I read “Isola” by Allegra Goodman. She said she “couldn’t put it down,” so maybe I’ll give it a read this weekend. My dad, on the other hand, is reading (not for the first time) “The Greek Plays: Sixteen Plays by Aeschylus, Sophocles, and Euripides,” translations edited by Mary Lefkowitz and James Romm. Talk about a theater nerd.
