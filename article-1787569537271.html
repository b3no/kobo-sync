Four things I changed my mind on after a year of polling

What Americans really think about COVID, AI, Twitter, and the elites

Lakshya Jain
Aug 24
∙
Paid
 
READ IN APP
 

When The Argument‘s editor-in-chief Jerusalem Demsas and I first began talking about building a polling program, we were both obsessed with one problem: Issue polling was dominated by interest groups.

So many of the public opinion polls you have probably seen over the past few decades have been written and released by people who have both financial and professional incentives to reach a predetermined answer. That means they will often write questions in biased ways and even withhold negative findings from the final published results.

This has polluted the information environment. Agencies put out surveys with loaded wording on everything from climate change to the Middle East, and prominent media outlets uncritically run with them.

We wanted to do something different. So, we made three decisions:

  1. We were going to create a panel of experts across the ideological spectrum to review our language for accuracy, bias, and effectiveness.

  2. We would release everything we polled — no matter how the results impacted our preferred political ideology.

  3. We would dive deep into issues, testing from multiple different angles so we could try to figure out what was really going on.

Over the last year, we’ve conducted 11 national surveys on topics ranging from artificial intelligence to personal health (you can see every single one of those surveys here). These surveys provide us with an immensely rich dataset on how Americans really think, which forces us to recalibrate our view of public opinion and reevaluate bad priors.

In that vein, I wanted to share some of the findings that surprised me the most over the past year.

1. Everyone’s moved on from COVID-19

I came to The Argument thinking that Democrats still had to reckon with a deep, persistent set of COVID-induced scars for the electorate.

In some cases, this might be indirectly true — for instance, Democrats face a staggering deficit on crime with critical portions of the electorate, and part of that is likely due to the COVID-era crime spike. But when it comes to topics like school closures and lockdowns, there’s little evidence that Democrats are paying a sustained penalty for any perceived excesses of the early 2020s. In fact, when we polled this late last year, people gave Biden and the Democrats better grades than they gave Trump and Republicans.

Voter grades on COVID-19
Do you approve or disapprove of how the following people or institutions handled the COVID-19 pandemic?

It’s not as if there were no lasting effects from COVID in public opinion, though. Public health experts have taken a deep and sustained hit, with 45% of voters saying that they “often hide or distort information for political reasons.” While nobody tested our exact wording pre-pandemic, the closest counterparts we have suggest a deep decline from the pre-COVID consensus; in a 2019 survey done by Pew Research Center, trust in medical scientists was at 87%.

Anti-vaccination sentiment is also worryingly high, with 42% of registered voters claiming that the side effects of vaccines outweigh the benefits. That shift is mainly thanks to a deep decline in Republican support for vaccinations. And voters in our survey did not want another set of COVID measures in the event of another pandemic; they believe such measures may have been necessary the first time, but they also think that once was quite enough.

But that’s different from whether Democrats are paying a political price for their COVID policy. And there’s little evidence to support that hypothesis. In fact, even after all the hullabaloo over mandatory vaccinations and masking, Democrats enjoyed a large and clear edge over Republicans on personal health. That is not what you would expect in a world where Democrats are continuing to experience an asymmetric partisan backlash to COVID.

Democrats hold an edge on personal health
Thinking about personal health, which party's approach do you think better reflects your values? (“Personal health” refers to your own physical fitness, nutrition, mental health, and lifestyle choices, and the role that government or society should play in supporting or facilitating them.)

It’s not like this topic has gone away from the news; recently, former National Institute of Allergy and Infectious Diseases director Anthony Fauci’s top aide, David Morens, pleaded guilty to concealing COVID records. And yet, Democrats haven’t dipped at all on the generic ballot since then — their lead has grown from D+6.2 to D+6.5 in Nate Silver’s tracker.

It doesn’t seem to have changed public opinion on the issue either; after Fauci’s July 29 Senate testimony, The Economist/YouGov asked Americans “Which party do you trust more to handle vaccines and public health?”. Democrats led Republicans 40% to 26%.

Some people might be surprised by this. But there’s a long history of voters moving past scandals and evaluating parties based on the here and now, rather than what happened in the past. After all, Americans moved past January 6, Watergate, and Iran-Contra fairly quickly, and Democrats were never as underwater during COVID as Republicans were during said scandals.

There’s no reason politics should only work in one direction.

2. Twitter really is not real life.

Before removing social media from my phone and decreasing my posting frequency by a factor of 10 in 2026, I used to get a lot of my news from Twitter and other social media applications. Removing them from my life has done an immense amount of good for my mental well-being and my ability to understand what the average person thinks about anything.

I don’t think we properly understand just how bad social media is — and what a skewed picture it provides of the world. As an example, in the immediate wake of the January Minneapolis shootings, there was only one cohort in which positive views of Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE) broke 40%: people who got their news from Twitter.

How audiences on different news platforms view ICE
The favorability ratings of Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE), split by news platform

It’s possible, of course, that these are pure selection effects and that Twitter is not actually causing people to think differently — unsurprisingly, Twitter is also the platform that has the most favorable view of Trump. It’s actually the only news source where his approval splits evenly; everywhere else, he is noticeably underwater (including cable TV, local TV, and podcasts).

Trump approval across different news platforms
Do you approve or disapprove of the way Donald Trump is handling his job as president?

But we also have evidence that Republicans on Twitter are very different from Republicans on other platforms, which compounds the information/“vibe” distortion you might experience on the platform.

For instance, Twitter Republicans are twice as likely as other Republicans to view journalists as elite — 24% of Republicans on Twitter classify journalists as part of the elite, whereas just 12% of Republicans not on Twitter do. And they are far less likely to defect from Trump: Our combined 2026 polling data shows 90% of Republicans on Twitter approving of his performance, compared with just 83% of Republicans not on Twitter.

It’s hard to identify causality, but that’s part of why I think there probably is something to the theory that Twitter is making people slightly more conservative than they would otherwise be. At the very least, though, there’s clear evidence that people on Twitter are really weird in a way that most Americans are not.

So, if you’re using it as a primary part of your information environment, you might not understand how Americans really see things today.

3. Wait. People actually like OpenAI and Big Tech?

You can’t go a second without seeing an uproar over data centers. So imagine my shock when I saw our poll on artificial intelligence and found that a plurality of people actually liked OpenAI — and that they actually liked Big Tech companies as a whole.

Most Americans like Big Tech platforms

To be clear, voters continue to have major qualms with some of the consequences of AI, including widespread concerns about societal unrest and displacement: they fear AI could increase economic inequality, they blame it for spiking electricity prices, and they fear that it could replace humans in several industry sectors. Voters see the problems that AI could cause.

Voters are worried about the consequences of AI
Within the next 5-10 years, how likely do you think each of the following scenarios is?

They’re also extraordinarily skeptical of many of the potential positive effects of AI. Very few people think it will actually improve education or create an immense abundance of resources (they are more positive on the potential for medical breakthroughs).

Voters are skeptical about the benefits of AI
Within the next 5-10 years, how likely do you think each of the following scenarios is?

Yet, at the same time, voters say they actually like the Big Tech companies that could induce most of these negative changes. Google and Amazon are extraordinarily popular, and OpenAI is net positive with the electorate.

Why? I think my colleague Milan Singh had the best explanation: People like companies that competently deliver products or services that they enjoy.

Companies like Amazon are associated with cheap goods and convenience, rather than a spike in electricity costs. ChatGPT condenses an immense amount of information at a time when search technologies across the internet are falling apart. Google powers the web and everyone’s email.

At the end of the day, that’s winning out right now in public opinion. Whether that remains the case is yet to be seen.

4. The right-wing theory of the elite isn’t clicking with voters

Whether on Twitter or the Opinion section of The Washington Post, pundits have spilled an extraordinary amount of digital ink talking about the backlash to left-wing “elite culture,” which arguably peaked during “Woke 1.0” in 2020. By this definition, voters had developed a deep and serious mistrust of left-wing elites, who forced their values onto the rest of the country with their cultural power.

I’ll confess that I gave this theory some consideration. It certainly seemed plausible, at the very least. So I was surprised when our polling found that the public was far more aligned with the left-wing theory of the elites, which indexes more heavily on economic power. That means billionaires and business executives count as elite, but college professors and journalists don’t.

Everyone agrees:

It’s not like the picture is uniformly rosy for the “left-wing” elites, as some on the right may call them. For instance, 49% of voters really do think that journalists are more liberal than the country as a whole, compared with 11% who think they’re more conservative. And voters are evenly split on whether the media can even be trusted to cover the news fairly.

Do Americans trust the media to cover the news fairly?
Generally speaking, do you trust the national media — such as TV news programs and national newspapers — to report the news in a fair and unbiased manner?

But that’s different from subscribing to the Manhattan Institute’s views on who actually holds power in society. Overwhelmingly, voters were in agreement that the elites were those with financial power, rather than any perceived cultural power.

Part of this might be because “Woke 1.0” is over. The power of the social justice movements from the activist left has long since faded from public consciousness. But the strategic retreat by Democrats also means that Republicans can’t rerun the culture-war tactics that may have helped them in 2024. Today, the battle is fought on Donald Trump and economics — and neither issue is great for the Republicans right now.
