Medicare for All, one organ at a time

A Nixon-era blueprint for health care expansion

Eli Richman
Sep 14
∙
Paid
 
READ IN APP
 

The words “Medicare for All” may not immediately bring to mind President Richard Nixon, but perhaps they should. Unlike any of the contemporary luminaries staking their names on expanding Medicare access, Nixon and the 92nd Congress actually did something about it.

In 1972, Nixon signed into law the renal disease entitlement program, which provided Medicare coverage to nearly all patients with end-stage renal disease (the most severe form of kidney disease), regardless of age. While I understand why many may be reluctant to follow in Tricky Dick’s footsteps, it turns out this is an ideal model for expanding Medicare and stabilizing U.S. health insurance more generally.

Disease-specific coverage targets precious social-safety-net dollars precisely at the most expensive patients and can even lower their per-patient costs thanks to Medicare’s bargaining power. Plus, by removing particularly expensive patients from the commercial insurance pool, it stabilizes costs for everyone else along the way.

Share

“Medicare for All” advocates should take note. While Sen. Bernie Sanders became nearly synonymous with the phrase thanks to his extraordinarily generous version of the proposal during the 2016 presidential primary, other politicians trying to get in good with the left have increasingly used the slogan to signal more limited expansions.

Abdul El-Sayed, the Democratic candidate for U.S. Senate in Michigan, has labeled his health care proposal “Medicare for All,” even though it would not actually provide Medicare to all.¹ Slow Boring and The Argument columnist Matt Yglesias argued that this could create a permission structure for other candidates to push for more measured policies under the same label.

Medicare for All fans can certainly view policies such as a buy-in or a lower eligibility age as the opening salvo in a process that will ultimately insure everyone. But these proposals should interest those who see a role for commercial insurance as well. Both would end up putting more expensive patients on Medicare, which would take the burden off commercial insurance. With lower claims costs, commercial insurers could lower premiums for the majority of Americans.

So why target a specific organ or disease instead of lowering eligibility or offering a buy-in? The main reason is that the latter options use imperfect proxies to identify expensive patients, otherwise known as sick people. Yes, 55- to 64-year-olds have worse health, on average, than younger people, but expanding the program by lowering the eligibility age would sweep in lots of people in good health as well. Yes, a Medicare buy-in would likely be most sought by those with health challenges, but any healthy person can also buy in.

Now, giving people health care coverage is hardly the worst thing you can do to them. But a healthy patient — someone who pays premiums without using many health care resources — is someone insurance companies need to keep their books in order. And in a world of scarcity like ours, we want our in-demand federal dollars going where they’re most needed.

That’s why disease entitlement, such as Medicare’s kidney coverage, is such a good option. It is a perfect proxy for sickness. By definition, there are no healthy patients with end-stage renal disease.

So, if we’re looking for other options to expand Medicare, I think we would be much better off continuing with the approach we started in 1972: expanding it one disease at a time.

Designing such a policy necessarily means choosing which chronic disease we should cover next. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention has identified nine of the costliest chronic health conditions, and a quick look at total health care spending attributable to each of those diseases gives us three obvious candidates.

Cardiovascular disease and cancer appear nearly tied as the most expensive chronic diseases, followed more distantly by diabetes. But if we look back at our success story, kidney entitlement, we can see why diabetes is the clear front-runner.

Why diabetes?

End-stage renal disease had three main features that made it a good starting point for Medicare expansion:

  1. It struck patients more or less randomly, which made it difficult to blame them for their own illness.

  2. Outside of receiving a kidney transplant, it was irreversible, so Medicare would not need to be in the business of rooting out cured patients and disenrolling them.

  3. It ensured that affected patients’ health care costs would far exceed those of a healthy person, making such patients borderline uninsurable in the days of medical underwriting.

Only diabetes fits all of these criteria, and only Type 1 diabetes fits them without caveats.

Cardiovascular disease, Type 2 diabetes, and several cancers have behavioral contributors, such as unhealthy diets, smoking, drinking, and inactivity. I don’t personally think this creates any moral hazards in terms of encouraging those behaviors, but many conservatives would beg to differ. Even before the paternalistic MAHA right ascended, Republicans would routinely invoke personal responsibility against proposals to aid sick people — even when talking generally about illnesses that have zero behavioral component.

During the Obamacare repeal debates in 2017, for instance, then-Rep. Mo Brooks argued insurance should be cheaper for people who have “done the things to keep their bodies healthy.” His colleague from Kansas, then-Rep. (now Sen.) Roger Marshall, suggested poor people “don’t want health care and aren’t going to take care of themselves.”

Cancer has a secondary complication: Thanks to the miracles of modern medicine, it has become far more common for cancer patients to beat their disease. A person in full remission from cancer has health care costs more similar to those of a healthy person, which suggests they should be in the commercial market. But do we really want to be in the business of disenrolling them from Medicare?

Now, Medicare does have a disenrollment procedure for end-stage renal disease patients who receive a kidney transplant. But far fewer patients receive a kidney transplant every year than beat cancer, and when they do, their transplant is a well-documented, inarguable event. “Beating cancer” is far less well-defined, and conditioning patients’ coverage on continued illness would provide an understandable, but problematic, incentive to hide the fact that they had improved.

But none of these complications accompany Type 1 diabetes. The disease affects people of all ages (often diagnosed in children, teens, and young adults). It appears entirely unassociated with lifestyle. It is irreversible, barring exceedingly rare pancreas transplants. And despite coming third in overall health care spending, it tops the per-patient list of most expensive chronic diseases, demanding huge amounts of health care resources to treat a not-insignificant 2.1 million Americans.

Moreover, Medicare has already targeted a major cost in diabetes care by sharply negotiating down the price of insulin. As a result, while care for commercially insured Type 1 diabetics costs patients and insurance companies around $25 billion total per year,² we would expect that number to drop once those patients gain Medicare coverage.

I don’t think I need to waste pixels explaining why such a policy would be good for Type 1 diabetics. But this would not primarily be for their benefit. Instead, the much bigger group of beneficiaries would be the commercially insured public, who would no longer be on the hook for Type 1 diabetes treatment.

Make insurance insurance again

The increasing unaffordability of health insurance is no secret. Since the Affordable Care Act (ACA) became law, and particularly as health care costs have risen in recent years, the public has been hard hit with surges in premiums.

In some ways, this was the ACA working as intended: With more care being delivered more regularly to more patients (especially sicker ones who couldn’t previously get insurance), of course premiums would rise to compensate. After all, people with chronic diseases and mental health conditions account for 90% of all U.S. health care costs. The ACA included several cost controls to limit the damage, but many of those, such as the individual mandate, were either effectively repealed or proved less effective than intended. That’s how we’ve arrived in a situation where health insurance payments are rivaling housing in some families’ budgets.

Insurance is not supposed to work this way, conceptually. Insurance is meant to cover unexpected costs, not anticipated costs.

For instance, house fires are uncommon but very expensive for the unlucky few who experience them. So everyone pays a little bit in homeowners insurance, and that collective pot can pay for the unexpected costs when and where they arise. Since no one knows who will be affected by the next house fire, this is considered a good deal by most consumers even if they never get unlucky enough to need a payout from the insurance company.

But you don’t bill your homeowners insurance for replacement HVAC filters. Anticipated costs, such as those faced by chronically ill patients, are not typically considered a good fit for an insurance structure. This is why conservatives sometimes argue that such patients should not be in that structure.

But no one wants to argue for going back to the pre-Obamacare days when insurers routinely refused to cover people with “preexisting conditions.” So instead, you get conservative praise of insurance schemes such as so-called high-risk pools, which separate expensive, sicker patients from the commercial insurance market.

The theory is that because many chronically ill patients reliably have bank-breaking health care costs, insuring them in the same pool as largely healthy patients makes the whole situation look more like a cross-subsidy than actual insurance. If you take the chronically ill out of the equation, insurance could go back to being, well, insurance again: a safety net if you happen to get hit by a truck (or with an exploding appendix).

The problem is that the astronomical costs of treating chronically ill patients don’t just go away, and conservatives don’t actually want to foot the bill for the added expense. This has historically left high-risk pools underfunded, with costs falling on the people they were supposed to cover.

That’s why putting these patients on Medicare makes so much sense. Here we have a high-risk pool that’s already set up, funded by nondiscretionary federal dollars, and, crucially, large enough to negotiate prices.

Replacing a distributed cost with a distributed cost

Of course, the big limitation for any Medicare expansion is that it must be paid for. I’m skeptical about our ability to just hand the bill to Elon Musk, as progressives seem to want. And our budget is already in rough enough shape that if we adopted this policy, tax hikes would probably be warranted.

If all we were doing was taking health care costs that were distributed through premiums and instead distributing them through taxes, that would not make a big difference in terms of overall cost of living. But Medicare is actually able to deliver cost savings because of its monopsony power: Since the federal government is nearly the sole purchaser of all end-stage renal disease and geriatric care,³ it is able to bargain on price with providers of that care.

Some providers aren’t happy with that arrangement and therefore refuse to take Medicare patients at all. And that is a concern; monopsonies — markets where there is only one buyer — can be more dangerous than monopolies. But Medicare knows its own strength and sets prices modestly, and enough providers are satisfied with the trade-off (they get a reliable influx of patients with little worry of denied payments) that we have typically had plenty of participating providers.

Not to mention, the bigger the Medicare population, the harder it is for providers to look elsewhere.

Medicare for All or Medicare for some?

There’s one big question with all Medicare expansion proposals: Are they the beginning or the end? And I don’t mean to sound disingenuous by simultaneously touting disease entitlement as a patch for commercial insurance and as a model that will eventually expand to cover all chronic diseases.

But the answer is genuinely ambiguous. After all, we’re not talking about expanding Medicare for its own sake; we’re talking about it because our health care system is a profound market failure. If we can address the failure without putting everyone on Medicare, great! If not, then maybe we need to keep expanding.

The truth is, I cannot tell you without more data how much Medicare coverage we would need to normalize commercial health insurance. A lot of money is spent on Type 1 diabetes; maybe that would be enough. Or maybe we would need to do another round and cover Type 2 diabetes or cardiovascular disease after all. Or maybe we would need to start going down the list, covering people with arthritis, Alzheimer’s, or epilepsy.

But the great thing about taking an incremental approach is that it’s incremental. It allows us to take a beat and evaluate how things are going before moving forward. If commercial insurance normalizes or it’s looking like it will be too expensive to cover the next disease, then we can just stop. If something goes wrong, such as ballooning care costs or shortages, we can more easily pull back.

Whatever happens, we won’t have upended the entire health care system in the process.
