What happened to the working-class husband?

The degree divide haunting American marriage.

Jordan McGillis and Cathy Reisenwitz

The panic over American family life has found its villain: The Girlboss. Like The Career Woman before her, today’s college-educated Girlboss, the theory goes, prioritizes work over family. As prominent conservative Katie Miller put it: “Our biological destiny is to have babies — not slave behind desks chasing careers while our civilization dies.”

Emma Waters, a Heritage Foundation policy analyst, argued even more bluntly that “’Girl-boss feminism’ has a body count — in birth rates.”

But is The Girlboss really to blame for declining fertility?

With girlbosses drawing ire from the political right amid a national birthrate collapse, Nobel laureate Claudia Goldin redirects attention to men, who have adapted unevenly to rising female educational attainment and economic agency. She’s justified in doing so.

Goldin has a knack for introducing pithy concepts that explain complex economic dynamics. Adding to a corpus of bangers that includes “greedy jobs” and “the quiet Revolution,” Goldin coined the phrases “dependable dads” and “disappointing duds” to describe a problem facing women weighing careers and children. She argues that for a college-educated woman, mate selection amid uncertainty — will he be a dependable dad or a disappointing dud? — puts her hard-earned wage premium at risk. Uncertainty begets delay, and delay begets, well, fewer kids.

But by focusing on women in the top half of the socioeconomic distribution and the dilemma they face in the marriage market, Goldin sidelines the women most exposed to disappointing male duds: those without bachelor’s degrees. As Marc Novicoff pointed out in a critique of Goldin in The Atlantic, these women “are shifting toward childlessness faster” than women with degrees.

If anything, Novicoff may be understating just how misleading the hand-wringing over college-educated women really is. In a new analysis of recent marriage and fertility data, we find that among college-educated American women, rates of motherhood and marriage are higher than they were a generation earlier. The real crisis lies among Americans with less education and income — the country’s socioeconomic bottom half.


Building on the methodology economists Melissa Kearney and Phillip Levine used in an April 2026 paper, we analyzed five-year birth cohorts of American women by measures of motherhood and marriage, separating those with and those without bachelor’s degrees. Specifically, we focused on the first crop of millennials, women born from 1980 to 1984 who have all passed 40. Comparing them with the cohort born a decade earlier (1970-1974), the heart of Gen X, reveals a host of troubling data.¹

While millennial women in both educational groups have had fewer kids on average than Gen Xers, non-college millennial women have fared much worse than their Gen X predecessors in numerous ways. They are not just having fewer kids but are increasingly missing out on family life altogether.

First, we looked at which women had any kids at all. Between the two cohorts, the share of women who had become mothers by age 40 fell by 2.6 percentage points among those without bachelor’s degrees and rose by 2.3 points among graduates.²

Among millennials alone, college graduation made very little difference in whether the woman became a mother — just 4 percentage points. The fact that 84.2% of non-college women and 80.1% of college women have become moms cuts against the millennial girlboss caricature.

The marriage rate shows an even starker divergence. In the Gen X cohort, 83.5% of non-college women had married by age 40, roughly at par with the 84.7% rate for college women (and nearly identical to the rate for non-college women born in earlier cohorts). The millennial cohort shows a devastating cleavage. While millennial college women married at an even higher rate — 86% — just 75% of their non-college peers have ever married by 40.

Examining marriage and motherhood together — still the preferred family outcome across all American classes — displays the divergence most clearly. At age 40 among the Gen X cohort, non-college women who had ever married and had at least one child led college-educated women (76.4% to 74%).

But among the millennial cohort, that flipped: 75% of college-educated women born in the early 1980s had both married and become mothers by 40. Just 68.1% of non-college women could say the same.

Disappointing duds

The diminishing family formation prospects of less-educated American women are downstream from the economic and social standing of non-college men.

Millennial men began looking for jobs in the early 2000s — a difficult time in the labor market, particularly for those without college degrees. Before they were even born, the American economy had begun its shift away from routine labor, and manufacturing employment had fallen from its 1970s peak. The economy no longer needed 20 million people working in manufacturing; instead, jobs in food service, cleaning, care work, and personal services took off, as did jobs that required a bachelor’s degree.

In earlier eras, many non-college men found their economic footing — and their contribution to a household — in industrial labor. Since then, globalization, automation, and increasing returns to education and skills have eroded the relative standing of non-college men.

Real median hourly wages for male production and nonsupervisory workers rose just 15.6% between 1980 and 2023. While data constraints preclude a direct male real-wage comparison with that era, the Economic Innovation Group (where one of us works) calculates that all workers’ average hourly earnings rose by a stonking 57.3 percent in the male-dominated labor market from 1950 to 1979.

The bleakest low stretch of low wage growth predated millennial men’s labor market arrival, yet nostalgia for the working-class golden age remains entrenched. In light of the returns to American workers in the postwar decades, our longing is understandable. Those 30 glorious years of wage growth established the concept of the working-class male breadwinner, a norm that still has a hold on our collective consciousness.

“[Non-college men] cannot, for the most part, get jobs that provide the status their father or grandfather had,” John Burn-Murdoch wrote of young men in North America and Europe in the Financial Times in August. “They know it, and so do their would-be partners.”

What their would-be partners, non-college women, also know is that non-college men are not making up the difference at home.

In Goldin’s dad-versus-dud framework, college women may hold off on having kids because they can’t be sure their husbands will share enough of the child care to let them maintain their careers. Statistically, it’s clear that college men are far more likely to share the child care and household burden than non-college men are. Burn-Murdoch’s analysis of American Time Use Survey data shows that in 2000, when the Gen X cohort was of typical marriage age, college and non-college men were almost indistinguishable in their tendency to contribute at home: Neither group did much relative to women. But over the past 25 years, college-educated fathers have more readily adopted an egalitarian ethic than their non-college counterparts.

The problem for college-educated women is that, even if they know their options have improved relative to their mothers’, it’s not until they’ve had a child that they can be sure their husband is a dependable dad.

The problem for non-college women is even worse — the average man in their dating pool probably doesn’t meet their expectations for marriage and family.

The working-class male-breadwinner expectation has long outlived the economic conditions that created it after World War II. As Johns Hopkins University sociologist Andrew Cherlin wrote in his 2014 book Labor’s Love Lost, the postwar era was “the only period in American history in which the nation’s economy was able to support large numbers of working-class families almost solely on the wages of husbands and the household work of their wives.”

Of course, “supporting a family” is not a fixed standard over time. Expectations of material comfort have risen alongside expectations about what a good husband looks like.

As early as the 1970s, economist Gary Becker hypothesized that large gaps between male and female earnings incentivized people to get married. If so, wage parity would likely weaken this incentive.

In his 1996 book When Work Disappears, sociologist William Julius Wilson argued that the loss of industrial jobs weakened the foundation for stable relationships in Black urban communities. When getting married doesn’t improve people’s chances of getting ahead, they have less of an economic incentive to marry before having kids.

These theories received empirical backing from a 2019 study showing that when men’s employment and earnings took a hit after a trade shock, marriage and fertility rates fell.

We’re not turning back the clock on skill-biased technological change (nor on women’s educational and workplace advances — sorry, trads). But the fact that the consequences of economic change have penetrated so harmfully into the family sphere demands a reckoning. “The problem,” Cherlin observed of the working-class male-breadwinner model’s demise, “is that nothing stable has replaced it.”

Goldin’s presentation of the trade-offs facing college women amid dad-versus-dud uncertainty is informative. But the most important family formation story today is that our society’s top and bottom halves are coming apart. The problems facing the bottom half are evident; the solutions less so.
