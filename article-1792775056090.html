There are few outlets delivering regular news on book publishing these days. That’s another reason why the Frankfurt Book Fair is such an important industry event. Its primary purpose is to serve as a trade fair for books. Publishers and industry professionals from around the world gather in Frankfurt, as they have been doing for more than half a century, to buy and sell literary rights and conduct related business. Because it’s one of the few events that brings all the important people in the book industry to one place at one time, it also serves as a significant clearinghouse for book publishing news. And it occurs during the busiest time of year in publishing, when most houses are bringing out their biggest books and have more than usual to talk about. That makes October the heaviest publishing news month on the calendar.

What follows is a non-exhaustive list of what struck me as the most interesting stories out of or around Frankfurt this year.

  1. Artificial intelligence made its presence felt at the fair in several ways, mostly related to labour. Ghostwriters, for instance, were reporting serious declines in commissions as prospective clients turned to chatbots to tell their stories; at the same time, ghostwriters were frequently being approached to edit AI texts.

  2. One of the fundamental tensions with AI is that it simultaneously makes some tasks easier and threatens the ability of anyone to make a living doing the task. Translators, like ghostwriters, are feeling this keenly. About 70% of translators, according to a 2026 survey, have experimented with AI for research and to deal with specific words and phrases. And just under 30% said they were receiving less work, a decline they attributed to AI. Like the ghostwriters, many had received requests to review and improve AI-generated work.

  3. AI seems to be scuppering the self-help market. Print sales of self-help were down 23% in the US through the first nine months of 2026 compared to 2025. Some of that is the lack of a killer 2026 book comparable to Mel Robbins’ The Let Them Theory (2025), but there appears to be truth to author Tim Ferriss’s observation that “the market for information is collapsing into the chatbot.”

  1. Publishers are in an increasingly awkward position vis-à-vis AI. They are simultaneously a) arguing that large language models have used their copyrighted content without permission, b) selling AI rights to their books to AI firms (one Frankfurt session was titled “How to Unlock Untapped Revenue Potential With AI Licensing”), c) acceding to the demands of their writers that they keep AI away from manuscripts and draw a sharp line between human- and machine-generated content, and d) experimenting with ways to integrate labour-saving AI into their operations. WIRED reported that HarperCollins, S&S, and Hachette are using LLMs for publicity copy and other marketing chores. A Frankfurt panel discussed how AI would likely replace routine junior work such as data entry, research, administration, and marketing tasks, threatening the entry-level positions through which many people find their way into the business.

  2. Sweden is kind of its own literary market: its domestic giant, the audiobook provider Storytel, has convinced hordes of people to listen to books rather than read them (Spotify has since got into the act, too). Audiobooks are roughly a third of publisher revenue in Sweden, and more than half of fiction revenue. Storytel is aggressively using AI translation and narration to expand its offerings, and the platform is now widely available in Europe and the US. It maintains that AI translation and narration are more perception problems than quality problems. Storytel is growing 10-13% a year by revenue, and its profitability at a faster rate. Difficult to say if Storytel is a Swedish anomaly or the future.

  3. Audible, meanwhile, said at Frankfurt that its ambition is to carry “every book in every language” and that it will use AI to get there. In other words, Audible, owned by Amazon, wants to be the Amazon of audiobooks. Which means all the good books in the world and mountains of crap besides. I sure hope the platform does something along the way to improve its terrible search functionality, which is inadequate to the volume of product currently available. Audible, by the way, does the vast majority of its business selling someone else’s audiobooks rather than producing original content, so it has less sensitivity than most publishing houses to the use of AI in making content. It released a survey of 18,000 audiobook listeners that found roughly half were open to listening to AI-narrated content.

  4. Here’s a new measure of how the industry is changing. In its last reported financial statements, Simon & Schuster, one of the Big Five publishers, had annual revenue of about $1 billion. Reports now put Tencent’s China Literature at about $1 billion in annual revenue, while Webtoon Entertainment, which owns the originally Canadian Wattpad, reported $1.35 billion for 2024. These platforms combine writing, reading, subscriptions, and adaptations in different ways, so the comparisons are inexact, but they show how much activity happens outside conventional publishing. Kindle Direct Publishing, the Amazon self-publishing engine, may be bigger than both of the services mentioned above. Simon & Schuster’s new Simon Stream imprint looks like a response to all this. It targets self-published authors, offers no advances, pays 50% of net ebook receipts monthly, and releases books simultaneously in ebook, print on demand, and audio, backed by S&S marketing and publicity.

  5. English books are becoming too global for the old territorial rights market. European publishers (not unlike Canadian publishers) fear they are losing control over their markets to outsiders. For many years, the Europeans supported their domestic production of native-language books—original French books for the French, original German books for the Germans, etc.—with translations of popular English-language books. An Italian company could do quite well translating John Grisham or Dan Brown for Italians, so long as the UK or US editions were difficult to obtain in their market. That business is deteriorating because English proficiency is increasing, online retail has improved, and English editions now arrive quickly and at reasonable prices. Many European readers won’t wait for the translation of a popular book to drop—they’ll take the English version on release. So some European publishers are now requesting a window of time in which the English-language version is withheld from a European market until the translation can be prepared. A number of publishers are apparently trying to crash their translations so they appear simultaneously with English releases. Still others are considering buying English rights for their market and publishing in English as well as in translation. This is an incredibly thorny set of problems involving differently situated English and American publishers, European publishers, agents, and authors. It has been percolating for years and appears to be a long way from any resolution, not least because English and American publishers seem to be enjoying heightened access to the European book buyer.

  6. The Federation of European Publishers made news at Frankfurt with a report that its members’ revenues fell by 1.2% in 2025, that its unit sales are down 6% since 2021, and that, adjusting for inflation, the European book publishing sector has shrunk by about 34% since 2007. I don’t think they included population growth over that time, in which case the shrinkage would climb to about 36%. We estimated the decline in the US market in SHuSH 296. Taking into account US book sales, inflation, and population growth, we found that book publishing had shrunk 40% relative to the broader American economy between 2008 and 2022. Best not to think too hard about these numbers.

  7. There was some encouraging news from Frankfurt about people actually buying books. NielsenIQ’s figures for the first half of 2026 showed print sales, measured by revenue, were up in a majority of markets tracked. Measured in copies sold, Brazil was up 10.5%, India 8.3%, New Zealand 5.5%, Mexico 4.7%, Ireland 2.8%, South Africa 2.7%, and Spain 2.4%. France, presumably preparing for the riots, suffered a substantial decline in sales, as did Poland. The US and UK were down slightly. Adult fiction grew in fourteen of eighteen territories surveyed, with crime, thrillers, fantasy, and science fiction in the lead. Nonfiction declined in all but three territories.

  8. What was hot at Frankfurt? In fiction, horror attracted a lot of interest, not least because of demand from film and television producers. Dark romance has become a huge business. Multigenerational family sagas remain popular, especially those involving three generations of women. Same goes for absorbing, emotionally satisfying novels in the vein of The Correspondent and Broken Country. Literary publishers, facing declining sales, are increasingly seeking books with a genre hook—a supernatural or speculative element. In nonfiction, agents were talking up history and true crime, as well as ambitious books explaining changes in politics, tech, culture. Also in demand: puzzle and gaming books, anything that helps people put down their phones.

Sutherland Quarterly is pleased to announce its latest edition, Rob Csernyik’s A Losing Hand: The Human Costs of Canada’s Gambling Epidemic:

Recent decades have seen an explosion of government-approved casinos, sports betting, and online gaming opportunities. The politicians and officials behind them meticulously track the revenue they receive, but turn a blind eye to its often devastating human costs.

Through gripping personal stories and a comprehensive investigation, journalist Rob Csernyik shows how public officials evade accountability for lives shattered by gambling addiction―for instance, rarely recording gambling as a risk factor in suicide. They refuse to confront the disturbing question of how many Canadians are dying from activities actively promoted by their governments.

From the rise of video lottery terminals to the explosion of smartphone betting, A Losing Hand shows how modern gambling has become more accessible, more immersive, and more dangerous than ever. The result is a crisis hiding in plain sight, one that policymakers have ignored for decades.

A powerful work of investigative journalism, this book forces a reckoning with the true cost of gambling in Canada.
