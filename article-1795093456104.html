When the Grandparents Quiet Quit your Kids

Disengaged grandparents probably didn’t enjoy parenting either.

Cartoons Hate Her
Nov 19
∙
Paid
 
READ IN APP
 

I’ve heard a common (and perhaps surprising) complaint among millennial parents, which I am lucky enough not to have experienced myself: their own parents (or their spouse’s parents) aren’t interested in their kids, and would rather do literally anything else than spend time with them.

It’s not just that they aren’t interested in babysitting (although this is usually the bulk of the complaint) but that they aren’t interested in being grandparents at all. They don’t want to visit, even if they live close enough for it to be easy. They expect their adult children and young grandchildren to travel to see them but are unwilling to reciprocate. They don’t ask about the kids or want to see photos. They aren’t excited about pregnancy announcements—and maybe they actively discourage their adult children from having more than one or two. On the occasion that they visit, they spend all their time on their phones, asking you if that video of Charlie Kirk turning into a strawberry with a BBL is real. And it’s often not a matter of being too old or disabled to have the energy—they are going on vacations, carousing with friends on the weekends, and basically doing everything other than spending time with their grandkids.

I had fairly disengaged grandparents myself (I’ve often joked that I’m so off-putting that even my own grandparents didn’t fw me) and my grandfather was too busy vacationing in Turkey to attend my wedding, so this is not a brand-new phenomenon, but I hear about it a lot more than I used to. Again, lots of potential sampling bias here. I wasn’t on Parent Twitter when I was nine years old, so of course I’m hearing it more now than I used to. But, totally unscientifically, I think something is going on.

I’m not the only one who noticed it. When Newsweek covered this issue last month, the explanation that “experts” gave was the same explanation for any modern and antisocial behavior: work and cost of living. Apparently, today’s grandparents are all too busy working their five jobs under late-stage capitalism to see their grandkids. But the stories I’ve heard aren’t of overstretched grandparents working into their golden years, but of comfortably retired grandparents who have no physical or material blockers to being involved with their families, but have simply chosen to deprioritize them.

The other popular and rarely-questioned “cost of living” argument is that parents today are working more than ever and therefore demanding more of grandparents than ever, requiring grandparents act as full-time childcare as a baseline. This also seems obviously untrue. For some reason, “experts” continue to act like this generation of moms is the first to work (“both parents working” is supposed to be some big, new thing) even though the share of full-time working mothers has been pretty much the same since before many millennials were even born. Most likely, today’s moms were also raised in dual-income households. And I doubt today’s parents are making stronger childcare demands of their parents than generations before. For one, people are more likely to live in different places from their parents now, so it’s a non-starter!

These are easy arguments to make, because if you say “people are working too much and things cost too much,” nobody will disagree with you. But I will! Instead of hedging and clarifying and disclaiming, I’ll just be blunt: I think a lot of disengaged grandparents today are quiet quitting their grandkids because they also did not enjoy being parents, and unlike previous generations of grandparents, there is no social pressure forcing them to remain part of a family unit or community. They were raised on traditional social obligations, but then retired into “I don’t owe you anything” culture.

Before you say this is a huge leap: lots of parents hate being parents. They don’t hate their kids, but they look forward to time without them, find any excuse to get away from them, and don’t really enjoy taking care of them or playing with them. (I’m not talking about just wanting a break from time to time, which is true of every parent.) Some straight-up regret becoming parents at all, according to the 52,000 weekly visitors of r/regretfulparents. Obviously, most parents don’t regret it (but also, most grandparents are not disengaged either.) My argument here isn’t that everyone regrets being a parent (I certainly don’t, and I imagine many of you don’t either) just that enough parents dislike parenting for me to extrapolate that a great deal of disengaged grandparents probably felt the same way as parents, and have carried this perspective with them into grandparenthood. When you grew up, your parents were relieved to be done and didn’t want to revisit that stage of their lives.

Likely, there have always been a non-trivial minority group of parents who didn’t particularly like children, even if they technically loved their own. Most boomers can tell you stories from their youth of a cold and distant chain-smoking mother (or completely absent father) who probably would have been better off not having children at all. But women of these previous generations did not have the option to opt out, (although from the stories I’ve heard, some of them did all but fully opt out, engaging in a style of parenting that would be illegal today). But either way, they were still mothers. They likely had no access to birth control, or social pressure was just too heavy for them to even consider not having kids. Then, when those women became grandmothers, they stayed at least somewhat engaged because they had nothing else to do and everyone, once again, expected them to enjoy being around kids. The grandfathers may or may not have been engaged at all, but nobody expected them to be anyway.

Also, the standards for parenting changed, and just as those standards affect young people’s willingness to become parents, they also affect older people’s interest in being active grandparents. Birth rates are declining in large part because people just aren’t pairing up as easily, but there is some evidence to suggest that young women are less enthusiastic about becoming mothers than previous generations. There’s likely no specific explanation that applies to everyone, but I’ve heard plenty of anecdotal reports of young childfree people, especially women, saying that parenting in 2026 simply seems…miserable. Not only are the negative stories of mom guilt and burnout plastered everywhere, with insinuations that every mother is just one bad day away from losing it, but even the more positive portrayals of motherhood seem to require that you absolutely adore spending all of your free time with your kids and giving up all your hobbies, body and social life—something which may be appealing to some people, but is likely not appealing to most. That doesn’t even touch the safetyism arms race or the fact that the cops can be called on you if you let your kids play outside.

Therefore, it’s possible that grandparents are reacting to the same changing rules. They raised their kids with a certain degree of effort, but now, to help raise their grandkids, they will need to expend even more effort. They would rather go on a cruise, quite frankly. As I’ve said before, it used to be normal in the nineties to just let your kids watch TV without specific limits in mind. Today, many parents might tell the grandparents that, while babysitting, they will need to be consistently present and engaged with the children and are forbidden from enlisting Elmo and Cookie Monster in such a task. For older people who already have less energy at baseline, that could be at least somewhat of a deterrent. For many millennials, grandparents played the role of the “fun ones” who would allow more sweets and TV than their parents. Parents who are unwilling to bend their rules for grandparent time may see themselves with less engaged grandparents. One popular “granfluencer” also reported that changing parental standards (or differing parenting styles) could scare some grandparents off, afraid that they will be either too lenient or too strict.

Not to mention, today’s grandparents are becoming grandparents for the first time at older ages. This doesn’t account for all forms of grandparent quiet quitting, since many of them are still young and energetic enough for extensive travel and social lives, but engaging in intensive parenting when you’re seventy-five isn’t exactly easy, given that it’s already hard enough for people in their thirties.

All that said, I think the easiest explanation is just that most disengaged grandparents never really liked being around kids, and being an involved grandparent is by far the most optional kid-duty they’ve ever had. When they had their own kids, they were bound by the law to be involved, and even if they weren’t having a good time raising them, they still loved them and probably enjoyed some moments. When a person who doesn’t like being a parent becomes a grandparent, it probably feels like Katniss being re-drafted for the Third Quarter Quell.

There’s really not a whole lot a parent can do about a grandparent who clearly doesn’t want to be involved. Provided you aren’t demanding anything wildly onerous of them, and provided you don’t have a million unreasonable rules, the main reason they would rather travel and hang out with their friends instead of doing grandparent stuff is that they simply don’t want to do grandparent stuff, because they likely didn’t enjoy being parents all that much either.

But if having involved grandparents still matters to you, and you’re unlucky enough to wind up with grandparents who don’t want to be involved, I do have one piece of advice: find one thing they can do with your kids that taps into something they do enjoy. Yes, it’s catering to them more than you’d probably like, but it’s either this or not having them around at all.

For example, let’s say your mother really likes painting, and would rather go on painting retreats than see her grandkids. If she’s willing to cooperate, try to get your kids involved in her painting, or ask her to teach your kids to paint. If your dad really loves history and would rather read history books than play with the grandkids, try to get your kid (provided they’re old enough) to find a history topic he or she might enjoy discussing with Grandpa.

But, unfortunately, if they are just addicted to short-form video content, you’re simply SOL.
