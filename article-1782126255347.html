The spectacular failure of the first AI Super PAC

The crypto playbook doesn't work for AI

Kelsey Piper
Jun 22
∙
Paid
 
READ IN APP
 

Last August, a press release announced the formation of a new $100 million super PAC, Leading the Future.

The group was funded by OpenAI President Greg Brockman and VC firm Andreessen Horowitz and, according to its press release, represented “a coalition of leading AI companies and innovators” aiming to “support candidates aligned with the pro-AI agenda and ensure America leads the world in AI innovation and oppose those that do not.”¹

Leading the Future was modeled on Fairshake, an extremely successful cryptocurrency PAC that Andreessen Horowitz had invested in during the previous election cycle. Fairshake’s theory of political pressure was simple: If you were against cryptocurrency, they would run primary and general election ads against you or for your opponents.

And it worked. Fairshake spent $40 million to defeat former Sen. Sherrod Brown’s 2024 reelection bid, another $10 million in a successful effort to defeat Senate candidate Katie Porter, and $2 million against former Rep. Jamaal Bowman in New York. In all three of those contests, Fairshake got its way and probably made a lot of other candidates extremely hesitant to put forward anti-crypto regulation as a result.

The plan with LTF was to repeat the successful experiment, this time to go after candidates bent on regulating AI. But it’s taken less than a year for that plan to crash and burn spectacularly.

Share

AI isn’t crypto. Fairshake’s playbook worked because crypto is a low-salience issue where most voters aren’t paying attention.

AI is completely different: Americans are beginning to care intensely and likely will care more and more as the technology spreads through various sectors of the economy. LTF was a bet against AI’s own importance, placed by many of the people building the technology. And it’s a bet they’re losing.

The Alex Bores referendum

One of the super PAC’s first targets — announced in a November statement — was Alex Bores, an underdog for New York’s 12th Congressional District race, who had earned OpenAI and Andreessen’s ire for his work on New York’s RAISE Act as a state assemblymember. Bores’ candidacy — which will be decided in tomorrow’s New York Democratic primary — rapidly came to be seen as a referendum on the political power of a few anti-AI regulation billionaires. LTF has funded several million dollars of attack ads against him.²

This all might seem excessive even if you take AI policy seriously — millions of dollars to take down one state assemblymember’s congressional run? Bores only had, according to the prediction markets, a 10% chance of winning before LTF jumped in. But the point of the super PAC’s intimidation strategy was to prevent future Boreses from opening their mouths about AI regulation.

Once LTF set its sights on Bores, the PAC got to work. One contractor it worked with spun up a fake AI newsroom that sent a fake AI reporter to conduct interviews for articles about why we shouldn’t regulate AI. The PAC later had to apologize for this, saying that it was a third-party contractor whose behavior it had been unaware of.

Another subsidiary also contacted influencers and media personalities, offering to pay them to do promotional work for the cause of not regulating AI. This, too, likely proved counterproductive after journalist Taylor Lorenz decided to instead write a story for Wired about the paid lobbying it attempted to recruit her to do.

Hiring journalists to do undisclosed campaign work for you is generally considered pretty sleazy, but LTF defended it as part of an “all-of-the-above communications strategy.”

Other elements of the “all-of-the-above communications strategy” were even sleazier. LTF resorted to buying likes for their posts on Twitter, and it didn’t even do a good job of that! Internet sleuths discovered that a huge share of the bots mass-promoting LTF posts claimed to be 18-year-old girls named Melanie. They also discovered that some sock puppet accounts purporting to be crazy anti-AI doomers were also run on LTF’s behalf.

None of this worked at all to discredit Bores, and it may well have ultimately worked in his favor; markets now give him a 43% chance. The race is competitive — featuring a Kennedy, anti-Trump critic George Conway, and a state assemblyman leading the polls — so Bores could lose for entirely unrelated reasons.

But, more importantly, while LTF pursued a strategy that was based on petty grudges and making an example of a random candidate, the AI regulation conversation blew past it at the speed of light.

LTF has already lost the fight to block AI regulation

Right now, the most powerful AI model in the world — Anthropic’s Fable/Mythos model — is unavailable because the administration export-controlled it several days after release. Even before that, an executive order created a voluntary preapproval pathway for new releases that was substantially more restrictive than the RAISE Act.

Everything LTF feared came about and then some.

The moral failure behind LTF was a belief that sleazy tactics and intimidation are the way to bring about policy change. The tactical failure was a belief that AI was, ultimately, not going to be that big a deal: that its conduct would fly under the radar because voters don’t have that much of an attention span.

In a way, Brockman’s investment in LTF was a bet against his own technology: that it would not rapidly become one of the world’s most pressing political issues. But it did, and LTF has almost nothing to say about where we should go from there.

In 2024 and 2025, the debate about AI regulation was dominated by fighting over New York and California bills that would impose fairly minimal standards on companies making frontier models. California, with SB 1047, and New York, with the RAISE Act, wanted to create state offices competent to review these frontier model releases and require companies to disclose the results of various internal reviews.

Even in their strongest forms, neither bill would have dramatically changed the pace of AI deployment. But something like those bills’ proposals was almost certainly a good idea.

Don’t believe me? Take it from, uh, LTF, which, after spending the last five months trying to end Alex Bores’ political career for his promotion of the RAISE Act, now says that the law is good and it actually supports it: “we support the final version of New York’s Responsible AI Safety and Education (RAISE) Act, which Gov. Kathy Hochul signed into law after lawmakers negotiated key amendments.”

Why, you might ask, is the PAC going after Bores if it actually supports the RAISE Act? It explained (it’s a very long Twitter article): “We are opposing Alex Bores, who authored the pre-amendment RAISE Act, which was very different from the version that passed. This earlier version of the RAISE Act did not include a clear mechanism for harmonization, meaning companies could have been forced to navigate conflicting state and federal requirements even if they were complying with a national standard.”

The RAISE Act was, indeed, majorly weakened by Hochul before she signed it, but I don’t really buy this as an account of LTF’s reasoning. What happened, I suspect, is not that LTF was in favor of the RAISE Act after the negotiations. If it were, it wouldn’t have put so much energy into trying to coax Congress or the White House into preempting all state AI regulation.

What happened instead is that its opposition to the RAISE Act became untenable. As AI has become more powerful, the idea that we should have significant monitoring of frontier models went from a niche concern to blindingly obvious. As a result, LTF has been executing what conservative AI policy analyst and recent OpenAI hire Dean Ball called “a hard pivot toward supporting sensible AI laws.”

Or, at least, it said that it is going to do that.

This spring, Anthropic announced the model Claude Mythos, which is excellent at cybersecurity. It’s great at finding ways that someone could exploit our computer systems for unauthorized access. And while Mythos has claimed the largest-scale impressive cybersecurity results, you can also get serious cybersecurity vulnerabilities from other top-tier models.

After a spring of feuding with Anthropic, Mythos initially marked a change of approach for the administration. The federal government moved to secure access. And the government’s involvement went way beyond using Mythos themselves: The White House has reportedly been actively involved in managing which companies had access to it — until it ordered the model taken down entirely over jailbreak concerns.

The position that no companies building frontier AI should have reporting responsibilities of any kind is no longer a credible one. It has become clear that the RAISE Act is the moderate option — as many of its proponents said at the time!

More ambitious measures that would damage AI companies much more are now on the table. Voters overwhelmingly favor making AI companies liable for harms created by their technology. (Depending how this was done, it could easily make it untenable for AIs to answer medical and legal questions at all.) Data centers are overwhelmingly unpopular, and a ban on building new ones also polls well.

And, ultimately, all of this is small potatoes compared to the tsunami we’ll see if a recession hits and unemployment spikes, or if AI-empowered hackers take down a significant chunk of the banking system or the power grid. It would not surprise me if, the first time something goes seriously wrong from AI, we impose a lot of new rules overnight — including, assuredly, a lot of stupid ones even if we also manage to include a lot of smart ones.

LTF more or less acknowledged the vibe shift: “Since we got started last year, the policy conversation in Washington, D.C., has shifted such that banning data centers is now in the Overton window,” its Twitter article laying out their AI positions said. “We believe that such extreme actions would be bad for America and would result in ceding the potential benefits of AI (such as curing diseases and empowering people to build new businesses) while failing to mitigate the risks.”

It may be right about that. But it should prompt a serious rethinking of its strategy to date.

The successful, slimy, and underhanded campaign against cryptocurrency regulation worked because basically nobody cares very much about cryptocurrency regulation. As a result, the team behind Fairshake clearly anticipated little pushback for tactics like paying people to do undisclosed ads, pumping out AI slop articles, and buying likes from pornbots.

But people do care about AI, and they are comfortable with way more extensive regulation than the RAISE Act ever contemplated. And now, we have more extensive regulation than the RAISE Act contemplates, just rolled out reactively and badly. So the same strategy fell catastrophically flat.

Perhaps the worst part of all of this is that many of the AI leaders involved with LTF are now at odds with the people willing to develop serious, technocratic, and sensible AI regulation. If Congress is intimidated out of acting on AI, the alternative won’t be free market capitalism — it will be a personalistic, vibes-based, chaotic system where models are blacklisted or whitelisted based on the administration’s whims.
