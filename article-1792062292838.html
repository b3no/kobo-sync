The perils of organizing chum

Zohran Mamdani, as mayor of New York City, is not really a key actor in the Israeli-Palestinian conflict.

Conversely, handling the Israeli-Palestinian conflict is not very high up on the list of things within his area of responsibility. But he is central to the symbolic politics of Israel within the United States of America because he’s both a vocal and uncompromising anti-Zionist and mayor of the city that has long been the center of Jewish-American political, economic, and cultural power.

So I’m not particularly surprised that Mamdani found himself heckled by anti-Israel protestors who shouted “We charge you with genocide” when he attended an October 7 vigil. This happened in part because the global Palestinian activist movement is full of people with extreme views. But the specific thing they’re asking for here — a new police commissioner and a disbanding of the N.Y.P.D. unit that does crowd control at protests — is not per se extreme. It just seems tangential to the Israeli-Palestinian conflict.

These activists are picking a nasty fight with a guy who seems to be mostly on their side about something that seems low stakes because of a larger structural theory of organizing and political power, one that is widespread in left activist circles but I think is consistently misunderstood and under-explained in journalistic circles, even by those with deep left-wing sympathies. Mehdi Hasan, for example, whose views on the substance of the conflict are much closer to the protestors’ than they are to mine, is baffled by this.

I agree with Hasan that this approach of targeting Mamdani is not a good way to do politics. But dismissing it as “dumb” is a mistake.

The activists are deploying a playbook that left-wing activists use across different issue areas in a way that connects pretty deeply to their overall view about how politics works. They’re aware that there is no short-term pathway to transform American policy toward Israel, but they don’t want to just tell everyone to go home for two years and worry about it then. So they’re picking some short-term political fights over the way the N.Y.P.D. handles protests and the identity of the police commissioner, demanding that Mamdani fire Jessica Tisch and replace her with some unknown alternative.

The fact that Mamdani is so well-regarded by so many progressives that many outside of activist circles are inclined to see this move as irrational is, I think, a teachable moment and worth a conversation about whether the broader arc of left-wing activist strategy makes sense.

Activists pick fights to drive more organizing

The underlying ideas in play here have nothing to do with the particulars of Palestine, New York City, or Mamdani and instead relate to a broader theory of political organizing.

If you read Saul Alinsky’s “Rules for Radicals,” for example, the whole idea is that you need to build political power outside the arena of electoral politics and the way to do this is through picking specific fights that may only relate tangentially to your ultimate policy goals.

On his list of rules are items like number two, “never go outside the experience of your people,” number eight, “keep the pressure on,” and number 13, “pick the target, freeze it, personalize it, and polarize it.”

Back in Barack Obama’s second term, when people were arguing about the Keystone XL pipeline, I was deeply confused as to why so much of the climate movement had pivoted away from talking about things that could have a major impact on the world’s long-term emissions trajectory and toward fighting about a pipeline whose actual climate implications were minuscule.

David Roberts at the time explained this to me and others as a gap between wonk logic and activist logic.

The wonks saw Keystone as a distraction from more important things and found it perverse to be picking fights with Obama (who broadly favored climate action) rather than with Republicans (who did not). But activist logic, as Roberts writes, does not see activist campaigns as expenditures of political capital; it sees them as opportunities to build strength.

“There are benefits to an activated, impassioned constituency and the social and political machinery that brings them together in large numbers,” he wrote, and “intensity is built through conflict, through the drawing of political and moral lines.”

To me, that line-drawing exercise always felt unfair. You had the most visible faces of the climate movement painting Obama as this awful bad guy on climate because he was reluctant to expend political capital on an anti-pipeline campaign that was marginal to climate change. Similarly, the proximate thing that activists are demanding Mamdani do is fire New York’s well-regarded police commissioner — a move that has a fair amount of downside political risk and no real upside for him.

It would be one thing to ask him to run a big risk for the sake of dramatically transforming the situation in Palestine, but obviously, changing the leadership at the N.Y.P.D. isn’t going to accomplish that. The cost of personalizing and polarizing a fight is that you end up misstating the stakes in a way that has never sat well with me.

That said, the core inspiration for a lot of left-wing work in the United States is the civil rights movement. And you can certainly imagine someone sitting around in 1955, wondering whether the question of seating arrangements on the bus in Montgomery, Ala., is really the main question about racial justice in the United States. And moderates within the civil rights camp absolutely did raise objections to the whole idea of a 1963 March on Washington, specifically on the grounds that it didn’t make sense to make life more difficult for the Kennedy administration, which was broadly on their side.

The general theory of the bus boycott, though, was that picking a concrete winnable fight was not going to be a “distraction” from the larger cause but an opportunity to get lots of people involved and build more power. I think that anchoring on the civil rights movement is a mistake — that the movement is such a striking example precisely because the dynamics around it are historically unusual — but it’s not crazy or stupid to take inspiration from it. That’s a deep and profound idea worth taking seriously.

Why the left picks fights with friends over small beer

In a world with a lot of partisan polarization (unlike the 1950s and 1960s), one thing that makes these activist campaigns so contentious is that they tend to involve activists protesting their friends rather than their enemies. That feels a little perverse. But the basic organizing logic is that to have a compelling fight:

  1. You always need to be asking for something from someone.

  2. The someone has to be someone whose reputation you can wreck.

  3. The something has to be something the someone can give you.

The case against picking a fight with Mamdani about the police commissioner is that (a) Mamdani is an ally of the movement and (b) the identity of the police commissioner has basically nothing to do with conditions on the ground in Palestine. But the case for it is that Mamdani clearly has the power to do this and Mamdani has built a lot of his political identity around the idea that he is an ally of the Palestinian cause. If you can make a lot of people think that his management of the N.Y.P.D. constitutes a betrayal of Palestine, that has a meaningful impact on his political standing. Marco Rubio and Donald Trump and JD Vance don’t claim to be allies of Palestine, so pro-Palestinian activists saying things about them doesn’t hurt them.

If you want to flex short-term muscle, you need to target someone you can hurt, and that means a friend rather than an enemy. And if you target a friend, you need to pick something under his control, which in this case means blowing the stakes out of proportion.

We saw that with Obama and Keystone. We also saw it during the 2020 presidential campaign when pressure was (successfully) brought to bear on Democratic candidates to come out in favor of a “deportation moratorium.”

The idea was that you shouldn’t let Democrats off the hook by allowing them to just say they were in favor of a path to citizenship for millions of long-term unauthorized residents, because whether this happened was subject to dynamics in Congress. You had to ask them for something objectively less important that they could unilaterally deliver because that’s a proper organizing target.

Similarly, there was a lot of focus during the early years of Joe Biden’s administration on executive action to unilaterally cancel student debt. From a wonk viewpoint, it was a distraction from systemic reform of higher education finance. But from an activist viewpoint, it was important to have a concrete fight with the Biden administration and not get bogged down in the vagaries of legislation.

I think it’s important to understand this logic not because I agree with it but because it’s worth appreciating just how widespread it is.

And while activism as a genre of activity can be very helpful in specific situations, it’s actually not a good idea to take the civil rights movement as a model for political change. The activist viewpoint on these specific controversies isn’t exactly wrong; it’s more that Do More Activism is wrong as a general approach to politics.

Rules for meliorists

I think this whole episode is really interesting. As someone who doesn’t really agree with Mamdani’s takes on Israel but who also acknowledges that pro-Israel politics is fading fast in the United States, it feels obvious to me that one of the best things that could possibly happen for AIPAC would be for Mamdani to screw up some totally unrelated aspect of New York City governance.

Fundamentally, his success or failure as a mayor is not going to hinge on anything he says or does about Israel, but when winners win, that generates a positive halo for everything they’re associated with.

By the same token, successful campaigns to push Obama and Hillary Clinton to take political risks on climate and immigration ultimately helped elect Trump in 2016.

Conversely, I think that if Mary Peltola and James Talarico can win elections in Alaska and Texas on platforms that emphasize their support for American oil and gas production but that also acknowledge the existence of climate change as a real problem that deserves solutions, that will be a huge win for climate as a substantive policy issue.

I know some people in climate world who believe that the drift of activist energy away from climate and toward Palestine has been bad for climate as a cause. I actually think it’s potentially quite good for climate as a cause because it’s created space for politicians like Martin Heinrich and Sheldon Whitehouse to engage in pragmatic deal-making. The smart thing to do politically is normally to identify your champions — Mamdani on Palestine, Whitehouse on climate, etc. — and then let them cook. I think YIMBYs keep putting legislative wins on the board by trying to have a good attitude about incremental change rather than coming up with new demands to put on Scott Wiener.

I do want to acknowledge, though, that these are not just tactical disagreements.

The rise of interest in pro-Palestinian activism has unquestionably been bad for climate activism. I think it’s been neutral-to-positive for progress on climate change because I don’t think activism is as helpful as its proponents believe. But activists, of course, do believe in activism, so telling them “just do nothing and let Zohran handle the police department however he wants” is not very compelling. And while I think the civil rights movement is more the exception that proves the rule than a model for others to emulate, I do understand why people want to align themselves with the spirit of the single most high-profile and morally urgent campaign for progressive change in American history.

Accusing Mamdani of complicity in genocide is a kind of reductio ad absurdum of this whole approach to politics, but it really does follow from the broader logic.
