Democrats are winning the polls but losing the plot

Primary voters are far more focused on fighting each other than on fighting the G.O.P.

Halina Bennet
Aug 3
∙
Paid
 
READ IN APP
 

The basic midterm scenario remains familiar: Republicans hold a Senate majority, and Democrats need a net four-seat gain to flip it. Democrats continue to lead on the generic ballot and have a better chance of controlling the House than they do the Senate at this point. There’s a realistic chance of victories in both, as the president’s approval ratings remain underwater. But while Democrats had a good week in the polls, they are mired in infighting and conflict. How the primaries continue to shape up across the country could have a major effect on how the numbers shake out in November.

Michigan

Michigan’s Senate primary, one of the most closely watched races for Democrats, will finally end on Tuesday. Most recent polling has Dr. Abdul El-Sayed leading Representative Haley Stevens by a wide margin. At the same time, Stevens has consistently come out ahead against the likely Republican nominee, Representative Mike Rogers, while El-Sayed has come up short in general election polling.

An Emerson poll of 700 likely Democratic primary voters taken July 26 and 27 had El-Sayed leading Stevens by 15 points (54 percent to 39 percent). In a general election survey of 600 voters that was taken by EPIC-MRA between July 24 and 31, Stevens led Rogers by two points (44 to 42 percent), while Rogers led El-Sayed by three points (46 to 43 percent); both races were within the four-point margin of error.

Endorsements have, so far, split along establishment/progressive lines. Stevens has public support from Senators Chuck Schumer, Jeanne Shaheen, Gary Peters (who is retiring from the seat in question), Ruben Gallego, Chris Coons, and Catherine Cortez Masto. El-Sayed has backing from Senators Bernie Sanders, Chris Van Hollen, Elizabeth Warren, Tina Smith, and Ed Markey. Both candidates have collected additional endorsements from House members, governors, and party figureheads, largely along predictable lines.

Iowa

Former President Barack Obama endorsed Democratic Senate candidate Josh Turek last week in a video of the pair playing basketball. (Turek is a two-time Paralympic gold medalist.) According to X metrics, the video on Turek’s account alone has received more views than Iowa has residents.

That Obama is supporting a Democrat is not exactly a huge surprise, but for a candidate who had been lagging behind other top-tier Senate recruits in fundraising, the national attention should provide a welcome boost.

The race between Turek and Representative Ashley Hinson is close. A New York Times/Siena survey of 600 likely voters conducted from June 15 to 27 found Hinson leading Turek 48 to 46 percent, while a Fox News poll of 1,003 registered voters conducted from June 23 to 27 found Turek leading Hinson 50 to 46 percent.

Texas

Democratic nominee State Representative James Talarico leads Ken Paxton, the Republican attorney general of Texas, in three recent polls. Surveys taken in July by Texas Public Opinion Research, Fox News, and Wedgewood Polls found Talarico leading Paxton by between two and five points.

Talarico’s lead is particularly strong among Hispanic voters, with whom he is favored across recent polls. His lead among that demographic balloons to 18 points (in the case of Fox News) and 46 points (in Texas Public Opinion Research). If his advantage holds true, it will mark a shift from the Trump-era erosion of Democratic support among Hispanic voters that has been consistent over the last two cycles.

Talarico is polling about 10 points better than Beto O’Rourke was at this point in 2018, and Paxton’s net favorable rating is about 15 points lower than Senator Ted Cruz’s was, CNN reports. Despite a positive preview for Talarico, Texas has not elected a Democrat statewide since the 1990s, so Republicans are still favored. Still, the state has emerged as a surprising pickup opportunity for Democrats if Talarico’s lead holds and the political environment breaks their way.

Wisconsin

The race to succeed retiring Governor Tony Evers has drawn a crowded field, but that field narrowed Thursday when former Lieutenant Governor Mandela Barnes dropped out amid concerns about his treatment of women that prompted a state party review. This withdrawal follows another point of volatility in the race after current Lieutenant Governor Sara Rodriguez departed last month. As he exited, Barnes refrained from endorsing anyone but said, “It’s become very clear who our nominee is going to be,” referencing State Representative Francesca Hong, a democratic socialist whose polling lead has expanded in recent months.

Marquette Law School poll Director Charles Franklin estimated that reallocating Barnes supporters by second choice would put Hong at 43 percent. A separate State Navigate poll taken July 23 to 26 (before Barnes dropped out) had Hong at 44 percent, leading the next candidate by 29 points.

Over the weekend, U.S. Representative Ilhan Omar and progressive Twitch streamer Hasan Piker stumped for Hong in rallies across Wisconsin. The Milwaukee Journal Sentinel reports they were met with enthusiasm from supporters but criticism from her opponents, including the likely Republican nominee, U.S. Representative Tom Tiffany.

“Francesca Hong choosing to campaign with individuals who said America ‘deserved 9/11,’ called communism the ‘honorable end goal,’ and believe capitalists should be killed is certainly a choice,” Tiffany said.

Republicans are not the only ones who take issue with Piker’s involvement. “I am not going to be in the same party with Hasan Piker,” said James Carville, the longtime Democratic strategist, in an interview with Fox News.

“If he becomes a force in a Democratic Party, I’m out of here. I have no intention of ever being in the same political party with that guy.”

The party is, indeed, shifting. A new Ipsos poll found that 38 percent of Democrats and Democratic-leaning independents would prefer a “democratic socialist” over a “Democrat.” Fifty-seven percent would prefer a Democrat. The responses follow a sharp generational and educational split, with voters under 55 and college-educated voters leaning toward the socialist label. These numbers are more telling for coalition management than electability, since primary electorates skew older than the group driving the pro-socialist number.

Montana

Of course, the most progressive candidates cannot win in states that are still majority-Republican. A race that I have not covered much but am paying attention to is unfolding in Montana, where there is a three-way contest for the Senate. A recent poll published by G. Elliott Morris has the independent candidate Seth Bodnar leading Republican Kurt Alme 50 to 44 percent in a hypothetical head-to-head matchup, while Alme leads Democrat Alani Bankhead 52 to 44 percent. Many Democrats want Bankhead to withdraw from the race, allowing Bodnar to run as the non-Republican.

Bodnar has received endorsements from Nancy Keenan, the former executive director of the Montana Democratic Party, who wrote that “there is not a viable path for a Democratic victory in this race.” Former Senator Jon Tester has also endorsed Bodnar.

Tester, a Democrat, served in the U.S. Senate for 18 years until he lost re-election to Republican Tim Sheehy in 2024.

Democrats face an uphill battle for a Senate majority

Even with Democrats appearing to lead in some battleground states, it is worth noting that the Republican efforts are intensifying. Elon Musk said he would put at least $100 million behind Republican Senate candidates in Alaska, Maine, Iowa, Michigan, and Ohio. He is considering spending in Texas, North Carolina, and Georgia as well, the New York Times reports.

Some candidates, such as Democrat Troy Jackson in Maine, say they won’t be threatened by that kind of cash.
