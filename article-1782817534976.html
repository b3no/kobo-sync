How to quit a quarter-trillion-dollar gambling habit

We legalized sports betting. Tens of millions are hooked. Now what?

Joseph Politano
Jun 30

Americans are on track to gamble away a record-high quarter-trillion dollars this year. Since the Supreme Court stripped away federal bans on sports gambling, regulators have behaved like negligent parents: good enough to keep their charges alive, but only just.

The rise of American gambling
U.S. gambling losses continue rising to record heights

Total losses have grown 67% since COVID-19 started and are up another 8% over the last year alone, increasing faster than at any point from 2000 to 2020. That’s excluding gambling-adjacent activities that happen via crypto, prediction markets, stock options, or other avenues.

America’s modern gambling boom really kicked off after the 2018 Supreme Court decision in Murphy v. NCAA overturned the federal ban on sports betting; since then, 39 states and D.C. have legalized the practice in some form.

Then, during the pandemic, many consumers were stuck at home bored, isolated, and often with money to spare. This further accelerated gambling activity. Were it not for the two most populous states, California and Texas, holding out against the tide of legalization, an even larger supermajority of Americans would now have easy access to official sports betting.

Share

Plenty of people can gamble without letting it ruin their lives. They can play blackjack and bet on their local team without expecting to win big or risking unwise amounts of money, similar to how most people can consume alcohol with no worse outcomes than a hangover. Nearly all vices imply some risk of addiction or self-harm; that doesn’t mean we should automatically ban them.

But it does mean we must take seriously and guard against the potentially life-altering consequences of sports gambling. One brutal finding shows just how devastating losses can be to families: In states where sports betting is legal, an NFL home team upset loss raises intimate partner violence by 10 percentage points more than in non-legal states.

The industry regularly produces addicts who bet to their own detriment, losing large chunks of their income and still convincing themselves to bet more. These are people who gamble a rent payment, credit card bill, or student loan and lose — just to come back next month and gamble more. Research by New York Fed economists Jacob Goss and Daniel Mangrum, looking at millions of credit reports, showed that debt delinquency rates increased as states legalized sports gambling, especially for men and people under 40.

Fundamentally, betting losses cannot continually grow 10% per year without bilking a growing well of problem gamblers.

The pro-gambler, anti-casino lane

Murphy v. NCAA prevents the federal government from mandating state-level bans on sports gambling, but it does not prevent it from creating new national-level rules.

Reforms that are pro-“shark” can be an easy way to worsen the economics of casinos. Sports bettors or card players who are extremely skilled can end up as liabilities for the house if they can consistently earn money from gambling, so casinos often expend plenty of effort identifying and banning those people. The government could make it difficult for casinos to kick sharks out for “winning too much,” gaining support from gamblers and costing casinos money in one fell swoop.

Similarly, regulations that increase the perceived or genuine “fairness” of gambling could unite the pro- and anti-betting coalitions against the casinos.

Players, coaches, referees, and other personnel directly involved with professional sports should be kept as far away from gambling as possible — banned from betting on, investing in, or partnering with sportsbooks. This would prevent things like NBA star Giannis Antetokounmpo becoming an investor in Kalshi while being able to stir up his own trade rumors that Kalshi allows betting on. (In between drafting this piece and publication, Antetokounmpo did get traded.)

Even if nothing untoward is actually happening, these types of partnerships reduce trust in the prediction market’s impartiality. Banning them would increase trust while hurting gambling companies’ ability to effectively advertise.

The federal government could also attempt to tighten up regulations for gambling advertisements. Time-of-day restrictions could be used to require gambling TV ads to air only late at night to ensure children are not watching. Congress could also follow Colorado’s lead in trying to limit betting apps’ ability to send push notifications to users’ phones, a key way of pushing gamblers to bet more in the modern era. These could face significantly more court scrutiny under interpretations of First Amendment Freedoms but would at least be worth trying to add on to more substantive betting restrictions.

A dozen other ways to fix America’s gambling problems

Regulating the types of games that online casinos can offer and the types of wagers that sports betting apps allow could curb the more problematic aspects of gambling culture.

Limiting the reach of digital slot machines, one of the most addictive and unfair forms of gambling, would hit online casinos hard. Restricting parlays, where gamblers stitch together multiple bets and only get paid out if all their predictions come true, could also hurt sportsbooks, as the complicated and unique nature of these bets allows companies to earn higher margins from overconfident consumers.

Likewise, Congress could try to restrict the financial tactics that companies use to acquire, retain, and drain customers. Setting limits on the amount of free introductory bets would hurt many of the tactics that apps use to bait in new customers. Banning loyalty programs, dynamic odds, or casino credit lines could stop gambling habits from turning into addictions. Finally, setting stricter loss or deposit limits could act as a last line of defense to prevent the worst of gambling’s financial fallout.

Then, of course, there is just the option of increasing taxes on gambling to squeeze casinos and betting apps. Winners are already taxed on any net gambling earnings, but the federal government could levy a direct tax on each bet in a similar vein to the “sin taxes” that already exist for other addictive substances like alcohol and tobacco.

Some states are already doing this, but the incentives often push against taxes meaningfully high enough to significantly discourage betting. After all, any New York gambling tax could just drive gamblers into New Jersey. States brought in around $3 billion in gambling taxes last year, a large amount on its own but paltry compared with the size of the gambling industry.

Taxing America's lottery boom
Tax revenue on sports gambling continues rising to new record highs

At the state level, governments can also try to displace unsavory private sector actors with their own gaming programs. Lotteries replicate some of the predatory impulses of casino and sports betting, but at least have the advantage of undercutting the worst of the gambling industry and directing the losses to more productive causes.

State-run monopolies can also limit the innovation common to more competitive markets, which in this case primarily means finding more and more creative ways of getting consumers addicted to gambling.

Some states have already expanded lotteries to great effect over the last decade, with net revenue approaching $70 billion in 2025. In the same vein, Delaware’s state-controlled online betting monopoly could also serve as a model for other states.

Americans are losing record amounts to state lotteries

The drawback, of course, is that building a revenue stream on the backs of a vulnerable, addicted minority is anathema to the purpose of the government in the first place. That tension runs underneath every proposed fix. It’s why the sin tax, the ad restrictions, the parlay limits, and the state monopoly, while all worth trying, aren’t comfortably clean wins. They only work insofar as they shrink the pool of gamblers.

Ending the race to the bottom

States that have not already legalized sports gambling are under immense financial pressure to do so as their residents take business and potential tax revenue across state lines to gamble. Commercial gaming paid a record $15.91 billion in direct state and local gaming taxes in 2024 (including the $3 billion in sports betting taxes previously mentioned). While, again, this is a tiny portion of overall state budgets, this new revenue source is coming while 13 states are projecting budget gaps for the 2027 fiscal year and 23 are experiencing flat or declining general fund spending for the 2027 fiscal year.

Even in a best-case scenario, tight national gambling regulations will still struggle to stop the kind of offshore crypto betting that has been flourishing in recent years.

Gambling is a niche industry with lots of money to spend, making it a difficult beast to properly rein in — just ask anyone involved in Nevada politics. Even as public sentiment continues to sour on casinos and sportsbooks, it will be extremely difficult to pass regulations. Casinos have the capability to buy off marginal races in a way akin to the crypto lobby, which dumped money to defeat key regulation proponents like former Sen. Sherrod Brown.

But the government has to act fast. Since Murphy v. NCAA, federal regulators have been embarrassingly absent, allowing states to race to the bottom on betting regulations.
