Andrew Potter: The separatists are in power in Quebec again, but not by much

For now, at least, Mark Carney’s popularity in the province should be a bulwark against secession plans

Andrew Potter
Oct 6
 
READ IN APP
 

By Andrew Potter

For the first time since 2014, Quebec will have an explicitly separatist government. The Parti Québécois, led by Paul St-Pierre Plamondon, won a minority government on Monday, taking 59 out of the 127 seats in the national assembly.

Yet the PQ victory was probably the least surprising result of the election. In the dying days of the campaign, Plamondon’s main opponents — CAQ leader and premier Christine Fréchette, and Liberal leader Charles Milliard — took advantage of their last media availabilities not to sell themselves and their party to undecided voters, but to apologize for running lousy campaigns.

At a stop on Sunday, Milliard said he did not have many regrets, except the fact that he didn’t make himself the face of his party’s campaign earlier — his campaign bus didn’t even have his picture on it for the first week. As for Fréchette, there was a window, early in the campaign, when it looked like she might be able to pull a Mark Carney and electro-shock her flatlining party back to life. But that slammed shut pretty quick, as she also conceded at a stop in Shawiningan on Sunday. Instead of rallying the faithful, she ran through a list of her campaign’s mistakes, including not warning strongly enough about separatism and not highlighting the CAQ government’s accomplishments during its eight years in power.

For her troubles, the CAQ were completely wiped out, with Fréchette even losing her own seat in Trois-Rivières. Meanwhile, the upstart Conservatives, led by former “radio poubelle” shock jock Éric Duhaime, won their first seats and gained official party status with 19 members. Adding to the fun is the distribution of the popular vote. The PQ took only 28 per cent, the Liberals got 24, Conservatives 21, and the CAQ just under 14. The left-wing Québec solidaire needed only 11 per cent of the vote to win their nine seats.

The upshot is that there is now a sharply sovereigntist government in Quebec City, and Mark Carney has yet another problem to deal with. Plamondon, or PSPP as he is usually referred to in the media, has pledged to aggressively seek independence, eventually through another referendum. In the meantime, he’ll spend as much time as possible trying to generate the “winning conditions,” which means picking fights with Ottawa in order to make it clear that Canada doesn’t work for Quebec.

One early example: he has said he will defund the position of the Lieutenant Governor, and spend the money on homelessness. The constitutional implications are unclear, but it is one of those things Carney will probably want to bend on, as long as there is some way of giving it a constitutional fig leaf. Quebec already pays the barest of lip service to the position, sidelining the office holder both practically and symbolically. It is hard to see how further reduction in either aspect of the position would matter much.

On the national unity question, there are a few things working in Carney’s favour. The first is the thin popular support for both the new government, and for sovereignty in general. The PQ’s share of the vote is the lowest ever for a new government, beating the 32 per cent that brought the PQ to power in 2012. At the same time, support for sovereignty currently sits around 30 per cent, suggesting Plamondon has a lot of work to do making Ottawa the enemy.

But more than anything, there is Carney’s own popularity. According to a new Angus Reid poll, the prime minister’s approval rating in Quebec is now 67 per cent, notably higher than that of Bloc Québécois leader Yves-François Blanchet, at 47 per cent. And a different poll has PSPP’s favourability sitting at just 40 per cent.

Ultimately, the reality of the situation in Quebec is that after eight years in power, the CAQ was a spent force, while the Liberals had a new leader who spent a big chunk of the campaign hiding from voters. A PQ victory was the only plausible outcome, and from a national unity standpoint, Plamondon’s position is about as weak as could be hoped for.

The big wild card in all of this, as usual, is Donald Trump. Rumours have been swirling throughout the campaign about possible White House involvement in supporting a PQ victory. It all culminated with a week to go when Fréchette went public with a think-tank report warning of an American psy-op aimed at stoking separatism in Quebec. That story didn’t go anywhere, but it was revived in the last days of the campaign when the Montreal Gazette reported that Plamondon had sought, and scheduled, a meeting with the U.S. State Department in Washington in early March. The meeting was cancelled and not rescheduled, and the State Department has otherwise kept its distance from the Quebec vote.

Plamondon will no doubt find ways to make trouble for Ottawa, but his efforts might meet with limited public support, at least in the near term. He might also discover that he has enough to deal with fixing the province’s roads, hospitals, and other services, with limited public finances. On that last item, it is worth noting that Plamondon has promised to hold a referendum on independence once Trump leaves office in 2029, assuming his government lasts that long.

Coincidentally, 2029 is also when the federal equalization formula, which this year will send $13.9 billion to the province, is up for mandatory renewal.
