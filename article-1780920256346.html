When OCD and Cancellation Collide

A hypervigilant fear of being cancelled is prevalent among OCD sufferers, and typical reactions to this fear make things worse.

People are pretty familiar with the typical OCD manifestations—cleanliness and germophobia (of course), magical thinking rituals to prevent peril, and hypochondria or worries about health and safety (this is how it usually goes for me.)

But there are many other forms of OCD—intrusive thoughts and fears about more taboo things, things which most people are scared of mentioning. Thoughts that you might harm children are common among OCD sufferers, for the simple reason that this is the worst thing those people can imagine, so their OCD attaches to it. This is precisely how intrusive thoughts and OCD work—I was once in an OCD support group with a nice woman who often worried that she might cheat on her husband without realizing it, and eventually developed a terrifying obsession that she could, at any moment, become a cannibal serial killer.

A less taboo, but also less well-documented version of this subtype of OCD is a fear of cancellation, or having your life/career ruined by the hands of an online hate mob, usually for perceived bigotry. Unlike a fear that you are one bad day away from skilleting and filleting your neighbor, a fear of cancellation makes a bit more “sense”—far more people have been cancelled (as in, lost their jobs or entire careers, not “got yelled at online for two days”) often for seemingly innocuous or minor things, than become serial killers. (Take, for example, the young man who was famously fired for making a middle-school-tier sex joke to a friend at a tech conference, within earshot of a woman he’d never met.) One could not become a serial killer by accident, but almost everyone who has been canceled over online slights (or worse, behaviors out of context, filmed by strangers in public) did not think what they did was wrong. And at least for a while, openly expressing this fear, or any criticism of “cancel culture,” made you a target of this same culture which supposedly didn’t exist. Which was, to say the least, bad for people with this subtype of OCD!

I followed an OCD therapist on Instagram for a while in the late 2010s who produced a video about how cancel culture had gripped the minds of OCD sufferers. She reiterated that a fear of cancellation was another way that OCD moral scrupulosity manifested, and that OCD sufferers should identify it as intrusive, and not feed the obsession by apologizing, ruminating, over-explaining, or deleting innocuous social media posts. Want to guess what her comments said? “Way to go to bat for problematic people.” “Tell me you’re privileged without telling me you’re privileged.” “Just say you’re racist and go.” To these people, admitting that cancellation was scary (even to anxious people who probably hadn’t done anything seriously wrong) was on par with doing something hateful and offensive that would warrant cancellation.

A braver woman than I would have responded to them and called them out for their ableism, but instead I scrolled.

I recently read Kat Rosenfield’s excellent, well-written (in my husband’s words, “amazing and terrifying”) piece about her cancellation in the late 2010s. I almost want to say “trigger warning” because this is exactly the kind of story that would terrify someone with this form of OCD. But that’s exactly why it needs to be shared. People with this OCD subtype freak out over a fear of being canceled because this actually happens, or at least happened for a while—and telling them that they’re imagining the whole thing (or that if they’re worried about it, they must have thoughts that make them evil enough to cancel anyway) is not the right move.

I had always vaguely known Kat had been cancelled before over something silly, but I assumed it was along the lines of “culturally appropriative Halloween costume,” “off-color joke on Twitter in 2013” or “inadequate levels of diversity in a novel.” I did not know that she was cancelled over something completely imaginary—totally false accusations of running a sockpuppet account that supposedly sent nasty DMs to writers in the young adult novel space. The only “real” sin she committed (which led to the false accusation) was obliquely criticizing the trend in young adult publishing of unhinged online mobs ganging up to get rivals’ books pulled from publication—and ironically, perhaps to avoid this exact outcome, she didn’t name specific people. True to the trend she had noticed, even identifying that this was happening made her a target. This isn’t happening, but also, it should happen to you.

It wasn’t just people yelling at her online—her career was ruined for years. She was dropped by her editor and, at least in the young adult novel space, became unpublishable. She lost personal friends and professional connections. Completely fabricated allegations that she was “unsafe around minors” began spreading. It took her years to recover, and many people in her boat might have never climbed out of that hole. What began with silly Facebook group drama culminated in real-world, life-ruining actions.

Regardless of whether you agree with everything she’s ever written, or better yet, agree with everyone with whom she’s ever interacted (for some reason this is the bar for many people online for “do I believe bad things should happen to this person?”) you’d be hard pressed to read Kat’s story and declare that “this didn’t happen.” Of course it happened. For better or worse, these things tended to happen on the Internet, where nothing is left up to unverified stories. True, this did not happen to everyone (and dare I say, once it started happening to more and more people, over more and more ridiculous things, it became less significant!) The only critique people seem to be able to level at Kat for this article is that it was “whiny” to write about it—which, okay, imagine that someone managed to ruin your entire career for years (or perhaps forever—remember, not everyone recovers) over something completely made up. I feel like you’d be justified in whining a little, as a treat.

What’s more is that these hate mob campaigns were almost always intra-party. Having more or less the “correct” politics did not save you. Notably, Ben Shapiro and Matt Walsh have never been “canceled.” These types of things only ever happened to leftists and liberals who pissed off extremely unhinged people, who probably still agreed with them on 90% of things. In fact, arguably the best way to avoid cancellation was identifying as a Republican (although you might just get a hate mob of unhinged right-wingers for not being fascist enough—a conservative acquaintance of mine was doxxed and had his employer called because he wasn’t racist, as he believed interracial marriages were fine. However, as you can imagine, nothing came of this.)

This is another reason that the cancellation fear is so sticky for people with OCD—you could not reasonably expect to be safe from cancellation because you were a reliable Democrat voter or even an ultra-woke socialist. It’s not terribly logical to believe that someone needed to be “deplatformed” because they held beliefs still far more left-wing than your median American, but those were always the people on the receiving end of it. Some folks are still trying to make this type of intra-party cancellation a thing, but not as successfully. On my feed the other day I saw someone “called out” for being “chummy with Nazis” and the Nazi in question was Chris Best, CEO of Substack.

Part of the problem with how people react to this type of OCD—and how they reacted to the aforementioned Instagram therapist I mentioned—is the whole, “mental illness doesn’t do that” phenomenon, excellently described by Freddie deBoer. Essentially, this is the idea is that yes, mental illness can make a person self-harm or even end their own lives, but there’s no way, in any possible scenario, that mental illness might make you say or even believe something offensive. If you do (or even think!) those things, you’re just evil, and if you say any of that could ever be attributed to mental illness, you’re giving mentally ill people a bad name and giving Nazis (like notorious fascist Chris Best) a pass. Yes, mental illness can make you kill yourself. But no, it can’t make you imagine a racial slur and then feel guilty about it.

This type of thinking doubles down on what many OCD sufferers believe—that their unwanted thoughts make them evil. I’ve seen OCD described as “being completely lucid, but possessed by a demon.” For many people, that’s about right. And if someone’s worst fear is being a racist, a sexist, or a homophobe, their intrusive thoughts will basically be, “What would AryanConquest 1488 think right now? Guess what, you’re thinking it too! Guess you’re a Nazi! It’s only a matter of time before your entire family and employer find out!” The more that OCD sufferers don’t want to think these horrible things, the more they think about them.

And yet, when OCD sufferers have opened up about this, they’ve been given the “mental health doesn’t do that” answer, or the “if you’re so worried about being cancelled you probably are a bigot anyway” answer. That was what happened to the Instagram therapist, who had OCD herself and who attempted to raise awareness of cancellation OCD. The denizens of Instagram decided that anyone who worried about cancellation probably deserved to be cancelled, because why would they worry about it if they weren’t bigots themselves? (I’m curious to know what they think is going on with all the people with pedophile OCD—do they believe all of those people—many of them women—are secretly pedophiles?) Similarly, a woman on TikTok opened up about struggling with racist intrusive thoughts stemming from her OCD (she did not name the thoughts specifically) and people told her she was probably just racist if she was capable of imagining those thoughts at all.

When I ran my survey on OCD and intrusive thoughts, several respondents mentioned these types of intrusive thoughts. A 37-year-old man said he worried he was accidentally including racial slurs in emails and DMs to his non-white friends: “I go through periods where I have to regularly read back texts and DMs I send to any of them to check for the racial slurs that could have found their way in there via autocorrect. But then ultimately I really feel guilty that I'm stigmatizing my friends in that way!” A 29-year-old man had a huge variety of intrusive thoughts, but one of them was cancellation, despite not recalling any actual offensive content he’s posted: “I'm constantly worried I've made some sort of post in my long digital past that is offensive, cancellable, or otherwise problematic….This causes me so much distress from time to time, that I get stuck trying to find any old account and wipe it, or taking down old posts on accounts I don't want to delete. Old Facebook picture from high school with two likes of a friend doing a weird pose? What if five years from now, the context looks completely different and it somehow became A Whole Thing?” A 42-year-old woman said she worries that she will accidentally unmute herself on a Zoom call and say something offensive: “[I worry about] leaving myself on Zoom etc and transmitting myself saying offensive things/getting canceled. Lots around social justice, maybe kinda like religious OCD but for internet leftists.”

That woman touched on something especially striking, because while cancellation OCD in the social media context is relatively new, religious scrupulosity is a standard OCD subtype that has been well-documented for a long time. In this common OCD subtype, religious people worry about having accidentally committed sins, going to hell, or having sacrilegious thoughts, even if they don’t act on them or say anything. Because people with OCD can have their brains so easily hijacked by intrusive thoughts, it doesn’t take much for someone with religious OCD to fall down a rabbit hole of thinking increasingly creative sacrilegious things, being flooded with disturbing and demonic mental images, and performing various prayer and religious rituals to “correct” them. It’s hard to listen to someone with this OCD subtype and not immediately draw a parallel between this and cancellation OCD—because both rely on your thoughts making you a bad person, and people (or God) “discovering” something secretly horrible about you. But perhaps where cancellation OCD feels more legitimate: God cannot curse you for a misunderstanding or target you for a personal vendetta, because God loves everyone, and God knows what’s in your heart. However, loving everyone and knowing what’s in people’s hearts is not a trademark of Internet hate mobs.

Most literature on OCD will not talk about cancellation-based OCD obsessions, but that’s because this kind of fear is relatively recent. Around the early 2020s, OCD treatment centers began directly addressing this subtype. But cancel culture is probably past its peak (I hope so, anyway) and it will be interesting to see if this remains a common OCD subtype as time goes on.

Either way, we should acknowledge that cancellations (especially cancellations over ridiculous things) are real, and “this never happened” as a hand-waving explanation doesn’t work when all of us were using the Internet in 2019 and actively saw it happening. We can argue over how likely it was to happen to any individual person (admittedly, not likely) but OCD does not listen to “this is rare.” Tell that to spontaneous prion diseases, rabies, and brain-eating amoebas (I almost didn’t write that last one because I’m lowkey very scared of it and I’m afraid speaking it into existence will curse me with it for various cosmic reasons. This is the same reason I refuse to say I don’t believe in ghosts, because that’s exactly how the ghosts get you.)

But of course, the answer to any OCD subtype is not to demand 100% assurance that this bad thing won’t happen. I know that all too well, given how many times I’ve asked ChatGPT to calculate the precise odds of my loved ones having certain diseases. To starve the OCD of what it needs to survive, one must allow these intrusive thoughts to happen, and simply respond, “ok lol.” But that’s a lot easier to do if people don’t insist that having these thoughts at all, in any context, make you an irredeemable bigot.

Although, I suppose it’s a good exposure.
