You don’t need to hate people to save the planet

Condoms not clean power

At a high level, a country could take three paths to reduce carbon emissions: It could build out a lot of clean energy and related infrastructure, it could make life worse for everyone by wrecking its economy, or it could try to reduce the human population. Naturally, most green organizations have come to prefer the first approach, and in recent years, they’ve grown increasingly alarmed at how difficult it is to build solar, wind, nuclear, and other carbon-free power sources.

Last week, they might have gotten a solution: the Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act, co-authored by Sen. Sheldon Whitehouse, D-R.I., arguably the chamber’s most climate-obsessed senator. The 400-plus-page bill streamlines the federal permitting process to make it easier to build all types of energy, the transmission needed to transport that energy, and basically any type of federal infrastructure project.

Not all the green groups were happy. The day after the text was released, roughly 90 environmental advocates gathered on a call to discuss it. One of the most prominent voices in the proceedings was Brett Hartl, the government affairs director at an Arizona-based nonprofit called the Center for Biological Diversity (CBD).

“This is an effort to stop this bill not to make it better,” he said, according to a transcript published by Punchbowl News. “If your organization is not opposing [the bill], I think you should step off.”

The senior director for government relations at The Wilderness Society chimed in: “This makes sense to me.”

No one on the call openly objected to Hartl’s scorched-earth stance (though, given that the transcript was leaked, we can surmise that not everyone was in total agreement). And it was hardly the first time CBD had taken on this role: When Congress tried to pass a permitting reform bill in 2022, the organization led a coalition of more than 650 groups opposing it. It later touted its role in killing the bill in an end-of-year recap.

The problem, in the view of CBD, is that any permitting reform effort limits the litigation potential of several environmental statutes, including the National Environmental Policy Act, the National Historic Preservation Act, the Clean Water Act, and the Endangered Species Act. “Essentially what this bill does is it hamstrings our ability to go after projects,” said Erik Schlenker-Goodrich of the Western Environmental Law Center during the call.

The ability to delay a project in court is a seductive tool that many environmental groups have used for good and for ill, but while other environmental organizations have begun slowly to accept the need for some kind of permitting reform, it is CBD that remains one of the bulwarks against reason. But the organization’s fringe opposition to human population growth, dubious claims to mass membership, and a record of obstructing projects that would reduce pollution do not set it as far apart from the broader environmental movement as one would hope. If it did, CBD wouldn’t be in a position to rally many of America’s major environmental organizations.

Environmentalism isn’t just one thing. Like all successful movements, it has absorbed people with different goals, backgrounds, and perspectives — and like all unsuccessful movements, it is sometimes paralyzed by internal contradiction. Some environmentalists are motivated by a monomaniacal focus on reducing climate emissions; others care more about building parks in suburban neighborhoods; and others still love to hunt, fish, or ski in relatively undisturbed wilderness.

What does the Center for Biological Diversity want? Instead of seeing humans as one of many species to nurture and protect — the “biological diversity” contained in its name — CBD is against building anything to make it possible for humans to flourish. Indeed, its actions and public statements suggest that its leaders think there should be many fewer people living altogether.

These are the people trying to steer federal climate policy. These are the people who every major green group in the country got on the phone with to plan their next strategy.

Who does CBD represent?

Antisocial politics breeds antisocial behavior.

Take, for instance, this public comment from CBD when the Office of Information and Regulatory Affairs solicited public comment about changing the federal public engagement process: Hartl, the government affairs director, quite literally told the agency to “please sincerely go fuck off.”

But what drew my attention was the claim made in the second sentence of Hartl’s response, that the Center for Biological Diversity represents “1.7 million members and supporters.” Could this be true? If so, it would mean CBD had almost five times as many members as the NAACP during its peak of the Civil Rights Movement in 1965 and nearly as many members as the American Federation of Teachers.

Checking one of its federal tax documents, I found that CBD reported having just 19 volunteers in 2024. It’s hard to imagine that a thriving grassroots organization with 1.7 million members has occasion for just 19 volunteers. On its website, the organization writes that “the Center has the support of more than 1.8 million members and online activists” (emphasis added). Could the organization be counting its social media followers as members? Well, on one of its newsletter pages, it says, “You can join the ranks of 1.8 million members and online activists who support our fight” by just… signing up for a free weekly newsletter.

Moreover, its webpage describes online activism as participating in petitions and email comment campaigns; it describes membership as a means to “contribute regularly to support our work.” And in a 2017 Freedom of Information Act request, CBD said it had just 58,016 members who receive its printed newsletters, 52,200 followers on Twitter, and 233,000 people who have “liked” the Center on Facebook.

Harvard political scientist Theda Skocpol documented the hollowness of many contemporary membership organizations in her seminal 2003 work Diminished Democracy: From Membership to Management in American Civic Life. The book argues that “professionally managed organizations [are] ... less democratic and participatory than the pre-1960s membership federations they displaced.”

CBD may have a dubious claim to representing any kind of popular will, but I’ll give it this: It punches well above its weight — so much so that it has arguably elbowed its way into a permanent role at various levels of government.

In California, an official at a governmental body that oversees water resources for more than 700,000 people said, “I have the Center for Biological Diversity and Endangered Habitat League at almost every single one of my meetings … So by having that open dialog, it’s really helpful to get a process started that you work through over the years of planning, so in the end, everyone knows what they are getting out of it.”

At the federal level, the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service is responsible for listing endangered species and designating critical habitats. In 2011, then-President Obama’s Fish and Wildlife Service director, Dan Ashe, testified that his agency had spent at least $15.8 million of its $20.9 million allocated for this purpose on actions required by court orders or settlements against the agency. One of its most frequent plaintiffs? You guessed it: CBD.

For groups like CBD, success means that democratically elected officials and their appointees are forced to spend much of their time and resources placating a fringe group with no popular mandate in order to keep the wheels of government turning.

CBD’s specialty is suing the federal government for procedural violations. Other than the Sierra Club and its local affiliates, CBD is the most common plaintiff in appellate lawsuits against the federal government under the National Environmental Policy Act.

So when a bipartisan coalition decides to make it harder to block projects created for the public good, CBD, naturally, is freaking out. This is its bread and butter. It does not have a mass movement of people behind it. It does not have a persuasion strategy. All it has is the ability to make life deeply unpleasant through litigation.

Environmentalists against mass transit and clean energy

In 2015, Texas A&M released its annual urban mobility report and found, unsurprisingly, that the D.C. metro region was the most congested in the country. Auto commuters experienced 82 hours of delays due to traffic, over 30% more than the national average for very large cities.

For decades, Washington-area lawmakers had kicked around the idea of a transit project connecting various large cities and towns to each other. It was supported by local elected officials, by advocates representing service workers, by environmentalists and by smart-growth advocates. But there’s one creature they forgot to ask.

The Hay’s spring amphipod is eyeless, unpigmented, and elusive. When found under the detritus and foliage of Rock Creek Park — the only place it has ever been found — it measures no greater than 10 millimeters. Scientists don’t know what it eats, they don’t know what it does, they’re not even exactly sure how it … reproduces.

Leslie Hubricht, a self-taught scientist, discovered the shrimp-like creature in 1938 and noted that some “believed water from a spring to be impure and unfit for drinking unless it contained them.” This is the only suspected use of the Hay’s spring amphipod. A harbinger.

In 1980, when the Department of the Interior proposed adding what was affectionately referred to as a “scud” to the endangered species list, no public comments were received. Two years later, and without much fanfare, the amphipod became Washington’s only endangered species.

Thirty-five years later, it made it on my shit list.

I read everything I could get my hands on about the Hay’s spring amphipod. A small part of me hoped that it was an integral part of the Washington-area ecosystem, that somehow this little creature was standing between my home and ecological collapse. Because the alternative is almost too absurd to fathom.

The alternative is that this useless, colorless creature has cost the state of Maryland billions of dollars and has prevented me, personally, from being able to ride the Purple Line train from Silver Spring, Maryland, to Bethesda, Maryland.

But it’s really not fair to heap my ire on this useless scud. It was a mere tool in the hands of greater foes.

In 2014, CBD joined with a handful of wealthy local residents to warn the Federal Transit Administration (FTA) that it hadn’t sufficiently taken into account the transit project’s impact on amphipods. The agency was at risk of violating the Endangered Species Act, cautioned those who signed the notice-of-intent passage.

At the time, the U.S Fish and Wildlife Service had already noted that the nearest spring where these creatures were known to live was 4.5 miles away and therefore unlikely to be impacted by the development of the rail line. The signatories demanded that the federal government invest more time and money into conducting a lengthy supplemental report before concluding that the amphipod was not at risk.

Not a single one of these legal challenges was upheld. Courts have largely found that government regulators appropriately assessed the risk, took care to consider alternatives, and have diligently worked to provide opportunities for public comment. But despite the eventual losses suffered by the plaintiffs, amphipod-related legal challenges have caused the project to balloon by more than $130 million. (And that’s before you count the costs to commuters still stuck in unresolved congestion.) Once slated to open in 2020, the Purple Line is now projected to open in late 2027.

These lawsuits were apparently too absurd for even CBD to officially join, but their involvement lent legitimacy to plaintiffs who were otherwise easily portrayed as unsympathetic country club snobs. The wealthy town of Chevy Chase gave one of the litigating organizations $10,000 to pay for biology professor David Culver, an expert on Hay’s spring amphipod, to traipse around the parts of Rock Creek Park that extend into Maryland to see if he could rustle up evidence of the crustacean. He was unsuccessful. But, in a more important way, he did exactly what the project’s opponents needed him to do: He gave them time.

Delay is the currency of groups like CBD. In general, the government eventually prevails in these lawsuits — suggesting that the problem here is not actual violations of the environmental laws in question — but the time it takes to inoculate yourself from legal challenge and then actually deal with that legal challenge is so costly and time-consuming that it stifles many projects before they start. In many cases, the government would rather reach a settlement quickly.

Basically every environmentalist in the world thinks we need public transit in dense areas in order to displace cars. So what do we make, then, of a group that looked at a dense, progressive county with more than 1 million people — a place that can in no way be categorized as the wilderness — and decided tangible emissions benefits, tangible congestion benefits, and tangible benefits to economic growth were worth less than the hypothetical well-being of a colorless, mysterious, undetectable amphipod that was ultimately never found anywhere near the construction site?

In some cases, CBD dispenses with the pretense of ecological concerns altogether when it tries to block a project. The group opposed a portion of the SunZia high-voltage transmission line, which was designed to deliver wind energy from New Mexico to population centers in Arizona and California, partially on the grounds that Arizona’s San Pedro Valley just wouldn’t look right if it had tall transmission towers running through it — even though the valley already hosts other industrial infrastructure, including smaller transmission towers and a major natural gas pipeline.

Climate journalist and The Argument columnist Robinson Meyer asked CBD co-founder Robin Silver why he thought the new line would degrade the “cultural” value of the valley while the earlier development did not. “The gas pipeline doesn’t have 200 foot towers,” Silver replied.

However specious its arguments, CBD’s sheer tenacity has struck fear into the hearts of regulators. In a December 2009 interview, CBD co-founder Kierán Suckling bragged about the power his organization had in forcing the government to the bargaining table: “The Forest Service needs our agreement to get back to work,” he said, adding that by using injunctions and bad PR, the organization could get the government to capitulate: “Psychological warfare is a very underappreciated aspect of environmental campaigning.”

Does CBD love animals or just hate people?

Among environmental organizations, CBD is an outlier in its unique distaste for the human species. Most greens have, at least outwardly, shed their bizarre preoccupation with overpopulation that plagued the movement in the 20th century.

In 1968, Stanford academic Paul Ehrlich’s book The Population Bomb supercharged concerns about overpopulation. Ehrlich himself served as the first president of the group Zero Population Growth, a group with a helpfully self-explanatory name. (As historian Keith Woodhouse once wrote of the group, though, “The means, troublingly, were not yet specified.”)

While you will still hear older environmentalists reference overpopulation concerns, those concerns have fallen out of fashion due to their obvious unpopularity and tension with the pro-immigration and pro-working-class left. CBD, however, still carries the torch, in part by distributing condoms under its “population and sustainability” initiative.

In fact, earlier this year, CBD went to the University of Arizona to distribute “sexual health emergency preparedness kits” in case students found themselves wanting to have sex during “climate-fueled severe weather emergencies” but couldn’t go out to buy “family-planning” supplies.

The motivating ideology of CBD is fundamentally anti-human. In one 2013 newsletter, it asked, “If Condoms Can’t Save the World, Will TV Do the Trick?” I’m sure you can figure out where this is going, but it’s still a shock to read:

It turns out there are direct correlations between TV viewing, access to cable, the number of TVs in a household and the decline in a nation’s fertility rate. More TVs being watched means fewer babies being made. So although there are decidedly some big minuses associated with obsessive TV watching (creation of passive, mindless drone-people and couch-potato obesity, to name just a couple), we’d like to point out there may be a silver lining in that cloud.

Endangered Species Condoms today, Endangered Species flat-screens tomorrow!

Listen, I love that America is full of weirdos with fringe beliefs. But what I don’t love is that a group of weirdos with fringe beliefs is A) portraying itself as a mainstream environmental organization, B) acting as though it’s on the same side as green groups who are primarily concerned about climate change and pollution, C) pretending to be a mass membership organization, and D) threatening lawsuits in order to prevent governments from being able to act in the best interests of everyone — not just the unusually litigious.

The new permitting bill in Congress would make it easier for human beings to flourish on this planet. It’s true that building transmission lines across the country will create consequences for local environments. We can and will mitigate those when possible. Wildlife is important, and biodiversity is worth protecting.

But so are human beings and our ability to live full, meaningful, and comfortable lives. We deserve a seat at the table.

Green groups, I’m begging you: Don’t negotiate with the terrorists; take away their guns.
