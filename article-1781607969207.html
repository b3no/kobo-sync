Sometimes you have to hurt people

Against the tyranny of the edge case

Jeremiah Johnson
Jun 16

∙

Phoebe Bridgers recently announced that her upcoming tour would feature “no phones” at all shows. Fans would be required to leave phones at home or lock them in Yondr pouches for the duration of the show.

While many fans expressed excitement and viewed the move positively, other fans went ballistic at the thought of not being able to use their phones. If we’re allowed to be honest, the root cause is obviously that Bridgers’ fanbase skews toward Gen Z, and many of them are utterly addicted to their phones. They find the thought of having to mainline reality without filtering it through the black mirror of their smartphone existentially terrifying.

But they couldn’t exactly say “I’m pathetically addicted to my phone,” so instead these angry fans made all kinds of other claims. The cost-of-living crisis entitles them to record concerts. Banning phones is classist. Comparisons were made to racial segregation. But the most interesting claim to me was that banning phones would hurt disabled people. One user claimed to experience “pseudo-seizures” and said that only looking at their phone could calm them down and stop the seizure.

One approach to this kind of ridiculous claim is to argue in detail about the merits of the claim. I could argue that “looking at my phone” is not a medically necessary intervention for pseudo-seizures. I could mention that pseudo-seizures are a murky concept, a psychogenic “diagnosis of exclusion“ that medical resources claim is difficult to distinguish from malingering,¹ and subject to abuse.

I could further point out that perhaps a gigantic arena concert is not the best place for someone who describes themselves as having seizures and passing out in response to overstimulation. I might even mention that if someone did need their phone, they could simply step into the venue’s concourse and use said phone there, away from the show.

But all of those arguments are in some way accepting a faulty premise. What I actually want to argue is that even if you take every single silly claim at face value, even if you allow that some small number of disabled people will be excluded or harmed by this decision, that’s OK. There are times when it’s fine to exclude or harm people. And we need to be better at saying that out loud.

The tyranny of the edge case

If there’s any possible way that an action could harm someone - no matter how unlikely the scenario is, how small the harm might be, or how easily it might be mitigated - there will exist people who demand that nobody take that action. God forbid the action intersect with any of the various -isms like racism, sexism, or ableism. You’ll probably get dragged to the Hague. This is what I call “the tyranny of the edge case.”

It would be bad enough if this absurdity were limited to social media discourses and online nonsense. But this worldview is now infecting society beyond the crumbling walls of Twitter and Reddit:

  • Standardized testing might conceivably harm students of color, so we must abolish it.

  • Common-sense criminal justice reforms might perpetuate racial disparities, so we can’t implement them.

  • A solar plant might endanger the threecorner milkvetch, so it must be delayed.

  • We can’t build housing because new buildings might cast shadows that would be mildly unpleasant, or because some people would be sad to lose a historic laundromat.

Recently, Texas Tech quarterback Brendan Sorsby was revealed to be an inveterate gambler, one who had even bet on games involving his own team. There’s not yet any evidence that he threw games, but he did bet on games as an insider who knew the state of his team’s readiness better than any outsider could have.

Naturally, the NCAA banned him, but Sorsby sued the athletic organization, arguing that he was a gambling addict and in recovery, and that he would be irreparably harmed by being banned from college football. This week a Texas judge granted him an injunction that will allow him to play anyway, and the injunction’s reasoning is filled with edge-case nonsense. It argues that Sorsby would be deprived of “coaching, resources and camaraderie” if removed from the team, that he would face “hardships” against his rights and interests if he can’t play, and that as long as he’s in treatment for his gambling addiction he can’t be prevented from being a member of the team.

In other words, you can’t ban a gambler who undermined the integrity of the sport because he might be addicted to gambling!

To state the obvious: yes, you can and should ban athletes who have been caught gambling on their own team’s games. This will surely harm Sorsby’s future, and nobody should care. You should build housing regardless of the fact that shadows are inconvenient. You should build solar plants even if they might endanger a weed.²

I wish I could tell you that this sort of ridiculous appeal to ableism is unique to BridgersGate, but it’s not. We see the same pattern repeated in all sorts of highly online controversies. DoorDash discourse features constant appeals to the needs of the disabled - such that certain people simply must order DoorDash because they have depression, anxiety, executive function disorder or, even more bizarrely, because of histamines. It was claimed by various parties that eating any kind of food you want is a human right, and that it was “eugenics by proxy“ to make fun of these kinds of posts.

While DoorDash discourse inspired more think pieces, I think an even purer example came in the form of 2022’s Chili Neighbor incident. A woman mused about sending some homemade chili to her new neighbors, and a group of commenters immediately made her behavior the main course. Was she a “white savior”? What if they have allergies? Or if they’re vegan? Have you even considered that they might not have bowls?

Again, you could argue with these folks on an object level. Most people have bowls! If they don’t have bowls, you could simply let them borrow some. But I think this misses the point. Even if we grant the insane premise that the new neighbors might not have bowls, and that they will suffer intense psychic damage when offered a pot of chili due to this lack of bowls, and even if there’s no possible way to mitigate that situation, it’s still fine to offer them the chili.

We’re going to have to accept a little risk

Marc Dunkelman’s Why Nothing Works offers a glimpse into how we reached this point. The book argues that American society has spent the last half-century constructing a never-ending set of safeguards, bureaucratic checks, appeals processes, and reviews. The fast, muscular government that implemented the New Deal was replaced with a cautious, vetocratic monster with a thousand ways to say no and few ways to say yes. Every individual step can be defended - this rule protects the environment, this review makes sure disabled people aren’t left behind, this court checks to see if the community has been consulted, etc. But the end result is a system where nothing gets done.

This is what the tyranny of the edge case looks like in practice. It’s not an exaggeration to say that we have entire systems of bureaucracy whose sole purpose is to identify hypothetical harms and potential victims and stop things from happening - sometimes until those harms have been addressed, sometimes just forever.

We live in a complex world, and virtually any policy change could feasibly harm someone. So in practice, progress halts altogether. We get stuck in the status quo, trapping ourselves in amber to preserve whatever the current arrangement of things is (no matter if the current arrangement also harms some people).

The way forward—and I mean this with complete sincerity—is to stop giving a shit about claims that “someone, somewhere might get hurt” as a general style of argument. Someone, somewhere is always getting hurt even if you’re doing everything right. By all means, let’s do some reasonable checks to make sure that people aren’t being hurt an inordinate amount. Let’s mitigate the harms where possible. But “someone might be harmed” is not an all-purpose argument.

In order for society to progress, we’re going to have to accept a little risk. You can’t develop vaccines without human trials. You can’t build a bridge or a road or a train station without displacing something. The correct response is to admit that some people might lose out, and that’s just fine. Some people might have to live with a shadow on their building because the need for more housing is more important than their annoyance at shadows. Certain people might be excluded from the Phoebe Bridgers concert because the phone-free atmosphere for the other 30,000 attendees is more important than their edge case.

Congestion pricing in New York City serves as a useful model here. During the process of implementing the congestion tax in Manhattan (which was already delayed by a multiyear 4,000-page NEPA review) New York had to deal with dozens of claims about who should be exempt. It wasn’t fair to people who lived in the congestion zone and drove. It wasn’t fair to people in New Jersey, or the outer boroughs. It wasn’t fair to students, or the elderly, or the working man. It wasn’t fair to low-income people, or the disabled. Exemptions were requested for farmers, artists, cops, people of color, judges, taxis, commuters, motorcyclists, trucks, teachers and tradesmen. There were 122 different formally requested exemptions! That’s insane!

What did New York do? They looked at all the complaints and all the requested exemptions, granted a small handful of partial exemptions, and steamrolled the rest of the people. Maybe your group is a net loser from this policy. Representatives of our democratic process weighed the pros and cons and decided that was the best thing to do. Deal with it.

These representatives believed congestion pricing was a good idea that would on net make the city a better place. They did not view “my extremely sympathetic group might lose out” as a reason to stop the program. Congestion pricing absolutely created winners and losers, and that’s ok. Government should be in the business of making positive-sum changes.
