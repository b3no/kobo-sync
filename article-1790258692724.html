The Q&A: “I don't think we're looking at pooled sovereignty, no”

Jonathan Wilkinson, Canada’s ambassador-designate to the EU, on how a country picks friends

Paul Wells
Sep 24
 
READ IN APP
 

I get that the EU is complex. When I was 26 I studied European institutions in Europe for a year; I’ve written about the EU in most years since then; and I still get nervous about whether I’ll keep the European Council and the European Commission straight in conversations. When he was Opposition leader, Stephen Harper visited an EU country’s diplomatic post in Ottawa. His questions were so full of the approximations that came naturally to an occasional reader of The Spectator that his hosts sent him home with assigned readings. (He read them; Harper always did the reading.)

So last week’s frenzy about whether Canada will be an Associate Member of the EU, when the term Associate Member has no historic meaning at all — one might as well ask whether Canada will become a scarlet tanager — was understandable. Labels are easier to grasp than actions. But I wasn’t sure we’d all learned much, so I asked Jonathan Wilkinson for an interview.

Wilkinson was variously Justin Trudeau’s minister of the environment or of national resources. He held the latter post in Mark Carney’s ephemeral first cabinet. This year he resigned his North Vancouver — Capilano seat and moved to Brussels to be Canada’s ambassador to the EU. Which he sort of is now. I’ll let him tell the story.

This transcript has been edited for length and clarity. To me one of the most amusing parts is that, after a week when everyone was trying to figure out what Canada and the EU might possibly be talking about, Wilkinson reminded me that Canada and the EU published a 40-point plan for these discussions a year ago and almost everyone ignored it.

Paul Wells: Jonathan Wilkinson, ambassador-designate to the European Union, thanks for joining me.

Jonathan Wilkinson: Not at all. Thanks for having me.

PW: Am I right that that’s where you are on your journey from member of Parliament to member of the invading European hordes? Have you presented your credentials? And who do you present your credentials to?

Wilkinson: [I’ve actually been on the job] for three months. But of course, Europeans tend to go away a lot in the summer. And the complexity with the European Union is, you have to present them to both President von der Leyen of the Commission [Ursula von der Leyen, former German defence minister] and President Costa of the Council [Antonio Costa, a former Portuguese prime mnister] in the same room at the same time. And getting them together has proved to be a little bit challenging. But honestly, it doesn’t really affect what I do.

PW: And that begins to get us into the complexities of the European Union. Now, what was your mandate from Prime Minister Carney when he sent you there?

Wilkinson: It was to essentially work very hard to help to deepen and strengthen the relationship between Canada and the European Union in some hopefully very substantive ways. I have hit the ground running. I’ve been very actively involved with folks across all three of the institutions, the Commission, the Council and Parliament.
And I think we’re making some good progress.

PW: Okay. And how does your work interrelate with the work of John Hannaford, the former clerk of the Privy Council, who is the Prime Minister’s personal envoy to Europe?

Wilkinson: Well, John and I work together very closely. It probably helps that when I was Minister of Natural Resources, he was my deputy for over two years. So we know each other very well. John obviously was working before I was, because he was named several months before I was actually sent over here. So he initially took the lead on a lot of different things. Presently, a large amount of John’s time is spent focused on aligning the various departments in Ottawa with respect to the work done there. I am much more, you know, customer-facing vis-a-vis the European Union, and we work on strategy together.

PW: To some extent, it sounds like he’s in charge of scrounging up what it is you can you can offer in these conversations.

Wilkinson: It’s more than what we can offer. A lot of the specific initiatives that we are contemplating with the European Union are collaborative. So there’s a lot of conversation that has to go on internally, as well as with the EU. And of course, there have to be conversations with our provinces and territories, just as there have to be with their member states.

PW: Then this thing happened last week where the Prime Minister visited Strasbourg and the Wall Street Journal had a story and, well, everybody in Canada got awfully excited about the terms of an eventual new arrangement. It seemed really vague and yet moving very quickly because there’s a Canada-EU summit in Canada in a month and it sounds like some decisions are going to be made or announced there. What’s the process?

Wilkinson: We’ve been working on this now for several months with the EU in anticipation of the summit at the end of October in Canada. And let’s be clear, there were things that were being done beforehand. I mean, CETA has been in place as a trade agreement for 10 years.

There were a number of commitments coming out of the summit last year that we hope to actually complete over the next month or so before the next summit.

But we’ve been working internally to develop a whole range of ideas in specific areas, often focused in areas the Prime Minister has described as being strategic capabilities, areas where you really want to have access to either technologies or products that don’t leave you fully dependent on the goodwill of one or both of the large entities globally.

We obviously did a lot of that work internally. We have subsequently been working with the European Union folks who were doing the same work internally. We hope to be in a position to announce a bunch of those things at the end of October. But the Strasbourg visit and the Prime Minister’s speech to the European Parliament was a step along the way, to give people a sense of where this is going — the details of which will become apparent quite soon.

PW: In what fields are these are these new arrangements likely to take place? I keep hearing about Horizon and Erasmus, one of which Canada doesn’t belong to and one of which Canada has only very recently joined, and nobody in Canada knows what either of those programmes are. So can you shine some light on this?

Wilkinson: Sure. I mean, the work has been going on in a number of sectoral areas. So AI, supercomputing, digital services and technologies is kind of one space. Energy, critical minerals, critical resources are another.

There is work going on on a whole range of security related-initiatives. There are some in the area of labour mobility. Erasmus is probably the highest-profile, the Prime Minister mentioned it in his speech, which is about youth mobility, youth being able to actually travel, go to school in any country in Europe. Canada’s interested in potentially joining that program.

But as you say, we have done that initially with SAFE, which is a defence procurement program, and then with Horizon, which is a research programme that encourages Canadian universities to work, for example, with European universities. And we are looking at furthering our collaboration there. So there’s a whole range of things. I think Canadians will see some good progress and some really interesting ideas when we get to October.

PW: Okay. All that sounds international — the common commerce of nations where we agree there’s going to be a soccer exchange between Canada and Slovakia or that sort of thing — as distinct from supranational arrangements which involve
pooled sovereignty and a decision to take common decisions, whether or not really you agree with them. I think this term “associate membership” suggests that these arrangements would slide from international into supranational. Do you get the distinction? And is that a road that Canada is interested in traveling?

Wilkinson: Oh, I get the distinction.

PW: I have to imagine that was on page two of your briefing book.

Wilkinson: It was probably on page two of my master’s degree. [He was a Rhodes scholar and then earned an MA in international relations from McGill.] So yeah, I think we’re probably looking largely at the international, although we’re interested in ongoing conversations at political and bureaucratic levels that probably are are more frequent in their nature and intensity than might be the case with other states. But I don’t think we’re looking at pooled sovereignty, no.

PW: Now, here’s the question: Why? Why do any of this? Your predecessor was a perfectly busy Canadian ambassador to the EU. Why push things?

Wilkinson: At least two reasons. The first is, the Prime Minister talked about the need for Canada to become more resilient. That certainly doesn’t mean we’re not going to have a relationship, hopefully a constructive relationship, with the United States. We will always do that just by dint of geography. But it is important for us to become less dependent on one particular market and one
particular country. The European Union is one of those areas where we actually think there’s significant things we can do to enhance the level of trade and to enhance conversations around economic security.

But it’s also the case that the European Union is a grouping of countries that share the values that Canadians hold to: a commitment to multilateralism, a commitment to democracy and democratic institutions, to a rules-based international order. By working with the European Union first, and subsequently creating a model to which other countries could effectively join — perhaps not exactly in the same manner as Canada, but could come together — we can buttress some of those values, in a world where they are certainly under pressure.

We are not prepared to simply give up to a world in which the great powers effectively make all of the decisions. If you want to be sovereign, part of it is actually figuring out your friends and trying to ensure that you are as strong as possible.

PW: Europe can be a strange place. You can’t find anyone to give your credentials to and you’re working to advance a trade treaty that 10 countries haven’t ratified.

Wilkinson: Well, I would say to you, they’re probably actually similar in terms of the response. At the end of the day, whether you presented your credentials or not, the day-to-day job is effectively exactly the same. And with CETA, yes, 10 have not ratified. We would like them to ratify. But 98% of CETA has been in force for 10 years. We’ve seen significant increases in trade on both sides. So it’s actually doing what we had hoped it would do. And I think that’s the real task.

PW: So there’s institutions, which are endlessly fascinating, and there’s the practise of relations between Canada and the European Union. I mean, I’ve seen figures that suggest that large numbers of Canadian businesses aren’t aware that we’ve got free trade with Europe, and that significant numbers of Canadian businesses haven’t taken advantage of what that offers.

Wilkinson: I think that’s true. And I think we have to do a better job, even though CETA has been quite successful. I think we have to do a much better job of engaging Canadian businesses and ensuring that they can understand and navigate what at times is perceived as the complexity of the European Union and the 27 member states.
So we have some work to do in that regard. I think the Europeans, on the other hand, probably also have similar work to do. But there is opportunity there beyond what we’ve seen in terms of the growth in trade that has come since CETA was in place.

PW: So what’s going to happen in October? First of all, is it all going to be leaked to the Wall Street Journal before it happens? And secondly, will there then be a draft treaty that has to go to the various parliaments?

Wilkinson: I certainly hope it’s not leaked to the Wall Street Journal. It’s not really up to me, I guess. I think our hope is that we can get to the summit and actually have the announcements made there and then have conversations with you and many in the media post-summit.

PW: You and me both, brother. But journalistic carping aside, are we going to be at some kind of decision point in October, or is it going to be sort of a report to parties about progress? I remember the negotiations around CETA. They lasted most of the time that Stephen Harper was Prime Minister. And he was there for a decade.

Wilkinson: As you will appreciate watching domestic politics in Canada, this particular Prime Minister really wants to see things accomplished, and accomplished quickly. So I think you will see a number of things have been completed by the time we get to the summit. Many of them were promised in the last summit declaration. And you will see a number of things that we are committing to do, but not a 5 or a 10 year timeline, committing to do in the next 12 months. This is something that we really want to move ahead.

PW: There is an impression in many circles in Canada that Europe is essentially a scary place. Runaway migration, runaway bureaucracy, over-taxation, under-representation. When you saw glimpses of that perception last week, were you surprised? What do you have to say to people who think that Europe is essentially more of the kind of mess that Canada should be trying to get out of?

Wilkinson: There certainly is a narrative that some who, I would say, particularly in Europe, tend to come from the farther right of the spectrum, have put out with respect to the European Union. But I would say this is a grouping of 27 countries, all of whom are wealthy. Enormous market of over 400 million people. It is a grouping of countries that share the values that Canadians hold to and that are very united with respect to things that we care about, like the defence of Ukraine. So I think there’s enormous things that we can gain by deepening the relationship with Europe, including ensuring that Canada is more resilient moving forward.

And to those who may try to say, well, there are certain problems in Europe and therefore they’re all going to be exported to Canada, I would just say that’s not a very thoughtful thing to say. They’ve had, for example, trouble with irregular migration in Europe. That’s because North Africa is proximate to Europe and they have much difficulty addressing their borders. That’s not true in Canada. We have oceans on three sides of us.

We are being very strategic about what we’re talking about, including in areas like labour mobility, where it’s, you know, things like Erasmus. I know that Canadians would embrace that kind of a programme for young people. It offers opportunity for Canadian youth. And this is not something that’s going to be deductive for Canada. It’s going to be additive, including to our overall sovereignty.

PW: I should probably wrap it up. How have perceptions of Canada been affected by Prime Minister Carney’s visit last week and by all of the parsing that surrounded it? How’s your job different than it was 10 days ago?

Wilkinson: I would just say for the Canadian ambassador here, it’s really easy to get meetings. Everybody wants to talk to Canada right now. And that’s not just after last week. A big moment was the Davos speech. Everybody still talks about the Davos speech here. For many, people see our Prime Minister as one of the leaders of the countries that continue to aspire to some of those values that I spoke about before.
