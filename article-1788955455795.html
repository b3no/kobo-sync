Fellas, Is It Cringe To Think 9/11 Was Bad?

The Irony Poisoned come for 9/11

Cartoons Hate Her
Sep 9
∙
Paid
 
READ IN APP
 

As we come closer to the twenty-fifth anniversary of the September 11 terrorist attacks, it has come to my attention that some younger folks don’t understand why 9/11 was ever a big deal or why we memorialize it at all. How cringe! How millennial! Slap a fucking mustache on the Twin Towers and call it a day, why don’t ya!

As irritated as this kind of rhetoric makes me, part of the issue is that tragic events understandably don’t register the same way for people who weren’t alive when they happened, and what’s more: over time, they become funny. (You know the old adage, “Comedy is tragedy plus time.”) There is no set period of time at which point a tragic event can become funny, but eventually, it just sort of dawns on a population that certain jokes are no longer “too soon.” (Most of us have no qualms making jokes about the Titanic or the Black Plague, for example.) But typically, when it comes to the death of thousands of innocent people, this line is not crossed for multiple generations. Even mass death events with fewer casualties than 9/11, which occurred around the same time (see: Columbine) are not treated with this degree of flippant levity.

While some edgy comics joked about 9/11 right away (and were typically heckled for it), it feels like 9/11 uniquely barreled past “too soon” in just one generation, or perhaps even faster than that. So what was it about 9/11 that made it both a significant traumatic memory for an entire generation and an easy laugh for the next one?

First off, I should point out that my experience with 9/11 was not the modal experience for a random person in my generation, but may at least partially explain why people like me are so “worshipful” about it. And even then, while I find 9/11 jokes pretty distasteful, I can’t say I think about it nonstop, talk about it in therapy, or even post about it every year (I think this is my first time, actually.)

I was raised right outside New York City, so my parents frequently went into the city for work, as did many of my friends’ parents. (Sorry, fact check time: I did not have enough “friends” for the sample size there to mean anything, but I did have classmates.) I found out about the attacks when my second period math teacher—the “fun” teacher who sometimes jumped on students’ desks during quizzes to throw them off—answered a knock on the classroom door and received a paper from a lone hand appearing from stage left. He furrowed his brow and started mumbling, “Oh, this is bad..this is really bad.” Then, he presumably read a sentence that told him not to tell the children what was going on, so he saved face by throwing the paper in the trash and saying, “Siiiiike! Extra homework tonight!”

A kid nearby whispered to another that someone had driven a plane into a tall building in New York City. I rolled my eyes and said, “Yeah right.” The kid shrugged and said, “It’s not impossible. The buildings are really high.”

I forget exactly how we found out that this crash had been purposeful, but at that point, all hell broke loose. It was, as Trump might say, “Bedlam.” Imagine 1,000 children in a middle school, the vast majority of whom have parents working in New York City, none of whom have cell phones to get in touch with their families, frantically trying to figure out if their parents were still alive, with the seventy-year-old front desk receptionist who probably had some name like Mrs. Cannoli as the bottleneck for incoming and outgoing calls. Keep in mind that we had no idea if more planes were coming, so we did not necessarily trust that the attacks would stop at the Twin Towers. Perhaps our parents didn’t work at the World Trade Center, but what if another office building, Grand Central Station or Times Square was next?

The issue was somehow even worse at my cousin’s school (also right outside New York City) where September 11 also happened to be picture day, and students were encouraged to hold back their tears and smile for their school photos as they wondered whether their parents were dead.

Obviously, 9/11 was not quite this close to home for kids all around America. But I’m sure it was still deeply disturbing for kids in Kansas or California. It sounds too obvious to say, but perhaps it needs saying: it’s traumatic for a child to suddenly find out that a plane has been deliberately driven into a building, both full of innocent civilians who died horrific and painful deaths. Not because the toll was especially high (and certainly not high compared to diseases and wars around the globe) but because it opens up a new possibility: you are not safe anywhere. This was not just my experience: anxiety disorders among children within New York City public schools dramatically increased after 9/11, with a particular sharp increase in agoraphobia.

I was probably always going to have OCD (genetics were not on my side here) but 9/11 was my first major anxiety-focused “core memory.” I don’t really remember feeling intense, everyday anxiety before that point. Suddenly, I was afraid to go to malls, to get on planes, or even to take a bus. Going into the city with my parents was unthinkable. One of the 9/11 after-effects that people rarely mention is the lingering anxiety that a “second 9/11” would happen. And…there were foiled attempts! Then, there were the hoaxes, which were oddly just as unsettling for a child. My family received multiple viral “chain emails” (the original misinformation) claiming to have inside information that terrorist attacks were going to happen at various suburban locations, most notably the local mall on Halloween (my parents scoffed at these, but I took them more seriously as I was a child.) In hindsight, some of these hoaxes were clearly ridiculous (My husband recalls his midwestern hometown being terrified that Osama Bin Laden would target their strip mall.) Silly as it may seem now, suddenly Americans were second-guessing the danger of public locations that previously seemed obviously safe.

Perhaps this feeling of anxiety is something baked into the minds of Gen Z (obligatory: not all) because they have been exposed to the worst of the Internet since early childhood, and can’t open their feeds without being bombarded with videos of people dying, stories of mass shootings, and nonstop updates about various atrocities. (No doubt many of these atrocities were happening in 2001, and most American children just weren’t privy to them.) But the correct response to this is not that millennials just needed to get over the 9/11 nonsense because it’s not a big deal if 3,000 people, including several children, are violently killed while going about their day, because after all, things are worse elsewhere. The correct response is that actually, it’s not normal to mainline violence and gore for multiple hours a day, and this type of behavior (intentional or not) creates dangerous and unhealthy desensitization to violence, not an appropriately nonchalant and edgy attitude toward it.

And yet, while clearly desensitized to mass violence on some level, many members of Gen Z aren’t afraid to say that other far more ordinary things are traumatic, from sleep training to graphic scenes in movies to four-year age gaps between consenting adults. So if all those things can be traumatic, surely we can understand why a terrorist attack that kills 3,000 people, which happens during your childhood, leaving you unsure of your parents’ fate for hours, might also meet the mark.

All this to say, even if the death toll didn’t compare to the covid pandemic, heart disease, or, for that matter, the criminal and unjust wars in the Middle East that came after 9/11, if you were a child when 9/11 happened you probably felt a lot like this, but unironically:

For the first time, I saw myself as living through history—something which I naively assumed was just kind of over by then. After all, there had been no American Girl Doll after World War Two! What other events could have possibly happened after that point? Oh shit..were more events going to keep happening? To me?

At this point you may be tempted to say I sound like the dumbest eleven-year-old ever. But I maintain my lack of awareness of modern tragedies had more to do with not having access to the Internet, and learning only what was taught in my school, than being irredeemably dumb. (Congrats if you somehow figured out by age ten, without living through it or anyone telling you about it, that the Rwandan genocide existed. Some of us are just not as intellectually gifted, I suppose.) The only historical tragedies with which I was familiar were the ones about which my parents and teachers told me. This left a lot of blank space, especially after 1970. Other than the trials and tribulations of the OJ trial (my dad’s special interest for about five straight years), I was innocent enough not to think horrific, violent things were still happening in the world on a mass scale.

But zoomers’ relative desensitization to “living through historical events,” true as it may be, is not the only reason that 9/11 so specifically became ripe for jokes and nonchalance. I have a strong suspicion that the zero-sum political polarization around 9/11 that actually began with millennials and our elders have seeped into Gen Z without much context.

Most of us are aware that 9/11 set off a chain of events that led to horrific wars and invasions, a global death toll far higher than that of 9/11 itself, not to mention the destabilization and further polarization of American politics, the Patriot Act, and perhaps most grim, the rise of Michael Moore. Because 9/11 was used as a justification for American aggression abroad and the ramping up of a surveillance state, reverence for 9/11 and its victims became seen as conservative-coded, even if you were not using it as justification for some other political agenda. This is not something that began with Gen Z. Even just a few years after 9/11, there was an unspoken rule (at least in my liberal teen circles) that caring too much about 9/11 implied approval of everything George W. Bush did, or implied apathy towards the millions of innocent people dying in the Middle East after the fact. This political polarization still exists today. According to a YouGov poll completed this year, Democrats are far less likely than Republicans to believe that we should have a national holiday commemorating the victims of 9/11. And while zoomers are far less likely than millennials to say 9/11 affected their lives, Gen X’ers and boomers (who also trend more conservative) are more likely to say it affected them.

As reverence toward 9/11 became seen as dorky and conservative, on the flip side, having “nuanced” opinions about how actually, America kind of deserved 9/11 even if it was sad was seen as the edgy, enlightened, leftist thing to say. See, zoomers? Millennials were doing it before it was cool. When I was eleven, it was expected that we would all be sad about 9/11. By the time I was sixteen, such behavior immediately singled you out as a future intern for Mike Huckabee.

Gen Z, predictably, followed suit. They were especially susceptible to this type of contrarian, edgelord, kneejerk thinking, in part because many of them are already steeped in a nihilistic and (dare I say) gullible worldview that just assumes everything their teachers and parents ever told them was a lie, and any evidence of these things is just more Western propaganda (see: life in North Korea is actually great, Ukraine is actually full of Nazis and Vladimir Putin is a good guy, the list goes on.) Therefore, it’s unsurprising that Osama Bin Laden (yes, Osama Bin Laden of “Osama Bin Wankin” fame) eventually fell into the role of “maligned victim of American propaganda.”

In 2023, a bunch of zoomers on TikTok discovered Osama Bin Laden’s Letter to America, outlining his reasons for loathing the West, and his reasoning for the belief that every American civilian deserved to die for the sins of their leaders because they paid taxes which funded the military. He was also not a fan of Western sexual mores, homosexuality, and, if you can believe it, the abundance of problematic age gaps in Western media (okay, I made that one up, but I’m sure it would do numbers on Insane TikTok.) Either way, a segment of TikTok-addled zoomers decided Osama Bin Laden actually made some great points. In an especially popular video with over 900,000 views, a creator proclaimed that reading the works of Osama Bin Laden enlightened them to the fact that, “everything we learned about the Middle East, 9/11, and ‘terrorism’ was a lie.”

Presumably, one can simultaneously hold the beliefs that the American military is responsible for some very awful things, and that American civilians shouldn’t be murdered for it. I still think most people hold this belief, including many young people! But you can see why, for some, 9/11 became a joke—they were negatively polarized into joking about it, because to be at all reverent of 9/11 meant that you were some kind of bootlicking rube who was buying all the “lies” fed to you by “them.”

Of course, as I mentioned before, blanket assumptions that everything you’ve been taught is a lie, and reflexive belief in the opposite of those things, is just as dumb and gullible as believing everything you hear. Not to give millennials too much credit here, but it feels like we were the first and perhaps last generation to be at all discerning about the source of the stuff that we hear, whether in real life or the Internet. But note that “discerning” does not mean “immediately distrusting.” Sometimes, everyone is telling you something because it’s true.

Then, there is the last component of my theory of zoomer nonchalance toward 9/11: a general distaste of earnestness. I have something else coming on this soon, so I won’t blow my load on this topic too quickly, but I have noticed that the desensitization of violence and gore came along with an oversensitive barometer for “cringe,” essentially triggered by any degree of earnestness. Yes, I’m aware this is just one tweet, so rest assured this is merely an example of a broader phenomenon:

Essentially, the idea is that millennials were uniquely cringe (ie: earnest, unironic) and this was a bad thing. Clearly, the correct way to be is sardonic, detached, and derisive by default. Therefore, any kind of emotion about an event—especially a conventionally sad event that is not particularly leftist or edgy-coded—is the ultimate cringe.

Perhaps we can convince them that we’re only mourning 9/11 ironically.

As annoying (and at times, offensive) as it can be, I understand why Gen Z does not feel the same way that their elders do about 9/11, especially compared with people like me, who were children living around New York City during the attacks. But, given everyone’s newfound obsession with “theory of mind,” you’d think they’d have the ability to understand why such a thing might be at least somewhat significant for someone who lived through it in their formative years—yes, even if doing so is unbearably cringe.
