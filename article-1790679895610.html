I’m with Nikole Hannah-Jones on this one

K-12 grades shouldn’t be meaningless

Kelsey Piper
Sep 29
∙
Paid
 
READ IN APP
 

Like many readers of The New York Times, I found Nikole Hannah-Jones’ reflection on her decision to send her daughter to one of New York City’s worst-performing schools both poignant and maddening. Hannah-Jones believed she could fix the school with the attention she would bring as a prominent journalist, but she ended up changing practically nothing as her daughter floundered.

Many elements of this story — Hannah-Jones’ reluctance to look at high-performing majority-Black schools, her lack of follow-through on her plan to fix the school, her continued insistence on the very educational policies that failed her family — have been discussed at length, but one piece of the picture remains, to my mind, underexplored: How did she not realize that her daughter was being failed?

Share

It took Hannah-Jones years to realize that her daughter was severely behind in school — that despite getting good grades, she had huge deficits in her math knowledge, for example, that led to her failing New York’s standardized Algebra I exam. Her daughter struggled on other standardized tests as well, but Hannah-Jones wrote, “I assumed she didn’t pass because, like many children, she has trouble sometimes on standardized tests.”

It’s easy to dissect Hannah-Jones’ choices, and it’s easy to say she in particular should have known better: She worked as a journalist on education policy! Why wasn’t she paying far more attention to how her daughter was doing in school?

But in this respect in particular, I’m on Hannah-Jones’ side. Student grades ought to reflect how well a student is doing. If a student is getting good grades, their parents ought to be able to assume that they’re adequately academically prepared for the next year’s material.

This is, these days, broadly not true.

When the University of California, San Diego, identified students who were extremely unprepared for college-level math, more than 25% of those students had a 4.0 in math on their high school transcripts. The High School Transcript Study by the National Assessment of Educational Progress (NAEP) has found the average transcript GPA climbing over the decades — from 2.63 in 1987 to 3.11 in 2019. Since 2013, it has risen even as student performance on NAEP testing falls.

In The Wall Street Journal, economist Roland Fryer related a story that shows how this trend can mislead even very involved parents:

Before one of those votes [to close an underperforming Massachusetts school], a Haitian woman approached me and explained what the school meant to her family. It was one of the few places where her daughter could speak their particular form of Creole. “Why are you closing our school?” she asked. “It’s a good school.”

I explained that the state data showed otherwise: Only a small fraction of the school’s students could read or do math at grade level.

“I don’t know about that,” she said. “But I do know this.” She reached into her purse and pulled out her daughter’s report cards. They were covered in A’s and A-pluses.

Grade inflation isn’t a new trend; as NAEP’s survey makes clear, it’s been on a steady march from the 1980s to the present. It has many drivers, including the fact that teachers are counseled for equity reasons not to give a student a zero for an assignment they didn’t complete. But in surveys, teachers report that the most common sources of pressure to change grades are students and parents.

Students and parents are most likely to pressure teachers to change grades

Well-intentioned efforts to better communicate with parents and set uniform standards have often just increased the confusion. Some schools have switched to “standards-based” grading in which the numbers one, two, three, and four are used instead of letter grades. While this was developed explicitly to be more accessible to parents, I have found that other parents often don’t know what a number grade means and can’t use it to tell whether their kids are behind or not.

In the late 2000s, teams of researchers from around the country put a huge amount of effort into writing the Common Core, a set of K-12 math and English standards that have been adopted in most states. The standards are meant to provide clarity about what students should learn at what level. But if you’re a parent trying to understand what your child should be learning, the Common Core is of limited assistance.

For example, the standards for third graders state that they should be able to “Recall information from experiences or gather information from print and digital sources; take brief notes on sources and sort evidence into provided categories.” That could describe a very wide range of levels of skill and execution, from “can read a book selected by the teacher and write down three bullet points, misspelled” to “can write a short research report.”

Which is actually reasonable to expect of third graders?

What you would want is a repository of Common Core work and guidance on how it is graded. Official examples of on-level third-grade work that parents can use as a reference to see how their own children are doing do exist; I make extensive use of them to understand my own kids’ performance. But these examples are mainly hosted in two places:

  1. A confusing, difficult-to-navigate third-party website that obliges you to download each student essay one at a time

  2. An even more inaccessible website that is meant for helping teachers grade to standards, not helping parents understand them

And if you try to learn what is being assigned or taught at your child’s school in particular, you will typically find very little information available.

The problem is not limited to traditional schools. For all the enthusiasm for school choice in some quarters, very little effort or energy has gone into making it easy for parents to know what different schools teach — or what they are expected to teach.

The case for school choice is that parents know best what works for their children and will pick schools where their children are succeeding. But that can’t happen — no matter how varied our options or generous our vouchers — unless parents can actually learn how their children are doing at school. Indeed, when Sweden massively expanded school choice, researchers found that competition among schools was a driver of grade inflation, as schools don’t want to lose customers by giving out bad grades.

So what are parents to do? Hannah-Jones, to be sure, could have known better. Among the luxuries afforded by a job as a Times education reporter is the fact that you can get any curriculum expert you’d like on the phone; you can check not only whether your student’s math homework is correct but also whether it is appropriate homework to have assigned in the first place. If Hannah-Jones had cared about the answer, she had the means to get it.

But most parents don’t. Most parents are dependent on their children’s schools for information, and many of those schools are not providing truthful information.

No one likes standardized tests. But ever since I started looking at the 2013 peak of NAEP scores in the U.S., it’s been clear to me that the accountability-and-testing regime under former President George W. Bush’s No Child Left Behind initiative was better than both what preceded it and what followed it.

Tests aren’t perfect, and, sure, it’s possible that your child has test anxiety and is performing below their innate capabilities. And it’s not as if schoolwide test fraud is unheard of. But on the whole, tests are the best option we have to communicate to families how their children are doing, relative to objective external standards. The retreat from testing as a focus and priority under the Obama administration has been bad for schools and students.

If your child gets good grades but poor standardized test scores — or good grades at a school with poor test scores across the board — they might just “test badly.” Or the school might be lying to them, and they might be extremely underprepared for future academic work.

There are ways to find out. You should check your kids’ homework; you should ask them questions about books that they’re reading or have them occasionally read to you. You should quiz them on simple arithmetic (children who struggle with it are likely to struggle enormously in middle school math).

It would be good if this were not necessary, because not all parents can or will do it. I would love to have a standardized system for routinely communicating to parents what constitutes on-level work and what their students are doing. Parents in many states never see their students’ actual answers on standardized tests; we could change that.

But until any of that happens, doing this work yourself is the only way to know whether your child is learning anything.
