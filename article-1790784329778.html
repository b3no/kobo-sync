You can't legislate community

There's no replacement for believing in things

Editor’s note: The first essay The Argument ever published asked, “How do we live with each other?” To mark our first anniversary, we’re returning to that founding question with a series about what we owe people whose beliefs and choices we may find difficult to accept or even understand. Familiar voices at The Argument and new voices alike have approached that question from different angles.

Today, the answer to the question of how we live with each other is “increasingly, not at all.” With eyes stuck on our phones or TVs at home, we’re spending less and less time in one another’s company, and it doesn’t seem to be relieving any of the tensions that come with living in a diverse society. Liberals are often criticized for leaving meaning-making to the market, but in today’s piece, columnist Matthew Yglesias objects to this “crude caricature.”

Probably the last thing the world needs is another basically secular Jewish intellectual opining that it would be good for Americans to go to church more.

But I’ll accept being a cliche in service of getting people to appreciate the actual role religion has played in public life.

As American associational life has declined, many secular commentators have waxed nostalgic about the community-building aspects of religion — the idea that people would be better off if they spent more time interacting with others and less time home alone. In this view, the case for caring about religious observance has less to do with the specifics of religious faith and more to do with the reality that, in practice, people are mostly spending would-be church time watching an endless feed of algorithmically distributed streaming video.

There’s something to this, but I believe it has received disproportionate attention relative to the actual content of religious belief. After all, while it is probably true that the social ties forged through regular attendance at in-person events have value, this kind of value could theoretically be created through other means.

But religions aren’t just regularly scheduled meetings. They are ethical systems that make claims about value in the world. They teach, normally, that you ought to care about other people — at a minimum about co-religionists — that you should donate to charity, and that there is such a thing as right and wrong distinct from what you can and can’t get away with.

Religion isn’t the only thing that can fulfill the human need for some sort of moral system. Personally, I identify with secular values of universal benevolence. And while I don’t find a ton of value in the effective altruism community, I do subscribe to many of effective altruism’s beliefs. As a result, I donate 10% of my company’s subscription revenue to GiveWell’s Top Charities Fund and have encouraged others to donate millions as well.

Yet I’m not a fanatic about it. There’s something to be said for community and localism.

My wife was deeply involved with the Parent Teacher Organization at our son’s elementary school, and I was a frequent volunteer at events. We give to a local food bank. I take an interest, in a low-key but real way, in the welfare of some of the kids at school who have more chaotic home lives than my son’s.

This is all, I think, fairly normal stuff: having values, having ethical commitments, having standards, not always living up to your own standards and feeling bad about it, caring about people you have incidental connections with, and being aware that you should care about people whom you’ve never met.

These can be secular values or religious ones, but in the aggregate, it seems young people are casting aside religious values, not for secular ethics, but just for money. A wide range of different surveys indicates young people are less interested these days in things like starting a family and more focused on making a lot of money.

In tandem with this, I have noticed a real shift in the content of progressive economic thinking. You hear less and less about the plight of the poor or the virtues of a Nordic-style social model with broad-based taxation and a universal safety net. Instead, you hear more about middle-class college graduates wanting the state to do more for them personally (cancel student debt, raise revenue exclusively from billionaires, address the high costs of DoorDash and junk fees at concerts).

At the same time, many people’s political engagement has become more totalizing and illiberal.

Liberalism leaves people free to decide what makes life worth living, but those of us who defend it need to take more responsibility for helping people find compelling answers. We should be making the case for commitments beyond personal enrichment and building communities that help people live up to them.

Keeping the government out of the business of prescribing a good life is good. Keeping liberals out of the business of prescribing the good life is an abdication of power to the illiberals snapping at our heels.

The communitarian moment

Liberalism is under threat, not just implicitly but increasingly overtly, as prominent politicians seek reputations as thought leaders explicitly by disavowing liberalism’s main elements.

Vice President JD Vance and Sen. Chris Murphy, D-Conn., come from opposite ends of the political spectrum and have little overlap on specific public policy questions. But they both have new books out arguing that America has badly erred by overemphasizing individual freedom at the expense of some vision of “the common good.”

Murphy breaks sharply with the left-liberal consensus around pluralism to overtly praise the Puritan theocracy of colonial New England. Meanwhile, Vance’s appreciation of Japanese strawberries has him abandoning the right-liberal consensus around markets and growth in favor of costly efforts to impose hegemonic conformity.

These ideas and critiques of liberalism are the opposite of new. They remind me of my undergraduate reading assignments, with Murphy mostly echoing the left-communitarian ideas of Michael Sandel while Vance is reminiscent of the right-communitarian ideas of Alasdair MacIntyre. These arguments, compelling to some, seem to me in practice to have the same flaws that they have always had — starting with a simple category error and failure to appreciate what it is that liberals are actually saying.

Both communitarian traditions make the point that there are compelling arguments in favor of moral concepts that are thicker and more demanding than what liberal politics will countenance:

  • That people derive value from membership in communities and from chains of mutual obligation to one another

  • That ideas like patriotism, fidelity, forthrightness, and honor have critical roles to play in what it means to live a good life

  • And most of all, that one ought to try to live a good life — not merely avoid making trouble for other people in a liberalish sort of way

Communitarians believe, rightly I think, that there are things in life that matter more than maximizing your income — and that if we aggregate this up, it leads to the conclusion that economic growth can’t be all it means for a country to be doing well.

The issue is that liberals simply refuse to live down to the crude caricature that is painted by our opponents.

I am sincerely happy that Vance has found solace in liturgical religion, and I feel bad for Murphy that his marriage collapsed. These are important parts of life, and it is absolutely true that more matters for human well-being than autonomy and material prosperity.

The liberal claim is that it is hard to constructively instantiate these observations into politics. Adopting bad economic policies is not going to make American strawberries better or our marriages stronger.

Religious life thrived much more in the formally secular United States than in European countries with state churches. No liberal is such a crass materialist as to advocate for policies that would discourage people from spending time with their families or doing hobbies in order to GDP-max.

What we are saying is that the government has to have an economic policy, so we should try to make it a sensible one that facilitates fair cooperation and broad prosperity. The government does not need to tell you which church to attend or when or how to find meaning in your life, so we should let people find their own way.

Liberals should not abandon our traditional views about this. But we should acknowledge that old critiques are finding new audiences because the structure of American society has changed.

Twenty-five years ago, one could read communitarian critiques and point to the real-world existence of thriving American community life as a refutation. But television, generational turnover, and the rise of the internet have changed things up.

Americans spend more time alone now. Religious affiliation has fallen. Materialistic values are on the rise. And people do feel palpably unhappy about the state of things. It’s folly to think that abandoning market economics or embracing theocracy would suddenly make everyone happy.

But while liberals have traditionally worried that an excess of strong commitments to visions — normally religious visions — of ultimate value and the good life would destabilize society, today we face the opposite dilemma: a world in which many people don’t seem to believe in much of anything other than a kind of callow selfishness.

Liberals need to respond not by abandoning liberalism and state neutrality, but by actually participating in civil society and doing our part to propound ideas about the nature of the virtuous life.

The threat of Potemkin community

Clumsy policies enacted in the name of the common good can denude real communities of value and replace them with Potemkin villages.

He argued instead that leaving things to the market already privileges particular values, especially making money. There’s something to that, but liberalism doesn’t require a government indifferent to exploitation or unwilling to provide public goods.

Public libraries and universal healthcare are perfectly compatible with leaving people free to disagree about what makes life worthwhile. It’s telling when Murphy goes beyond these normal liberal programs to something much more coercive.

Murphy, like many from the communitarian space, comes out in favor of a national program of mandatory service for young people. This is an idea that’s been proposed and rejected dozens of times since the end of military conscription without anyone ever working out the most basic logistical details.

In the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints, for example, it is already customary for young people to do an extensive public service stint as missionaries. Are we going to stop them from doing that — and disrupt a real community with a genuine service ethic — in the name of creating a fake one?

Unless the plan is to completely torpedo American democracy, a national service program would, under ordinary circumstances, have to be a bland, least-common-denominator affair.

Of course, during the extreme emergency of World War II, broadly liberal societies embraced conscription and developed very strong senses of national identity and national community. There is nothing wrong with observing that this had some positive downstream consequences, but you can’t just fake a global war that kills millions of people for the sake of the good vibes.

There is undeniably something admirable about the idealism of the young missionaries and about the whole larger tradition. But if you admire it, you would want to replicate something of the sincerity and authenticity of the practice, not create a hollow simulacrum that crowds out genuinely engaged communities.

After all, the reality is that most of the things that would feel compelling enough to provide a real sense of meaning and purpose in an individual person’s life are going to be too idiosyncratic to command broad social consensus. I know many progressives are enthusiastic about the idea of a Youth Climate Corps, in which young idealists can plug away at helping to address what they consider to be the most important problem in the world. But we know that conservative Americans don’t agree with that diagnosis, so it couldn’t possibly be the object of a national unity project.

I, frankly, think the conservatives are right, and I’d rather urge idealistic young people to do normal jobs and take the Giving What We Can pledge, thereby bolstering the fortunes of the neediest people in the world. But I am also aware that this is way too idiosyncratic to be a national political program.

Fundamentally, I don’t think it’s realistic or necessarily even appropriate for a democratically elected government to give these questions of global poverty the kind of weight that I give them in my personal decision-making. The American state and its elected representatives properly concern themselves first and foremost with securing the rights and interests of American citizens. So far as foreigners go, it’s a daily struggle to talk people out of a totally paranoid attitude toward mutually beneficial trade and migration relationships, much less actual foreigner-directed beneficence.

Accepting democratic limits on translating my own convictions into government policy is, I think, as much a part of the practice of liberalism as asking Calvinists and Lutherans and Catholics to set aside their disagreements about the nature of salvation and focus their political engagement on finding fair terms of social coexistence and cooperation. But it was never the intention to get believers to stop preaching about their faith.

This, again, is a big part of the point of liberalism. Not to encourage a flattening out of passionate belief and committed communities but to preserve divergent cultures and perspectives where they can exist.

The rightist version of the communitarian turn is, at times, more comical or more sinister, with postliberal American conservatives turning to Catholicism and integralist ideology.

Integralism originated in southern European countries where Catholicism is a majority faith, so if you had an illiberal regime, it would presumably be a Catholic one. In the United States, where Protestants outnumber Catholics by 2-to-1, an illiberal regime would either be Protestant in nature or else some kind of autocracy with a very narrow social base.

Underscoring how absurd this entire discourse is, the leading postliberal American Catholics, like Vance, don’t even agree with the pope’s political ideas and want him to butt out of policy debates. I sympathize with Vance’s view of this but — welcome to liberalism! That’s the political approach that lets leaders build strong communities around strongly held ideas without needing to impose those ideas on a mass public under conditions of pluralism and reasonable disagreement.

Go build the community of your dreams without watering the vision down to be politically palatable or plunging the country into irresolvable civil conflict. Just don’t forget to actually bother to try to build it.

Today, though, I think liberals have allowed our commitment to practicing a thin version of politics to create a caricature that we favor a thin version of life. When we say our aspiration is for people to find their own ways to a deeper sense of meaning and community, it rings hollow. Because, after all, almost nobody truly finds their way to these things all on their own. We are influenced by writers and thinkers and friends and family and people in the communities to which we belong.

What we are saying as liberals is that we don’t think the state should be trying to do this work for people. We don’t think politics should be dominated by clashes over the ultimate nature of values. But those of us who have some level of prominence or prestige in the world can still do the work of actually expounding what it is that we find valuable and meaningful in life — which communities we cherish, where we see beauty, and where we find purpose.

We cannot let amorphous feelings of isolation and meaninglessness become pretexts for destroying the greatest political systems the world has ever known. But liberals also cannot count on someone else doing the work of providing people meaning and connection, nor can we assume these days that the vast majority of the population will have strong comprehensive commitments derived from religion.

Liberals must continue pushing back against the idea that politics and government should give people meaning and fulfillment rather than freedom and prosperity, but it’s undeniable that people want more out of life — and in our capacity as liberal citizens, we should try to give it to them.
