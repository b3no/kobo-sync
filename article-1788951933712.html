I changed my mind about Big Philanthropy

The democratic case for billionaire giving

Editor’s note: The first essay The Argument ever published asked, “How do we live with each other?” To mark our first anniversary on August 17, we’re returning to that founding question with a series about what we owe people whose beliefs and choices we may find difficult to accept or even understand. Over the next few weeks, familiar voices at The Argument and new voices alike will approach that question from different angles.

In today’s essay, Dylan Matthews notes that the AI boom is poised to unleash billions of dollars in philanthropic giving as it mints new millionaires and billionaires — many of whom have, let’s say, idiosyncratic views about the world. Is this private exercise of power a subversion of the democratic order? Is it an opportunity to try out bold new solutions to thorny problems? A little of both?

Personally, I think philanthropy is one of many productive release valves for a pluralistic society. Maybe I can’t get the government to spend tax revenue on public health in developing nations, but I can help do it with my own dollars. Or maybe you oppose abortion and are furious at the government for not providing other options for reluctant mothers; philanthropy lets you fund those if you wish.

Dylan, a one-time skeptic of big-time charities who is now a program officer at the major philanthropy Coefficient Giving, considers the dilemma of billionaire giving from first principles.

Back in 2018, the release in quick succession of works like Anand Giridharadas’ Winners Take All, Rob Reich’s Just Giving, and Edgar Villanueva’s Decolonizing Wealth led to a broad reconsideration in the commentariat of the ethics and political justifiability of large-scale philanthropy. I should know: I was part of it.

In 2019, I hosted a season of Vox’s podcast Future Perfect that focused on the relationship between philanthropy and democracy, particularly on the ways in which the former can undermine the latter. It wasn’t negative across the board — the episode on AI safety praising early funders of the field like Skype co-founder Jaan Tallinn has, I think, held up particularly well — but the main message was, “be suspicious.”

These concerns had been bubbling up for some time. But the broad anti-tech backlash that was surging due to concern over Facebook’s role in the 2016 election — and the growth of anti-billionaire politics fueled by both that and Bernie Sanders’ 2016 run — made 2018-19 the moment when these worries became mainstream.

I agreed broadly with Giridharadas, Reich, and Villanueva that philanthropy allows the wealthy to exert excessive, and unjustifiable, influence over society; that the charitable tax deduction unjustly forces ordinary Americans to fund that influence; and that the unaccountable nature of foundations, not constrained either by voters (as governments are) or market pressures (as businesses are), made them inherently dangerous, sometimes to the point of enabling outright atrocities.

But the 2010s era of Big Philanthropy is going to look tiny compared to the giving unleashed by the impending Anthropic and OpenAI IPOs. The OpenAI Foundation’s shares in OpenAI are worth, as Stripe executive Nan Ransohoff has noted, around $220 billion already, larger (and much more poorly diversified) than any other foundation endowment on earth. All of Anthropic’s co-founders have committed to giving away 80% of their net worth, which, given Anthropic’s current valuation, should mean another hundred billion or more in giving.

Close observers have known this was going to happen for a while, but after Ransohoff’s essay, the impending flood of AI money has become common knowledge, sparking coverage from The New York Times to The Economist to the Financial Times. And with that attention has come some familiar debates about whether this level of giving is acceptable in a free society, or whether it’s, in David Wallace-Wells’ words, “an assertion of plutocratic control over yet more aspects of collective life.”

There was and remains plenty to be suspicious of in the past and present of big philanthropy. But my views have evolved a bit since 2019, which is perhaps unsurprising as during the same period I left journalism to join Coefficient Giving. Coefficient Giving (formerly “Open Philanthropy”) is a large philanthropic advisor and grantmaker that supports work in areas like animal welfare, global health, pandemic preparedness and prevention, and AI safety. Last year we recommended some $1 billion in grants; we’re on track to significantly increase that number this year, and our advisory work in particular has grown considerably as the AI rich seek to give away their new fortunes.

I wouldn’t work where I do if I didn’t think our project is compatible with, and indeed beneficial to, a liberal democratic society. More importantly, though, I think the events of the subsequent seven years, and specifically of the past two years, have made the most scathing version of this critique less tenable than ever.

Implicit in the critique that philanthropy conflicts with democracy is the notion that decisions about the distribution of wealth and power should be made principally by the government, not by private actors. I believed this to be true in 2019. But now I think this is mistaken — and, indeed, somewhat illiberal. The effects of the second Trump term on foreign aid and science are the clearest indication of the limits of this approach.

Rather, no single entity, including the government, should monopolize these decisions. Foundations and grantmakers make serious mistakes. But the important thing is not that grantmakers are perfect, but that our errors are different from those made by the state and private business. Multiple imperfect institutions making distinct errors together add up to a more stable and resilient overall system than privileging one approach over all others.

A role for foundations

In the field of risk assessment, analysts often talk about “Swiss cheese models.” The idea is that in a complex system, each step in the process will have natural failures. But if you layer multiple steps with different failure points over each other, you can emerge with a system that’s robust to each of those failures, the same way a block of 10 Swiss cheese slices does not have a single hole going all the way through.

You can think of the way we provide goods in American and global society as several different, and differently flawed, institutions overlapping: markets, governments, religious institutions, schools and universities, and philanthropy, to name some of the most important. If we’re lucky and/or politically wise, these institutions will complement each other well and help remedy each other’s flaws.

The failures of philanthropy as a model are clear. It over-privileges the views of the wealthy. It creates power-laden relationships that, even in my short time as a grantmaker, I have already at times found awkward and uncomfortable (and the grantmaker’s discomfort is probably the least of the issues there).

But the case for philanthropy isn’t that it’s perfect. It’s that philanthropy lacks certain key defects of its complementary institutions, like markets and government.

One key defect of government, for instance, is nationalism. Democratic governments are, to varying degrees, responsive to their own citizens, but they’re never especially responsive to the citizens of other countries. That translates into an underinvestment in global public health and global poverty reduction relative to the overall preferences of mega-donors.

This case was at least disputable before 2025. It’s not really disputable anymore. A dedicated push from a narrowly elected president, without much or any input from Congress, was all it took to dismantle America’s main foreign aid institution. Per estimates by my colleague Justin Sandefur and the Center for Global Development’s Charles Kenny, fiscal year 2025 saw cuts of some $1.6 billion to USAID HIV/AIDS programs like PEPFAR and over $160 million to anti-malaria programs. While fully replacing the cuts is beyond the capacity of big philanthropy, I was proud that Coefficient Giving helped fund the continuation of some key USAID efforts, like the Development Innovation Ventures program.

It’s a clear case of philanthropy compensating for government’s shortcomings.

It’s true that most billionaires don’t care at all about global poverty, or at least don’t donate anything to combat it. But philanthropy is not governed by averages; it’s governed by power laws. Most of the impact is caused by a minority of participants, and even at the level of an individual foundation, typically a minority of grants are responsible for the overwhelming share of impact.

Wiping out Bill Gates’ fortune, for instance, would plausibly lead, counterfactually, to the deaths of hundreds of thousands or even millions of people who would have otherwise lived. Doing so would prevent life-saving research and services from happening; the evidence for the effectiveness of past Gates-fostered programs, like the Global Fund, is at this point overwhelming. I think global health funders like Gates (and, if I may, like Coefficient Giving) serve an immensely useful function of counteracting a nationalist bias in government provision.

The sheer volume of possible philanthropic donors makes it more likely that neglected causes will get at least some investment from civil society. Per Forbes, the U.S. has nearly 1,000 individual billionaires and untold thousands more worth hundreds of millions. We have one federal government.

If one is interested in promoting, say, foreign aid, they have a few levers to influence (the State Department, the Millennium Challenge Corporation, House and Senate appropriators), but only a few, and ones whose failures are often correlated with each other. If we solely trust the government to fund areas like global health, we are relying on a fragile system with major points of failure. A diverse philanthropic sector, by contrast, offers a great deal more resilience.

Another weakness in government is underinvestment in basic research and science. It’s well-known that industry underinvests in basic research. One landmark paper looking at a sample of U.S. companies from 1981 to 2001 estimated that research and development conducted by those firms produced an average return to the firm investing of 20.8%, but an average annual return to society as a whole of 58%, implying that firms should, from the point of view of society, be investing two or three times as much as they were. That’s a market failure that implies a big role for government support.

And sure enough, the government invests billions through the National Institutes of Health and the National Science Foundation — but if scholars of innovation agree on anything, it’s that this support is insufficient. “Based on what we know now,” Benjamin Jones, a Northwestern economist and leading innovation policy expert, wrote, “the United States (and the world) appear to greatly underinvest in science and innovation.”

What’s more, publicly funded scientific research, insufficient though it is, has also been shaken by Trump II in much the same way foreign aid has been. My colleague Jordan Dworkin has been tracking spending rates for the National Institutes of Health, the National Science Foundation, and other federal funding agencies, and with the notable exception of NASA, every agency reports spending significantly more slowly than in previous years.

The past two years’ experience has only provided more justification for groups like the Howard Hughes Medical Institute, which complements NIH funding with a stronger focus on supporting individual scientists and fostering original research.

Reich, another of the 2018 vintage of philanthropic critics, previously made a similar pluralism-based case for the legitimacy of foundations. Support for basic science is one example of how “foundations can be a source of funding for the minority public goods or controversial public goods that a democratic state will not or cannot fund,” he wrote, adding that support for global health or, say, shrimp welfare are other good candidates for “controversial public goods” that philanthropy can support but governments cannot.

Obviously, one should not let entities accountable to neither the market nor voters operate without limits. Reich also emphasized the importance of reducing or eliminating the U.S. tax deduction for charity. Other changes could include a requirement that donor-advised funds actually see their donations put to prompt use and a ban on perpetual foundations that outlast their donors. (Perpetual foundations pose a particular accountability problem, given that they could theoretically persist forever if their investment returns exceed their outlays, and they make the inherent principal-agent problem between donors and their foundation employees infinitely worse.)

While the details matter, I would support all of these, not least because the U.S. needs to be raising more revenue, and ending the charitable deduction is a decent source. Philanthropy plays an important role in American life, but the U.S. is nonetheless an unusual kind of actor that should not operate with special subsidy or without reasonable limits.

There are program officers everywhere for those with eyes to see

While getting less attention than the likes of Giridharadas, the best critic of philanthropy from the late-2010s wave was always the political theorist Emma Saunders-Hastings. In her paper “Plutocratic Philanthropy” and later book Private Virtues, Public Vices, Saunders-Hastings put together the most considered and careful version of the case that big philanthropy is harmful specifically because it is undemocratic.

The problems are twofold. First, philanthropy violates an appealing democratic norm: some citizens should not, by virtue of their wealth, enjoy wildly greater power to influence political and societal decisions. The second problem is that philanthropy creates relationships of domination and control and is characterized by paternalism in the worst sense. “Some people’s altruism puts other people under their power,” Saunders-Hastings wrote in Private Virtues. “Even genuine altruism can operate through and reinforce social and political relationships that are inimical to democracy.”

Though well aware that government has shortcomings, Saunders-Hastings compared defenses of philanthropy on these grounds to Alexis de Tocqueville’s writings mourning the loss of distinctive aspects of aristocratic culture as Europe democratized. Those aspects were often the same ones that defenders like me praise in philanthropy, like a willingness to fund unpopular or risky causes that seem to be in the population’s long-run interest.

“This enumeration of aristocracy’s virtues has significant overlap with contemporary defenses of philanthropy,” she wrote. “As unaccountable, perpetual institutions, philanthropic foundations are insulated from electoral pressures and can afford long time horizons: they face no electoral disincentive to act in what they take to be the long-term public interest and may be uniquely positioned to place risky bets on untested strategies for the provision of public goods.”

But, “As Tocqueville recognizes, aristocratic trusteeship over the public good is democratically unacceptable.” And so, by implication, is elite philanthropy. I can wax rhapsodic about the importance of funding science and anti-malaria bednets all I want. It doesn’t answer the key democratic challenge.

What could answer the challenge, then? The philosophers Robert S. Taylor and Jacob Barrett have recently offered some clues. Taylor has mounted a defense of private charity based on the republican tradition in philosophy, which focuses on the need to protect individuals from domination, whether that domination comes from governments, private business, or charity.

Charity can, he conceded, create this kind of domination. A world where there is only one uber-rich philanthropist gives that philanthropist unjustifiable power over applicants for support. But a richer, more diverse pool of donors, all with different motivations and analyses, creates a very different ecosystem in which applicants for support are not required to follow the dictates of a single powerful donor.

Barrett’s defense is more direct: the democratic principle we should care about is that no one with a right to influence in a given polity is disenfranchised. Certain kinds of philanthropy, though, can reduce this kind of disenfranchisement. People not yet residing in a city have an interest in there being enough housing for them to move there, but they do not get to vote in city elections. Philanthropic support for YIMBY groups thus gives them influence they’d lack through formal democratic channels.

I’d go further and say that support for efforts to reduce suffering in factory farms represents a form of enfranchisement of creatures who matter morally and would not count in the eyes of legislatures and courts absent that philanthropic support.

This isn’t a new observation. Ultra-wealthy individuals bankrolling efforts for economic equality in the U.S. is a phenomenon at least as old as abolition, a movement heavily supported by the likes of Gerrit Smith, one of New York’s richest residents. When John Brown needed funds to launch a slave uprising, Smith was one of six people to pony up. The slave power — the consolidated lobby of slaveowners and those financially tied to slavery — was too powerful to be defeated without at least some countervailing pressure from other economic elites.

John Fabian Witt’s recent book The Radical Fund tells the more recent story of the American Fund for Public Service, a short-lived foundation of the 1920s and 1930s organized by the muckraking novelist Upton Sinclair and ACLU founder Roger Baldwin. One can hardly argue with the group’s leftist bona fides. By the 1930s, the main debates Witt depicted on the fund’s board were between the “moderates” like Norman Thomas (who ran for president as a Socialist as a more left-wing alternative to Franklin Delano Roosevelt) and the Moscow-controlled Communist wing, which refused to cooperate with merely socialist institutions and labor unions.

But the fund was nonetheless able to use the banking inheritance of a young Harvard dropout to fund efforts like Amalgamated Clothing Workers of America leader Sidney Hillman’s “new unionism” model of labor organizing; A. Philip Randolph’s Brotherhood of Sleeping Car Porters, the first AFL-affiliated union specifically organizing Black men; and, most importantly, the NAACP’s new legal department, which was initiated with a large Fund grant and led directly to the Brown v. Board of Education ruling.

The point is not just that the Fund supported projects that wound up bearing fruit. The point is that it was specifically able to fund movements that could then create alternative, less democratically fraught forms of influence than the philanthropy that ignited them.

Hillman and Randolph could eventually use member dues for their fights rather than begging foundations endlessly. Brown v. Board helped catalyze a grassroots movement largely funded from within the Black community. In a time of diminished unions and collapsed mass-member organizations, it’s not obvious whether the same strategy can succeed today. But funders can, and should, try.

The plutocratic elements of big philanthropy are hard to ignore, and it is fair to be uncomfortable with them. But there is no way out but through. I moved to this sector not because it’s perfect but because there is no path to a fairer, less cruel future without enlisting philanthropy in the fight.
