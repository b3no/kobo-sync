We Don't Need More Cold Approaches At Grocery Stores. We Need Bigger Social Circles.

Women say they want to be approached more often--but the key is shared social settings and activities, not pickup lines.

Exactly a year ago, I wrote about the fact that women enjoy men spontaneously flirting with them more than the 2010s #BanMen feminism trope would have us believe. Despite loud voices overrepresented in the media insisting that “women just want to be left alone” unless they’re on a dating app (and maybe not even then) women in my surveys, as well as women I’ve known in real life, have expressed frustration with the fact that dating apps are the only place where they can expect to meet men. (79% of single women on a recent survey indicated they wish they were approached by men more often.) A few years ago, I spoke with an approachably cute girl in her mid-twenties—the type of girl who should have received lots of male attention—who told me that outside of dating apps, men did not speak to her at all.

There is some eroticism and excitement to meeting someone spontaneously and feeling a spark, or developing romantic feelings for someone you already knew through other means. Dating apps remove a great deal of risk and embarrassment, thus why people like them, and thus why they are increasingly becoming the only socially acceptable method of meeting a partner. But along with the benefits of reducing opportunities for embarrassment, apps eliminate spontaneous and exciting situations where your attraction to a person comes out of knowing them first and experiencing a three-dimensional version of them—the things that cannot be captured in photos—instead reducing everyone to a few lines of text and some images, which are particularly unforgiving for men in particular.

But when I wrote about the death of spontaneous flirting, some binary thinking emerged in the resulting discourse, and maybe some of that was my fault for how I worded things. When I said that women would like to be “approached” more often, some people (both men and women) had the assumption that I was claiming that every woman is attracted to a wide swath of men despite clear evidence that women are actually pretty picky, and that most women would like to be approached anywhere, anytime. Obviously, had I been arguing this, I would have been wrong. The reality is much more nuanced, but I fear that the “leave women alone” narrative and the rise of dating apps threw out the baby with the bathwater. The “baby” in this scenario, is the “warm approach,” where a shared social setting (or assumed-to-be-social environment between strangers) facilitates the approach and makes it less weird, even if some risk is still involved. (As any seasoned pickup artist will tell you, it’s a numbers game, and rejection will be the most common outcome.)

The vast majority of cold approaches (ie: approaching a complete stranger in a non-social setting, such as the grocery store or DMV) are unappealing. Perhaps a resurrected JFK Jr. with a British accent would be able to pull it off, but most men can’t—even moderately attractive men who would have success through other means. Context is very important to women’s sexuality, and women aren’t typically in a sexy mood while buying detergent. I never called for a widespread return of the abrupt cold approach, although I think some of these are better than none. Instead, we need more lukewarm or warm approaches, which can’t happen without people leaving their houses more often, doing more social activities, being more open to the risk of embarrassment or mild discomfort, and most importantly: having bigger platonic social circles in the first place.

There is also selection bias at play here. The type of men who cold approach are usually not particularly appealing—not because they’re ugly but because they are…well, the kind of guy who gets horny at the gas station. You get the sense you’re the fiftieth woman he’s tried this shit with that morning, and you do not feel attractive or special at all.

I was a young woman before the rise of dating apps, and I got cold-approached quite often when I was running errands or going to work without my boyfriend. Given my relationship status, I would have said no regardless, but objectively speaking, 95% of these approaches were unappealing! In particular, I’m reminded of a man who approached me at the food court at the mall and offered me discounts at Foot Locker (where he worked) if I would go on a date with him. When I told him I had a boyfriend, he said, “What, he doesn’t let you have friends?” and then he promised he could throw in a Foot Locker discount for him too as long as I didn’t tell him about our arrangement (alternative theory: this guy really needed to offload some Foot Locker coupons.)

There were others too, equally irritating. One guy quite literally chased after me down the street, and when I turned around, terrified I was about to be mugged, he put his sweaty hands up and said, “Sorry if I scared you! I just really want to take you on a date and you were so far away!” I was so flustered that I just told him I was a lesbian, a ridiculous lie given that I give off just about the straightest vibes imaginable. Another guy stopped me on the street to ask directions, and after I answered him earnestly, he rudely cut me off and said, “Enough with this nonsense. What’s your number?”

One guy, who to be fair was probably on drugs, just followed me down the street, shuffling, grunting and saying, “Oh yeah.” I was not flattered. This guy, I’m confident, would have fucked a rolled-up yoga mat.

I wouldn’t say approaching a stranger in broad daylight is always a bad idea. There were a couple approaches that were pleasant and flattering, even if I was taken at the time. One guy stopped me after a long bus ride, said he noticed me on the bus, thought I was cute, and asked me if I wanted to get coffee with him. Had I been single, I might have said yes! But most were not like this. And when women talk about wanting to be approached more, I believe they are talking about warm or lukewarm approaches, not cold approaches.

The problem with warm approaches is that you need a full and socialized life to even have them in the first place, and fewer people have that today. When you ask miscellaneous chopped uncs like myself about how they met, it’s often through shared social settings like school, work, mutual friends or even bars and nightclubs. I would consider it lukewarm or warm to approach someone at a bar (doesn’t seem to happen much anymore—I still go to bars, and I only see people spending time with the group with which they arrived.) Same with the gym, but only if you are already engaged in a shared fitness activity, like a class, or have some kind of normal conversation first. A warm approach can happen at a hobby club, at work (provided you’re already friendly and neither of you report to the other one) or through any other shared social setting.

Given that my twenties were spent in the pre-Tinder era, I experienced many shared social settings and witnessed people meeting organically. My husband and I would attend parties basically every weekend—often invited by someone who was invited. We would then bring along some friends (mostly single) and they’d enter the party and immediately chat someone up. Most of the time, things didn’t click, because that’s just the way it goes. But sometimes, it worked out, and it often worked out with people who might not have been attracted to them if they had only seen them in a photo (or vice versa.) The idea of staying home all weekend was unfathomable to us, or to our friends.

But a lot of today’s young people don’t have easy opportunities for these shared social settings. College remains a good place to meet people, but given that dating apps are now the main socially acceptable way of finding a partner, there is less of an incentive for people to partake in the social activities that used to be ripe for flirtation. When I was in college, I went to every activity and party available because there was just no other way for me to meet people. For fuck’s sake, I once attended a hiking club meetup and that’s about the last thing I’d ever do if not for interest in meeting a man. (I did meet a man there. It was not a good match, perhaps due to our intense delta in how much we enjoyed hiking, but hey, that’s what dating is!)

Fewer people throw or attend parties. People have smaller circles of friends, if they have friends at all. Settings that used to be more social—work, parks, beaches, and so on—have moved into the “too risky to bother” territory. The issue isn’t that women are sitting around at the dentist office’s waiting room, clamoring for a man to hit on them while they try to avoid getting upsold into a deep cleaning. The issue is that women would like to meet men spontaneously, and there are now fewer appropriate and well-attended venues for that kind of thing.

Moreover, since approaching women has become a bit taboo, the only men who do it today (for the most part) are weirdos. Over time, women begin to associate approaches with these weirdos, and it becomes weirdo-coded. The 2010s “leave women alone” ethos greatly affected the men who never needed to hear it—the ones who were already terrified of making women uncomfortable. The guys who scream at women from their car windows were not thoughtfully reading Everyday Feminism and resolving to change their ways.

But the fear of rejection, especially among young men today, is not unfounded. It’s easy to tell these guys to get over themselves and their zoomer social anxiety, but they are dealing with an entirely different risk calculus than men in my generation. When I had Matthew Yglesias on my podcast, he pointed out that before the online dating era, approaches happened all the time (at parties, bars, and other social settings) and while rejection was a major part of that experience, it wasn’t a big deal for anyone involved. If a man approached a woman, she wasn’t into it and he went away, this interaction would not be viewed as a major social faux pas or devastating humiliation, just part of life. He would then try again with someone else, and the woman would also forget about it and move on.

Unfortunately, if fewer people are engaging in this type of behavior, it genuinely becomes more embarrassing to try it out. Yes, it’s good to get over a fear of rejection, but there is a systemic element here where a social norm has changed. Similarly, it would come off as socially inappropriate in 2026 to walk around the streets with a stack of newspapers and a bell shouting, “Hear ye, hear ye.” As much as I hate to use this word, we should probably “normalize” the “numbers game” of approaches again so that it becomes less humiliating to be rejected at a bar or at a party, but that would require widespread cooperation and for the adult binky of dating apps to be removed. I’m not hopeful about that.

Perhaps the answer is for women to start approaching men, although I have my doubts that this would work out, mostly because women often find this scenario deeply unappealing. Being the one to make a first move is a turnoff for many women, and no matter how bad the loneliness crisis is, most women are not lonely enough to bother with this strategy. Given that approaching a man is really uncommon, I have a feeling men would be suspicious off the bat, unless they are the type of mondo-attractive guy who’s used to it (and if they are, you shouldn’t bother, they’re full.) To be clear, I’m not saying women should never approach men—I could see this working quite well on an individual level—but I don’t think it would be any easier to “normalize” on a mass scale, because the ones in charge of the agency (women) mostly have no interest in doing it. Perhaps a topic for another article, so stay tuned.

My greatest hope for increasing the number of spontaneous, but not cold, approaches, is by ensuring that people have larger social circles and engage in more in-person interaction within these circles. This is something that most young people seem to want. Whenever the topic of loneliness comes up, I’m hearing less, “Leave me alone, I want to watch Netflix” and more “I really wish people had parties more often.” Perhaps I am filtering for the exact type of awkward extrovert that has a tendency to subscribe to the Substack of a…well, awkward extrovert, but I don’t think most people today want to be socially isolated.

When people talk about dating, they don’t tend to talk about platonic friendships, but having more platonic friendships is probably key to finding more potential partners outside of dating apps. Meeting through friends, or being explicitly set up by friends, used to be a very common way that people met. For people to experience the joy and thrill of spontaneously meeting someone they like, they should expand their social circle as much as possible, even among people they don’t want to date. They should keep in touch with family friends. They should ask for contact information when they meet a potential friend, even if they are not the gender to which they’re attracted. Thy should be friendly with people they don’t think are hot, assuming they get along. They should go to all the things, even things that seem corny. They should host things, even if they’re worried nobody will show up. They should avoid, at all costs, spending the weekend on their phones. Eventually, if enough people keep saying “yes” to these low-pressure, non-romantic things, people will have bigger social circles and more shared social settings, like parties and meetups. And approaching someone at a party is significantly less awkward than striking up conversation with a total stranger at the mall—yes, even if you have a surplus of Foot Locker coupons.
