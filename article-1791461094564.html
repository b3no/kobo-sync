You Don’t Need to Tell Your Young Child About the Bad Thing

And you definitely don’t need to film it for high fives.

One of the most infamous tweets of all time is now dubbed “Ruthkanda,” wherein a mother claimed her child was so heartbroken over the death of Ruth Bader Ginsburg that she crossed her arms on her chest as in Black Panther, and then declared, “Ruthkanda forever.”

As far as I can tell, the mockery over this “cringe” tweet got so intense that the original poster locked her account and has yet to unlock it. At first I thought this was a bit much until I saw a semi-recent promotional tweet from her employer where she was tagged, and two of the three comments referenced Ruthkanda.

But now, there are Ruthkandas all over the place, taking a slightly different tone, and mostly receiving praise. The new Ruthkandism is filming and posting a serious conversation you had with your young child, often about a topic you did not need to address, often with the specific motivation of showing everyone what a great job you’ve already done and how little you even needed to have the conversation in the first place. In fact, think of it less as a conversation and more of a rapid-fire quiz, showcasing how your child already knew the Right Answers.

Right now, it’s the Cornell sexual assault case. Yahoo News covered the importance of broaching the topic of the Cornell case with your children, including, according to the article’s header photo, children who are still wearing footie pajamas:

The Cut also uses a photo of kids who can’t possibly be older than six to make the same point.

Now, in all credit to Yahoo, in that article, the Cornell case is merely used as a titular prompt for a broader conversation about consent. And, yes, it’s good to talk to young children about consent in an age-appropriate way: you never have to let anyone hug or kiss you, you control what happens to your body, there are certain parts of your body nobody should touch, you should listen to other people when they don’t want to be touched, you should never touch anyone else’s off-limits body parts, so on and so forth. As they get older, you can be more specific, and discuss consent as it relates to sex, underscoring how binge drinking, porn and other factors can make things murkier and more dangerous. Fine! Great! This should already have been happening pre-Cornell, and the Cornell case should make no difference unless your plan is to ride the wave of a hot news story and get attention and praise (oh, nobody would do that, right?)

And yet, social media is littered with parents filming themselves talking with their children (often sons under the age of ten) “about Cornell.” (Again, sometimes they’re just talking about consent, while using Cornell in the title to get attention.) Now, don’t make any mistakes: they may say it’s about raising good men, but really, it’s about showing everyone on TikTok that they’ve already done this, and look at what a great job they’ve done. They are thinking so much about consent without stopping to wonder if a nine-year-old can actually consent to having this video with his face and name out there. Typically the caption is something like, “WOW, my 6-year-old understands consent better than a bunch of grown men!” These types of videos are obnoxious and a privacy violation (some show the child’s face and name!) but at least the content is age-appropriate.

Then there are…others.

In one video, a woman films herself talking about Cornell with her four-year-old. Instead of simply making it a conversation about consent, she opens up the conversation by telling him, in a dramatic voice, that “something very bad happened.” Keep in mind this kid is four. When a four-year-old hears that something very bad happened, they think their grandparent or dog has died, or that something happened which was somehow their fault. The child is audibly terrified, and asks if someone died. (I don’t approve of any of this, but if you must inform your child of something scary in the news, at the very least you should start by reassuring them that it doesn’t affect them or their family.) She then explains the Cornell case in a way she describes as “age appropriate” in her caption: “seven boys trapped a girl in a room for a long time and hurt her.”

Even without any sexual content, this scenario is not something a four-year-old has probably considered. A four-year-old can learn how to be a good person without being left to speculate about how seven boys “hurt” a girl in a room for a “long time.” Truly, the only reason to share that piece of information with him at all was to post it on social media to jump onto a viral trend (one mom even filmed the conversation with her seven-year-old son and added Paris Paloma’s Labour as a soundtrack).

Some videos showcase kids who are old enough to understand what happened (they’ve likely heard about it at school) so no harm no foul on explaining the situation, but then their moms quiz them on “what they would do” in that situation, posting their correct answers for an audience of adoring fans and…not so adoring fans. In one viral video, a mom shows her middle school age son expressing shock and horror that nobody in the group chat stepped forward to help Jane Doe. The video was immediately stitched by a strange adult man who was accusing the twelve-year-old child of narcissistically “wanting to be a hero” and not actually caring about the girl’s experience.

I don’t mean to condemn 100% of child-related content. I get that some parents may want to post the occasional photo, cute story, or funny mispronunciation (my daughter says “poo-yoo-ter” for computer, thank you very much.) I’ve written about my kids (hopefully in ways that don’t embarrass them) a few times, anonymously and without showing their faces. But at the very least, can we not expose our middle schoolers’ attempts to say and do the right thing against an audience of unhinged twenty-seven-year-olds with mullets who will look for excuses to criticize them? Most adults can’t even handle the crazies of TikTok, must we subject twelve-year-old boys to this?

The topic right now may be Cornell, but it wasn’t always. For a while now, it’s been trendy to tell too-young children about heavy topics, terrify them or prompt their correct answers, and then sit back and wait for Internet high fives over how we’re raising good fucking humans. There is usually some kind of sub-goal, which is almost always to show that your child is adequately terrified of something that actually does not concern them at all. Three examples in particular stick out to me.

In one, a woman joked that her seven-year-old son who was sick with covid pleaded for her to sit by him while he slept in case he died in his sleep. This was not particularly funny to me, given that this kid almost definitely either had an anxiety disorder or was deliberately fed untrue information about the risk of covid to healthy young children.

Another similar post involved a screenshot that a woman posted of her and her eleven-year-old daughter texting after Ron DeSantis was elected governor of Florida. The daughter expressed intense anxiety that DeSantis would “make it illegal to wear a mask.” The mother posted the screenshot with vibes of, “See? See what MAGA is doing to our children?!” even though you could tell the mother herself was likely responsible for this neurosis.

And in case you think it’s only the Left who does it, you are wrong! I also recall seeing a video where a Republican homeschooler quizzed her daughter on facts related to the founding of the United States (she knew everything) and then quizzed her on woke gender ideology (she knew nothing.) Wow, based!

And who could forget the countless parents and educators who reveled in children being adequately pessimistic and anxious about the climate? In fact, in 2019 a climate activist informed an audience full of young children that they might not live to see adulthood, in a move that was (thankfully) criticized by climate scientists.

Sometimes, the kid quite obviously has no clue what’s going on, and it’s transparently about the parent’s need for validation, such as this guy who filmed himself informing his infant that he brought her into a “dark and evil world.” Cool.

No matter the politics, no matter the topic, the motivation is sinister: using kids (and often but not always, purposefully scaring kids) to get praise and attention, often from strangers on the Internet.

These people also are not content to keep this weird choice to themselves. They demand other people raise their kids this way too. As they say, if you’re not terrified you’re not paying attention, and apparently the same should be true of children. A while ago, I made the grave mistake of admitting on Bluesky of all places (go ahead and clown on me for that, I deserve it) that I had not gotten into the details of Trump’s worst actions with my then-five-year-old. In particular, I had said that I had done nothing to warn him of the “rising tide of fascism,” given that his biggest fear at the time was the scene from Aladdin when the genie pops out of the lamp too abruptly.

I did not keep this information from him because I love Trump so much, but rather because I did not think it was appropriate to terrify a child that age with details of things he didn’t need to know. Someone kindly pointed out that it was actually pretty “horrifying” if not “inexcusable” that my five-year-old was ignorant to the fact that “ICE was going to kidnap all his friends.” They did not miss the opportunity to brag about how their child has been going to protests with them since toddlerhood and how their child lives in fear of ICE showing up and taking their friends away.

This did not feel like something worth terrifying my child about. Of course, if ICE actually was showing up at his school, it would be best to get ahead of this and discuss it with him before he experienced it in some other, more traumatic way. And surely, the same would be true if his friends were all talking about ICE (they weren’t). After all, when he has seen homeless people having mental health crises, we’ve explained why that might happen (although we didn’t get into the role alcohol or drugs might have played, but we told him they “needed help from doctors.”) We’ve explained poverty, sexism and racism in age-appropriate ways. But why create trauma where there is none?

My second grade teacher (who I’ve written about before) got in trouble with parents for teaching about the Holocaust when it wasn’t part of the curriculum, and when the kids were too young to hear about it. To make matters worse, she explained it poorly enough that the kids took it upon themselves to play Nazis and Jews on the playground at recess. (I’ll have you know I chose to be a Jew, because I’m a good fucking person.) Anyway, parents weren’t happy. And for good reason!

Young children are on a “need to know” basis with upsetting or adult topics. Terrifying a child about something they don’t need to know is not raising a socially aware kid, it’s scaring a kid for no good reason. And yes, it’s unfair that some kids need to know more than others. A child whose father is about to be incarcerated needs to understand some basic things about the criminal justice system and prison. But a child whose parents have never committed a crime and have no reason to believe they’re going to jail actually does not need to know anything about prison, other than perhaps the fact that it exists.

And moreover, even if you aren’t trying to scare your child, even if you’re just having a teachable moment or checking to make sure their values are where they should be…does such a conversation really need to be filmed?

Parents may differ on exactly what children should know at different ages. I’m sure we can agree to disagree on the specifics, and that’s fine. I think many parents are genuinely trying their best to raise kids with good values, and perhaps telling them a bit too much or a bit too little (and who am I to make a firm judgment about exactly what amount is right?) But the minute these conversations are posted to TikTok for millions of people to see, the motivation of “raising good humans” is less obvious to me, and it all becomes quite a bit more sinister. Especially if the conversation is less of a conversation, and more of a quiz to see if your kid already knew the right answers.

Now, if you excuse me, I’m off to film myself talking to my three-year-old about the fall of Yugoslavia.
