Welcome to The Weekly Scroll!

The Weekly Scroll is a once-a-week roundup of the funniest, most interesting, and strangest stories from around the social internet.

The internet is not just a strange, hyperselected web of people hiding from reality; in many ways, the internet is reality. What happens online shapes real life just as real life shapes what happens online.

Every week, I sift through social media so you don’t have to, collecting the most notable trends, memes, and online dramas in one place. Think of it as a guided tour through the more insane parts of the web, with commentary about what it all means.

If you enjoy internet nonsense but don’t have the time to doomscroll for hours every day, you’re in the right place.

This week, we’re diving into the Jason Arday plagiarism scandal, the death of a CancerTok star, the state of Substack, and narco-influencers.

Get in loser, we’re bundling

Last week, investigative journalist Scott Carney wrote a viral Substack column titled “The Real Reason that Substack is Collapsing,” which pointed the finger at subscription fatigue and the increased prioritization of free content on the platform. Substack began as a site that functioned like a neutral service provider, merely sending emails and hosting pages. But it has recently added features that make it more like a social media network, including Notes, livestreams, followers and short form video.

Tech journalist Taylor Lorenz has had similar concerns.

The top comments on Carney’s post are complaining about how expensive it is to follow multiple writers (which inspired a secondary round of discourse with writers defending the concept that writers deserve to be paid). If you want to follow more than just a couple of bloggers, it’s easy to find yourself paying far more than you would expect. When you add lots of Substack subscriptions to all the streaming services, it’s understandable that people feel stretched thin.

Substack, as a company, represents the peak of what Ben Thompson called the Great Unbundling: Cable packages used to bundle together hundreds of channels into one expensive package, but now you pay separately for a wide variety of streaming services. Newspapers used to bundle sports, news, lifestyle content, comics, classifieds, and weather, but now there’s a separate app or publication for each of those. And while magazines used to contain articles from a wide range of writers on a variety of topics, today you can directly subscribe to single blogs.

Everyone likes unbundling in theory. Why pay for the things you don’t care about in the bundle? Why not only pay for the thing you care about? But the end state isn’t always what you want — some folks find themselves paying more for all the streaming services than they ever did for cable, and paid subscriptions to 10 different blogs might cost more than their previous magazines and papers did.

If you’ll permit me a moment of navel-gazing, I think we’re due for a moment of re-bundling. That’s part of why I joined up with The Argument. There’s so much more value for readers if I’m bundled with all the other fantastic stuff being produced here than there would be if it was just me alone.

Sure, we’re basically reinventing the wheel with this new “bundle a bunch of good writers together” idea. But I think — and hopefully you, dear readers, agree — that publications like The Argument are the future.

Arday it ain’t so

Cambridge sociologist Jason Arday self-defenestrated this week, resigning his position after a series of escalating scandals threatened his public persona and career.

Arday became an academic superstar a few years back as the youngest-ever Black man to become a full professor at Cambridge. But rumors about academic impropriety have dogged him for years. Those rumors exploded last month when a philosophy postdoctoral researcher at Ghent University published a Substack article with evidence that Arday had extensively plagiarized his dissertation.

What took this from a minor academic scandal to a full-blown culture war fight is the nature of Arday’s accuser. Nathan Cofnas is a controversial figure who writes about racial hereditarianism and rejects the principle of equal human worth. Because the claims came from Cofnas, Cambridge circled the wagons, defending Arday and calling the accusations a “vile smear campaign to undermine his credibility.”

It is absolutely the case that right-wing critics of academia, DEI, and woke politics increasingly spend their time going after prominent Black and progressive academics, selectively searching through their past work for instances of plagiarism or dishonesty. This is the playbook Manhattan Institute anti-DEI activist Christopher Rufo used to take down former Harvard President Claudine Gay.

But it also appears to be the case that Arday is a serial fabulist.

The accusations against Arday led the public to take a more critical look at his non-academic achievements, which, according to Arday, are substantial. He claimed that he was unable to speak until age 11 and that he could not read or write until age 18. He claimed to have once run 30 marathons in 35 days — the last nine on a fractured leg! — and to have run 600 miles in six days.

Arday says that he once raised as much as $7.4 million for charity, that he was a high-level athlete in both soccer and snooker, that he was part of a British television program called Seven Up (which followed the lives of people born decades before Arday), and that he had a brain tumor removed just two weeks before defending his Ph.D. thesis.

Arday also claimed that he was the subject of an intense harassment campaign where he received a barrage of racist emails, was assaulted and spit on, was threatened on two occasions in his office by a masked man (once with a knife), and had a severed pig’s head mailed to his parents’ home.

As best I can tell, there is no hard evidence for any of these claims. No running buddies, no doctor statements, no early childhood records, no office CCTV, no pictures of the pig’s head. It’s not impossible for some of these claims to be true, but when viewed in aggregate, it’s clear one of two things must be true: Either Jason Arday is living a life that would make a Hollywood screenwriter blush, or he’s making a lot of it up.

Given the accusations of plagiarism (which have since metastasized into more serious allegations of outright fraud), it was probably wise that Arday resigned before he could be fired. It was probably also inevitable that the AI-detection program Pangram flagged his resignation letter as 100% AI-written.

The entire episode is deeply embarrassing for everyone involved. For Arday, obviously. But more importantly, it’s embarrassing for the universities that somehow hired him and put him on the path to academic superstardom, despite incredibly obvious signs of fraud and dishonesty, and for the people and institutions who reflexively defended him only to end up with egg on their faces.

It’s also notable that multiple skeptics within traditional institutions tried and failed to warn people about Arday’s pattern of deception. Two professors raised concerns in 2023, after which Cambridge responded that Arday had the department’s “full support.” Jack Grove of Times Higher Education was working on an exposé about Arday’s plagiarism, but the publication killed the piece after being contacted by a law firm, and Grove was later reported to British police. An investigation by Liverpool John Moores University, which awarded Arday his Ph.D., concluded the copied passages were an “honest and reasonable error.”

It wasn’t until Cofnas published his evidence on Substack last month that the scandal truly detonated. In last week’s Weekly Scroll, I wrote that “part of what happens when mainstream institutions weaken is that people look for alternatives. The failure of reputable systems leads disreputable systems to take their place.” I can’t think of a better example than relying on a race-science blog to take down an academic cheat who should have been caught by the gatekeepers employing him.

It seems obvious that Arday’s identity as a Black scholar is part of what made these institutions eager to overlook his confabulations. Yes, it sucks that there are conservative culture warriors selectively picking through the work of specifically every Black academic they can find for plagiarism and fraud; I think that fields like sociology are probably rife with bad work, sloppy attribution, and outright misconduct.

But the solution isn’t to claim that accusations of plagiarism and fraud amount to a racist witch hunt; it’s to not do plagiarism and fraud.

Life and death as an influencer

Sydney Towle died Wednesday at the age of 26 from complications relating to cancer of the bile duct. Towle gained fame over the last few years for documenting her struggle with cancer on social media platforms like TikTok and Instagram, where she had 1.1 million and 232,000 followers, respectively, at time of writing.

Towle was known for integrating her cancer treatment into her bubbly persona — she would do TikTok dances while hooked up to chemo, and “get ready with me” videos about early cancer detection.

Her insistence on remaining upbeat and normal attracted both positive and negative attention. Last year, The New York Times reported that she had a dedicated snark community on Reddit, many of whose participants didn’t actually believe she had cancer.

Users on the now-banned r/SydTowleSnark subreddit combed through her videos like detectives, attempting to prove she was faking her illness. “Now more than ever I am 100% convinced she’s lying about everything. She’s a callous, manipulative, performative little bitch,” wrote one user. Another simply said, “I literally feel like punching her in the face.” The Times wrote that the harassment reached a level where snarkers discussed contacting Towle’s sponsors and showing up at her events.

In a way, their deranged behavior was a testament to who Towle was. She was irrepressibly positive, and some people simply couldn’t believe a woman so bright and cheerful could have cancer. After being told by her doctor in mid-May that she needed to begin thinking about end-of-life care, her response was to walk the runway at Miami Swim Week around a week later. And she refused to change for her disease; despite actively dying of cancer, she was still doing hair care brand deal posts just a few weeks ago.

To be an influencer is to put one’s life on display as a kind of public performance. But Towle makes me wonder about what happens when influencers inevitably die. Does death become a performance as well? If you’re able to do it as gracefully as she did, perhaps that wouldn’t be so bad.

Cartels and influencers

Another grim death also occurred on social media this week. Mexican influencer César Gastélum was murdered while in the middle of a livestream from Culiacán, Mexico. Authorities suspect cartel involvement.

It’s unfortunately not the first time that cartels have killed influencers or online personalities. These murders usually fall into one of two broad categories:

  1. Outsiders who report on cartels or complain about them.

  2. “Narco influencers,” or online personalities who deliberately associate with cartels for money, influence, or just clout.

The kidnapping of Nicole Pardo Molina and the murder of Gail Castro are a couple of the many examples of this kind of violence.

It’s still unconfirmed, but there’s speculation that Gastélum was a narco influencer linked with cartel activity. Mexican authorities are investigating whether the murder was related to “various posts” on social media that Gastélum made in “reference to a faction of a criminal group.” The site Blog del Narco claims that he was related to previously murdered narco influencers and that he may have switched sides in a cartel vs. cartel dispute.

Obviously, we should tread carefully with this kind of speculation, but it does fit with previously established patterns of violence in Mexico. Cartel culture, just like fashion or books or anything else on the internet, seems to inspire a layer of influencers and hangers-on. And the rise of livestreaming means that these influencer murders are now taking place in real time for audiences that can stretch into the millions.
