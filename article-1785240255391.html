Why Don't Women Make the First Move?

Making the first move is a sexual turnoff for many women, even if they know they won't be rejected.

Now that we live in a world where men asking women out in real-life settings feels more awkward than ever, many men (especially those frustrated with dating or experiencing some degree of anxiety about rejection—aka, most single men) clamor for a world where the burden of taking the initiative is shifted to women.

It makes so much sense. Women reject men more often than men reject women, so presumably, more pairings would happen this way because fewer initiators would be rejected. Also, women, unless they are wielding a giant machine with “Scrotum Destroyer” printed on the side of it, generally do not make men feel unsafe, so there would be less risk involved for everyone (and besides, I have it on good authority that some men would be really into the scrotum destroyer.)

I’ve heard the following refrain from many men over the years: Okay, so making the first move is scary and women are afraid of rejection. Well, guess what? So are men! Why must men face the embarrassment but women get a pass?

So, if this is a genuine question and not just a rhetorical vent, this article will cover it, because there are several answers, all far more multifaceted than “women are afraid of rejection.”

Women do make first moves. They don’t make last moves.

Women are, basically, terrible closers. When men talk about women making the “first move,” they are imagining women approaching men in bookstores and asking them on dates, buying men drinks from across the bar, slipping their numbers to handsome waiters, and being the one to officially escalate a friendship with a coworker into something more. Some women do these things, but most don’t, at least not frequently. This type of initiative-taking is typically associated with men.

But these aren’t really first moves. These are last moves. Women do make first moves. The problem is, when men talk about “first moves,” they’re really talking about closing. Women are terrible at closing unless they’re selling you a revolutionary CRM system that’s completely disrupting the SaaS industry. As the saying goes, women chase a man until he catches her. Unfortunately, a lot of wires get crossed because men are bad at noticing that women are chasing, and women are bad at chasing, and even worse at closing.

Some examples of first moves that women make: instigating conversations (perhaps flirtatious ones) with men in their social circle who they find attractive, making flirtatious eye contact, asking a guy to hang out casually with plausible deniability, and of course, everyone’s favorite: asking “What are we?”

The problem is that for whatever reason, these moves are not always apparent to men. During my freshman year of college, I had a crush on a guy on the periphery of my social circle. I made a lot of excuses to talk to him at parties, made a lot of flirty eye contact, but never escalated to anything explicitly romantic, in part because I wasn’t picking up on any interest from him. In fact, he almost never looked me in the eye and seemed bored by my presence. Eventually, I moved on and began dating someone else. Later on, during a casual group hangout, I made a joke about how I used to have a crush on him but he clearly wasn’t into it. He overheard, and exclaimed, “What?! I’m only finding this out now?!” and summarily began banging his head into the table.

I have experienced multiple other situations—typically in late high school or college—where I made many moves to “hang out” with a guy, but none of the hangouts were explicitly romantic. I would ask to watch a movie, to take a walk, or to do basically anything else that I would also do with a girlfriend, and then just hope the guy escalated it to obvious romance. Sometimes they did, but sometimes they didn’t. During my freshman year of college, I hung out with a guy for about six hours straight, went back to his dorm, even slept in his bed, but nothing happened between us. It’s possible he just wasn’t interested but didn’t know how to get me to go away, or it’s possible he was like, “Why is this tired foid trying to sleep in my bed? Mogged by BedChad oncemore!”

I have a feeling that less extreme version of this happen between many men and women. The woman believes she has made the first move, the man never knew she was doing it because she did not do anything explicit, and the relationship never happens.

(For the sake of this article, “first moves” will refer to what we think of as first moves—the more concrete and risky moves typically associated with men.)

Women do get rejected.

This is not so much a reason for women’s lack of initiative (being rejected is hardly an excuse for refusing to take initiative ever again, and that goes for all genders!) but something you should understand if you’re genuinely curious about what dating is like for women.

A favorite online-man refrain is that women don’t get rejected unless they are either monstrously chopped or exclusively interacting with men who look like Jacob Elordi. This is simply not true. Almost any woman who has engaged in dating at one point in her life has been rejected. I am not claiming that women are rejected as often as men are (remember, women “approach” in ways with a lot of plausible deniability so that the rejection isn’t as explicit or humiliating, plus men are genuinely less picky) but the blanket statement that women—especially good-looking women—never experience rejection is just not true.

Any woman who has been dumped, ghosted, or stood up has been rejected. Again, I am not saying women’s experience with rejection is the same as it is for men, but “women don’t get rejected unless they’re ugly or ” isn’t true.

When I wrote about a boyfriend in high school who wouldn’t touch me (who, get this, wasn’t gay! He’s now married to a woman!) most of the men on Substack understood that perhaps he had social anxiety, felt like he’d be taking advantage of me, or that even though we liked each other on paper there was just no heat. Men on Twitter, on the other hand, were unconvinced. This was such an unfathomable story for them that they assumed the guy was gay after all (and is now in a lavender marriage) or more realistically that I was actually a disgusting bridge troll who didn’t brush my teeth. I don’t think I’ll ever fully know the reason, but you can be a slim, well-groomed, decent-looking woman and get rejected! It happens all the time!

Another story of how I got rejected: When I was seventeen, I worked at a summer camp, and I thought another counselor there was cute. I asked him on a date with some degree of plausible deniability (aka, I called it “hanging out”). I didn’t get many flirtatious vibes from him on the date, but I reassured myself that this was just negative self-talk and countered these “intrusive thoughts” by valiantly pushing through them and resolving to be bold. At the end of our date, he drove me home, and I leaned in to kiss him. He turned away and I wound up kissing his earlobe. Humiliated, I tried to vacate the car immediately but he had a child lock on, so I had to sheepishly turn around and ask him to unlock it. But at least I’d never see him again after that summer, right? Wrong. I started college that fall, many miles away, and by pure chance, he happened to be in my freshman class.

Rejection does not always happen because women are overshooting their league. In hindsight, that guy wasn’t even very conventionally attractive. He wasn’t even “camp counselor hot.” Almost all of the counselors had been female, and he was literally the only single male camp counselor in my age range. I have been rejected by multiple men who were my height (5’5”) or shorter, the exact guys who, according to online discourse, don’t have anyone wanting to date them and would be happy to take anyone (doubt.) Generally speaking, my rejections were never me using a pickup line on a random guy at the drugstore, because again, that’s not a woman-coded approach. Instead, we were in a casual environment (high school or college) where there was lots of “hanging out” that did not necessarily count as a date, I tried to escalate to more 1:1 hangouts, and the guy eventually stopped responding to texts or ignoring me. These rejections were nowhere near as humiliating as having a drink thrown in my face (does this really happen often, outside of Vince Vaughan movies?) but they happened!

It isn’t fair? That’s sexual dimorphism.

It is not, in fact, fair, that men are expected to make the first moves more frequently than women. It’s 2026. Why do women “wait” for a proposal instead of proposing themselves? Why do women complain that men don’t ask them out but then refuse to ask men out themselves? Well, the answer to those questions is actually the most important part of why women don’t make moves.

Heterosexual attraction typically revolves around sexual dimorphism, or emphasizing the differences between men and women. A sociology professor will probably refer to this as “hegemonic” masculinity or femininity, and lightly imply it’s a bad thing, but it’s just a core part of our sexuality. The prototypical man is bigger, stronger, hairier, tougher and more aggressive than the prototypical woman. This is why, for example, when it became fashionable to show more skin, it also became expected for women to remove visible body hair. It’s not fair that many men are turned off by hairy legs on a woman and women accept hairy legs on a man! But this is a preference for sexual dimorphism at play. Even though men and women naturally both have body hair, men have more of it, so by removing it, women are exaggerating their difference from men, and therefore their attractiveness (I diverge with the people who believe a preference for shaved pits is about pedophilia.)

This is also why men find Sydney Sweeney’s bosom hot, but when Bryon Noem showed up with his tig ol’ bitties, women thought it was weird, and his wife divorced him like a dog. It’s unfair! But such is life.

Women know the body hair double standard is unfair. Women have the right to continue to grow out their body hair, and some men will not care at all, but many will consider it a turnoff, and those are just the cards women are dealt. Similarly, a man who refuses to take any initiative because “it isn’t fair” is technically correct, but also shooting himself in the foot.

As I’ve written before, many women (myself included) are repulsed by the idea of initiating sex, even in relationships where they know their partner would be down for it. It’s not a matter of wanting sex less, necessarily. It’s a matter of, at least in part, wanting to feel like they want sex less than their partner does, when they secretly want it really badly. It sounds nonsensical, of course, and I’m not making a value judgment either way—you should just know that this is a common thing, just as it’s common for men to be turned off by what they know is perfectly natural body hair on a woman.

If you look at people who are into CNC (or consensual non-consent) the interest is overwhelmingly women who want to take on the role of the faux-non-consentor, and a smaller fragment of men who want to be the aggressor. There is a reason that almost zero women are interested in CNC from the aggressor perspective, (although I think there would be a non-zero amount of men interested in having their clothes ripped off by a sexually ravenous woman). In any case, being pursued is extremely important to the sexuality of many heterosexual women, in a way that being pursued is not central to straight men’s sexuality, even if it’s fun as an occasional fantasy.

A few charts to back up my point (sources are my own surveys.)

Straight women have a significantly stronger preference for wanting to be pursued than straight men (the preference was also more significant than all segments of LGBTQ respondents, but the sample size there was pretty small.)

As you can see here, there is no huge unmet need for men to be the pursued party. Some men would prefer being pursued by women, but most prefer an egalitarian dynamic where both people are making moves in harmony. There is a delta between how aggressive women want men to be versus how aggressive men want to be, but the preferences are not a flipped mirror where both genders hate initiating but only women are able to get away with it. Most men are not turned on by being the “conquered” party the way women are.

More proof that it’s not just about a fear of rejection: in a hypothetical scenario where one must initiate sex 100% of the time, but had a guarantee that they’d never be rejected, women were still not interested in being the initiators. Fewer than 10% of women (but almost 40% of men!) would be fine with this arrangement.

But why? Why are women so beholden to these rigid gender norms? We have at least three genders now! Well, because…it’s not just about societal messages. I’m sure socialization is part of it, and I understand I’m hedging a bit here, but I find it hard to believe that women are not at least somewhat hardwired, on average, to appreciate “safe” aggression—the caveman who is gentle enough not to kill you, but aggressive and strong enough kill any saber toothed tiger who gets near the cave. But women only want this from men they already find at least a little attractive—which yes, I know is frustrating because the blanket advice “be aggressive” can land you in jail.

And what women should understand is that men have good reason to be less aggressive—not to be terrified of speaking to women in public, but to be wary of instigating relationships with coworkers, or in settings where there could be significant social blowback. In these scenarios, a guy’s hesitance has nothing to do with his lack of interest! Which brings me to…

“If he wanted to, he would.”

Not every woman says this, but a lot of women feel it on some level. Basically, even if a woman is not particularly traditional, she still lives in a society where men making the first moves is the expectation. As a result, the prospect of making the first move conjures up the thought: if I have to make the first move in a world where men are expected to do it, shouldn’t I just assume he’s not that interested? If he was interested, wouldn’t he have already approached me?

Of course, that’s not always the case. Sometimes, other factors which are unrelated to a lack of desire can prevent a man from making the first move. One clear example would be if the man is very shy and anxious, and would rather just stay single than experience what he irrationally perceives as guaranteed humiliation. This was basically what happened with my senior year high school ex-boyfriend. We were already friends, and because of this, I knew that he had severe social anxiety, especially around dating. I heard through mutual friends that he found me attractive, but because I already knew him so well, I was aware that there was a zero percent chance that he’d ever take the risk. Because I had less anxiety about dating than he did, I took it upon myself to ask him out. (I apologize for how juvenile these stories are, but they are still relevant!)

This worked out because I already knew why he wasn’t going to initiate. (Granted, by “worked out” I mean we started dating. We broke up three months later because, well, I call it “being seventeen.”) Most women don’t know anything about the mental state of men who are not approaching them, and ultimately most men don’t have crippling social anxiety. So when a woman has been sending “signals” to a man, and he doesn’t react by asking her out, escalating the situation, or kissing her, she will likely write him off as just not interested, as opposed to thinking, “Oh, clearly he’s waiting for me to do it!”

A great example of how this dynamic plays out constantly in real life: the women who wait years for marriage proposals. In this way, women make lots of first moves, but refuse to make the last move. Many of the women who report waiting five, seven or even ten years for marriage proposals have repeatedly said to their boyfriend, “I want to marry you, please tell me when that’s going to happen,” and realistically speaking, that is a proposal that keeps getting rejected every time he says, “I’m not in the right place for it now because I’m still working on starting my brewery.”

My husband took four years from the time we started dating to propose, a waiting period that filled me with dread. Part of me was convinced he was going to waste eight years of my life, suddenly declare I wasn’t the one, and then marry the next woman he met after three months—a pattern you see far too often for the fear to be irrational. We talked about marriage a lot. I asked him how he felt about it. He told me he wanted to marry me. I asked him about a timeline. In this way, it felt like I was making plenty of first moves! Was I not repeatedly proposing to him, unofficially? But I drew the line on getting down on one knee, or slipping a ring on my own finger. Humiliating. Not because I suspected he’d say no (he already made it clear he did want to marry me) but because it would ruin the entire experience for me—an experience that was supposed to be one of the best moments of my life. I knew that if I proposed, for the rest of my life, I’d be wondering why I wanted it so much more than he did that I had to beat him to the punch.

As I’ve also written before, and hinted upon earlier in this article, being the more desired party is also core to a lot of straight women’s sexuality in a way that it’s not core to straight men’s sexuality. In fact, the opposite is true. Straight men revel in joking about scoring a woman who is out of their league, while women find that concept embarrassing. Women do not want to be the “ugly one” nor do they want to be the chaser, the pursuer, or the conqueror. Men, on the other hand, might get annoyed by the burden of asking women out, and they might fear rejection, but they are not fundamentally repulsed by the idea of being the initiator, or by being the less attractive one in a couple. Wanting their female partner more than she wants them is seen as the default, and not anything particularly shameful. It is very different for most straight women.

But, again, things have changed. A man can desire a woman very much, perhaps even more than she desires him, and still be afraid to do anything about it because the potential social consequences are too high. You could think your coworker is the finest specimen on Earth, but still be too afraid of losing your job or getting in trouble with HR to try anything. For these reasons, women should try to reframe making the first move in scenarios that feel especially risky for men. If he’s not doing anything, it probably doesn’t mean he doesn’t think you’re that hot—it could mean that he thinks he’s likely to be rejected!

A dating “hack”

While I’ve just investigated all components of why a woman might refuse to make the first move—even in situations where rejection is off the table—I would be remiss not to point out that if a woman is comfortable making first moves (aka, last moves) then she is in a wonderful position for dating. And as I’ve pointed out, I don’t have a great track record doing this myself, but if my husband left me tomorrow I think I’d have no choice but to get a little more aggressive since we are now living in a different world from one where women could expect to be frequently approached by decent-quality men.

This tendency of mine was disadvantageous even in the olden days. While my own dating history is not very long, I can recall many times that a romantic rival of mine succeeded where I failed. Of course, it’s possible that these girls were uniformly better-looking than I was, but I don’t think I’m delusional about my looks and I think everyone involved was in the same basic league. The issue, actually, was that they were more aggressive. I could never find it in myself to literally jump on a guy I found attractive as a way of “playing.” And yet, I have been romantically defeated by many girls who have engaged in this type of behavior.

If a woman is able to pursue men (and better yet, pursue men aggressively) she is at a major advantage in the dating market. There are plenty of men who are more passive or anxious, and other men who aren’t, but would still be intrigued by a woman who confidently approached them. If a woman played the role of an aggressive initiator, I think some men would be confused and perhaps search the premises for hidden cameras, but many others would just be pleasantly surprised or turned on. The problem isn’t that men would hate it, but rather that women would hate doing it so much that it would ruin the entire experience for them and forever leave them with questions about whether the man was ever that interested or whether they had started a relationship predicated on the woman desiring the man more than the man desired the woman—and if so, ew.

This is, perhaps, an irrational hangup (although not entirely irrational, in my view) but if a woman doesn’t view the world this way, she is at a huge advantage! For single women who read this entire article and came away thinking, “Well, God, I’m nothing like that, this woman has ridiculous sexual hangups,” then congratulations, you’re in a good spot. What stops women from making more explicit first moves is not necessarily society, expectations, and it’s definitely not men—it’s the limitations of their own sexuality. If you do not have these limitations, or if you are able to reframe assumptions about a man not approaching you first (perhaps it’s not about his lack of interest!) you will likely do quite well.
