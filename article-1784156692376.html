The abundance agenda has arrived

The biggest federal housing package in a generation becomes law.

Halina Bennet
Jul 15
∙
Paid
 
READ IN APP
 

The 21st Century ROAD to Housing Act is officially federal law. Congress passed it with bipartisan majorities but, rather than sign it, President Donald Trump let the constitutional clock run out and the bill became law on Saturday. Supporters are calling it the most significant piece of federal housing legislation in decades.

The bill includes supply-side provisions exempting infill housing from parts of environmental review, kills the chassis requirement that’s kept manufactured housing artificially expensive, and offers federal incentives to localities that agree to build more. It also folds in appraisal reform and rural-housing provisions.

One of the most politically distinctive pieces is Senator Raphael Warnock’s provision barring large private-equity firms from buying additional single-family homes following data showing that more than one in four single-family rental homes in metro Atlanta are now owned by large investors — one of the highest concentrations in the country. Warnock has spent years arguing that institutional buyers with cash offers crowd out ordinary families, and the new law caps future purchases by the largest firms, CBS News reports.

But beyond the investor provision, the abundance/YIMBY coalition is treating the monumental legislation as validation of its project. Derek Kaufman of Inclusive Abundance Action framed it in a statement to Slow Boring as proof that the argument has shifted from “how to help families afford homes that cost too much” to “why homes cost so much in the first place” and that the supply-side answer has now moved from an idea circulated among the in-crowd to a federal statute. He credited players across the ideological spectrum who collaborated to make the bill possible, including Senate Banking’s Tim Scott and Elizabeth Warren, House Financial Services’ French Hill and Maxine Waters, plus more than 100 pro-housing groups.

California overhauls affordable-housing finance

Governor Gavin Newsom signed a housing bill (AB 179) that reworks how California funds affordable housing by replacing multiple separate financing applications with a single “one-stop shop” application and coordinating bond, tax credit, and grant awards through a new Housing Development and Finance Committee.

His administration writes that the goal is to reduce delays that can add months and significant costs to projects while developers assemble multiple funding sources. The law also discourages local governments from imposing development impact fees on state-funded affordable-housing projects by reducing state funding to jurisdictions that continue to do so, creating a strong incentive for cities and counties to waive the fees. State officials estimate the plan could lower affordable-housing development costs by $60,000 to $70,000 per unit while getting projects financed and under construction more quickly.

San Francisco changes its inclusionary-housing requirement

In a nine-to-two vote Tuesday, the Board of Supervisors slashed San Francisco’s inclusionary-housing mandate from 15 percent to 5 percent in most of the city. The move comes after a court-ordered feasibility study concluded that market-rate construction has essentially stalled under current costs and interest rates and found that even a 5 percent mandate could render projects infeasible, Mission Local reports. The study asserts that “requirements significantly above 0 percent would further threaten feasibility” and would not, in the end, result in more affordable housing.

The move continues the city’s pattern of reversing earlier increases in inclusionary requirements when housing construction slows. It is San Francisco’s second cut since 2023, when the city reduced the requirement from 22 percent to between 12 and 15 percent. Supervisor Jackie Fielder got a partial carve-out passed, putting the Mission District, which had a previous requirement of 17 percent, at 8 percent instead of the 5 percent rate imposed elsewhere in the city, over the objection of supervisors who wanted a uniform rate. But an A.I.-driven influx of impending I.P.O. wealth is pushing Bay Area housing prices higher even as construction remains stalled, complicating the city’s usual boom-and-slump playbook and forcing it to make quick decisions about how it will solve the affordability crisis.

Also Tuesday, the supervisors passed by a seven-to-four vote an ordinance requiring that any permanent supportive housing the city fully funds on its own (without state dollars) maintain a drug-free policy, meaning residents using illicit drugs on-site could be evicted or relocated. It’s a departure from the Housing First model the city has followed for years, in which tenants in supportive housing are generally protected from eviction over drug use.

Because most permanent-housing programs rely on state funding, which still requires Housing First compliance, the practical reach of the new policy is narrow for now: The city’s only current drug-free example is a single 42-unit building. Opponents, led again by Fielder, argued the bill lacks protections for people who get evicted and could push them into shelters rather than housing, Mission Local reports.

Yes in God’s — and Massachusetts’s — backyard

State House lawmakers folded a “Yes in God’s Backyard” (YIGBY) provision into Massachusetts’s economic development bill, letting religious institutions build multifamily housing by right on land they’ve owned for at least three years without any special zoning permission required. The bill caps eligible parcels at four acres and excludes land zoned for schools. Those changes were made to head off objections from other House members, according to a Boston Globe editorial.

The case for passing the provision is mostly about land. A Lincoln Institute study commissioned by the Lynch Foundation found religious groups hold roughly 5,000 developable parcels statewide totaling 20,000 acres, much of it already near water and sewer lines and transit. Opponents, including the Massachusetts Municipal Association, argue the by-right structure cuts out the local input that normally governs infrastructure and neighborhood-compatibility questions.

Nationally, the housing deficit stopped getting worse

Zillow’s latest analysis of census data found the U.S. housing deficit held essentially flat in 2024 — growing by just 43,000 units, down from increases of 257,000 in 2022 and 159,000 in 2023. The study attributes the improvement to a 50-year high in multifamily completions, which helped new housing supply roughly match growth in demand for the first time since the 2008 crash ushered in a prolonged period of underbuilding.

The deficit itself is still an enormous 4.7 million units nationally, and it is heavily concentrated in the most expensive, most restrictively zoned metros: New York, L.A., Boston, San Francisco, and D.C. The pattern Zillow’s economists point to is the usual case in which metros that built more during the pandemic-era demand surge saw affordability recover faster than metros that didn’t.

Housing beats hurricanes in Brooklyn

New York City has finalized plans to build a three-tower, 1,300-unit complex on the Greenpoint/Williamsburg waterfront, right next to Bushwick Inlet Park.

Twenty years after the area’s original rezoning, only about a third of the parkland promised has actually opened, and developer Gotham Organization is now taking over cleanup and buildout of the rest as part of its deal to lease the site for 99 years, the New York Times reports.

The site sits in one of the city’s highest-risk hurricane evacuation zones. It’ll be elevated and built behind a new steel bulkhead, which critics say could worsen flooding elsewhere and cut off public access to the water — the opposite of the soft, absorbent parkland some residents and a Columbia geophysicist argue flood-prone sites like this actually need, according to the Times.

A Regional Plan Association report cited by the Times estimates the New York metro area could lose up to 80,000 homes to flooding over the next 15 years, even as the region may need a million more homes by 2040. A similar fight is playing out over a roughly 6,000-unit redevelopment in Red Hook, another low-lying waterfront neighborhood in Brooklyn.

The abundance case for building more housing and the climate-adaptation case for retreating from vulnerable waterfront land aren’t automatically aligned and are often, in fact, in direct tension. For now, density wins.
