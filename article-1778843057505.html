MAGA loves the deep state now

Republicans hated the FBI until it became Trump’s FBI

Kobe Yank-Jacobs
May 15
∙
Paid
 
READ IN APP
 

Welcome back to The Argument’s poll series, where we survey Americans on the issues everyone’s fighting about. Our full crosstabs are available below the paywall at the end of this post. Our last surveys have asked about crime and public safety, the economy, gender issues, immigration, education and parenting, the lingering politics of COVID-19, immigration, AI, and free speech. The Argument’s full methodology can be read here.

All of a sudden, Republicans love the deep state.

In The Argument’s poll on crime and public safety, fielded from April 20 to 23, 2026, among 1,516 registered voters, we tested support for various law enforcement agencies. Among respondents overall, local police got the highest favorability (70% had a very or somewhat favorable opinion), followed by the TSA (58%), while agencies like the FBI (50%) and CIA (45%) got lower marks. ICE was not only the least popular (40% support) but — as you might expect — the most politically polarized. Seventy-nine percent of self-described Republicans supported ICE. Just 10% of Democrats did.

That Republicans like cops (81%) more than Democrats (65%) is old news, but what caught my eye was how quickly Trump has managed to make federal law enforcement agencies — that his supporters once derided as full of corrupt, biased, deep-state Democrats — popular with his base.

Over the past decade, the FBI has been more widely supported by Democrats after Trump declared war on it in his first administration.

To put this in context, between 2022 and now, the partisan gap in opinion of the FBI has shifted from D+50 in a Gallup poll asking about job performance to R+34 in our poll asking about favorability. Republicans’ favorability for the CIA was also 27 points higher than Democrats’.

These massive shifts far outstrip the effect you would expect merely from the change in administrations. In the past year and a half, Trump has installed loyalists and pressured out agents who he viewed as political enemies for working on cases like those against the Jan. 6 rioters. As a result, partisan sentiment on agencies like the FBI has flipped.

The FBI’s partisan polarization

Trump fired FBI Director James Comey in May 2017 for refusing to end the Russia investigation — the first blatant case of him trying to bring a nonpartisan agency under his control.

At that point, Trump’s authoritarian impulses had manifested as cases of petty lying (the size of the inauguration crowd) and in the targeting of foreigners (the travel ban from Muslim-majority countries).

The Comey firing was a different kind of step: It was a clear sign that Trump wouldn’t just bluster like an authoritarian — he was ready to govern like one, too.

Partisan tampering might seem unfortunately familiar now. According to Reuters, at least 247 people or entities have been singled out by name for retribution by Trump or his administration in the second term.

But back in 2017, The New York Times reported the firing as a “stunning development.” In a now-familiar pattern, the left called the firing an abuse of power and pleaded with Republicans to acknowledge this, while the right mostly turned a deaf ear, citing liberal paranoia.

“Anyone is welcome to believe that this firing was unwise, and to make the case for that,” Mollie Hemingway wrote in The Federalist. “But it’s wise to make that case calmly.”

“I’m not going to credit theories about Watergate-level conspiracies without evidence,” Charles C.W. Cooke wrote in National Review. “That’s the line that divides us.”

It did not take long for this divide to appear in public opinion.

The following year, in a 2018 Pew Research Center poll, FBI support among Republicans had slipped 10 points from the year prior. The 23-point partisan gap on the FBI was the widest in the whole 2018 poll on federal agencies and the widest on record. That partisan gap remained strong throughout Trump’s first term.

Crucially, many other agencies remained unpolarized at the same time. For instance, Republicans dislike taxes, but in 2019, with Trump in office, the IRS was essentially break-even at 53 to 52 between Republicans and Democrats (R+1). This was not the case with the FBI under Trump, which remained underwater with Republicans throughout his first term. The FBI showed how Trump could polarize a nonpartisan agency just by attacking it, the first real example that shows up in opinion polling from the past decade.

The tyranny, of course, is that once this happens, even legitimate moves by an agency can be reframed as partisan attacks.

In August of 2022, during the Biden administration, the FBI raided Mar-a-Lago and found boxes of classified documents sitting in a shower. By October of that year, only 29% of Republicans supported the agency, down from 65% at the start of Trump’s first term. (This put the partisan gap at D+50.)

Even with clear evidence of presidential wrongdoing, Republican partisans blamed the agency for doing its job.

In our polling from April, we saw this partisan divide flip the other way. Republicans like the agency by a large margin. This is not a good thing, nor is it the end of the agency’s polarization. It represents the success Trump has had subduing it, along with much of the rest of the federal government, by installing loyalists.

Evidence of this is sometimes too on the nose. FBI Director Kash Patel literally released a children’s book referring to “King Donald” before he joined the FBI. After years of disliking the FBI, Republicans have come around now that their own people run the agency.

Public support for law enforcement agencies
"Do you have a favorable or unfavorable opinion of the following law enforcement agencies?"

Agencies like the FBI and CIA used to epitomize the “deep state” for Trump Republicans. Now that the intel community is filled with loyalists, his supporters have come around.

Is the Fed next?

The FBI was the first agency to polarize in this way, but it won’t be the last. The DOJ has already become an R+34 institution after polling nearly evenly at 60% among Democrats to 57% among Republicans in his first administration, and it’s temporarily being led by Trump’s former personal lawyer.

Similarly, Trump ally Kevin Warsh — his pick to replace Fed Chair Jerome Powell, who he’s repeatedly tried to bully to lower interest rates — was just confirmed for the role this week.

Beyond the public attacks on Powell, Trump’s DOJ has also opened investigations into one of the Fed Board governors (for alleged errors on mortgage forms) and into the Fed itself (for allegedly misspending money allocated for renovations). Both of these are thin pretenses for pressuring the independent agency.

Support for the Federal Reserve was break-even at a high level in both parties in 2018 — 65% to 65%. But polls from 2025 showed that Democrats supported the Fed at a level 19 points higher than Republicans did.

If polling shows a sudden flip after Warsh’s confirmation, we will have seen the FBI pattern playing out again, with partisans coming around to support an agency once the loyalists take over.
