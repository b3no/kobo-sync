#MeToo didn’t go far enough

Can women ever be free from fear?

Justin Zuckerman
Aug 6

True freedom requires a person with a will — and a society that is organized to allow the expression of that will.

I can want something as simple as to walk my dog at night. But if roving gangs threaten my life, then my freedom to pursue that desire is only theoretical. Even worse is if the threat is so pervasive that I can’t even imagine walking my dog at night. In neither case am I really free.

This diminished freedom has long been, to greater and lesser degrees, the status quo for most women in most places.

Even in a society as egalitarian as the contemporary U.S., polling shows that women feel less safe to roam. Globally, “men are over 20% more likely than women to say they feel safe in their communities.” One paper about women in Delhi finds that women will choose to go to much worse colleges in order to feel safer while traveling, whereas men with similar choices will choose to attend the better colleges more frequently.

One researcher, Alice Evans, recently argued that “our analyses of progress towards gender equality still focus on economic activity and political representation, omitting a far more basic question: can women walk down the street alone at night and be at ease?”

It is not immediately obvious that women should feel less free than men: Men are much more likely than women to be victims of homicide and robbery. Of course, a big part of this is that boys are more likely to be in public spaces unsupervised in the first place.

The freedom to move in the world unburdened by the fear of violence and harassment is a fundamental prerequisite to basically every other freedom we care about: to say what you really think, to associate with whomever you wish. It can be hard to imagine a world where the average woman remains physically weaker than the average man and yet feels as free. But one thing gives me hope: If you were to describe the freedom enjoyed by many women in rich, low-crime neighborhoods in modern-day America to a woman living in the 1700s, it would become clear just how much legal, social, and cultural progress can be made.

#MeToo’s unfinished revolution

The slogan Me Too was first used in 2006 by the activist Tarana Burke to promote what she called “empowerment through empathy.” The idea was that victims of sexual violence and harassment often feel isolated by their experiences. Making people aware that there are others who have gone through the same thing plausibly makes them feel less alone.

But after the hashtag went truly viral in 2017, the slogan’s original purpose no longer seemed to meet the enormity of the task. The allegations against Harvey Weinstein and other abusive male power brokers were horrifying, and the veritable downpour of crimes revealed by women everywhere felt endless. We couldn’t wait for the broad social awareness being cultivated by the hashtag to trickle down into people’s behavior. Responding to stories of assault, harassment, and inequality with mere “likes” on social media was clearly not enough.

But the signal failure of #MeToo was that in seeking to counteract the norm of doubting women’s accounts, “accused” became nearly synonymous with “guilty.” More broadly, #MeToo, by highlighting women’s vulnerability, cast all men in the role of perpetrator.

In 2022, Burke wrote a retrospective in which she gave examples of the accomplishments of #MeToo. Besides removing “captains of industry” from positions of power, her examples were largely expressive:

  • “More than any law that has been passed or policy that has been changed in the past five years, this movement has created visibility and community.”

  • “The language we use is shifting, replacing words like victim with survivor.”

  • “We talk about mental health and healing.”

  • “[E]mployers are crafting thoughtful human-resource policies.”

  • “Consumers are more conscientious about the cultural content they consume.”

  • Consumers are “vocal about calling out rape culture.”

Burke, not totally comfortable with this accounting, quoted another feminist author, Imani Perry, who argued that “awareness is not a virtue in and of itself.” Burke expressed ambivalence about incarcerating more rapists and ended her piece by calling on the reader to do their part.

But what is their part? If awareness is not enough, and the goal is to effect mass cultural change without incarcerating a lot more rapists, what exactly should we be doing?

My cohost Matt Yglesias argued that the #MeToo movement was thin on specific demands and failed to accomplish very much, which is reasonable enough. Earlier feminist wins were concrete: Recognize our right to vote. Recognize our right to own property. Recognize our right to divorce our husbands and attend college.

The main problem is that both critics and supporters of the #MeToo movement tended to underrate just how radically society would have to change for women to feel as safe as men, or even to come close.

For starters, there is no specific legislative demand that could eliminate sexual harassment. And sexual violence is different from other serious crimes like murder or theft. There is often not dispositive physical evidence like there would be if someone shot you or broke into your house, and therefore cases are typically based almost entirely on witness testimony.

Moreover, victims themselves are often torn about going through the legal system. Sometimes they just want to move on rather than deal with a years-long process that may retraumatize them while hinging entirely on their perceived credibility as a witness. Sometimes the perpetrator is a friend, partner, or community member who they don’t actually want sent to jail.

And, of course, so much of what makes women and girls feel unsafe is not rape itself but the threat of sexual harassment that does not rise to the level of criminal activity.

True equality for women cannot exist in a world where girls are afraid to walk home from school alone and women have to remain on high alert while going about their normal lives. The unfortunate fact is that you can’t legislate freedom.

You can’t bar men from catcalling 13-year-olds, or from leering at women jogging at night. So, what do you do? I don’t think there’s an obvious answer.
