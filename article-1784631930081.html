How the center-left can come back in 2028

There are a bunch of issues where the establishment is to the left of most Democratic primary voters.

Jul 21
∙

A recent Nate Silver post posed “12 questions for the center-left.”

It’s one of the best pieces I’ve read on the factional politics of the Democratic Party, in part because it raises great questions, but in large part because unlike most commentary, it actually acknowledges a difference between the center-left factional project and the ongoing efforts of “the establishment” to fend off the left-wing insurgency.

In other words, the faction in question is (by definition) the faction that is trying to mount a centrist critique of the Clinton-Biden-Harris-Schumer status quo, not the faction trying to defend that status quo.

Rather than try to answer all 12 questions individually, though, I’m going to say that I think the answer to all these questions and dilemmas is pretty clear: The center-left faction needs a 2028 presidential primary campaign that articulates something that resembles our critique of the status quo.

Ideally, of course, that campaign would win.

But it’s hard to win a presidential primary, and the odds of any given person or strategy prevailing are low. You can nonetheless accomplish important things by running a good campaign, things that help the movement gain traction and attract support.

One of the most important is bending the larger arc of the primary toward your ideas.

Elizabeth Warren’s 2020 campaign didn’t win — she ultimately didn’t even come close. But her ideas attracted a lot of attention, and other candidates (including more moderate ones and Bernie Sanders) seemed to feel they had to play catch-up on a number of issues. And the personnel associated with her campaign eventually played a large role in the Biden administration.

Another is what Sanders did in 2016, laying the groundwork for future campaigns by seeing who rallies to your standard.

This is why I emphasize the idea that this campaign should articulate something that resembles our critique of the status quo. I get a lot of questions about specific names or personalities, and there is a known universe of statewide elected officials from frontline states. A couple of them seem unsuitable (Tony Evers is too old, Katie Hobbs is too press-shy, John Fetterman has marginalized himself), but it’s a big universe of reasonable possibilities.

Precisely the thing we don’t need, though, is a quest to attach a somewhat more appealing personality to the face of the same-old same-old.

We also, I think, clearly don’t need something doomed, like a Josh Shapiro campaign that emphasizes Israel and antisemitism as a factional topic. A lot of different candidates could run the kind of campaign that we need.

But the campaign needs to meet two criteria:

  1. It needs to emphasize moderation on the kinds of issues where being too far left has been electorally costly to Democrats (i.e., not Social Security or health care).

  2. It needs to emphasize moderation on issues where rank-and-file primary voters might agree with moderates (i.e., not Israel).

This in turn requires tough-mindedness on two points.

First, you can’t take the attitude heading into the primary that the worst possible outcome is A.O.C. or Ro Khanna becoming the nominee and that this needs to be stopped at all costs. The optimal way to stop A.O.C. is to try to box her out by refusing to articulate any moderate critiques of the status quo at all. But a campaign like that won’t accomplish anything useful.

Second, you need to articulate a moderate vision that appeals to voters rather than to donors. It’s great that a lot of donors are worried about the leftward drift of the party, and you of course want their money.

But politicians are smarter about politics than donors. We need a smart political argument, and then we need to sell donors on that argument, not run a campaign focused on expressive politics for donors.

Where Democrats are to the left of their own base

I think the beginning of wisdom on this topic is a paper I’ve referenced before by David Broockman and Josh Kalla titled “Should Moving to the Middle Win Candidates Votes? It Depends Where Voters Are.”

Their point is that while it’s true that moderates in general do better in elections, the specific issues on which candidates moderate matter quite a bit. On a handful of issues, notably Social Security, the Democratic Party position is so popular that moving to the middle would actually result in a worse electoral performance.

On other issues, such as guns and abortion, moving to a more centrist posture wins votes, but not very many because while status quo Democratic Party positioning is unpopular, it’s only slightly unpopular.

By contrast, they find that moving to the center on affirmative action makes a Democratic candidate a lot more popular because the establishment view is deeply underwater.¹

One important function of this paper is that it helps explain why the electability gap between leftist Democrats and establishment Democrats, though real, is modest.

The positions where Democrats would help themselves the most by moving to the center mostly deal with issues, like affirmative action, where establishment Democrats and leftists don’t disagree.

The problem with affirmative action in a primary, though, is that in the Broockman/Kalla polling, the unpopular establishment stance is popular with Democratic Party primary voters. And since Democrats don’t disagree about this, it probably won’t come up in a primary and there’s no reason to affirmatively bring it up if it’s just going to make it harder to win the primary.

But here’s the good news. On a related topic, the Biden administration proposed targeted assistance to Black-owned small businesses. If you reject that view in favor of “the government should provide special loan programs and business support for small businesses in low-income neighborhoods, regardless of the owner’s race,” you have a position that not only gains you votes in a general election but is also more popular with Democratic Party primary voters.

There are a number of issues like that in their survey, including hot-button culture-war topics like sex-segregated sports teams for kids and gender reassignment surgery for minors as well as technocratic issues like charter schools and performance pay for teachers. Probably the most dreary and technical one is that if you pair standard Democratic proposals for forcing down the price of prescription drugs with a promise to “cut regulations that make it hard to build drug factories in America,” that’s more popular with both swing voters and primary voters. I wish they had tested a message that involved regulatory changes to clinical trials rather than just factories because I suspect that might be powerful.

Two ideas they tested that I haven’t heard discussed much relate to immigration. Democratic Party primary voters have progressive views on many immigration topics, of course, but there are two notable exceptions:

  1. Keeping the number of refugees and asylum seekers admitted from poor or war-torn countries at its current level is more popular among Democratic Party primary voters than promising to let in more asylum seekers.

  2. “Allow people whose parents brought them to the U.S. illegally as children to become U.S. citizens if they pass a background check” (i.e., something like the DREAM Act) is the median preference among Democratic Party primary voters, with a broader path to citizenship just under 50 percent.

Finally, the issue where the moderate view most badly beats the progressive one among primary voters relates to nonviolent crimes.

Rank-and-file Democrats strongly prefer a promise to incarcerate repeat offenders for things like shoplifting to a promise to exclusively focus on violent criminals. I fade that particular moderation a little simply because it’s the one case where I think they may be straw-manning progressives a little. But the point is, it wouldn’t kill you with primary voters to visit a CVS in a low-income neighborhood and say, “We should be locking up criminals, not toothpaste.”

To be clear, I’m not saying you should run an entire presidential campaign exclusively on deregulating prescription drug factories (too wonky) or race-neutral small-business loans (too low-salience) or vowing not to increase asylum admissions (you can’t run on promises to not do things). The centerpiece of any Democratic Party primary campaign should obviously be the two things that all kinds of Democrats care about a lot: defeating MAGA and expanding access to health care.

But I want to see someone add some moderate sizzle to the steak! “I am mad as hell at a Democratic Party that lost twice to Donald Trump, and I want to throw those morons out of party leadership — we’re going to defeat MAGA and expand access to health care by refusing to lose elections over dumb shit.”

Don’t lose elections over dumb shit

“Moderate” is obviously terrible branding, to the point that it’s borderline impossible to get any moderate organizations off the ground because it immediately devolves into a conversation about what you should call it given that nobody wants to call it moderate.

One thing that’s bad about it is that on any given day, the modal moderate is going to be someone who represents a red state or district and, therefore, spends a lot of time trying to duck acute partisanship. That’s totally reasonable micro-politics, but obviously you can’t run a presidential primary campaign while ducking acute partisanship.

That’s why the key message needs to be about ditching loser politics that squanders winnable elections over dumb shit.

It needs to speak to the silent majority of Democratic Party primary voters who are not single-issue obsessives about which athletes play on which sports team but who are sick and tired of losing races over issues they don’t really care about. We’re going to turn the country over to fascists because we can’t stop banning plastic straws and won’t send petty criminals to prison? We need to be honest with ourselves that it was the Democratic establishment that came up with the idea of trying to nationalize the school sports issue.

Table-pounding partisanship also helps you get out of the trap of being pressured by the left and the groups to release endless reams of plans.

What’s your plan for taxes? Well, we need more revenue, and so the rich are going to need to pay more. What’s your plan for the deficit? Well, we need more revenue from the rich and we need to cut wasteful spending. What’s your plan for health care? Well, we need to expand public programs like Medicare and Medicaid. What’s any of this exactly going to look like? It’s impossible to say because it depends on the composition of Congress. We’re going to stop doing dumb stuff like picayune infighting about the details of hypothetical bills and try to elect lots of Democrats all up and down the ballot because Republicans don’t share any of these goals.

Fearlessly drop some moderate positions into your answers depending on what you’re asked, but don’t make “being moderate” the goal of the campaign — just be moderate.

Change starts at the top

The reason I’m focused on a 2028 primary as the answer to Silver’s questions rather than trying to answer them all in detail is that realistically, change in American parties comes from the top down. It sounds better to talk about grassroots change, but the way the G.O.P. changed its image is that Trump ran in a sharply contested primary with a distinct message, and he won.

You can see one reason it works this way if you think about the two education issues on the list.

If charter schools and performance pay are both popular with the general electorate and with Democratic primary voters, why has the party ditched its Obama-era support for these things? Well, the answer is teachers’ unions.

But not in the sense that union money or volunteers are so terrifying. The main thing is that Democratic Party primary voters have favorable attitudes toward teachers and favorable attitudes toward labor unions, so when a labor union full of teachers says, “Candidate X is good on labor and good on education while Candidate Y is bad on labor and bad on education,” that carries a lot of weight. The union doesn’t have to explain specifically what its beef with Candidate Y is.

That, in general, is how the groups wield power.

If you’re a backbench Democrat hoping to run statewide one day or chair a committee or get a cabinet appointment, it’s in your interest to have the Human Rights Campaign seal of approval. If they say you are “bad on L.G.B.T. issues,” they can just hit you with that label without getting into details.

This power matters all the time, but it matters less the more people are paying attention. And presidential campaigns are the apex of attention.

A presidential candidate who says exactly what he or she thinks about the issues is reasonably likely to actually be heard saying the thing. A candidate who is charismatic and skilled at contemporary social media can talk above the groups directly to the voters. That means they can articulate a message that is more moderate than the Democratic Party establishment in ways that Democratic Party primary voters agree with — just as Trump moved the G.O.P. to the center on Social Security and Medicare — and have a shot at either winning or else forcing the rest of the party to move toward them.

I think, despite all the gains the far left has made, the path remains clear for someone to do this. But it does take a politician — not a blogger or a newsletter writer — and someone has to actually try.
