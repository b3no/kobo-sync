What do we owe the embryos we leave behind?

The reproductive technology testing the limits of liberalism

Leah Libresco Sargeant

Editor’s note: The first essay The Argument ever published asked, “How do we live with each other?” To mark our first anniversary, we’re returning to that founding question with a series about what we owe people whose beliefs and choices we may find difficult to accept or even understand. Over the next few weeks, familiar voices at The Argument and new voices alike will approach that question from different angles.

Leah Libresco Sargeant is a sharp writer and policy wonk whose core arguments center on family, feminism and religion. In today’s essay, Sargeant, who is a pro-life feminist, considers what we owe the growing number of frozen embryos left behind or discarded. She pushes readers to question whether we have any obligations at all to potential future children and sketches out a view of future pro-life politics shaped around the advancement of reproductive technology.

Four years after the Dobbs v. Jackson Women’s Health Organization decision, America has settled into an abortion détente. Blue states are abortion oases, in some cases removing any limits on abortion by gestational age. Red states set limits that are highly restrictive on paper, but, due to mail-order abortion pills, possible to evade in practice. The bans are most restrictive for women who learn they are pregnant outside the window for medication abortions and who don’t have the funds or flexibility to travel out of state.

The détente seems to suit both parties’ leadership (though not activists on either side of the issue). Democrats get to talk about restoring Roe v. Wade, but (as with many issues) they are in no danger of having the majorities needed to deliver. The Republicans, led by Trump, have declared “Mission Accomplished” and scolded pro-life activists seeking federal action.

Even for the most engaged activists, the rise of medication abortion is turning down temperatures on the ground. A woman seeking a medication abortion may never need to visit a clinic in person at all. The pro-life activists hoping to make contact and persuade her to let her baby live will never see her. The clinic escorts will still find opponents to engage with, but the stakes of their fight will be lower. There will be fewer clinics to protest at, even in blue states, as the rise of telehealth medication abortions prompts in-person clinics to close.

Abortion has become, in practice as well as in rhetoric, between a woman and her doctor.

How do we live with each other? For now, like this: with no constitutional protection for abortion access or for life in the womb. With activists on both sides of the issue sliding down their party’s prioritization list. With at least 3,000 abortions daily — a number that represents a 9/11 every day for pro-lifers and which falls short of what pro-choicers view as demonstrated need.

What has the potential to jar us out of this new normal? The boosters of artificial wombs, a hypothetical technology that could allow a fetus to develop outside a woman’s body, hope it could separate pregnancy from a particular mother without abortion. I’m extremely bearish on the potential of this type of technology to allow gestation without women. Might medical advances allow babies to survive outside the womb at 20 weeks, or even 18 weeks, in my lifetime? Perhaps. But I’d be happy to bet that 50 years from now, we still won’t be able to take a 12-week-old baby out of the womb and sustain its development safely.

Even more modest advances might not upend today’s political settlement. Under Planned Parenthood v. Casey, states could prohibit abortion after viability — the moment at which a baby could survive outside the mother. Moving viability earlier under that framework would have allowed states to ban abortion earlier in pregnancy. But the shift to abortion on demand, without apology, and after viability in blue states suggests moving the viability line wouldn’t destabilize abortion compromises.

But I do believe that one technological advance is creating new fault lines in reproductive justice: the ever-growing number of embryos on ice with the rise of in vitro fertilization (IVF). IVF itself is popular, and its prevalence is likely to keep it politically untouchable.

In 2024, 1 in 36 babies born in America was conceived through IVF — almost everyone has someone they love who exists because of this procedure. But as IVF becomes more widely used, there will be more and more uncomfortable questions about whether the embryos on ice are property or people.

Right now, fights over who can claim (or destroy) embryos are mostly limited to divorce cases. In the most notorious case, a judge struggling to find a legal precedent for a property dispute over persons reached back to Virginia jurisprudence on chattel slavery. However, every day, more and more embryos are banked that will not ultimately be carried by their parents. The families with spare embryos are unsure of what to do with these excess children.

Between 5% and 7% of parents who bank frozen embryos fall out of contact with their clinics, with the ultimate disposition of their embryos uncertain. It’s hard to anticipate their preferences — the same study found that 40% of families experience “high decisional conflict” about what to do with their excess embryos. Another national survey found that, among parents with banked embryos who did not want to have any more children, 20% expected to freeze their embryos indefinitely, and 40% have not made any choice about what to do with their embryos.

As time goes on, more and more children will grow up and inherit the duty to decide what to do with their potential siblings. Children have been born from embryos kept on ice for 30 years — no medical concerns prevent Zoomers from seeing their siblings born at last.

If they don’t elect to see their siblings born, they will inherit their parents’ burden of choosing. If their parents didn’t choose to thaw and throw away these embryos, will they similarly choose indecision? Will they pass the burden down to their own children (the embryos’ biological nieces and nephews)? Will it make a difference if they know their parents used a service like Herasight to rank their embryos by anticipated height or IQ, meaning they were specifically chosen over the siblings still on ice?

I don’t know what will happen as this awkward inheritance is passed on, but I think it will put pressure on a liberal “whatever is right for your family” approach. Children have always posed challenging questions for libertarians and the liberals most skittish about talking about a common good. Tolerating your neighbor’s right to swing their fist into their own nose is one thing, but how much latitude do parents get to make bad choices for their children?

When can a parent refuse a vaccine or a blood transfusion on their child’s behalf? How unusual can a family’s homeschooling be before the state determines they have failed in their duties to their child?

And when, if ever, can a parent hold the power of life and death over their child? In the womb? In someone else’s womb, as in the recent case of Gabriel/Rumi, the baby carried by a surrogate who fled to Texas rather than comply with the biological parents’ alleged demand for an abortion? When the parents decide to discard their excess embryos?

I have mixed feelings on many of these issues, but the case for the state to intervene is strongest when parents’ preferences pose immediate dangers to their child — when they remove the chance for the child to later choose differently. It’s hard to anticipate how these tensions will resolve, but IVF stands out as a technology that guarantees hard choices much further down the line.

Programmers talk about “technical debt” as shorthand for the compromises and sloppy code you write in the moment. It gets you through the current crunch, but it makes your codebase weaker when you try to use it as a foundation for future projects. Eventually, you’ll have to rewrite the whole thing the way it should have been from the start, but it’s always tempting to put it off one more time. As the bad code persists, and more weird kludges are built to compensate for its weaknesses, the complexity of the eventual, necessary refactoring grows so large that no one wants to tackle it until there’s a cascading crisis.

IVF and the ever-growing number of embryos on ice have the potential to pose a moral form of technical debt. As someone who considers these frozen embryos to be just as human as the siblings who were carried to term, there’s no solution I feel totally comfortable with.

The best option available is “snowflake adoption” — allowing infertile couples to carry other families’ frozen embryos to term. This still involves deliberately separating these children from their biological family, and I expect many parents who bank embryos will not want to participate.

It is hard to give up your child to be raised by someone else, and the popularity of DNA testing means there is no way to be assured of anonymity. Plus, there are so many frozen embryos that it would take a long time to work through the backlog, even if freezing stopped today.

The other likely option is “compassionate transfer,” in which embryos are transferred at a time in a woman’s cycle when they cannot implant and grow. This strikes me as a morally mixed approach — it’s hard to endorse actively choosing when the children’s lives will end. It seems analogous to the ethical strain families sometimes feel at the end of life, when they agree to extraordinary measures in a crisis and then regret it when they have to decide whether to withdraw life support. IVF has allowed us to create far more children than their parents are prepared to welcome, and there may be no good solution to the duties we owe but cannot discharge.

The compromises parents make when they assume they will know what to do later will eventually come due. But it will be 20 or 30 years after the initial decisions were made. The next generation of kids who will need to play a role in welcoming or disposing of their frozen siblings is being born now, at a rate of over 270 per day.

Give them 40 or 50 years, and they will face a very specific, very hard question about whom we can live with, or live without.
