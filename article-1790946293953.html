What’s So Scary About This Book?

Twenty years ago, Patricia McCormick’s “Sold” united readers around the horrors of child trafficking. Now it’s one of America’s most banned books.

Ron Charles
Oct 2
 
READ IN APP
 

Every author hopes their work will endure. But Patricia McCormick’s novel Sold has attained a painful kind of currency. In 2025, almost 20 years after it was published, Sold was the most challenged book in the United States (full list).

Next week is Banned Books Week, and on Monday, a report from PEN America will show that Sold remains high on the list of the 10 most banned books in schools.

That’s a sad, strange fate for a young adult novel that was a finalist for a National Book Award, that’s used in high school and college courses, that confronts the horror of sexual slavery.

McCormick tells me that when Sold first appeared, “child trafficking or the fight against trafficking was something that both the left and the right found common cause around, and the book was embraced.”

But something’s gone wrong in America.

Even while reading scores decline and teenagers drift away from books, activists have whipped up a moral panic about pornography in schools.

As McCormick suggests, it’s a movement based on a particularly destructive kind of misreading.

“Books aren’t being banned because of their content or their quality or their message,” she says. “People are taking a single page — and in the case of this book, it’s the page where the main character is sexually assaulted — and reading that out of context in a way that triggers or troubles people.”

“I always say, ‘It’s supposed to be upsetting!’ You can’t write a book about child trafficking without having a scene like that in there. But it’s written from the point of view of a 13-year-old, so her language is not graphic. It’s the way a 13-year-old would experience this kind of trauma. There’s bewilderment, there’s physical pain, confusion, a sense of betrayal, but it isn’t graphic and it isn’t pornographic.”

“I can’t tell you how many schools have launched fundraising efforts after having read the book. It activates kids. It turns them into activists. And so we’re shutting off that opportunity.”

Even more important, McCormick says that when she visits high schools to talk about Sold, a student will frequently take her aside or stand up in front of the group and say, “Something like this has happened to me.”

“There’s a way in which reading about a girl in Nepal — like six degrees of separation — allows kids to see their own situation with empathy. And those kids can get help. And it also never fails that their friends are incredibly respectful of this moment when they come forward. I’m always amazed how many boys look at me so soberly. And when you think about what’s happening at Cornell right now, this is a book everybody needs to read.”

With a background in restorative justice, McCormick has tried repeatedly to reach out to organizations — such as the so-called Moms for Liberty — working to ban her novel.

“Usually, we can find common cause. I try to do that right off the bat. We all agree that the sexual exploitation of children is a bad thing. And invariably, they all say, ‘Oh, the book has literary merit. It’s just that one page.’ And invariably they say, ‘We’re going to reconsider.’ But then it’s not in good faith. They only double down on their attacks on the book.”

The head of one Moms for Liberty state chapter told her that depictions of sex that are violent or not consensual make girls want to become gay.

“I’ve heard this more than once,” McCormick says.

Her heart goes out to librarians and teachers. “They have it the worst,” she says. “I’ve talked to librarians who’ve been called ‘groomers’ when they’re out at the store, and a neighbor who has a child in their school will say, ‘You are a pornographer!’ Librarians have had their personal information put out online. They’ve lost their jobs, or they’ve had their jobs threatened, or they’re living in a kind of crouch for fear.”

That fear functions as an effective preemptive censor, too.

Students in the MFA program at Stony Brook Southampton often asked her, “Should I even attempt this book? No one’s going to buy it — an agent’s not going to pick this up.”

“That’s such a denial of who they are, an erasure of who they are before they’ve even gotten a chance to get the words on the page.”

“And so I always say to emerging writers, ‘What happens if you don’t write this book? You’re the one person who can say whatever it is you are yearning to say. What happens if you’re silent?’”◆

Once Upon a Midnight Creamy

I truly enjoy my work, but serving the literary community is not all fun and games. Like last night I had to eat four different pints of ice cream inspired by Edgar Allan Poe.

You’re welcome.

These ghoulishly delicious new flavors come from Clementine’s, a Midwestern creamery that makes some of the finest ice cream I’ve ever tasted (details).

Clementine’s Edgar Allan Poe collection includes:

  • Midnight Dreary: A chocolate so dark it’s like staring into the pit of hell — with raspberry jam.

  • NevermOrange: Sharp blood orange flavored with olive oil — a dreamsicle for which I’d sell my soul.

  • Thing of Evil: This non-dairy purple-people-eater crumbles gluten-free chocolate sandwich cookies into caramel-miso coconut ice cream and screams, “Feed me!”

  • The Raven: In an eerie feat of transfiguration, a blueberry muffin becomes a mouthful of frozen cookie butter.

Jovana Vukanic, Clementine’s very lucky marketing manager, tells me the idea was to take familiar flavors and subject them to the uncanny transformations of darkness.

“Last year we did cryptid flavors for Halloween, which are, I hate to say, made up,” Vukanic reveals. “So, that was spooky in a creative way. This year we thought, how can we be spooky in a literary way? Let’s use a poem to inspire these flavors and see what happens.”

They landed on “The Raven.”

Only this, and nothing more.

It was, Vukanic says, a strange way to develop ice cream flavors.

For instance, they realized they had to have a brilliant orange color for Halloween. Then someone in R&D came up with the name NevermOrange. “And as soon as she said it, I was like, ‘Oh yeah, that’s got to be in there.’ So the actual flavor — the blood orange and olive oil — came last.”

As Vukanic and the team studied the poem, the Raven flavor followed an entirely different flight path. “We looked at how things change or become different after midnight — like the way a shadow at 9 p.m. is a little less scary than a shadow at, say, 2 a.m.”

Take a core memory such as a warm blueberry muffin.

Then, methought, the air grew denser, perfumed from an unseen censer.

“So we challenged our chef to come up with a way to make an ice cream taste like that, but just slightly different so that it was almost like you could recall having this before ... but not really.”

Not every flavor sparked to life. A pig’s blood ice cream designed to resemble a beating heart never crept out of the laboratory.

If Edgar Allan Poe walked into Clementine’s tonight in the Central West End in St. Louis, Vukanic would offer him a cone of NevermOrange.

“It’s just the most unexpected,” she says.

This and more I sat divining, with my head at ease reclining
On the cushion’s velvet lining that the lamplight gloated o’er.◆

This Sucks

It might feel superfluous to celebrate anniversaries if you’re immortal, but two undead classics are rising up again this month.

Somehow, it’s been half a century since the Queen of Darkness, Anne Rice, published Interview with the Vampire. Her bestselling novel about Louis de Pointe du Lac (1766—?) launched a series that’s sold a gazillion copies, along with movies and TV series. (Brad Pitt hated playing Louis so much that he tried to quit but was told that would cost $40 million, which is a lot in Romanian lei.)

Next week, Knopf is releasing a 50th anniversary edition of Interview with the Vampire with blood-red edges and gold-embossed silhouettes on the boards. Photos of the first four pages of Rice’s typed manuscript at the end feel almost as old as Louis de Pointe du Lac himself. And the afterword, by Rice’s son, Christopher, is deeply personal and surprisingly moving.

One of the innumerable literary creatures that Rice spawned was a YA romance novel called Twilight, by Stephenie Meyer, whose Wikipedia page says she was inspired by the work of Jane Austen and William Shakespeare, which just caused all the blood to drain from my head.

Next week, Little, Brown Books for Young Readers is releasing a 20th anniversary deluxe collector’s edition of New Moon, the second volume in the Twilight saga.

Aside from its content, the slipcased book sports a little magic of its own: The edges are gilded with gold, but fan them just a jump to the left or a step to the right and a portrait of Bella or Jacob appears.

P.S. If you’d rather sink your teeth into something truly bloody, Bayliss Rare Books in London is offering a first edition of Dracula accompanied by a handwritten letter from Bram Stoker. Just $20,209.◆

Picture This

Looking back on the creepy effectiveness of his Turn of the Screw, Henry James explained how it unnerves the reader with calculated vagueness: “Make him think the evil, make him think it for himself, and you are released from weak specifications.”

Now imagine doing that for readers as young as 3.

Jon Klassen accomplishes the impossible in his new ghost story, a house-shaped board book called The House with Nobody in It.

Across 14 die-cut pages, Klassen leads readers through a house that contains a clock and a few pieces of furniture.

That’s all.

The tone is flat and simple.

“There is a small stool on the floor in this room. And a lamp.”

It’s like a gothic version of “Goodnight Moon” if there were no one left to say goodnight.

“There is nobody in this house,” Klassen writes. “There used to be somebody. But there is nobody now.”

As they turn these thick cardboard pages, observant little ones will notice the house isn’t entirely uninhabited. Carefully positioned cutouts in each page reveal — and obscure — a big-eyed ghost standing against a wall.

But the ghost is never mentioned.

It’s just there. Haunting.

Or is it?

As James said of his ghost story, “There is not only from beginning to end of the matter not an inch of expatiation.”

The illustrations, the paper engineering, the haiku-like text of this book are simple, spooky genius.◆

This Week’s Poem

Ellie Black’s manuscript for Revelator won the APR/Honickman First Book Prize, which is particularly valuable because, in addition to $3,000, it comes with a publication contract from The American Poetry Review and national distribution by Copper Canyon Press.

Poets need money, sure, but they crave readers, which reminds me: If you enjoy these weekly poetry selections, please seek out and buy the collections they’re drawn from.

Black writes along the contested border between spirituality and sexuality. She borrows promiscuously from religious language and pop culture — from revelatory angels to Taylor Swift — in pursuit of what it means to be alive in this strange moment.

I love the witty way this poem captures the anxieties we toy with, that toy with us.

My Shark

Every day I invent a shark

to follow me around. This part isn’t hard
if you’ve met enough sharks. But when

I look behind me he isn’t always there —
that would be too easy. My shark
is smart. He’s waiting underneath.

I want my shark to want something
besides me. The raddest thing I can imagine
is to be left alone. Nothing looming

in the waters behind you. Still,
I make him up every day, dangling
my legs off the side of the boat,

daring him to take a bite. I think
maybe if I make this easy

we can get it over with. But

he always comes back. Of course —
he’s mine. I asked him to. And honestly
I love the part where I get to start

over, and the part right before,
the moment I realize he’s back, which
is to say (ugh) I’ve done this to myself

again, and when he drags me under,
I love to watch the swirl of my own blood
in the water, the bubbles encasing

my scream — how, predictably, though
I know how this goes, I always try
coming up for air.

Carry On

In 2009, when The Washington Post dispersed its standalone Sunday Book World section into other parts of the paper, a group of local writers began plotting how to save serious book criticism.

The Washington Independent Review of Books launched in 2011.

The beginning was tough. WIRB struggled to attract funding and readers. Critics submitted their reviews gratis.

But look who’s still standing.

The newspaper owned by the world’s richest bookseller recently decided it couldn’t afford to publish book reviews anymore. (Spaceships are so expensive!)

On Sunday, scores of local authors, critics, and editors celebrated the 15th anniversary of the Washington Independent Review of Books at the home of biographer Nancy Greenspan.

“How are you doing these days?” Ms. Greenspan asked while I ate a lemon tart.

“Okay,” I sighed. “I didn’t expect to be laid off so close to retirement.”

“Well, I didn’t expect my husband to die,” she said. “We carry on!”

Biographer Kitty Kelley, a major supporter of WIRB, was the day’s guest of honor. Her face appeared on book totes being handed out at the door.

“They managed to dig up a photo of me from eighth grade,” she quipped.

I couldn’t help telling Ms. Kelley that we still talk about the Gucci box of fish heads she sent Washington Post book critic Jonathan Yardley after his savage review of her 1981 biography of Elizabeth Taylor.

“That wasn’t me!” she laughed. That was a friend of hers, the late photographer Stanley Tretick. “I was so angry with Stanley,” she claimed, still laughing.◆
