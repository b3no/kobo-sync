What is Build Canada, anyway?

Lucy Hargreaves on a new vehicle for pumping ideas into the national conversation.

Paul Wells
Oct 28
 
READ IN APP
 

On February 4, 2025, the latest 338Canada polling average showed Pierre Poilievre’s Conservative Party of Canada 19 points ahead of the suddenly leader-less Liberals. A new federal agency in the US, the Department of Government Efficiency, led by Elon Musk, had begun sending teams of techies into federal agencies to look for easy cuts and layoff targets. And The Logic, an online tech-industry publication, covered the launch of Build Canada, “a new platform to influence government policy on issues ranging from immigration, health care and transportation.”

The Logic’s piece featured a photo of Shopify co-founder Tobi Lütke, who was “one of a number of prominent entrepreneurs backing Build Canada.” At the time Lütke was showing considerable sympathy on X for Donald Trump’s tariff threats. The new group — which has nothing at all to do with the Carney government’s housing agency, Build Canada Homes; the two things just have confusingly similar names — “is not directly affiliated with the Conservative Party, though its members widely expect the Conservatives to win the next election,” The Logic reported.

So that’s how Build Canada started. Since then the group has hardly been shy about telling anyone what it’s about. Its website is here. Anyone can sign up for their newsletter, and a lot of people, mostly entrepreneurs who head start-ups, have written the “Memos” on various policy areas that constitute Build Canada’s most visible output. This one calls for assorted tax cuts to keep STEM talent in Canada. This one says the feds should hold back federal transfers from provinces that fail to recognize other provinces’ professional credentials. This one calls for tax deductions for individuals’ direct donations to national-team athletes. This one says the first $100,000 of income should face no federal income tax, essentially a six-fold increase in the Basic Personal Amount.

All this has led to a measure of unease in public discussions of Build Canada’s work. At least in places where entrepreneurs and start-up businesses are a novel sight. When the Carney government seemed to incorporate one of Build Canada’s most novel ideas — a secondment program that would see private companies pay for employees to spend time working in the federal bureaucracy — the coverage from Canada’s National Observer, which broke the story, was distinctly nervous. What were “corporate leaders” going to be doing in the federal government anyway? (So far, it turns out, the answer is “Not a lot;” the government didn’t start seeking applicants for the program until mid-September.)

All of which is the long way around to explaining why I decided I’d ask Build Canada itself what Build Canada is. Or at least its CEO: Lucy Hargreaves, a former staffer from the early days of Justin Trudeau’s Liberal government who left thoroughly disillusioned, worked for a carbon-exchange startup and came back to try to encourage Canada’s federal government — any federal government — to encourage competition.

Lucy Hargreaves, Build Canada’s co-founder and CEO, joins me to explain what the organization actually does, who it’s trying to influence, and why she thinks Canada needs a more ambitious economic conversation.

The interview you’ll hear in this episode shows Hargreaves to be a bit guarded, but increasingly open in explaining what she and her colleagues are up to. “We are testing a new approach to inject fresh, bold, actionable ideas into the public realm,” she told me, “and generally shift the public conversation on growth and the Overton window around that conversation to one that is more pro-growth than it has been in the past.”

So is it a think tank? A lobbying organization? An industry association? No three times, she said. “I wouldn't characterize this as a think tank. We're not a lobbying organization. We're not an industry association.”

What it is, is getting talked about, she said: 4 million social-media impressions per month for ideas about increasing competitiveness. Ideas that aren’t necessarily actually Build Canada’s, she argues: the group is, to some extent, in the business of immaculate conception, generating ideas but not claiming ownership over them.

I’ll let her explain that one, a few times, for you. And to be fair, it’s no wonder she was guarded; at one point, making myself the ambassador of the group’s critics, I asked whether she went to Silicon Valley, got “red-pilled,” and came home to “DOGE us.” These aren’t my ideas, mind you; I’m just counting social-media impressions. Undeterred, Hargreaves goes on to explain why she wrote in the Toronto Star that Mark Carney’s Davos speech was a bad idea because it might “provoke” Donald Trump.

I ended the interview with some sympathy for what Build Canada is trying to do and two pairs of nifty socks. There used to be more places where provocative ideas could pick up a head of steam. Nobody should have a monopoly over debates and there aren’t a lot of people who should be barred from debating. I myself have been greeted, more than once in my career, with that dreariest of rebuttals, “You shouldn’t say that because most people don’t agree with you.” What’s warped the American conversation so much is a 2010 Supreme Court decision that makes it legal for a trillionaire to buy elections. Build Canada is solidly situated in a country, and a polity, where no such thing is permitted. I’m grateful to Hargreaves for this thoughtful conversation.
