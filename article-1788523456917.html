We Can’t Help Hurting People by Abandoning Basic Epidemiological Rigor

it’s never been less popular, or more important, to be willing to challenge claims about medical disorders and how they’re caused

Freddie deBoer
Sep 4
 
READ IN APP
 

Writing about disability as often as I do, and in particular writing this new book, has frequently put me into the strange position of defending ideas that I would assume do not need defending: that people sometimes claim to have illnesses or disabilities they do not have, whether as a matter of deceiving others or of self-deception, and that people frequently misunderstand the origins of the symptoms they experience. To my surprise, many people now push back against these ideas, often couched in the rhetoric of academic disability theory. They seem angry at the notion that we should ever subject illness claims to any kind of gatekeeping or review. I just don’t get how you can have blanket faith in disability claims in our complicated, unhappy world.

Most anyone would have to admit to prominent examples of people who deliberately fake illnesses (cancer fakers, people defrauding the government with phony disability claims, etc), sincerely but falsely believe themselves to be sick (hypochondria, delusional beliefs about parasites or cancer in people with psychotic disorders), or lie somewhere in between (certain somatic disorders, what doctors sometimes call partial insight). I wrote recently about the phenomenon of travelers requesting wheelchairs at airports to skip lines, and there’s also the ever-growing number of people who get ADHD diagnoses to get a leg up in tests like the LSAT. That’s just old-fashioned fraud, sometimes called malingering. There’s also the people who know that they are engaged in deceptive behaviors about illnesses but engage in them not for material gain but for the psychological and social satisfactions that come with being sick; this used to be called Munchausen’s and is now more often referred to as factitious disorder. Then there’s illness anxiety disorder, more commonly called hypochondria, which refers to a sincere but false belief that the subject is ill; typically, the feared disorder changes over time and is part of a greater overall anxiety. I have been chronicling a lot of this stuff because I think it matters, because its prevalence is growing, and because the current liberal attitude that we can never question medical claims seems to be coming to some sort of a breaking point. We can’t go on this way.

Yesterday, New York magazine published an essay that sits right at the heart of concerns about medical disorders, their causes, the role of experts, and who has the right to define their own illnesses and their origins. The piece could hardly be more naturally sympathetic. In the essay, writer Lukas I. Alpert talks about having been near ground zero on 9/11, suffering a variety of medical ailments following, and gradually discovering that many other people had similar experiences. He then proceeds to make a series of claims about the attacks and their environmental effects, the government’s response, and our stumbling efforts to provide medical care and monetary compensation to those who were harmed on that day and its immediate aftermath. There’s a lot of righteous anger, a hint of conspiracy, and plenty that inspires genuine compassion. But the good stuff, I’m afraid, is wrapped up in what are fundamentally empirical, scientific claims about who got sick from 9/11, and in what proportions, that are simply not worthy of publication in a major magazine that regularly publishes rigorous journalism. Epidemiology is serious business and Albert’s essay does not meet the minimal standard of care and precision that a national journalistic publication should aspire to.

To be clear, I of course do not think that Albert is engaged in any conscious deception, and I certainly don’t doubt that Albert has experienced the symptoms he says he has. What I am suggesting, however, is that this broad topic of interest falls directly into the morass of real, imagined, and fraudulent illness - no doubt many people really got sick on and after 9/11; no doubt some people have the kinds of vague and mysterious symptoms that have prompted an explosion of contested syndromes and attributed them to 9/11; and no doubt some people have lied about getting sick on 9/11 to access sympathy, attention, and financial compensation. What all of this means is that we have to be careful, and I will argue that Albert’s essay is not careful in ways that really matter.

Here’s my summary of the essay’s conclusions:

  1. During, immediately after, and some months after the attacks on September 11th, exposure to airborne environmental contaminants led to elevated levels of cancer and other serious diseases among people who had been in Lower Manhattan and immediate environs

  2. Contrary to public belief, these elevated rates of illness can be found in many populations beyond first responders and even beyond the office workers and students who lived and worked closest to ground zero

  3. The government made repeated assurances that the air in Lower Manhattan was safe when it was not, leading to more people getting sick

  4. Enrollment and certification data from the WTC Health Program can be used effectively as measures of illness prevalence over time

  5. The number of people known to have gotten ill thanks to the attacks on the World Trade Center continues to grow and the scope of the problem is probably larger or much larger than current figures show.

By my lights, point one is true and supported by data but at lower scales than Albert implies; point two is derived from data that cannot actually support it; point three is half right but half unexamined - those assurances were made and made prematurely, but the implication that people got unnecessarily sick because of them is unproven in the piece; point four mistakes administrative data for epidemiological data; point five, as proffered in the essay, is non-falsifiable, with every possible observation being read as evidence for it. Growth in the count of people in the WTC Health program is evidence of growth in the sick population; a plateau is a problem of under-enrollment in the program; a decline means people dying before they gained access to the program…. As is so often true with vague and mysterious medical claims, all roads lead to the same outcome. But of course an aging population has seen more cancer! Which speaks to what’s missing: any quantitative notion of excess illness, excess cancer, excess mortality. What’s important is observed cases minus what we should expect from the underlying population’s baseline expected cancer prevalence. This is, I concede, a difficult thing to determine. But we have to try. Alpert doesn’t.

Fundamentally, Alpert’s piece lacks any real attempt to establish how many people would have gotten sick absent the 9/11 attacks or absent the government’s assurances that it was safe to be in Lower Manhattan. The administrative data that’s used in the piece is not irrelevant, but it can’t prove the epidemiological conclusions that the essay wants to reader to come to. And the only evidence of rate, of relative incidence of disorder or disease, is left entirely to anecdote, when relative rates are at the heart of epidemiology.

Numerators Without Denominators

This is the most obvious and damning fault in the essay: it constantly provides headline numbers (numerators) while doing nothing to establish basal rates or expected figures (denominators). In other words, Albert gives us mortality and illness numbers of varying levels of credibility but does nothing to inform readers about what we should expect in an ordinary population. Consider this paragraph:

In the 25 years that have passed since that day, 154,700 people have entered the World Trade Center Health Program, which provides care and health monitoring to people who were exposed to toxic air after 9/11, many of whom have come down with illnesses linked to the attack. Of those, 9,224 have died, according to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention.

OK. 154,700 people out of what exact larger population is an unanswerable question; who passed in or out of Lower Manhattan, and what exactly that geographical region constitutes, and for how long, and whether indoors or outdoors or in a cab or a bus, etc etc etc, no way to know. 9,224 have died - died of what? That’s about 6% of those 154,700 people; is that a high figure? A normal figure? It doesn’t seem high; we’re talking about a quarter century since the attacks, after all. But there’s no information through which a reader can even hazard a guess.

Every year, the number of sick people rises. In 2025, some 10,500 more people enrolled in the program — an all-time high.

I’m sorry, but this has to be said with some gusto: the second sentence simply does not follow from the first. Enrollment in the program can be driven by all sorts of factors - outreach and information campaigns specifically designed to increase enrollment; the advertising done by the firms that represent 9/11 victims (real or dubious); changes to the WTC Health coverages list, most notably a 2013 addition of ~70 types of cancer; and crucially for a 25-year-old event, aging, given that cancer incidence doubles about every five years or so after 50. Dr. Michael Crane, the head of the WTC Health Clinical Center, concedes this - “Age is the hidden force, it’s changed everything” - but the piece characteristically lets it drift off into the atmosphere. The simple reality is that advocates and activists have tried to get more and more people onto the rolls of the relevant programs and that matters. Albert quotes Crane as saying that the WTC Health program has thus far “only succeeded in enrolling a proportion of the actual, exposed population.” I don’t blame a doctor for wanting to treat patients. But there’s a clear circularity problem here - the WTC Health Center’s enrollment statistics are the primary source of data in this piece and people involved in leadership in that program are clearly looking to add more people. The increasing number of enrollees justifies efforts to increase the number of enrollees.

The problems are obvious. Indeed, they’re so obvious that the WTC Environmental Health Center is admirably straightforward about them in published work. They point out in a 2024 paper that “the Survivor population is self-selected and eligibility for cohort entry is linked to having reported symptoms of a WTC-related condition. Therefore, comparisons with the general population are subject to selection bias.” This is fine when it comes to the program’s purpose, which is helping sick people get medical care. But once we start making claims about how specific diseases were actually spread - and, to make things a little more touchy, who might receive financial compensation - we are inviting levels of scrutiny that the WTC Health people say they can’t meet.

Look, a lawyer whose firm has represented thousands of 9/11 claimants says in the piece “The process is non-adversarial.” Which is fine by me - I want people to get healthcare. They deliberately made the program generous because the alternative would be morally worse. But it doesn’t change the fact that, in Alpert’s piece, the cohort is defined by the administrative outcome and the administrative outcome is not determined by a scientifically rigorous process.

Of the 15,500 FDNY members who have been enrolled — which the agency believes account for nearly all its members known to have been at the site — 77 percent have developed a verified 9/11-related illness or injury, said Dr. David Prezant, the FDNY’s chief medical officer. Of those, some 4,257 have been diagnosed with cancer. More than 600 members of the department have died of suspected 9/11-related illnesses, eclipsing the 343 who were killed on September 11.

I have little doubt that many first responders became ill as a result of the obviously terrible air quality and level of environmental contaminants that they encountered on 9/11 and immediately after, nor do I doubt that they deserve governmental assistance. But their plight is tied to far broader claims that Albert is making, and as crass as it may be to say, “many” is not a satisfactory term when determining the sources of illness. The fact that nearly twice as many are claimed to have died of 9/11-related illnesses as died on the day itself is emotionally quite moving, but let’s please use our heads: almost 16,000 firefighters, almost universally male (and thus likely to die earlier), many of them middle aged at the time, in a profession that’s prone to unhealthy environments, over the course of 25 years…. Certainly, the expected all-cause mortality for such a group over a quarter-century would have to be in the thousands, right? I’m tempted to say that these figures don’t seem that high. But again, we don’t have the information necessary to make that call. What’s the expected level of cancer, of illness, of death in this population over such a period of time? And what does “suspected 9/11-related illnesses” really mean? If the illness and injuries are verified, what standard are they verified against?

“I have gotten sick. My dad, who has since passed away but worked on Rector Street, got kidney cancer. My favorite high-school teacher has gotten breast cancer. My best friend has breast cancer right now and is in treatment for it. We went to high school together. Her mother-in-law was volunteering down there as a Lutheran minister and has gotten oral cancer. Another relative of mine, who worked downtown, has gotten uterine cancer,” she said. “I shouldn’t know this many people who were down there who have gotten cancer at this point.”

These words are from Dana Nelson, a woman who was exposed at Ground Zero and whom Alpert quotes without any skepticism. But this is a notoriously bad way to understand cancer. Depending on who you ask and how the question is defined, something like 40% of Americans receive a cancer diagnosis in their lifetime. A woman in her late 30s naming five or six people in her extended family, who went to her school, who were in her broad social network - an essentially unbounded group - seeing that many cases of cancer in a 25-year span? That’s just entirely unremarkable. The essay, however, essentially operates on this exact logic. And I simply have to hold a piece published in a major magazine to higher standards than I would hold that woman to.

The Sensitive, Tricky Question of Thyroid Cancer

Alpert is a survivor of thyroid cancer; one of the precipitating events of him beginning to think of himself as a victim of WTC environmental contaminants is when he talks to an old colleague of his who also worked at Ground Zero and learns that he had thyroid cancer too. “I don’t think it’s just a coincidence,” says Alpert’s old colleague, “that both of us had thyroid cancer after working at Ground Zero.” They are both, of course, very sympathetic. But again, this is generally a terrible way to determine if diseases are connected, and more importantly, thyroid cancer is particularly troublesome here, as it’s the example of an overdiagnosed cancer, in an era where we’ve discovered a major overdiagnosis problem in oncology broadly. I’m certainly not saying that one or both of them did not actually suffer from thyroid cancer. But like most essays of its type, Alpert’s essay broadens out from the particular to the general… and in general, we have to be very careful when it comes to thyroid cancer.

The South Korean example is what helped make thyroid cancer an example in a thousand public health seminars. In the 1990s, the South Korean medical system began bundling neck ultrasounds into many broad health screenings. Within a decade and a half or so, the thyroid cancer rate in the country had spiked by 15x - but mortality didn’t change at all. Why would this happen? Because as we’re learning more and more, many people have what we would technically call cancers, even malignant cancers, that nevertheless will never cause real harm. Many people die with cancer without dying of cancer. And it turns out that a lot of people in the world are walking around with papillary carcinomas on their thyroid glands that will never become symptomatic. How much incidence of this we see seems to depend a great deal on how intensely we look; with really intense postmortem investigations, you can get to incidence rates of 30% or more of the population having what is, technically, evidence of cancer. If you ultrasound enough people’s necks you can raise the number of people with thyroid cancer because you’re looking at a zone in the human body where there’s a lot of “indolent disease.” South Korea did a big public health campaign with its doctors and citizens and the rate dropped dramatically, which shouldn’t happen with cancers that are actually going to kill people. Alpert himself says that “five-year survival rates for the kind of thyroid cancer I was diagnosed with are nearly 100 percent.” This is likely due in some substantial part to the fact that many with the biomarkers for that cancer are never going to become sick from it.

I stress again that I have no reason at all to doubt that Alpert’s cancer, or that of his old colleague, were real and dangerous. And though I can’t find a link now, I recall seeing evidence somewhere that a higher percentage of people with these preexisting malignant thyroid nodules who were exposed at Ground Zero go on to develop symptomatic illness than the general population. But it remains the case that thyroid cancer is a consistently and widely overdiagnosed cancer, that potential WTC patients are more apt to get the kinds of screenings that are highly likely to produce these false positives, and that thyroid cancers play a disproportionate part in WTC medicine. This 2020 paper on WTC-related cancers says specifically that thyroid rates may be exaggerated “possibly because of surveillance bias from increased monitoring.” I don’t expect Alpert to be some sort of extremely harsh prosecutor of all of this, but he does have to mention it. Not having any reference to this well-known reality about thyroid cancer in that essay is malpractice.

Expanding Our Compassion Means Being Careful

I reckon there’s five types of people among the 154,700 that have joined the WTC Health program in the past 25 years. The first is people who got cancer and other serious illnesses from exposure to Ground Zero on 9/11 and in the days that followed, who deserve healthcare under the terms of the program and are getting it. The second is people who already had cancer or would have developed it anyway, irrespective of 9/11, who deserve healthcare by dint of being human beings and have received it, along with a potentially false narrative about why they got sick. The third group is people who had or developed small indolent tumors that would have not made them sick or been discovered in their lifetimes but which were found thanks to the heightened scrutiny of potential exposure to 9/11 environmental hazards. The fourth group is people who were at Ground Zero who are perfectly healthy but who live in fear of getting sick because people like Alpert have make it seem like it’s inevitable that they will. And the fifth group is people who have knowingly defrauded various 9/11-related programs like the WTC Health program and perhaps were never at Ground Zero at all.

The provisioning of healthcare for all of these people, including the frauds, is a good thing in a country where such access is fraught for so many. None of this is an argument for taking anything from anybody. And as I said at the top, and will say again, it’s impossible to not see Alpert or all of the people he interviewed in a sympathetic light. So what’s the problem? The problem is that epidemiology is serious business with serious consequences, and we can’t do the best for everyone by being sloppy in how we discuss it. Being imprecise in the way that Alpert’s piece is imprecise costs us the ability to say anything meaningful about the estimated quarter-million people who had exposure to Ground Zero and are fine, people who don’t deserve to live under a cloud of baseless fear. It makes it harder to tell whether the cancer malignancy signals are meaningful or simply a matter of looking much more closely at this particular population than we normally would; if it’s the latter, we very well might be putting healthy people through surgery and other unnecessary treatments. It confuses important questions about mesothelioma, which are some of the more glaring and consequence-laden elements of the whole 9/11 exposure issue. And it contributes to a broader public crisis of medical information, where the internet has encouraged people to diagnose themselves and others in completely unjustifiable ways - from teenagers who think they have Tourette’s because of TikTok to parents who forego vaccination because of baseless fears to those who clog medical resources thanks to their endlessly-cycling fears of various forms of cancer.

Alpert clearly wants people to understand the true scale of 9/11’s ongoing health effects. So do I. But frequently, when reading his piece, I feel like he assumes that the larger number is always the right one. I want to arrive at the correct figure, for a hundred reasons, the most important being that understanding the true scale of the problem is necessary for providing the best care to those who really need it. And what I’m asking you to consider is the possibility that true compassion for people in pain can only stem from telling the best, most rigorous version of the truth we possibly can.

If you are interested in these issues, I highly recommend Toms River by Dan Fagin, which considers just how difficult and agonizing proving causation of disease and disorder can be. And I of course would love it if you would preorder my forthcoming book, which touches on these topics directly.
