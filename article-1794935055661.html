Late Ottoman Holocausts

Part I: The Greek War of Independence

John Ganz
Nov 17
 
READ IN APP
 

There’s a lot of discussion of Ezra Klein’s interview with streamer and internet personality Hasan Piker. Klein pressed Piker on his identification of Zionism with Nazism and the moral and political stakes of making that analogy. I actually thought that Piker’s response to Klein was quite reasonable:

What made the Nazis the Nazis were gas chambers. Just straight — right? Liberal Zionists who believe there’s an apartheid scenario happening in the West Bank, who believe that Gaza was a genocide, or at least war crimes — I don’t really understand the defense of calling them akin to Nazis.

Now, it’s hyperbolic, but the reason I’m not so precious about the utilization of the term is because of its historic significance. The entire principle behind Holocaust studies is to understand how society could move in a direction where people could turn a blind eye to such devastation, to such systematized forms of violence — this machine of death and destruction. Its utility is in learning about it so that we don’t repeat those mistakes.

And right now, obviously, it’s not to the same degree as the Holocaust, but right now we are watching a genocide unfold. So I don’t mind using this kind of incendiary language with the hopes that people understand exactly how devastating the situation has become — and take the sufficient response against it to try to stop it.

To me, that’s a more or less defensible use of heated rhetoric.

I have, on occasion, compared Zionism to fascism and even Nazism. In some cases, that’s not mere analogy, but actual genealogy: the Likud Party descends from Revisionist Zionism, and Ze’ev Jabotinsky recognized his movement’s kinship with Mussolini. This descent is even more stark in the case of Kahanism, the radical right tradition that supplies two cabinet ministers to the present government of Israel; Meir Kahane advocated the expulsion of Arabs and Jewish supremacy. Nor is the term “Nazi” merely applied to Israel in external polemics: The Israeli philosopher Yeshayahu Leibowitz, a self-identified Zionist, famously compared the occupation to Nazi behavior in Eastern Europe and warned of the infection of the society with “Judeo-Nazi” attitudes.

The recent documentary NAZA makes another point of comparison unavoidable: Hannah Arendt’s observations on bureaucratic terror and “the banality of evil” are directly relevant to a technical and industrialized system of killing in which personal responsibility is broken into moments of calculation and administrative procedure. Modern mass killing doesn’t require everyone to identify as an exterminationist; in fact, its abstract machinery can obscure exactly that moral self-recognition.

But Klein’s reference to the gas chambers makes clear a potential pitfall of the analogy to the Shoah: It can set too high a bar for recognizing genocide and perversely assist Israel’s apologists. Anything short of the extreme terminus of Auschwitz can be presented as something categorically different.

I think, then, it’s worthwhile to return to the cases that provoked the concept of genocide in the first place. Raphael Lemkin, who coined the term, repeatedly said that learning as a young law student about the Ottoman destruction of the Armenians convinced him of the need to identify a crime that involved the destruction of entire peoples. But the Armenian genocide was itself part of a wider late-Ottoman history of mass violence against its subject populations—Armenians, Greeks, and Assyrians—amid imperial collapse.

I believe this history provides more than mere analogy. The disintegration of the Ottoman Empire is quite literally part of the concatenation of events that produced the modern Middle East, including the partition of Palestine. And the Ottoman imperial crisis produced a repertoire of terror that remains grimly familiar: collective punishment, forced displacement, the destruction of civil society, and the idea that security requires changing the ethnic character of an entire territory.

Prologue: The Greek War of Independence

Perhaps no modern conflict in Western history has been as romanticized as the Greek War of Independence that began in 1821. I mean this quite literally: Lord Byron lent his fame and fortune to the cause; Shelley declared, “We are all Greeks”; philhellenic societies fired by visions of ancient Greek heroism sent volunteers to fight, who imagined themselves refighting Salamis and Marathon. In his classic history, That Greece Might Still Be Free: The Philhellenes in the War of Independence, William St Clair showed how this romance often covered up an uglier reality. Western Europeans saw a struggle between the descendants of classical Greece and an Oriental despotism, but, on the ground, it was a savage communal war in which Greeks massacred Muslims and Ottoman authorities answered the rebellion with the indiscriminate collective punishment of Greeks.

St Clair estimates that more than 20,000 Muslim men, women, and children were killed in the Peloponnese in the opening days. “Within a few weeks of the outbreak of the Revolution, the Turkish and Moslem Albanian population of the Peloponnese, previously about a ninth of the whole, had ceased to exist as a settled community,” he writes.

Much has been made of the brutality of Hamas on October 7th as an example of “Muslim barbarism,” but how did the Greeks, those Christian darlings of liberal and enlightened Europe, behave in their independence struggle? Well, here is the account of the siege of Navarino in St Clair’s books. The Greeks accepted the starving Turks’ offer to surrender and then immediately did not honor the terms of the agreement:

The Greeks…offered a convention whereby they would be granted a secure passage to Africa. They had neither the intention nor even the means of doing this and one of the Greek negotiators boasted ater that he destroyed the copy of the agreement so that no evidence should remain. When the gates were opened the Greeks rushed in and the whole population of between 2,000 and 3,000 were killed with the exception of about 160 who managed to escape. Some of the Turks were left to starve on an uninhabited island in the harbour. A Greek priest who was an eyewitness described the scene as the Turkish women were stripped and searched to see if they were concealing any valuables. Naked women plunged into the sea and were shot in the water. Children of three and four were thrown in to drown, and babies were taken from their mothers and beaten against the rocks.

The analogy can be made to run in both directions. At Navarino, the Greeks induced surrender with promises of safe passage they never intended to honor. In Gaza, Israel has repeatedly ordered civilians to “safe areas” that themselves came under attack.

The Ottoman response rested on the same principle of collective responsibility as Greek atrocities: The Patriarch of Constantinople was executed despite having excommunicated the rebels, and ghastly massacres followed in Constantinople, Smyrna, Cyprus, and elsewhere; in St Clair’s formulation, since “the Greeks as a community had revolted,” the Greeks as a community were punished.

The massacre at Chios offers a particularly vivid and horrible example of the latter. The island had remained loyal to the Ottoman government, but in 1822 armed rebels from nearby Samos landed, burned mosques, and proclaimed the revolution. The Ottoman response was meant to “set an example” to other Christian communities. The Samians retreated, leaving the largely unarmed islanders behind; in the subsequent reprisal, tens of thousands were killed and enslaved—perhaps 20,000 massacred and as many as 40,000 taken as slaves. Chios had been one of the richest commercial centers in the eastern Mediterranean, renowned for mastic production and for its silk industry. The massacre shattered the society: the island was depopulated, its merchants scattered, its ancestral knowledge of silk production lost, and its former commercial status never fully recovered.

The Chios episode became a sensation in Europe, where philhellenism already cast the conflict as Christian Greece against Turkish barbarism. Ottoman atrocities were reported widely abroad in gruesome detail, while Greek atrocities were often ignored. And when Greek atrocities were not ignored, they were justified: Philhellenes argued that the violence of the rebels was the understandable, even inevitable, revenge of a people brutalized by centuries of oppression. St. Clair, “It was seriously argued on a number of occasions that it was the Turks not the Greeks who should be blamed for the massacre at Tripolitsa since the Greeks ‘may justly impute to the oppression of their not only the degradation of their persons but the debasement of their minds.”

Propaganda indulged as much in hatred of the oppressor as in sympathy for the oppressed. “It is difficult to avoid the conclusion, in reading the English pamphlets, that the authors were more inspired by hatred of Turks and Moslems than by concern for the Greeks. They cheerfully demanded the wholesale expulsion of the millions of Turks settled in Europe,” St Clair observes. The extremism of these missives often turned off would-be sympathizers to the Greek cause and undermined the philhellene project: “Many men who would willingly have contributed money were ashamed tobe allied with such unattractive purveyors of hatred.”

Ottoman repression was not mere “Oriental” cruelty. The use of terror had a clear and brutal instrumental logic. St Clair points out that in Constantinople, Asia Minor, and other regions where the revolution had not yet taken firm hold, exemplary violence succeeded for a time in frightening Greek communities into submission and deterring them from joining the revolt. Collective punishment was therefore not simple vengeance: it was a powerful tool of counterinsurgency, intended to demonstrate the costs that an entire community would bear if some of its members rebelled.

Western European society was not united in its sympathies and identifications. Popular opinion tended to back the Greek rebels, while established diplomatic and political elites tended to view the revolt as one of the unfortunate distempers that had plagued Europe since 1789 and defended the Sublime Porte as the legitimate sovereign power. In Britain, home to some of the most famous philhellenes, the Tory establishment initially regarded the revolt as another dangerous challenge to the post-Napoleonic order, while Whigs, radicals, intellectuals, and, increasingly, the public at large rallied to the Greeks. One had to pick a side. St Clair writes, “When the Foreign Secretary, in trying to defend British neutrality in Parliament, remarked that there had been atrocities on both sides, he was branded as pro-Turkish.”

In Britain there was a growing sense that great power created moral responsibility. St Clair notes that philhellenic writers often assumed that Britain, victorious at Waterloo and now the most powerful country in the world, had a special responsibility to make the slaughter stop. British policymakers did not favor weakening the Ottoman Empire, which they viewed as an important counterweight to Russia, so they charted a course of official neutrality. But neutrality was now understood by much of the public as complicity: if Britain possessed the power to prevent atrocities and declined to use it, it shared responsibility for what followed. Here we can see the early outlines of a doctrine of humanitarian intervention, arising not from foreign-policy elites but from mobilized publics demanding that power be used on behalf of suffering peoples abroad.

Former partisans could change sides when confronted with the brutality of the struggle. Some European philhellenes who went to Greece returned deeply disillusioned; a few became outright “mishellenes” or even pro-Turkish after witnessing Greek factionalism and atrocities firsthand. The same was true in reverse: reports of Ottoman massacres could turn cautious neutrals or believers in Legitimacy into stentorian advocates of intervention. Sympathy and hatred were not fixed by ideology alone; confrontation with the violence could rapidly reorder them.

It’s also worth considering the use of the mythologized past by nationalist movements. The philhellenes presented the Greek insurgents—many of them little more than bandits before the rebellion—as modern heirs of Leonidas and Pericles. The Greeks were also understood by many Europeans as an outpost of Christian civilization beating back the Islamic hordes. It goes without saying that Zionism in like manner draws heavily on the Biblical narratives of exile, redemption, and return to provide an imaginative aura and bring its adherents to the point of religious fervor. Palestinian nationalism also has its own compressed civilizational narrative, drawing on the Islamic past of Jerusalem and al-Aqsa as well as the living memory of dispossession and the hope for return.

In fact, one could say Philhellenism and Christian Zionism grew out of a similar milieu: the 19th-century Western European fascination with the “regeneration” of ancient Eastern peoples. In some cases, they were positions held by the same people. William Wilberforce, the great abolitionist hero, was both an outspoken supporter of the Greek cause and an advocate of the restoration of the Jews to Palestine. Wilberforce’s evangelical humanitarianism encompassed projects that now are in disrepute while others are justly celebrated.

One further moment of pause should come from this history: Muslims in Greece could fairly be described as descendants of conquerors, occupiers—even colonists. At the time of the revolt, many were still directly tied to an Ottoman order that dominated and oppressed the Christian majority. Did that make their extermination legitimate? I believe only the most hard-hearted can say so without hesitation. Most had lived in Greece for generations and knew no other home.

We now have some basic elements in place: rebellion, reprisal, collective punishment, massacre, and foreign tendency to excuse the violence of their favored side as vengeance, liberation, or historical necessity. In the next installment, I’ll turn to the Bulgarian uprising of 1876 and then the Sasun rebellion of 1894, where these elements crystallize further: first into exemplary counterinsurgency and international atrocity politics, then into the treatment of entire populations as threats to internal security.
