“Why Would Women Choose Spreadsheets Over Babies?” Says Guy Who Chose Spreadsheets

It shouldn’t be hard to imagine why a woman would want a career.

Cartoons Hate Her
Aug 25
∙
Paid
 
READ IN APP
 

One hallmark of a guy who spends way too much time on Twitter is an insistence that having a career (or worse yet, enjoying it) is something that women have been “brainwashed” into, and that any woman with her head on straight would run as far as she could away from the prospect of her own paycheck. A common refrain: a photo of a mother and her baby (it’s always a baby, never a teenager) with the caption, “How could women choose spreadsheets over this?” Alternatively, a photo of a woman in the kitchen surrounded by well-behaved, adoring children: “How did they convince women that this was oppressive, but spreadsheets were liberating?”

What I find odd about this belief system is that the men in question never stop to ask themselves if they would choose spreadsheets over babies. 99% of the time, they have chosen spreadsheets. If they have wives, those wives would probably love some more help with the kids, and they (again, I’m aware I’m making assumptions but they feel like pretty safe assumptions) are not jumping at the opportunity. In fact, they appear to spend more time fantasizing about killing people to save their children than actually performing the routine tasks of childcare.

Before you say this is just wacky right-wing trolls on Twitter, I’ll admit that even I wondered about this from time to time—the idea that I had been “lied to” about work being empowering or worthwhile. I felt this way mostly because I hated my own job, and kept getting fired. Nothing about my work experience was “empowering,” especially given that one of my bosses threatened to spank me and forced me to take shots of whiskey at 10 AM—and strangely enough, that wasn’t even my worst work experience. When your work situation sucks, it’s easy to assume that you don’t want to work at all, and while men might just say “Wow, my boss sucks,” women have a very easy “maybe I’m just a tradwife” pipeline set up for them. Hence the wave of women in 2021 who fantasized about being homesteaders when they actually just wanted to be aristocrats.

I get it—I fantasized about ending my career long before I actually did it to become a writer. (While I refer to myself as a SAHM because that’s my day-to-day lifestyle, I very much have a career, you’re looking at it now! It’s just one that I like.) Eventually, I realized that I was in the wrong line of work and I wanted to write full-time. But I hated my job long before that revelation, and for most of that time I thought I just hated the concept of working entirely. Cue: this is the tiny baby deer you’re asking to make spreadsheets, aren’t I adorable? No, you just hate your job and should probably be doing something else.

But for many of the men who believe women have been “tricked” into working, the thought process is a bit different: they imagine women are so different from them, wired so differently, that there’s no way they could possibly have the same aspirations or desires as men, even something as simple as wanting a stimulating career outside the home with adult interaction. These men might enjoy their own careers (even if they’d prefer to be independently wealthy, because wouldn’t we all) but can’t conceive of a world where women enjoy their jobs—or more shockingly, where men actually want their wives to work too.

But alas, while many women are happy to be stay-at-home moms (including, in some ways, me) many other women genuinely love their jobs. And it’s probably important to really understand what being a SAHM involves before you insist that every woman would be happier doing it.

First of all, when I describe my own lifestyle as a SAHM, I feel the need to unironically check my privilege. I am a very fortunate kind of SAHM who is basically having her cake and eating it too, and I’d be dishonest not to come clean about that. While being a SAHM has become associated with wealth in online discussions (“Not everyone can afford to stay home!”) it’s actually often correlated with lower overall socioeconomic status. It makes sense when you think about it. If your annual salary is the same amount or less than the cost of daycare, and did not require a college degree, staying home with your kids feels like an obvious choice. The below graph is not limited to parents, but as you can see, there is no inverse relationship between a man’s income and his wife’s. There is no magical threshold of earnings where a man can finally “afford” to retire his wife. Higher-earning men have higher-earning wives, and lower-earning men are less likely to have wives who work at all. This is true at least up until a man makes $275K, where part-time working and non-working wives become more common. But either way, working wives are still more common in this echelon than anything else. More on that here.

In my case, it’s different. I am a SAHM in the sense that my work is flexible enough for me to spend more time with my kids—basically, nearly all my time. My husband is also lucky enough to have a very flexible remote job across a slightly different time zone, which enables him to help with the kids so I can have breaks to write, work out, or sew. And as I’ve written about before, my husband handles more housework than your typical husband-of-SAHM. (This is in part because he’s just way more fastidious about cleaning than I am to the point of neurosis, but he does help out a lot.)

I don’t say all that to brag. I say that because when I say, “I love being a SAHM, this is so much better than working in tech!” I am talking about a very narrow definition of “SAHM” that miraculously also includes writing for a living, financial independence and a supportive spouse. For that reason, I cannot in good faith say, “I can’t believe I was ever tricked into believing working was empowering.” Working is empowering. It’s empowering to make your own money, and if it doesn’t feel empowering it’s probably because you hate your job.

That’s not to say being a SAHM (the non-writer kind) is always disempowering. But, like anything else, it’s a tradeoff, and it’s a tradeoff most men never even consider making. Hmm, I wonder if someone just tricked them into thinking spreadsheets are empowering.

For one, a SAHM is not just giving up her career for a couple years to raise babies. Some can (I know a psychologist with a private practice who was able to do this) but many others find that even taking a couple years off can be a long-term hit to their careers. Many women who take some time off to be a SAHM with the intention of working again find it very difficult to compete in a job market with a gap in their resume, especially if new technology or skills have become important during that time. That’s not to say a SAHM can never get back into the workforce later, only that this is a risk, and the opportunity cost of such a risk is higher if you started out with a higher-earning job or a job that required particular training or education. Early on in my tech career, I worked alongside a forty-five-year-old woman who had just returned to the tech scene after having kids. I never stopped to wonder about how much she was making, but in hindsight she…had the same role as me, and I was twenty-two.

There is also the practical, day-to-day reality of what it means not to have financial independence. Women have worked for all of human history—the “housewife” is more of a recent phenomenon—but women having a say over what they do with their own money (something we all take for granted, I think) is also pretty recent. And yes, plenty of modern-day SAHMs share their finances with their husband and have an equal role in determining how the money is spent. But many others don’t have this option. In a worst case scenario, a woman having no financial independence opens her up to abuse or infidelity with very little recourse (no, it’s actually not that easy to get a divorce when you don’t have a job or a realistic way of making money.) And in a less dramatic but more common scenario, a man may undervalue a woman’s domestic work and opinions because she doesn’t “contribute” to the household finances.

Many women become SAHMs and are plenty happy with this decision, and have husbands who value them and pull their own weight around the house. But given all the tradeoffs involved in such a choice, is it that surprising that some women might…want to have a job? And, yes, some women hate their jobs, and some men hate their jobs, and some people go to work and clench their ass cheeks in case their boss finally snaps and spanks them. But mostly, men and women aren’t too different in our degrees of passion for work (or lack thereof.) A while ago, I ran a survey on this topic, and what do you know, men and women both felt almost equally passionate about their own careers:

And yes, before anyone says it: I’m fully aware men are not literally aroused by women’s paychecks (not the point, anyway) but men aren’t turned off by women’s careers, and often prefer a woman to make her own money just for pragmatic reasons. The myth that all men would prefer their wives stop working is another Twitter delusion. If anything, the opposite dynamic is more common, but even then, most couples are aligned on whether the wife should work or not.

Men who ask about whether spreadsheets are more “empowering” than taking care of babies also tend not to be intimately familiar with what being a stay-at-home parent entails. Financial tradeoffs aside, most people are not actually cut out to care for young children—often more than one at a time—all day, without any help, breaks or adult interaction. It’s easy to compare today’s stressed out moms with moms in the past, and decide they’re just wimps who can’t cope with the reality of adulthood. But mothers of yore often worked in tandem with other women and relatives in the fields and had adult socialization all day long. That’s not to say that olden-day moms were doing great. People often look to the vague “olden days” as a time when everything must have been fine, and then decide that anything we’re doing differently today must be making us comparatively more miserable. But it’s true that modern-day SAHMs are taking part in a lifestyle that has not been normal for most of human history, most notably their degree of isolation. I quite enjoy being around my kids all day, but again, I have Substack. I have a close friend who lives a few doors down. I have a husband who can pop in and relieve me from time to time. Many SAHMs do not have these things.

There are aso downsides to “spreadsheets” which disproportionately affect mothers. As Stephanie H. Murray brought to light, via Pew Research, mothers are more likely than fathers to be extremely or very upset when they have to miss a child’s activity because of work. And in a separate survey I conducted, when limiting to women who have or want children, 81% would prefer 12-18 months of paid maternity leave over 18 months of free daycare. Most moms have a very strong drive to spend time with their kids, and most working moms probably wish they had more flexibility to enable more time with their kids. But wanting better work-life balance is not the same thing as wanting to end their careers entirely.

And is it not likely that men would also like more family time, and a less cumbersome commute? I believe (and I’m aware this is an overly charitable interpretation) that some men overestimate just how different men and women really are. There seems to be a push against a late-2000s feminist ethos that claimed men and women were actually the same (or at least that’s how many people remember it.) See, for example, the idea that casual sex was just as fun for women as it was for men, and that you were a conservative killjoy if you pointed out that many women were actually having hookups in the hopes of securing monogamous relationships. But I fear we’ve overcorrected, and have gotten to a point where some men cannot possibly imagine women having any of the same desires or thoughts that they have. I’m reminded of a legendary Reddit post on r/AskWomen where a man, who did not consider himself particularly sexist or misogynistic, asked the women of Reddit if they had internal monologues, because he assumed this was just a guy thing.

In many facets of attraction and sex, I’ve written about how men and women are different. When I wrote about how women often need “permission” to be horny, a lot of women were like, “Wow, yeah, that’s me!” while the men just looked confused. And yes, certain hobbies and interests are likely to skew male or female. But when it comes to the big picture stuff, like wanting friends, loving your family, or having career goals, we really aren’t all that different. And if women have been lied about spreadsheets being empowering, men seem to have fallen for that lie too.
