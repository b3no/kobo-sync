Maybe The Kid Who Eats Paper Isn’t a Misunderstood Genius

Being oppositional and lacking organizational skills does not make you Einstein.

Somewhere upstream of the hatred of “HR ladies” is the hatred of “highlighter girls,” or Type-A overachieving girls who do really well in school but are only smart insofar as it refers to useless organization skills and rote memorization.

As the legend has it, highlighter girls grow up to be, you guessed it, HR ladies, or perhaps even marketing executives and other species of girlboss, while not actually having any original ideas or true intelligence. Now, the world is run by highlighter girls, everything is pink and organized, and everyone is miserable. The true geniuses—the pyromaniac boys who couldn’t sit still for five minutes, who kept turning their eyelids inside out and drawing that S—should be running the world, but because education is so feminized, it cannot illuminate the full extent of their elusive erudite. (Because, of course, no boy was ever expected to sit still, follow rules or obey his teacher until the rise of insipid female teachers in Ms. Rachel overalls.)

I’ve seen this idea parroted online a bunch of times, but this isn’t one of those wacky Internet ideas that you can avoid by logging off. At one of my earliest jobs at an advertising startup, I had a boss named Vince who didn’t go to college. Given that all the roles in the company required a college degree, I was a little surprised, but he informed me he went to the “school of hard knocks,” and that he had “street smarts,” so he was actually smarter than anyone who went to college because he could “really think.” He was giving big “Michael Scott rips textbooks up at business school” energy.

Unfortunately, Vince’s superior street smarts stopped short of the ability to use Powerpoint—he would frequently ask me to show him how to use basic software programs “just to see if I knew how to do it” when he clearly didn’t know and was unwilling to learn because learning was for squares. He would send emails with incorrect capitalization and punctuation. He was a decent salesman, but frequently relied on lying to close deals (he called it “telling the truth very well.”) Vince’s company also went out of business because he was selling a fraudulent product. All the while, Vince would pull me aside and chuckle with me about the “choirboys” at our company who thought they were smart because they studied in school.

Now, I actually did not perform very well in school, which might be why Vince sought me out as a companion at our company. I had been hiding my low college GPA until I realized it would earn me some street cred with Vince, and it did. Throughout my youth, school felt like going to work, except I wasn’t paid, I wasn’t good at it, and everything was boring. Memorably, I was once reprimanded for writing a play during a math lesson in third grade. At the beginning of every school year, I would rush to Staples with my mom to find some perfect, color-coordinated school supplies, and I would promise myself that this was the year I would become the right type of girl at school. A highlighter girl. I wanted to have tiny, neat, bubbly handwriting. I wanted to win awards. I wanted to be the girl who raised her hand and elicited a response of, “Anyone else, for a change?”

But I wasn’t that girl. And I understand why other people who weren’t that girl might be keen to rebrand her as “not really that smart” and undeserving of her success, while the true geniuses (the slackers, me, Vince, the inside-out eyelid boys) got the short end of the stick. But organizing, attention to detail, social skills, and the ability to remember things are actually important skills. Being able to focus on things you don’t find innately interesting is a skill I wish I could acquire. And even if we don’t want the world run by highlighter girls, I’m not sure I want the world run by Vince (or me, quite frankly) either. Because sometimes the kid who can’t learn in school isn’t a misunderstood genius—just a kid of average intelligence who also can’t follow instructions and doesn’t listen.

As a certified Not Highlighter Girl, a student who once got a 0 on a math quiz because I forgot to write my name, I also told myself that perhaps I was just smarter than everyone. I was the zany, creative genius who forgot to wear pants, and none of these people would ever fully comprehend my brilliance. And maybe that was true, if intelligence was solely measured by who would later grow up to be the most successful Substack writer and Trump impersonator, but I don’t think it is. As a not-great student, I was also a not-great employee who repeatedly got fired. This was not because my employers were all busybody girlbosses who got mad at me over stupid things like using the wrong icons in my decks, or because society and late stage capitalism is unfairly neurotypical, but because I have terrible organizational and prioritization skills. I forget things constantly. I would get busy with a work task and completely miss an important meeting. I nearly flunked a class in college because I simply forgot we were presenting our theses that day and missed the entire thing. At school or at work, no matter how hard I tried, when I attempted to engage with material I found boring, I was actually incapable of retaining it. And that doesn’t even scratch the surface of my notoriously poor interpersonal skills in office settings.

Jobs aside from “writing one article every day about my favorite topic” do not work out very well for people like me. Being able to remember things, organize your thoughts and actually get work done (even if you think it’s stupid or boring) is incredibly important. Perhaps AI will level the playing field at some point and make it easier for people like me to stay on track (maybe it already has!) but that doesn’t make the skills I lack worthless.

But of course, we do have our fair share of misunderstood geniuses. We all know that Steve Jobs, Bill Gates, and Mark Zuckerberg dropped out of college, which has now become evidence for the fact that people who can’t focus on school are actually smarter than the losers who study. But, of course, this is selection bias. Dario Amodei, the CEO of Anthropic, has a bachelor’s degree and a PhD. Jeff Bezos graduated from Princeton. Most of the CEOs of Fortune 500 companies have bachelor’s or graduate degrees. And the famous tech CEOs who dropped out of college did so to pursue the company they founded, not simply because they were understimulated. Moreover, in order to get into Ivy League schools in the first place, all those “dropout” CEOs still had to be highlighter girls throughout high school. One does not simply stumble into Harvard!

You see this perception a lot with “maverick” characters who are always doing outside-the-box things. Dr. House might be the best doctor at his hospital, but Dr. House at one point was a medical student, taking notes and actually studying. Of course, Dr. House wasn’t real and probably would have been fired much earlier in his career for all the insane stuff he does, but even if he was real, you don’t get to be Dr. House (or Zuck) without first being a highlighter girl.

Part of this false dichotomy of highlighter girls versus secret genius boys is just misogyny. That’s why it’s about “highlighter girls” and not “protractor boys.” There are plenty of obedient male poindexters out there, and you don’t see anyone second-guessing their intelligence, no matter how similar their behavior is to highlighter girls. But there’s another less bigoted element to this, which is the idea that ADHD or neurodivergence necessarily correlates with hidden gifts.

Who among us has not heard of an autistic person who was a savant with math but too bored in high school to bother with his work? A great artist with ADHD who flunked out of college?

As a literally neurodivergent person myself, I’m all-too-familiar with the urge to rebrand my disability as a secret strength. Recently, at lunch with a family member, I lamented my ADHD and OCD but then added, “Actually, maybe if I was cured, I wouldn’t be as good of a writer! After all, most of the great creatives were mentally tortured and disorganized!” My lunchmate pulled no punches: “Actually, you’d be better at writing, because you’d spend less time worrying about everything and you’d manage your time better.” Damn.

While we all hear stories about neurodivergent people with special talents, it’s likely those people would be just as smart if they weren’t neurodivergent. I’ll concede that ADHD or autism (or OCD, for that matter) might help someone hyper-focus on one area of obsession, which could make them particularly talented in that one thing, but typically at the detriment of many other skills they should be learning, but aren’t. So even when there is a secret talent (and there isn’t always! Plenty of neurodivergent people are just totally average, or even below average) it usually comes with a deficit in other areas.

And as uplifting as it is to view neurodivergent people as misunderstood geniuses, we’re not. ADHD is not correlated with IQ. Executive function and social skills, which are often lacking in neurodivergent people, are instrumental to success in many workplaces. No, this isn’t because imperious highlighter girls are running the world, but because if you’re the CEO, it’s necessary that you remember things, prioritize, and stay on track when a topic is boring. If you’re horrible with people, it’s not our rigid neurotypical society that prevents you from getting ahead. It’s your bad social skills and your unwillingness to learn them.

Then there’s the last element of this misconception: the idea that expending effort is always counterintuitive, and the person who tries the least is actually doing the best job. This belief persists across multiple different areas of life. If you have to try at something, you aren’t actually good at it, ergo, someone who doesn’t try is probably more talented or more likely to find success. When my husband and I were trying and struggling to get pregnant, almost everyone we told informed us that actually the secret to fertility was “just not trying” and having fun. Everyone knew some couple who thought they were infertile, but conceived as soon as they threw out the thermometers and graphs. Of course, everyone knows these stories because they have a surprising and happy ending, but they don’t reflect reality. 1 in 6 people of reproductive age actually is infertile. For every couple who stopped “trying” and got pregnant (if you’re fucking, you’re still trying) there are probably many others who never conceived because they couldn’t afford IVF.

Take longevity, too. How many times have you heard the gleeful prediction that rural Russian grandmothers who never see a doctor, who smoke, whose diet consists of sausage and vodka live to 120 while biohackers will die of a heart attack at 40? (Oh? Just me? Well, I see it a lot.) Of course, there’s no real evidence to support this. Life span tends to correlate suspiciously with countries that have access to healthcare, and there is real evidence to suggest that a well-rounded diet, not smoking and not drinking are good for you. But we don’t want the tryhards to win.

Fashion, too, is littered with the belief that true style comes from a lack of effort. I wrote about the declaration that “true fashion people” don’t wear mascara or nail polish, and working too hard on your appearance signals low status within the fashion world (even though realistically speaking, most people who are not well-known fashion icons look best with a bit of effort.) Fashion is supposed to be effortless, which was why every woman on r/femalefashionadvice in 2018 was obsessed with looking like Cara Delevingne on her way to the airport. Beauty is no different—I hear from all sorts of people that using “too much skincare” actually makes you look worse, and that washing your face with Dove soap and then using basic moisturizer is the secret Holy Grail of youth, despite zero evidence that this is true. Likely, anyone who looks freakishly young while not doing anything to maintain their skin just has good genetics (although really, people are pretty deluded about this and it’s rarely true.)

So it’s a mix. One part misogyny, one part self-imposed noble savage view of neurodivergence, and one part anti-tryhard propaganda. Either way, it’s annoying.

Of course, there is no need for everyone to be a highlighter girl (or protracot boy). Just as I appreciate the importance of typemaxxing for your clothes and hairstyle (ie: grooming and dressing in a way that maximizes your natural strengths) I think that kids should be encouraged to focus on the things they really love, and the areas where they have natural gifts. It always drove me nuts when college applications demanded I share a story of a time I showed leadership, because what if I wasn’t meant to be a leader? (I wasn’t!) And surely, the world would be in a very unpleasant place if everyone wanted to be in charge! Neurodivergent people should work on their skill deficits, yes, but if you have a special interest, you should focus on that special interest and ideally get a job doing something with it so you aren’t miserable throughout your career. But studying well across all topics, paying attention and having good executive functioning are not useless either. Highlighter girls genuinely do have marketable and hirable skills. If I ever were to hire someone to work for me, it would be for an admin role to manage my business crap and money stuff that, quite frankly, scares me. I would jump to hire a highlighter girl (or protractor boy, or calculator they/them, or whatever.)

Diversity of thought is necessary to make innovative ideas a reality—you need the right brains and the left brains, the creatives and the logical thinkers, the social butterflies and the introverted spreadsheet people—but perhaps more importantly, being unable to study, focus, sit still or have a conversation does not a quirky genius make. Sometimes, these are just skill deficits, and perhaps you should be asking the highlighter girls for tips.
