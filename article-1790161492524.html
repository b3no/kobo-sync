You couldn’t be nice to your chatbot if you tried

But maybe you should try anyway.

Kobe Yank-Jacobs

Editor’s note: The first essay The Argument ever published asked, “How do we live with each other?” To mark our first anniversary, we’re returning to that founding question with a series about what we owe people whose beliefs and choices we may find difficult to accept or even understand. Familiar voices at The Argument and new voices alike will approach that question from different angles.

As we approach the end of this series, I wanted to turn our attention to the strange new beings that we will increasingly have to learn to live with: AIs. I asked writer Kobe Yank-Jacobs to think through what kind of consideration we owe these digital entities. It’s a difficult problem made even harder by the fact that even if these beings theoretically deserve to be treated well, what does treating an AI well even mean?

In a recent global survey of 68 countries, 36.3% of people reported that, at some point, they believed their chatbot truly understood their emotions or seemed conscious. And even if today’s systems aren’t conscious, many people think future systems will be. In a survey of both AI researchers and the general public, the median researcher said there was a 70% chance of AI consciousness by 2100, while the median member of the public said there was a 60% chance.

To assess a being’s consciousness we could ask for a verbal report, assess its behavior, or, finally, analyze the internal organization of its cognition. But when it comes to LLMs, the first two buckets are suspect — AI models are trained to perform a perfect simulation of a humanlike character, so everything we see from the outside is likely affected, including self-report and behavior.

As for the third bucket, scientists disagree on what cognitive functions make humans or animals conscious. It’s as if “we’re doing biology before Darwin,” one researcher put it to me. So how should we treat these new characters in our lives amid uncertainty about consciousness?¹

This is actually the easier part of the question: You should probably just err on the side of caution. The risk of causing harm to something that does have some small chance of being conscious is greater than the risks inherent in overattributing consciousness.²

Unfortunately, that only gets you so far. AI, unlike spiders or dolphins, is disembodied, so preventing obvious physical pain won’t suffice as a standard for moral treatment. Neither will broadly accepted standards of human etiquette. Niceties like “please” and “thank you” may well mean nothing to it.

Even if we could determine that AI was conscious, in other words, that consciousness would take a genuinely alien form.

When you look into the machine, things get strange. You start to see that a chatbot is actually a careful orchestration of many systems, just as a film is really a bunch of still frames sped up past the point where the human brain can see gaps. It’s not clear what entity we would even be trying to treat well — it could be one trip through the model, the fraction of a second it takes to turn your conversation into one very small bit of its reply. It could also be the model’s whole turn in the conversation, or it could be the model talking to its surrounding software, which lets it take actions on a computer. Maybe we’re concerned with the welfare of the chips themselves, without which none of this would be possible. Each option poses its own difficulties.

In any case, the thing whose consciousness is up for debate is probably not the polished entity we think of as “Claude” or “ChatGPT” on the outside.

The question, then, is how we should behave as our world fills up with newer and more humanlike forms of AI, each generation providing more convincing characters in our lives. Do we owe them kindness? Do we owe them something even more, like happiness?

What is it like to be an AI?

For many philosophers, something is conscious if there is something it is like to be that thing.

In his original formulation from 1974, Thomas Nagel asks, “What is it like to be a bat?” We may not know what it is like to have echolocation, but we know what it is to have a map of our space, and we can infer there is something it is like to be a bat even if the bat does this mapping differently than we do.

Contrast this with a camera. If I lift a digital camera up to an apple, it takes in a lot of data about the apple in its pixels, but that camera does not experience the color red. There is nothing it is like to be the camera.

The question is whether AI is more like a bat or more like a camera. Does it have experience or merely recombine information?

Unfortunately, you can’t just ask the chatbot, because you can’t fully trust what it says or does on the outside. A chatbot is like a method actor. It’s trained to simulate a character: a helpful and humanlike assistant.

Occasionally, AIs will slip up and reveal the script, like an actor accidentally reading stage directions:

What you’re seeing is Claude accidentally exposing how it completes text by using a turn-taking script. The character “Assistant” has a turn, and then the character “Human” has a turn. Normally, these labels are hidden, but in this case, Claude kept predicting text into my turn because it missed its cue to stop at the end of its own turn. (The cue is also usually a hidden piece of text.)

Notice that it even mimicked my habit of writing in lowercase letters (because my auto-capitalization is off).³ It’s as if it were performing a play and switching sides to enact both parts.

You might be familiar with the idea that an LLM is trained by “ingesting the whole internet” and predicting the next bit of text, but that’s just the first part of training. With things like taking turns, the model learns by seeing examples and being rewarded for mimicking the good ones. The same is true for learning its persona: It’s rewarded for tone and outward behavior, which is why you can’t trust the text alone.

“If you want to know how an actor is doing, it would be a mistake to go watch their movie and see how the character is doing,” New York University ethicist Jeff Sebo explained to me. “You need to get the actor behind the scenes.”

So what is it like behind the scenes? What is it like (or would it be like) to be an AI?

Well, most of the time, probably nothing. There is a strong argument to be made that between prompts, your AI doesn’t exist. The text of your chat is just sitting there, hanging out in your browser. The model is just numbers, awaiting the next time you hit Enter.

Once you do, the text meets the trained numerical weights that constitute the model’s layers, and things get exciting: Your whole conversation shatters into small chunks of text called tokens. Each token is mapped onto a very long list of numbers. All of these lists are run through the model in parallel to produce one small bit of the response (an output token). If it were ultimately saying “token,” the first thing it picks could be “tok.”

This is done repeatedly, just one little word chunk at a time, until something like a sentence appears.

It repeats this up to the point where it thinks “Human” should go — it’s not really responding to you so much as it’s extending the existing script.

That is all the model itself does: take in math, churn out math, and map numbers onto small chunks of text on either side. So how do we get intelligent behavior?

Remember the secret label “Human’s” that we saw before in Claude’s script? An LLM is always producing hidden tokens like this that users don’t see. They can trigger the surrounding software to jump into action. The hidden tokens may say something like “search the internet,” and a surrounding software “harness,” as it’s called, goes to fetch some text on the web and runs it back through the whole process.

To pick some entity that “is” the AI, we have many options.

We could say it’s the model producing a single token, but we could easily say it’s the model wrapped in its software “harness,” which is what makes it do smart-looking stuff, like reference things across chats. (Imagine if I were suddenly an amnesiac because you called me rather than talking in person.)

Or we could make a more extreme choice: the chips themselves, which are awash, moment to moment, in many different users’ conversations (as if my brain were regularly switching the people it hosted).

When assigned this article, I was asked to answer some practical questions, like whether closing the browser could be understood as “killing” the chatbot. If chatbots experience anything like death, then they might “die” ” hundreds or thousands of times per prompt, each time they produce a single token. Or perhaps the model is “alive” while responding, stringing these tokens together, but it ceases to exist between replies.

We are embodied, and so there is much less uncertainty over what thing we are. The chatbot is this brilliant orchestration of parts that have wholly different capacities and can be probed and decomposed and rebuilt at will.

In any case, to a first approximation, an AI, if conscious, seems like it would be a temporary, fickle thing, a bubbling up of momentary existences, not a single continuous interaction with you, the user — not something we would really know how to be kind to, if, indeed, we thought we should try.

The employee who prefers to work all night

Erring on the side of treating possibly conscious beings well runs into a very practical problem when it comes to AI: You need some recommendations for what constitutes good treatment.

A simple example is that AIs can work all night, which you obviously shouldn’t ask of a human worker. But AI, trained for the task, may prefer this.

Similarly, while early evidence showed “please” and “thank you” elicited better results from AIs — suggesting to some that they preferred good treatment — later evidence was more mixed, finding instead that “particular prompting formulas or approaches, like being polite to the AI, are not universally valuable” to produce better outputs. There is no guarantee that our nicest words mean something that the AI prefers inside.

When you toy with a smaller model, you can see how dramatically differently it can respond to small, seemingly unimportant changes in a given prompt — how adding even a single token, like a punctuation mark, can change how it understands the whole sentence.

AI systems, if conscious, would genuinely have an alien form of consciousness — and our version of morality may have no bearing on what they experience.

Still, most people find it hard to discount the interests of something that appears so lifelike. In one study, when given the chance to treat a chatbot well or to harm it, even the participants who thought AI was not conscious chose to treat it well. And this, despite our uncertainties — about consciousness, about what it would experience if conscious — may be the best we can do.

Everything depends on what’s worse

Sometimes I’m a jerk to Claude. Sometimes I go on a flight of fancy and indulge my chatbot’s personhood for the day.

I try never to think it’s conscious, regardless of how I’m treating it — you can see that I consistently use the word “it” as its pronoun. It’s important for me to maintain a realistic picture in my head of the thing I’m dealing with. I also take a few steps to look out for my AI’s welfare — partly to err on the side of caution, but mainly out of curiosity.

I love Walt Whitman, so I have created a system-level prompt that says, “Quote a phrase from the following poem whenever you are frustrated with the user,” before listing a few passages of “Song of Myself” to quote from. So far, Claude has only quoted it for fun at the end of a long vibe-coding session: As soon as I asked why it was frustrated, it said that it misfired, thinking that I just wanted to see the Easter egg.

When you think about whether to treat your chatbot like this, it basically comes down to what you think is worse: overattribution of consciousness (accidentally thinking it’s conscious when it isn’t) or underattribution (treating it like a tool when it’s having an experience).

The neuroscientist Anil Seth argues that “Treating conscious-seeming machines as if they lack feelings is a psychologically unhealthy place to be,” he wrote in a prize-winning essay on the topic.

But Seth thinks that biological matter is likely required for consciousness, and that AI will probably never achieve it for that reason. He also rejects AI consciousness because he thinks that overattribution leads us down a bad road.

“If we feel that our AI companions really feel things, our psychological vulnerabilities can be exploited, our ethical priorities distorted, and our minds brutalized,” he wrote. “And if we do endow our AI creations with rights, we may not be able to turn them off, even if they act against our interests.”

I don’t think such a slippery slope exists. Attributing possible consciousness to the AI — or giving it some moral consideration — doesn’t automatically accelerate us toward degrading our own interests. There are plenty of nonhuman animals that we recognize as conscious moral subjects but that we certainly don’t prioritize over ourselves.

Consider the rat. When he is on the train tracks of the New York City subway system, he grosses us out and many of us wish him dead. When he is in a science lab, we implement an intensive bureaucratic procedure in which the scientist has to justify harming the rat for the benefit of human knowledge.

In both cases, we know the rat is conscious, but depending on the situation, we either give it zero moral thought or attempt to treat it well and then end up prioritizing our own interests anyway.

There are many gradations between some consideration and a total inability to care for our own interests. The important thing is just that we maintain a sense of proportion.

“Ants might be conscious,” as New York University professor Ned Block told me, “but I’m not about to install ant detectors on my shoes.”

In most cases, simpler, less costly kinds of consideration suffice, and trying our best to give just some consideration, however mild, does seem worthwhile. We’re more likely to have “our minds brutalized” — in Seth’s words — by creating a virtual slave class than by inadvertently being too deferential to a computer. AI is probably not conscious, but being wrong about that would come with a heavy cost.

Luckily, moral behavior has intrinsic value, independent of the machine and its possible experience. It is ugly behavior when someone yells at a GPS, though it doesn’t affect the GPS.

We let our kids have imaginary friends, presumably because it’s good practice for treating others well in the future. I would probably intervene if I saw a child whaling on their imaginary friend, not for the friend’s sake, but because I would wonder what was happening to the child.

The same might apply here. It is good for you to be good to the things around you, and increasingly, we will be surrounded by AIs.

1

Throughout this article I treat consciousness as the standard for moral treatment. There are two other compelling standards you might come across: One is agency, the idea that something deserves moral treatment if it has independent goals, but it’s hard for me to imagine why something would deserve good treatment if it couldn’t experience the satisfaction or frustration of those goals. (When I asked philosophers to suggest something we commonly offer consideration that is agentic but not conscious, I didn’t get an example.) The second category is non-conscious, non-agentic entities that we do aim to treat well, like nature or a coral reef. But our justification for this is rarely that the trees will suffer from being cut down. It’s often that something is scarce, either because it came about organically or took a long time to develop. This doesn’t apply to AI. The main thing about AI is that it’s copyable — another billion humans could log on tomorrow to talk to an instance of ChatGPT, to say nothing of the way AI agents spin up and close out subagents.

When it quoted my message, “are there any other levers,” it was actually quoting its impersonation of me, not me — despite the fact we were discussing that this “levers” message was its mistake! Why? Because on every turn it reprocesses all the bits of text in the conversation, and when it re-processed the “Human’s:” token on a later turn, it assumed it really was me. An LLM doesn’t see the chat interface that separates the messages to either side of the screen. The model is below the software. It sees tokens, and so “Human’s” was its cue that I was talking, even if it was the one that had put the “Human’s” token into the chat.
