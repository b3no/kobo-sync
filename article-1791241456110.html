With a new agenda, the Build America Caucus gets ambitious with abundance

The bipartisan House caucus hopes to apply housing momentum to energy, child care, and transportation, expanding the federal imagination of abundance.

Halina Bennet
Oct 5
∙
Paid

This was a slow week in housing news, but I had the chance to talk to Representative Josh Harder about the rollout of a new bipartisan Build More Agenda so wrote about that instead — your regularly scheduled housing roundup will return next week.

The bipartisan abundance-oriented Build America Caucus in the House of Representatives is getting more ambitious with a multi-issue Build More Agenda that aims to expand the scope of its legislative work beyond the housing legislation that passed earlier this year. Billed as the largest bipartisan governing agenda in years, the package contains more than 60 proposals spanning housing, health care, energy, scientific research, transportation and infrastructure, child care, education, workforce, and the machine of government itself.

Chairs Josh Harder, a Democrat from California, and Celeste Maloy, a Utah Republican, framed the agenda as a step toward ameliorating the housing shortage and, with it, the root of dysfunction in American life. The caucus was founded last May to bring the growth-oriented policymaking that was already thriving in localities across the country to Congress and has since helped negotiate and pass the 21st Century ROAD to Housing Act and led efforts on tech-neutral reforms to make permitting more predictable.

I spoke with Harder in an interview Monday. He said the agenda has two jobs: one is to advance ambitious ideas on housing and energy, and the other is to weigh in on what “abundance” ought to mean in sectors where people at the federal level are thinking less obviously in those terms. Harder said the caucus itself was formed to bring about at least one big piece of legislation this Congress and to turn abundance from an abstract principle he said had been better defined in state and local government into real federal action. On both counts, he said, the caucus is feeling good.

ROAD is now law, and Harder said he believes permitting reform is possible. The success of key housing and energy legislation in a polarized, widely unproductive Congress, he said, is not a coincidence: Both bills reflect a bipartisan feeling that the country is far too expensive and that it must build more.

“Nobody thought we were going to pass a housing bill or an energy bill, and now I think we’re going to get both done this Congress,” he said of ROAD and the Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act — the energy permitting bill currently in the Senate.

Now, with a broader agenda, the caucus is trying to take the growing enthusiasm for building housing and apply that supply-side approach to other key areas.

Its primary policy area is, of course, housing. Harder was candid that ROAD alone will not close the nation’s gap, which the caucus says is about five million units. One of its suggestions is a proposal for Right to Build Zones, which he called an aggressive approach but “the right direction to go for a crisis that is a really big deal and one of the most important policy issues anywhere.”

With this idea, cities would designate zones where residential projects are automatically approved if they meet standardized pro-housing rules, and the federal government in return would pay the city a fixed amount for each home completed above its construction baseline. In the agenda’s example, the federal government would pay $10,000 per unit — roughly what local governments typically charge developers in impact fees for new multifamily housing. This plan allows for communities to maintain control over where and how they grow, but the federal government rewards the localities that build.

The agenda includes expanding the number of physical locations where housing can be built through rezoning and federal incentives for localities to build, cutting costs and timelines, unlocking and simplifying more funding streams for projects, and fully implementing ROAD through federal financial and logistical oversight. One proposal, for example, would provide federally backed second mortgages to finance accessory-dwelling-unit construction. Implementing ROAD, the agenda notes, requires 124 actions across 11 agencies, most of them relying on a Department of Housing and Urban Development that is facing significant cuts to staffing in key offices.

The agenda includes six other policy areas:

  • Health Care: Expand the availability of care, cut paperwork and waste, and lower drug prices.

  • Energy: Accelerate permitting, make more efficient use of the existing power grid, and invest in expanding new sources of energy, combatting a 33 percent increase in energy rates over the past five years.

  • Science: Simplify grant applications and cut red tape, fund ambitious, high-risk, high-reward projects, and implement artificial intelligence in research.

  • Transportation and Infrastructure: Speed up approvals, rethink and reduce spending, and reward coordination foresight.

  • Child Care, Education, and Workforce: Make child care more accessible and affordable, encourage reading, invest in tutoring and A.I. adoption, fund job training, and increase access to apprenticeships and licensing.

  • State Capacity: Focus on skills-based hiring for federal roles, reduce red tape, and integrate A.I. and digital technology.

Harder said that in an agenda that includes 62 proposals, not all ideas can be created equal.

Four key issues

For the father of two children under five years old — and a founding member of the Congressional Dads Caucus that highlights issues working parents face — child care is personal. Harder said that he knows firsthand how hard and expensive care is to find. A bipartisan child-care proposal itself is notable, he said, and increasing accessibility will take the same supply-side approach as housing. The agenda encourages states to remove zoning and licensing barriers that stand in the way of allowing people to operate child-care centers out of their homes, with the aim of unlocking slots for about two million children.

Clinical trial reform, he said, is another key proposal. According to the agenda, it takes more than a year on average to prepare the Food and Drug Administration application for an early-phase trial. The caucus proposes a streamlined application pathway that could allow trials to start up to a year sooner.

On transit and infrastructure, Harder highlighted an idea to “do transit projects concurrently instead of sequentially,” letting agencies do early work like land acquisition and utility relocation while environmental review is underway. That idea would be simple in practice and produce bridges, highways, and mass transit much more quickly and cheaply.

Rounding out his list of priorities is reducing health-care paperwork. Harder sits on the Committee on Appropriations, where he is a member of the Subcommittee on Labor, Health and Human Services, and Education that manages the National Institutes of Health budget. He said that nearly every conversation he has on that subcommittee is about whether to spend a dollar more or a dollar less and that almost none of those conversations are about “How do we make sure the dollars we’re spending are going further?” The agenda proposes simplifying grants so scientists spend less time on administration, which it says consumes 44 percent of their federally funded research time. In another proposal, the agenda would eliminate insurer approvals for low-risk and routine care, noting that more than 90 percent of requests under Medicare Advantage plans are approved.

“You go into most doctors’ offices and you will see more people behind a desk filling out paperwork and working with the insurance companies than you will see treating patients. That doesn’t seem to be the right ratio for doctors’ offices,” he said. “How do we reduce the amount of time that folks are spending dealing with insurance complaints while also not increasing the costs to a system that is already overloaded?”

The A.I. piece

Artificial intelligence comes up in a few places in the agenda in regard to how best to utilize the rapidly evolving technology. Harder said it is hard to write a policy agenda in Washington right now without addressing A.I., which he said is the center of two different conversations: how to keep the technology safe and how to regulate and build data centers. The caucus is not focused on the first, and the second is mostly happening elsewhere in Congress, including in the aforementioned permitting package.

The caucus’s focus, on the other hand, is a third question Harder said receives little attention: how to capture the public benefits of A.I. and clear the red tape that stands in the way. He cited drug discovery as a place where the technology could dramatically increase the number of promising candidates.

“We could dramatically increase the number of new drug candidates that are promising to cure cancer and Alzheimer’s,” Harder said, explaining that clinical trial reform is necessary to maximize impact. “It’s not going to do us any good if we don’t actually fit our system of how to test and make sure that those drugs are safe, and to try to make sure that they’re getting affordably in the hands of patients across the world.”

He said that there is a significant resource gap between the National Institutes of Health’s computing power and that of companies like Anthropic and OpenAI, even though there is broad agreement that medical research is an area where the best technology should be applied. The agenda includes proposals to expand shared computing and technological resources for researchers and to make federal scientific data usable by A.I. Most federal data, according to the agenda, is not maintained in a standardized or accessible way, making it difficult to share or analyze with A.I.

On education, Harder said he sees both risk and promise with the emergence of A.I. systems. Cheating is prolific, he said, but if we can find a balance where students are doing most of the cognitive work, A.I. could be a powerful tool for learning and teaching. The federal role, as Harder sees it, is to fine-tune the incentives and then develop and study a feedback loop so that what works can be scaled. The agenda would direct existing federal education research programs to evaluate A.I. tools so that districts do not have to do it themselves.

An ambitious agenda, a dysfunctional government

Asked whether the agenda can move in the next year and a half, Harder acknowledged that some proposals push the Overton window, which he said was intentional.

“We wanted to put some ambitious ideas here that would stretch our minds of what we can actually do,” he said.

None of the agenda’s proposals, Harder argued, depend on one party having control over both legislative chambers and the White House, pointing to ROAD as proof that bipartisan efforts can still pay off.

“All of it can get done, even in a divided government,” he said. “Even when President Trump is in the White House, and I think we’re going to have a Democratic House, if not a Democratic Senate, after November.”

Harder said there is evidence that many of the agenda’s proposals already have support from legislators on both sides of the aisle. Clinical trial reform has a bipartisan, bicameral bill behind it — the Clinical Trial Modernization Act — and Congress is already moving on reading and literacy reforms.

“None of it requires a perfect alignment of one party or another in some mythical trifecta.”
