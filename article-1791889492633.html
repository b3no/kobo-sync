Trump’s student loan rule doesn’t go far enough

Arbitrary degree requirements devalue higher education

Kelsey Piper
Oct 13
∙
Paid
 
READ IN APP
 

Growing up in a household where both of my parents had graduate degrees, I got one piece of emphatic advice about graduate school: I should only do a program that would pay me, not one that wanted me to pay (with the exceptions of law and medical school). If you’re paying, I was told, you’re the product.

I never did go to grad school.

When I read about the Americans taking on life-ruining amounts of debt to do advanced degree programs from which many of them never graduate, I was struck by how often they are first-generation college students from working-class families — no one told them how to tell whether a graduate program was worth it. The rule my parents gave me would have served them well.

The Trump administration’s changes to federal student loan eligibility rules have been described as part of the president’s broader war on American universities. But as The Argument columnist Matt Yglesias argued in his newsletter, the new regulations prevent the federal government from subsidizing loans for programs that leave graduates financially worse off than they would be without the degree. This helps protect the very students least likely to have the kind of information my parents gave me.

Critics of the move don’t seem to understand that it will actually have very little impact on most undergraduate degrees. In general, studying art history or philosophy at your state’s flagship university will increase your income relative to having only a high school diploma.

And where the regulations will have an impact — mostly on low-end cosmetology programs and some master’s programs in low-earning fields — I’m prepared to argue it will be a good one.

Trump’s new rules apply only in cases where the graduates of a particular school’s degree program do not have a higher income than people without a degree at that level. So if there’s an economic case for the degree, student loans for it will still be available. And when there’s no economic case for the degree, the students are often being exploited to pay the institution’s bills.

Another problem comes when there is an economic case for a questionable degree — but that case is an arbitrary result of bad regulation. And that’s a problem that it will take much more than common sense rules about federal loan subsidies to solve.

A tale of two degrees

In general, the discourse about student loans seems to me to be profoundly confused, suffering from a lack of clarity about what they are and what purpose we want them to serve. Private companies will, of course, give you a loan if they expect you to pay it back. Federal student loans are preferable to private ones because they are cheaper — but this is a government subsidy for education. It’s being offered presumably because the government thinks that education is valuable and would be underprovided by the market.

But in that case, the government should, in fact, make sure both that what it’s subsidizing is valuable and that a loan is a good way to subsidize it. That’s why the Obama administration was also involved in cracking down on student loans to for-profit colleges. Obama officials seemed to understand that, if you can get a huge loan to go to any degree program at all, some unscrupulous schools will try to sign up students who won’t actually benefit from the program in any way, collecting the government’s money and leaving the students in debt.

ProPublica reported in 2016 on one such college, which signed up homeless participants, saddled them with thousands in student loan debt, and advertised programs like “ultrasound tech school” that it didn’t even offer. In other cases, for-profit colleges advised students on how to qualify for aid, including sometimes advising them to lie; the schools didn’t care if students were left with unmanageable debt, having already collected their pay.

Surely we can all get behind cutting this kind of program off from federal student loans. In a case this extreme, it’s clear that banning federal student loans isn’t about devaluing education; it’s about ensuring that programs don’t have reason to go out and recruit unqualified students who won’t benefit.

But those tactics are hardly limited to the for-profit sector.

In 2021, The Los Angeles Times released an editorial on a scandal at the University of Southern California (USC), which had adopted “for-profit recruitment” tactics to fill out its master’s in social work program. “Recent USC social work graduates who took out federal loans borrowed a median of $112,000,” the Times wrote. “Half of them were earning $52,000 or less two years later.”

Most of these students never even set foot on USC’s campus; instead, they took online classes that they alleged were much lower quality. (After USC came under fire, the university adopted some changes, including making the two-year master’s program markedly cheaper.)

Should the government loan students $112,000 each to get master of social work degrees from USC? Surely the answer to that question depends on how valuable that program is. Surely we cannot answer it with a blanket insistence that education is always valuable; that may be true, but it doesn’t mean every educational program is worth going into six figures of debt for.

It’s not just government subsidies that give master of social work programs the power to overcharge students. If you want to be a licensed social worker in many states, you have no choice but to get this exact degree, whatever it may cost. In fact, in California you need a master’s degree to even start the process of becoming a licensed clinical social worker.

A fuller reform of American graduate education (and its exorbitant costs) can’t escape the question of whether these occupational licensing requirements are justified in the first place.

Getting a master’s degree doesn’t make you a better teacher

The new federal student loan rules will affect bachelor’s, associate, and masters’ programs alike in the rare cases where a program fails to increase the income of the students who attend (relative to their peers who didn’t pursue any degree at that level). Much of the discussion has focused on programs like masters’ programs in social work or education, and for good reason: We do want social workers and educators! Those jobs are poorly paid, but they’re important. It would be harmful if, as a consequence of student loan reforms, those careers became less accessible.

But there’s a different solution: Advancement in these careers could stop being built around masters’ degrees.

Teachers can make more money by getting a master’s degree. It’s worth more than significant seniority on most teacher pay scales. Many programs report how many of their teachers have a graduate degree. But one large North Carolina study compared students’ test scores before and after their teacher got a master’s degree and found zero effect on student performance. A different report by the Midwestern Higher Education Compact found that “having a graduate degree outside one’s primary teaching area was generally linked to null or negative outcomes for student achievement.” (That said, getting more subject-specific education in the area you teach in — a math teacher getting a math degree, for instance — was worth it.)

Let’s turn back to social work: Since every state has different requirements, you can compare child-welfare outcomes in states that require a master’s degree of social workers with states that only require a bachelor’s degree. One analysis of child-welfare social workers found longer family reunification timelines in states that required a master’s degree — precisely the opposite outcome you would hope for in states with better-qualified professionals.

In another recent study, federal requirements obliged some nursing homes to hire more licensed social workers. But this had no effects on patient care quality.

Rather than pay for teachers to get more credentials, we should probably take a step back and ask: What are we hoping the credentials will accomplish? Surely, with teaching or social work, we want more effective teachers or social workers. But it’s not clear that requiring people to go into staggering amounts of debt makes them even the slightest bit more effective!

The same principle applies to efforts to require an associate degree (or equivalent credential) to run a home daycare. Such rules have been implemented in Washington, D.C., regulating women out of their chosen occupation or requiring them to go back to school and take on significant debt to stay employed. There is no evidence that this requirement improves outcomes for children, it doesn’t seem to improve the kids’ academic performance, and it definitely leads to shortages of care for families that need it. (It is true that providers with college degrees tend to provide slightly higher quality care on average, but this is likely not causal.)

Income may not be the be-all, end-all of a degree’s worth, but that doesn’t mean we should give up on trying to find metrics that shed light on degree value.

To my mind, the standard of evidence to set a degree requirement for entry into a profession (as opposed to setting a requirement to pass specific profession-related training) should be substantial. There should be at least some evidence that getting a degree makes people better at the profession, and the gains should be large enough to be worth it given the fairly extraordinary costs — costs that can also be measured in years of people’s lives they are required to spend on further study.

When it comes to daycare, this standard is not met, so we should not require a degree. We can require skills training that is specifically relevant to providing good care to children (as most states do) without requiring a full two-year college degree program. There’s some evidence that this targeted skills training, unlike a degree requirement, actually improves outcomes.

I’m not being a hypocrite here and proposing low standards for other people while personally holding higher standards. My favorite teacher I’ve ever worked with, the one my kids liked best and learned the most from, did not have a college degree.

In general, college is extremely expensive in both time and money. It will never be accessible to everyone, regardless of the scale of our efforts to expand scholarships and loans. We should continue working to make college accessible, but we should not artificially close off other pathways to good jobs.

Whether or not the government is willing to subsidize their debt, social workers shouldn’t need to go into upwards of $110,000 of debt to pursue their chosen career in the first place.

Education is about more than just income

Critics of the Trump student loan rule argue that income is an impoverished measure of what someone got from a degree. I agree.

I’ve been reading a lot of history books recently; will this increase my income? Unlikely. But I do think that it improves me as a person: It makes me more thoughtful, more intellectually careful, more principled, and more appreciative of the good things in my life. Surely education is a worthy endeavor when it achieves that, even when it doesn’t achieve increases in income.

But I think that rolling back a lot of our credentialing requirements strengthens the case for education as a source of personal meaning and civic strength, rather than weakening it. If you need a master’s degree in order to get a raise or in order to work in your chosen field at all, everyone in a degree program will be there to knock this requirement out of the way, not to broaden their minds and expand their horizons.

There might be less demand for a master’s in social work or education program if it were not a requirement in order to enter the field, but I suspect there would be better demand: people pursuing that study out of intellectual curiosity and a desire to advance the frontiers of human knowledge. To defend education as being about more than income, we have to stop mechanically herding huge numbers of people through increasing years of postgraduate education that they don’t want or benefit from. We have to think, instead, about what we have to offer them besides one more credential.
