Jensen Huang Scandal Confirms: Everyone Is Lying About How Busy They Are at Work

If Jensen Huang had time for at least three affairs, I don’t believe that you’re as swamped as you claim.

Nov 16

Another day, another Jensen Huang affair revealed. As of now, the count is up to three, and we may never know the real number, which some experts believe may be growing exponentially. While many are focused on how this scandal affects the economy, the stock market or Big Tech, I am preoccupied with something else: finally, I have airtight proof that for the ten plus years when I worked in tech, all my coworkers were lying to me about how busy they were.

If anyone in the world should have been too busy to carry on three separate extramarital affairs (plus the tremendous effort of covering up these affairs) it should have been Jensen Huang. He was one of the most powerful men in the world, if not the most powerful. If I had to think of one person in the world who would be least likely to have an available slot on his calendar, I would have said it was Jensen Huang. And yet!

This is personal to me. During my decade plus in tech, I noticed that I had a lot of downtime, but none of my coworkers did. Sometimes, I worried I was missing some important task, about which nobody had informed me. Other times, I wondered if I was just a genius who was freakishly good at my job, but I did occasionally get fired, so that clearly wasn’t true. To add to the confusion, I was gaslit constantly with every work conversation, every piece of small talk, every greeting in passing: everyone but me was extremely busy, and their overloaded schedule, endless to-do lists, and late nights were all they could talk about. What was I missing?

Clearly, I was missing awareness of the massive open secret that everyone (including but not limited to Jensen Huang) is lying about how swamped they are with work.

Was I a perfect employee? Obviously, no. I admit I occasionally procrastinated on a project, but it’s not like I was deliberately avoiding important tasks so I could play Candy Crush at my desk. Typically, I would finish my work and then have nothing to do, at which point I would try to find some way to browse the Internet without people staring at my screen. It’s possible that I could have used this time to read up on industry news or read the company blog (see above: I was not a perfect employee) but if someone spent all day reading industry news I would not consider them “totally slammed,” as my coworkers constantly claimed to be.

My surplus of free time followed me, to different degrees, at different companies. At one of my first jobs, in tech sales, I was asked to mentor a new girl on our team. On her first day, she stayed late—past six PM—plugging away at her computer. Since I was mentoring her, I knew she didn’t have any work to do. We didn’t even have clients yet, she wasn’t allowed to email any prospects until she had shadowed me for a week, and this company was too janky to have HR so she didn’t even have mandatory trainings to complete. And yet she stayed late, doing God knows what, theatrically wiping her brow and complaining about how busy she was. Obviously, this put us in an arms race since I didn’t want to be out-worked by someone on her first day (neglecting the obvious fact that she was doing fuck all) so I too proceeded to pretend to work while actually browsing ASOS. Eventually, I believe we came to some sort of truce or understanding and went home by seven. But I had my eye on that faker.

I worked at another tech company in San Francisco that fashioned itself a literal adult daycare, with various arcade games, dinosaur decorations and a kiddie pool filled with ball pit balls (yes, really.) Naturally, I assumed these things were there because lots of people at the company had free time. Why else would you be playing air hockey at 2 PM? But arcade games weren’t really my thing, so when I had downtime I shopped online. But, after a coworker snitched on me, I got in trouble for doing “non work things” at work while other people were treating the office like a Chuck E Cheese. Sure enough, everyone at this place claimed to be “slammed” all the time, and not only to avoid hanging out with me, although I’m sure that was part of the motivation. Anyway, the next time that coworker materialized behind my desk, I promptly opened Google and typed “How to get rid of snitch coworker.”

I also worked at a big tech company which became the subject of my most-popular personal essay—and I’ll admit this place kept me a bit busier than other places, in part because they required us to have a certain amount of external calls every quarter. Of course, this meant that I was calling clients all the time for no reason at all, including the ones who explicitly told me they only wanted to communicate via email. (My mom witnessed this behavior over Christmas break and referred to me as a “professional nag.”) So yes, I was busy, but was I really? This entire company was full of very busy people doing useless things to create the illusion of being busy. In particular, we were constantly being corralled into rooms to listen to trainings and “talks.” Sometimes, these trainings related to actual products that we sold. Other times, the motivation was a bit more questionable, like when we all had to sit down to listen to this woman named Amy—the star student of the office—give a talk with a Powerpoint presentation about the importance of “just going for it.”

And even at that company, I had downtime. Everyone did. Even Amy, I’m sure. Nobody comes up with the idea of a Powerpoint presentation about “just going for it” without being astronomically bored.

I think I would have been fine with this setup—having lots of free time but pretending to be busy—if I knew everyone else was in the same boat. But no matter where I was, no matter what department or team I occupied, my coworkers talked nonstop about how busy they were. If I asked them what they did last night, they’d say they worked late. If I asked them how things were going, they’d tell me they were busy, slammed, stressed, or some combination of the above. I suspected that many of these people were lying. But I never had confirmation.

Perhaps my biggest frustration with this charade was that it ironically took a lot of mental energy to effectively pretend to look busy, and I wasn’t very good at that. In fact, at multiple companies, I met all quantiative goals but was chastised for “not appearing hungry” or not having the “perception” of someone who was hardworking. This nonsense eventually triggered me to attend a totally bogus conference just to look like I was busy, which ironically took me away from my work and wasted actual working hours (Little industry secret: most conferences are bogus and are only there so people can look busy and tell everyone they attended a conference). Anyway, I think a lot of my coworkers fucked around just as much as I did, and perhaps lied about their workload even more than I did, but they evaded detection because they were better at playing this game.

Many of these people were also probably “busy” in the sense that they had to work on overdrive to try and justify their existence. A while ago, I interviewed a bunch of people in “bullshit jobs” who readily admitted to doing this. At one of my last jobs in tech, I did this myself, after years of learning the hard way that I just had to straight-up lie to avoid being fired. Eventually, I started making my calendar public but keeping the meeting names private, and then adding a bunch of fake meetings to my calendar.

Keep in mind, my “busy” coworkers were all so falsely busy before the advent of AI. It must be a million times worse now, and the lies must be even more obviously ridiculous. Today’s white collar workers are actually having a lot of their work performed by Claude, so what the hell are they doing? How are they backing up the assertion that they’re totally swamped? Maybe they’re not claiming to be busy, which would be a nice change of pace. Maybe everyone is just openly leaving work at 1:30 PM every day because they know everything is on autopilot, and then they get some time back in their days so they can watch those crappy true crime documentaries on Netflix comprised entirely of security camera footage. But I have a feeling this is not the case. I have a feeling people are still performing the whole song and dance about their wildly busy schedules, pretending to be Angelica’s mom in Rugrats always yelling at her assistant Jonathan on the phone.

Anyway, I can rest easy, knowing that my coworkers were all massive liars and weren’t nearly as busy as they claimed, and I can only thank Jensen Huang for confirming this.

All that said, I did want my husband’s perspective, given that he actually manages people in tech, and unlike me, has actually been very successful in his career path. I’m sure when I asked him, “If you wanted to have an affair, would you have the time?” he thought this was some variant of “Would you still love me if I was a worm?” But quickly I explained the purpose of my query. He pointed out that because Jensen Huang had a private plane and flew for business often, shitting where he ate actually made sense from a time management perspective. Although Huang was not under any pressure to make bogus Powerpoint presentations about having a growth mindset and “just going for it,” (although who “went for it” more than Jensen? I’d hear his presentation on the topic!) he probably did have a lot of free time to kill on his private jet, plus lots of downtime in hotels on work trips. Perhaps if Jensen Huang did not have a private plane or so much work travel, he actually would have an overloaded schedule, and perhaps, no time for hanky panky. So maybe my coworkers were telling me the truth after all.

I really hope that’s not true, because I feel better believing that everyone was lying. I like that answer far more. But, alas, there is a secret third explanation: the only reason my coworkers were so busy and stressed was because all their free time was spent having sex with Jensen Huang.
