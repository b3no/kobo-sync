Our culture is way too eager to embrace deception

We desperately need to rebuild an ethic of probity and integrity.

Matthew Yglesias
Oct 20
∙
Paid
 
READ IN APP
 

This post discusses “You Can See Everything” in some detail. If you have somehow avoided spoilers to this point but are also interested in seeing a highly touted three-hour documentary, I would strongly suggest you go see the movie first before reading the article. The upside is that if you haven’t seen the movie and don’t want to or don’t care about spoilers, I think you should understand the article and its argument just fine. Consider yourself spoiler-warned!

At one point in the brilliant new documentary “You Can See Everything,” Nathan Fielder is pressing Elizabeth Holmes on her honesty, and she preposterously proclaims not only that she was honest about everything that happened with Theranos but that she’s never lied, ever, about anything.

In this moment more than any other we can see that she must be a truly sociopathic liar, because nobody would say something that obviously false. And Fielder really presses her: You never told a white lie? You never said you couldn’t make it to a party because you were feeling sick when actually you just didn’t want to go?

She kind of relents at that point, suggesting she didn’t really mean that kind of lie.

Which is striking because her insistence on her own honesty deprives her of the best possible defense of her conduct, namely the clear reality that lots of successful entrepreneurs lie.

Whatever line divides Elizabeth Holmes, a criminal, from any number of successful and celebrated tech C.E.O.s, it cannot actually be that she said things about her product and her company that aren’t true. Just as we’ve all told white lies to get out of awkward social situations, basically everyone in Silicon Valley is telling some whoppers.

In other words, while almost everyone agrees that lying is bad and you shouldn’t do it, it’s also the case that everybody lies. That’s true to the point that the most obviously dishonest thing a person could do, genuinely, is claim to never lie. And yet, despite the universality of this behavior, I think we’ve come to a point where we’re too tolerant of dishonesty — and, even more so, willfully misleading behavior.

Tech entrepreneurs say lots of stuff that isn’t true

I’m wildly more literal-minded and less creative than Fielder or Lance Oppenheim, so if I were to produce something about Holmes, it would be about my obsession with the fact that she was operating in an industry where people are constantly saying things that aren’t true.

I don’t know anything specific about the Alex Gibney documentary on Elon Musk, but it’s by all accounts a very negative portrait of Musk, and I don’t see how any negative portrait of Musk could avoid the fact that he says a lot of things that aren’t true. Musk’s aspirations for self-driving cars, for example, are a notorious cesspool of overpromising and underdelivering:

And yet, it is also true that in 2026, the Tesla self-driving features are extremely impressive. Tesla provides the best vehicle autonomy available in a consumer car product, and it’s second best behind Waymo in terms of developing an autonomous taxi service. It’s not clear to me that Musk is right that his approach to developing this technology will beat the Google/Waymo approach, but it’s also not clear to me that he’s wrong.

Similarly, SpaceX has been consistently behind Musk’s promised timelines. But if somehow Musk had never spoken in public about SpaceX and just released the products, then I think everyone would agree that the rockets work and Starlink works and it’s all very impressive.

As Musk has become an increasingly political figure, his habit of lying has shifted into increasingly political terrain, which makes him seem increasingly villainous.

But Steve Jobs was also a notorious overpromiser who nonetheless really did deliver impressive technology. As a journalist who is aware of the tech industry but doesn’t really cover it professionally, it’s clear that this kind of whopper is an endemic business strategy. I don’t think you’d reasonably say that Apple or SpaceX are fraudulent business enterprises. But it’s also the case that part of how business gets done in the tech field is you need to paint these glorious visions of the triumphant future, and it doesn’t always work out.

This is not to say that Musk’s behavior was identical to Holmes’s — this was actually litigated and a court ruled that a certain amount of “corporate puffery” is an acceptable, non-fraudulent business practice. Musk’s cars and rockets work; Theranos’s product didn’t. But I do think this culture of hype and overstatement is important context for Holmes’s behavior, along with the evident desire not just in the media but among investors to get behind a heroic female founder.

It also made me think about a more recent fraudster, Sam Bankman-Fried, who pulled a con that was if anything more egregiously illegal.

In contrast to Holmes insisting to Fielder that she would never lie about anything, part of how Bankman-Fried tricked people (certainly including me) is that he was pretty open about the fact that he was a bit shady. His infamous explanation to Matt Levine of “yield farming” made smart people (or at least me) trust him more rather than less because it seemed like he wasn’t insulting our intelligence.

Even in retrospect, I find the whole episode extremely vexing because looking back, the Apollo Program for Biodefense that he was trying to get Congress to enact was a really good idea and FTX’s investment in Anthropic would have paid off enormously had Bankman-Fried gotten away with his scam. FTX’s valuation peaked at $32 billion in 2022 and the Anthropic shares it owned would be worth $75 billion today if they hadn’t been sold in a bankruptcy auction.

That’s not to defend Holmes or Bankman-Fried (whose fraud was, if anything, more egregious) but just to emphasize that there’s a wide range of “fake it ’til you make it” practices out there in the business world, some of which are more legal than others and some of which do pay off.

The era of “reality”

The film does not explore any of this wider context. Instead, it dives deeper into questions about the nature of truth.

In the second half of the film, Fielder collaborates with Amanda Seyfried, who portrayed Holmes in a Hulu dramatization of the Theranos story. The film includes both Fielder’s (supposedly) real interactions with the real Seyfried and Seyfried re-enacting conversations that Fielder had with Holmes but could not film because she was in prison at the time. Fielder also confronts Seyfried with the fact that the events portrayed in “The Dropout” are not 100 percent accurate, which makes her a little defensive, but she says it’s just television. This, I think, raises the question of whether the re-enactments that Seyfried and Fielder stage are, in fact, accurate depictions of the conversations they purport to be documenting.

We seem to have a consensus in society that what Holmes did (lying for personal gain) is not okay but that introducing fictional elements into a “based on a true story” miniseries for personal gain is totally fine.

I personally am not so sure about this.

I watched and enjoyed “The Social Network” when it came out, but I think the power of the film is considerably compromised by the fact that in the real world, Mark Zuckerberg and Priscilla Chan have been dating since before TheFacebook launched. It doesn’t change the fact that it’s a well-made movie on a lot of levels, but to me ranking it the 10th best movie of the 21st century (like the New York Times did) or even the best movie of the century (like “The Big Picture” did) is a little bit crazy. “Citizen Kane” is inspired by the life of William Randolph Hearst, but it does not present itself as a documentary about William Randolph Hearst. It’s about Charles Foster Kane, a fictional character.

I think it’s cheating to add weight and significance to your character study by saying it’s about a real person but then add depth to your character study by making up critical facts.

Part of the strangeness of the documentary is that while co-director Lance Oppenheim is a documentary filmmaker, Fielder just isn’t. “Nathan for You” and “The Rehearsal” are unscripted and feature real people rather than actors, but they’re not news shows or documentaries. They’re closer to the spirit of what we’ve come to know as reality television, which is fake — it just doesn’t involve professional actors or screenwriters.

The current president of the United States, of course, was a very successful reality TV personality before he was a politician. And I’m sort of obsessed with the fact that a guy who is transparently a huge liar is often praised by his supporters for authenticity.

On the left, too, people who want to underplay the importance of issue-positioning often talk about the value of authenticity. Clearly the thing that people call “authenticity” is in fact a relevant political skill. But Donald Trump is a critical illustration of the fact that the actual skill here is not being authentic; it’s acting. Seyfried-as-Holmes seems much more authentic — which is to say relaxed, un-mannered, and natural — than Holmes as herself. That’s because Seyfried has been acting professionally for her whole adult life and she’s very good at it. A normal person’s authentic reaction to being asked questions on camera is to be nervous and weird.

Accurate information is important

Probably my worst take ever was that back in 2010 I defended advocates making overstated ridership estimates for high-speed rail projects on the grounds that investing in high-speed rail was a good idea and that the advocates on the other side were also being dishonest so it didn’t really make sense to insist on unilateral disarmament.

The vast majority of people, I think, manage to avoid ever stating “lying is fine” as baldly as I did.

But I also think it’s pretty clear that the vast majority of the people involved in funding political advocacy organizations — on most sides of most issues — have decided that the stakes of politics are so high and their enemies so low and unprincipled that the only way to succeed is primarily by funding propaganda efforts.

Very little priority is given to trying to support truth-seeking inquiry. You can see this in the generally low quality of the work coming out of political advocacy shops. But you can also see it in the overall shape of the staffing. The teams are too big and too heavily weighted toward relatively cheap people with relatively little experience or technical credentials. And these kinds of attitudes spread. Just as the line between “corporate puffery” or a Jobsian “reality distortion field” (his ability to convince people that the impossible was possible) and an outright fraud can be murkier than we might like to think. Movements that start out doing a little light propaganda, deliberate oversimplification, or aggressive spin eventually start putting out commentary that is completely nonsensical.

The MAGA movement reasoning that cutting imports will increase G.D.P. is a particularly egregious big-picture example. And it’s notable that nothing has done more damage to the MAGA political project than the analytical errors behind the war in Iran and Trump’s trade policy. The basic normative core of Trumpism would be in much better shape if the actual policy analysis had been better.

The ongoing fiasco of California high-speed rail is trivial in contrast to the invasion of Iran, but it’s salient to me because that was the specific thing I embarrassed myself defending propaganda about.

The truth is, a high-speed rail network linking San Francisco, San Jose, and Sacramento to Los Angeles with onward connections going to San Diego, Phoenix, and Las Vegas is a perfectly reasonable idea. If such a train existed, lots of people would ride it and there would be ancillary benefits for the smaller markets on the way like Fresno and Modesto. But building trains turns out to be a somewhat complicated engineering project that involves a lot of tradeoffs. A train like this would be useful but not infinitely useful (people fly and it’s fine), so whether it’s worth it actually depends on the technical implementation details. Pushing ahead with furious political momentum and no real plan turned out to be a recipe for a train to nowhere. Not an administration-killing fiasco like the war in Iran, but certainly a huge waste of money that torched the credibility of rail advocacy and left the whole project further from its goal than it was before.

What these things have in common is that public policy questions are genuinely hard.

The bigger and more important the issue you’re working on, the harder they get. You can’t transform the global energy system or rework all of American health care with a lot of propaganda and nonsense. A logic of superficial sloganeering is how you get reasoning like “if exclusionary zoning is bad, then inclusionary zoning must be good” when, if you actually model it properly, inclusionary zoning makes housing shortages worse.

A culture of probity

If Steve Jobs says Apple is never going to make a stylus and then years later they roll out the Apple Pencil, it’s not the biggest deal. Jobs was notorious to Apple-watchers for pulling this kind of thing.

When the first iPhone launched, it had inferior data speeds and there was no App Store. Instead of saying something reasonable like, “this is an incredibly impressive product in a lot of ways that also has some obvious shortcomings that we will iterate on in coming years,” he made a big deal of insisting that actually the data speed was fine and web apps were way better than native applications anyway.

Then lo and behold, he introduces an iPhone with 3G speeds and it’s way more popular.

I think it’s interesting that successful tech companies have managed to create the kind of corporate culture where they bullshit about complicated technical issues (literal rocket science in Musk’s case) while retaining a reasonable degree of internal discipline around the fact that the products have to actually work. Obviously this does generate the occasional Holmes whose products don’t work or investor enthusiasm for something absurd like WeWork. But products that do work keep emerging from this milieu.

With Jobs, the bullshit was at least cabined to a defined sphere of consumer products. Musk, though, is deeply involved in real-world politics while constantly saying untrue things about public affairs in a way that I find deeply disturbing. Sam Altman is not perpetrating a fraud on the public — OpenAI’s products are incredibly impressive — but he also has a terrible record on honesty that I find very disturbing. Anthropic’s claims to be more ethical strike me as credible, but that is possibly the Bankman-Fried version of ethical rather than the “I’m telling you true things” version of ethical.

Holmes herself probably meant well in some sense. She wasn’t running a smash and grab. She didn’t sell her shares and abscond to Malaysia. Had her machine worked, that would’ve been great for humanity. But it didn’t work. She needed more time and money to keep working on it, and the way to do that was to lie — fake it ’til you make it.

Trump’s supporters think that he is basically right about the need to crush the woke left and that the ends fundamentally justify the means on this score. The climate movement thinks their issue is so critically important that they need to tell everyone that a crash decarbonization program can be executed with zero tradeoffs.

As Dan Williams writes, the idea of “speaking truth to power” becomes an excuse for all kinds of intellectuals to skip the part where you just speak the truth — your allies are the noble underdogs, after all.

In the realm of public affairs, I think these attitudes are toxic. They make it extremely hard to actually govern because you create a group of confused people who don’t understand the levers of power they’re trying to operate. You’re propagating a set of norms in which clear, accurate communication is disparaged and it’s hard to adapt to new questions swiftly. You’re also creating a landscape that is friendly to frauds and grifters. As bad as Trump is, there’s something almost more disturbing about the way that JD Vance and Marco Rubio both explained the mechanics of the Trump con perfectly before deciding to become the two leading succession options.

As a work of art, the nesting doll of Nathan Fielder interrogating Amanda Seyfried in character as Elizabeth Holmes about the nature of truth is fascinating. But the extent to which the very top layers of politics and business now operate on this level of mind-bending dishonesty is disturbing.

And the answer, to be clear, is not to say that the bad guys are so vicious and opportunistic that we need to beat them by any means necessary! It’s to try to rebuild an ethic of basic probity, honesty, and factual accuracy from the ground up.
