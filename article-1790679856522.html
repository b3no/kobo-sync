The truth about rich people in America

A new book reveals the vast wealth of America’s banal small-business owners.

On my income tax return, the largest source of income by far derives from the fact that I am the co-owner of a business whose profits “pass through” to me as an individual without being separately taxed at the corporate level.

These kinds of pass-through business entities are increasingly common in the United States, and you see them in many fields. To reach a certain scale, of course, you need to be able to take on more investors, and it would no longer work to organize as a pass-through. But for smaller firms, it’s an advantageous way to organize.

The upshot of this is that on the official balance sheet of the United States, I am an example of a person who is earning a high income primarily thanks to my status as an owner of capital. Wealth begets wealth, and the working man gets screwed.

But of course in the real world, I work at the company I own and my blogging labor is our main product.

This is an idiosyncratic industry, but it turns out to be a pretty typical Rich Guy Scenario.

That’s the message of a great new book, “The Everywhere Millionaire,” by Owen Zidar and Eric Zwick. They look at the roster of American households who have a net worth of more than $5 million — a nice round number that gets you about 4 percent of the population, or about five million households. And of those five million, roughly three million are what the authors call Main Street Millionaires: owners of privately held businesses rather than C.E.O.s or investors in giant corporations.

The average wealth of these Main Street Millionaires is $25 million per household, and because there are millions of them, they collectively hold dramatically more wealth than the billionaires of the Forbes 400. Plus, in absolute terms, way more wealth has been added here than in the Forbes 400, since the wealth of the Main Street Millionaires started so large and the cohort grows rather than being capped.

Much of the book is dedicated to telling the stories of individual Main Street Millionaires.

The publicity for the book says that these stories “suggest the American Dream is not dead and many paths to prosperity remain open today.” And that is definitely one takeaway here — it’s simply not the case that you need to get into a super-elite college and then go found a tech company or work at one of the prestigious companies from the Chetty, Deming, and Friedman study of the Ivy League to become rich. Indeed, if you’re smart and hardworking and really just want to make a lot of money, these prestige pathways may be a bad idea. A very large share of America’s Main Street Millionaires own and operate companies in banal fields; they’re HVAC contractors or beer distributors who out-hustle the competition in a defined geographic area and make a lot of money.

But what I think is more interesting about the book is the somewhat unfortunate implications for politics.

Tax problems everywhere

One issue with this proliferation of highly profitable small businesses is that they’re a tax nightmare. The way the tax code really “wants” to work is for everyone to work for a giant company. Some people earn modest wages and others are highly paid executives, and everyone is taxed on a progressive basis. Then everyone who earns money spends some of it and saves some of it. Money that you save earns a return in the form of capital gains or dividends, and that income is also taxed but at a lower rate than wage income because it’s good for long-term growth to encourage saving. The corporations themselves also pay corporate income tax, and that income is their revenue less their costs.

People owning small businesses messes things up.

One thing is that when your business is small and closely held, the distinction between business expenses and consumption by the business owner becomes a little poorly defined.

On my desk in my office, I have a very nice computer monitor. That’s a perfectly legitimate business expense. But to be totally honest, one reason I have it is that there are tax benefits to reducing the profitability of the business by spending money on making my own working conditions nicer. Of course, this exists in large corporations too — the C.E.O. gets to ride the company jet — but at a smaller company, where the owner is a larger share of the employee base, it’s a bigger issue.

And that’s just looking at legitimate business expenses. I was once at the beach talking to some dads I’d just met while our kids played together in the sand. These guys owned various businesses in specialty trade contracting, and they were bragging happily about all the ridiculous shit they characterize as business expenses on their taxes. This is not legal, but in practice it’s relatively easy to get away with.

Small-business structures are opaque, and you’re not accountable to public shareholders to whom you need to disclose things.

The constraint is that you might get audited, but audit risk for wealthy business owners has been steadily falling. One of the Biden administration’s biggest revenue raisers was to simply invest in tax enforcement, but as soon as Republicans took power again they began aggressively defunding the tax police.

Part of how this happens is that as Zidar and Zwick emphasize, Main Street Millionaires are very politically influential. For starters, they’re rich, and thus exert normal Rich Guy disproportionate influence. But they’re also broadly distributed throughout the country — every district has some. And they read as more politically sympathetic than far-off C.E.O.s or billionaires.

They also wield influence more directly. Around a quarter of the House Ways and Means Committee own privately held businesses versus just 2 or 3 percent of the American population, and you see an even larger share making up state legislatures. The needs and problems of the mass affluent are widely catered to, and they sort of fall into the cracks between general Republican indulgence of rich people and Democrats’ hyper focus on the narrowest possible set of billionaire oligarchs.

A rentier class?

A key point the authors make is that a lot of the fields of endeavor that are most likely to produce Main Street Millionaires are also cesspools of limited entry.

One headline example the authors give is the case of beer distribution, an industry that probably shouldn’t exist at all. The way this works is that when alcohol was re-legalized after Prohibition, states established a “three-tier” system for beer distribution where breweries cannot sell directly to bars or stores. Instead, purchases need to flow through a class of middleman distributors. This pushes up the price of beer by preventing more efficient dealmaking. Because cheap booze is a public health hazard, having inefficiency in this process is hardly a great social tragedy. But in principle, that goal should be accomplished through taxes that generate revenue for public purposes, not through arbitrary featherbedding.

Another big class of Main Street Millionaires are doctors and dentists who own their own medical practices and also lobby for laws to block direct competition from nurse practitioners and dental hygienists.

Auto dealerships are protected by franchising rules and oftentimes by laws preventing car companies from conducting direct sales. The real estate and title insurance businesses are, likewise, intimately tied up in American regulatory arcana in unnecessary and inefficient ways.

That’s not to say these are all bad businesses or to deny that many of the people who’ve found success in these fields work hard and are good at their jobs. But as Republicans thunder against big government and Democrats rage against the rich, remarkably little political attention accrues to these many instances of rent-seeking in highly fragmented industries dominated by Main Street Millionaires rather than remote investors.

Even on more prosaic tax-policy matters, if you’ve ever covered a fight in Congress about the ability of rich people to pass vast fortunes on to their kids with light taxation thanks to various trust structures, step-up in basis, or the ever-shrinking estate tax, you’ll see that of course part of the problem is super-duper rich billionaires. But the lobbying efforts of billionaires are greatly enhanced by the support of the much larger cohort of people with fortunes in the mere tens or hundreds of millions. People who not only lobby Congress more effectively because they are more numerous, but who in the aggregate have more money and really need to be taxed if people want to close the budget deficit, secure Social Security, invest in geopolitical competition with China, and take care of America’s poor.

Secret labor income

The funny thing about this research is that one of the major findings is that America’s wealthy are working harder than it appears at first glance.

The labor share of national income has fallen over time, and this appears to be in part a genuine shift in the national economy with workers earning less and capital owners earning more. But a significant share of it, as Zidar and Zwick discuss, is rich people characterizing labor income as capital for tax purposes.

Hedge fund and private equity managers classifying a large share of their management fees as capital gains is a famous example of this. But there’s also the Gingrich-Edwards loophole, which lets a person characterize the speaking fees they earn as business income and thus avoid the hit from the Medicare payroll tax.

That one is famous because it’s clear that the non-labor nature of the income is totally fake. What’s trickier is when it’s only partially fake. When you own a real business with employees but it’s also a business that you personally work at, it’s normally tax advantageous to characterize your income as overwhelmingly business profits even though clearly some of it is accruing to you in virtue of your work as an employee of the business.

It’s funny because “oh actually Mr. Jones over there isn’t actually just some idle coupon clipper living off business profits, he busts his ass every day of the week to make the company run” sounds to a normal person like you’re complimenting Mr. Jones. But from the standpoint of optimal tax theory, it’s just the opposite: Business income gets favorable treatment because the goal is to avoid discouraging saving too much.

The fact that he’s actually working for a living means that he’s not taxed enough.

The book itself leans more into the descriptive facts about Main Street Millionaires and the profiles of different genres of America’s provincial elites than into the policy weeds.

In lots of respects, it’s an admiring book. But the authors also wrote a piece for the Economic Strategy Group in 2023 about nitty-gritty ideas for business tax reform, and the proposals on offer are not favorable to the Main Street Millionaires. Which is the basic paradox of the topic: If you understand the situation more clearly, your estimate of America’s rich will probably go up in terms of the vague moral economy of social esteem. A lot of rich people in this country are really out there grinding away in non-glamorous businesses, creating tremendous wealth for themselves but also value for their customers and employees.

But the policy upshot is that to address the revenue side of America’s fiscal issues, we need to widen the lens and tackle not just the tiny class of oligarchs but the broader set of millions of banal rich people who receive extraordinarily favorable tax treatment despite being harder to demonize.

To link back to Slow Boring’s broader themes, I think a big question everyone in the progressive camp should ponder is what kind of political regime would be capable of enacting broadly progressive taxation.

Bill Clinton raised much more tax revenue than Barack Obama or Joe Biden, and he did it by taxing a broader swath of rich people while relying on a political coalition that was capable of winning more downscale voters by being considerably more attentive to actual working-class political opinions.

Mike Johnson, talking about the One Big Beautiful Bill Act’s regressive tax cuts, said, “This is not giving tax cuts to millionaires; it’s the opposite. The people in the tax bracket that you’re referring to, many of them are small-business owners.”

The truth is that a lot of millionaires own small businesses. But it’s also true that every time the pendulum swings back to Democrats, various members end up getting cold feet about all kinds of revenue-raising proposals. And it’s almost always because they’re hearing from sympathetic small-business owners when what they had in mind was some kind of tax that only Elon Musk would have to pay. But this broader group of Main Street Millionaires owns a huge share of America’s economic output, and they receive very favorable tax treatment in a way that is not justifiable, even if you don’t think they’re all terrible people.
