Does central planning always end in dictatorship?

Reading The Road to Serfdom in 2026

Jerusalem Demsas
Sep 17
 
READ IN APP
 

Friedrich Hayek’s most famous book argues that Nazism emerged in part from Germany’s interwar embrace of central planning. Composed during World War II, The Road to Serfdom was written as a counterargument to the British Labour Party’s proposal to nationalize large swaths of the economy.

The Labour Party did, in fact, win the 1945 election in a landslide and subsequently did a bunch of central planning: Under Clement Attlee, the postwar nationalization program brought coal mining, railways, electricity, gas, and much of the iron and steel industry into public ownership.

As a prediction, The Road to Serfdom clearly fell flat, in the sense that it did not result in literal serfdom — that is, a system where people are legally bound to a lord who controls all their economic decisions, including the ability to move freely and profit from their own labor. Nor did it even lead to a softer sort of serfdom where people lost all their meaningful economic freedom.

Whatever one thinks of central planning, Britain’s experience is that extensive public ownership can clearly coexist with competitive elections, peaceful changes of government, and successful efforts to liberalize the economy. Conservatives began privatizing steel again in the 1950s, though Labour reversed that in the late 1960s. Under Margaret Thatcher and John Major in the 1980s and 1990s, Britain largely privatized all of these industries.

But while I think Hayek severely undercut his project by hyperbolically comparing central planning to serfdom, he does persuasively argue that control over the economy can easily become control over people’s lives. Labour didn’t just nationalize a few industries in 1947. Attlee’s government also reintroduced controls over hiring to steer workers toward industries it considered essential. This wasn’t just the government owning coal mines; it was the government controlling access to employment.

Employers seeking men aged 18 to 50 and women aged 18 to 40, with some exceptions, had to go through a government employment exchange or agency in order to find hires. There was at least one instance where a company was prosecuted and convicted for hiring a typist without the permission of the Ministry of Labour.

This is a pretty big infringement on freedom! Notably, some of the backlash came from within the Labour Party itself: Rhys Davies, a Labour member of Parliament and former miner, argued that “a pair of handcuffs are no easier to wear because they happen to shine with a Socialist solution” and quipped that his party should “try freedom for a change.”

The insight that central planning can undercut important freedoms is overplayed by conservatives but underplayed by liberals. That said, Hayek missed the many ways that government intervention can enable freedoms as well.

For instance: I didn’t get married for health insurance… but I also didn’t not get married for health insurance.

In early 2025, I decided to start my own media company. The problem was that my wedding wasn’t scheduled until September. So, my then-fiance and I decided to accelerate things and get courthouse-married right away so that I could get on his health insurance.

Debates about economic liberalism tend to focus on the costs that state intervention in the marketplace imposes on people and firms. But just as important is the question of what types of state interventions enable people and firms to exercise true freedom. Universal healthcare and other social welfare programs enable people to move, start new businesses, try to write the next great American novel, or do anything else they might find important.

Amartya Sen and Martha Nussbaum developed versions of what’s called the “capability approach”, which asks what people are actually free to do and be. Legal permission is part of what enables freedoms, but so too are resources and circumstances that make more choices available. You might be legally free to learn how to read, but without access to books or schools, that freedom is worth very little. You might be legally allowed to leave a job, but if you depend on it for medical care, how much is that freedom worth?

My co-host, Matt Yglesias, makes a related point about people who aspire to work in the private market finding that economic regulations can be frustrating barriers to freedom. If you dream of being a chef, but opening a restaurant in your city requires a yearlong permitting process that costs you tens of thousands or hundreds of thousands of dollars, how free are you to pursue your life goals?

The problem, of course, is that invoking freedom doesn’t settle these questions. A health inspection can constrain a restaurant owner while protecting customers; health insurance can expand a worker’s options while requiring resources from others. There are disagreements about the consequences of these policies, but also about which freedoms can be weighed against other goals and which are sacrosanct.
