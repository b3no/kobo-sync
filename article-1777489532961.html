Maps, maps, and more maps

The fight over congressional districts is entering its most consequential chapter yet.

Halina Bennet
Apr 27
∙
Paid
 
READ IN APP
 

The redistricting wars continue to be a dominant storyline this midterm cycle, with Democrats notching up a narrow referendum win in Virginia and Republicans preparing to retaliate with a new map in Florida. Weak polling for President Donald Trump and a broadly favorable national political environment for Democrats means this back and forth almost certainly won’t be decisive no matter what happens. But ongoing events in Virginia underscore that even clear victories can unravel quickly, and the result may hinge as much on courts and overreach as on the maps themselves.

Virginia Democrats win the vote, lose the ruling (for now)

Virginia voters approved a Democratic plan to redraw the state’s congressional map through the 2030 election cycle, which on its face is a significant win. The final tally was close: Roughly 51.5 percent of voters chose “yes,” compared to 48.5 percent of voters who chose “no,” with nearly all ballots counted. Democrats had proposed a new map aimed at flipping four Republican-held House seats, slicing up heavily Democratic districts in the D.C. suburbs and around Richmond and drawing a new district linking Democratic-leaning towns across the center of the state.

The Democratic win is already complicated. On Wednesday, the day after the election, a circuit court judge issued a final order ruling that the Virginia House bill authorizing the referendum was void from the beginning. He found it violated two resolutions of the General Assembly, portions of the state code, and the Virginia Constitution. The judge also permanently enjoined the State Board of Elections from certifying the results or implementing any new map. Virginia Attorney General Jay Jones announced an immediate appeal, and the Virginia Supreme Court is expected to decide whether Democratic lawmakers violated procedural rules in referring the amendment to the ballot. They heard oral arguments on Monday afternoon.

The White House tried to spin the narrow margin as good news for Republicans. White House Deputy Chief of Staff James Blair noted that Virginia went Democratic by 15 points in the 2025 governor’s race and about eight points for attorney general, calling the redistricting margin a “three-point overperformance” of President Trump’s 2024 showing in the state. That reframe is creative, but it’s not incorrect — a polarized electorate in a blue-leaning state still produced a very close result on a ballot measure explicitly framed as a rebuke of Trump.

Regardless, after the Virginia vote, Democrats could tentatively be able to claim gaining 10 seats nationally from mid-decade redistricting, compared with nine for Republicans, if all goes as expected. Even the best-case scenario for Democrats is contingent on the Virginia Supreme Court.

The next redistricting battleground

Florida’s special session begins Tuesday, April 28, and runs through May 1, with congressional redistricting at its center. Florida is set to become the eighth state to redraw its maps ahead of the midterms. Governor Ron DeSantis released his proposed map today, which would give Republicans four extra seats in a neutral political environment. Republicans currently hold 20 of Florida’s 28 districts, and Democrats hold seven.

Today’s national environment is not neutral, though, and in the context of a strong year for Democrats these maps could put solid Republican seats in jeopardy, WLRN reports. “If Republicans are too aggressive in their new map, they risk watering down their own districts, allowing Democrats to win at the polls.”

Several prominent Republicans, including longtime G.O.P. consultant Karl Rove and Representative Daniel Webster, have publicly warned against overreach. House Minority Leader Hakeem Jeffries told NBC News that Democrats will be targeting Florida seats: “Our message to Florida Republicans is, ‘F around and find out.’”

Florida also has a legal wrinkle that other states involved in this redistricting war do not. Its state constitution prohibits maps that “intentionally” favor one party, so Republicans drawing up the proposal must avoid using partisan language. That’s unlike in Texas or Missouri, where legislators openly discussed picking up U.S. House seats. Democrats and advocacy groups will almost certainly sue, and the constitutional prohibition on partisan gerrymandering gives them a stronger legal hook than they had elsewhere.

Trump’s gambit on redistricting was always risky, and it is increasingly looking like a wash or worse for his party. Ari Fleischer, who served as press secretary for George W. Bush, summed up the Republican predicament bluntly after the Virginia vote: “The G.O.P. will now lose net seats across the country. If you’re going to pick a fight, at least win it.”

Florida could still tip the balance back toward Republicans if DeSantis’s map survives legal challenge and actually results in more seats. But the structural numbers are not looking favorable for the G.O.P. right now.

David Scott’s death opens up another House seat

Representative David Scott died on April 22 at age 80. He had represented Georgia’s 13th Congressional District for more than two decades and was the first Black chair of the House Agriculture Committee. His name will remain on the May 19 Democratic primary ballot because it is too late to reprint ballots before early voting begins, but any votes cast for him will not count, CBS reports.

Governor Brian Kemp will call a special election to fill the seat for the remainder of the term. With three other Georgia districts already holding competitive open-seat races, the state now has four congressional contests to watch in 2026.

Georgia’s 13th Congressional District is safely Democratic, so this is more about logistics than anything else, but the special election timeline and crowded primary field will bear watching.

The polling environment

This nationwide redistricting scramble is best understood as a response to a fundamentally bad electoral environment for Republicans, and the polling released this week underscores just how bad it has gotten.

Trump’s approval rating on the economy dropped to 30 percent overall support in an April AP-NORC poll. That’s down from 38 percent in March, with even Republicans showing diminished faith in his leadership. About three-quarters of U.S. adults now describe the economy as “very” or “somewhat” poor, up from about two-thirds in February. That deterioration over a short period was likely driven at least in part by the Iran war’s effect on gas prices and continued market volatility. Trump’s approval rating on Iran — 32 percent — is similarly underwater and has been unchanged since last month, suggesting the conflict is not going to become more politically palatable until it is resolved.

This month’s survey from Echelon Insights (a right-leaning firm, though a methodologically serious one) found that voters disapprove of U.S. military operations against Iran 42 percent to 51 percent.

The Silver Bulletin generic ballot average puts Democrats at roughly a six-point national lead, which translates, Nate Silver and Eli McKown-Dawson write, into projected Democratic advantages in North Carolina, Georgia, and Arizona — all states Trump won in 2024.

If the environment holds, no amount of map-drawing is going to save Republicans from a blue wave. If the gap narrows — as midterm environments sometimes do as Election Day approaches — then the maps might matter a lot.
