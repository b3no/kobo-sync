We won’t stop climate change by talking about it

Confessions of a “climate husher”

The OG climate deniers were crank scientists who attributed climate change entirely to natural variability (or sunspots) instead of humanity’s greenhouse gas emissions. Their dubious work was cited at the highest levels of government, including by the Bush administration’s Council on Environmental Quality and, perhaps most notoriously, by the late U.S. Sen. James Inhofe, who suggested in 2003 that global warming was “the greatest hoax ever perpetrated on the American people.”

That outright rejection of scientific consensus has largely lost in the court of public opinion: Recent surveys show strong majorities of Americans accepting the reality of climate change. With such denial in retreat, today’s climate activists have a new pejorative specifically for the Democratic elected officials and analysts who are conspicuously less outspoken on climate change during the second Trump era: climate hushing.

These so-called hushers are “meekly shying away from righteous and winnable fights against evildoers,” according to Democratic Sen. Sheldon Whitehouse. Party moderates are the canonical hushers, at times directly urging Democrats “don’t say climate change,” as Searchlight Institute did last year. But the ranks of the hushers appear to grow larger by the day: The Washington Post recently reported that the word “climate” has vanished almost entirely from public statements by Sen. Ed Markey, a longtime Green New Deal champion who just prevailed in a tense primary election against a more moderate challenger.

Democrats’ descent into infighting is most often taken as a sign of retreat for the causes of decarbonization and climate resilience. But that need not be the case.

Indeed, there are reasons to think that climate hushing will promote more ambitious and durable climate action than what came before. Unlocking the next wave of U.S. clean energy will require a return to the bipartisan compromise politics that have historically driven the country’s decarbonization. Maximalist climate activism can only harm these efforts.

In an era when voters are laser-focused on cost-of-living concerns and emerging challenges like artificial intelligence, climate hushing may well be the thing that saves the climate.

The long road of denial

After climate advocates waged a fairly straightforward campaign against climate denial during the Bush presidency, the concept underwent a kind of semantic inflation during the Obama years. A “climate denier” became anyone who opposed any plank of the climate movement’s policy agenda. In 2015, the activist Bill McKibben accused President Obama of “climate denial of the status quo sort” for refusing to block oil drilling in the arctic.

This appeared to be part of McKibben’s attempts to punish the Obama administration for prioritizing health care over climate change. “He talked very little about climate,” McKibben wrote bitterly in 2009, “and in the process left the door open for climate deniers to have a field day.”

“It will be painfully easy to tell if President Obama is going to take a serious stab at doing something about climate change in his second term,” McKibben wrote after Obama’s reelection in 2012. “The purest, starkest test he faces will be the proposed Keystone XL pipeline.”

But the expanding definition of denial didn’t stop at pipeline protests. In 2015, historian Naomi Oreskes criticized pro-nuclear climate scientists, calling their support for zero-carbon nuclear power “a new, strange form of denial … one that says that renewable sources can’t meet our energy needs.”

My organization, the Breakthrough Institute, was once lumped in with a “kinder, gentler” climate denial for the crime of arguing that policymakers were overestimating energy efficiency measures’ ability to mitigate carbon emissions.

And so, new battle lines were drawn, between the climate activists who argued that a “climate emergency” demanded a brute-force transition from fossil fuels to 100% renewable energy (defined more or less exclusively as wind, solar, and batteries) and the climate pragmatists who argued comprehensive climate action was dependent on a broader portfolio of clean energy technologies and infrastructure — including nuclear power as well as carbon capture and removal, hydrogen fuels, crops genetically engineered to be climate-resilient, and more.

By conflating public acceptance of mainstream climate science with a much narrower and contested vision of climate policy, climate activists convinced themselves that public opinion supported even the most expensive and sweeping regulatory interventions into the economy and energy system.

As it happened, the activists would win on the rhetoric, while the pragmatists prevailed on the policy.

“There are a lot of threats to our children, grandchildren, and great-grandchildren,” said President Joe Biden in 2023. “[Climate] is the only truly existential threat.” This echoed the increasingly dire framing of the climate movement, including Extinction Rebellion’s assertion that “the climate crisis is the biggest story in history” and Sunrise Movement’s claims that climate change represents “the biggest existential threat we face.”

But the Biden administration stopped short of declaring a presidential climate emergency, as Sunrise demanded. Though it explored some of the climate movement’s more aggressive asks, like banning new oil and gas leases on federal lands and prosecuting an anti-fossil fuel policy agenda through banking regulation, these efforts were stymied by the courts and by Congress.

While Democrats certainly prioritized climate change above other issue areas during the Biden years, the Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act and the Inflation Reduction Act (IRA) delivered investment to a wide range of low-carbon technologies. The upshot was a de facto national climate push that displaced greenhouse gas emissions, not by depriving Americans of fossil fuels, but instead by flooding the zone with next-generation alternatives.

In other words, though the administration flirted with the degrowth paradigm favored by the activist community, it ultimately followed a program of energy abundance in its practical policy push.

Admittedly, the escalating wars on “climate deniers” did help push climate change to the center of Democrats’ policy agenda. It’s hard to imagine climate becoming the main legislative focus of the Biden administration without events like the Sunrise Movement’s 2019 sit-in protest at Rep. Nancy Pelosi’s congressional office.

But given the impracticality of the activists’ vision — and the fact that the general public never cared nearly as much about climate change as the activists claimed — even the historic legislative accomplishment of the IRA proved disappointing to both the public and the activists. Surveys found that most Americans had heard little to nothing of the IRA just before or even after its passage, while many climate activist groups like Friends of the Earth and the Climate Justice Alliance actually opposed the law.

And, of course, neither the rhetorical nor policy victories would secure Democrats sustained political power. After the Republican rout in 2024, the blame game began.

Do we still need to talk about climate change in order to stop it?

About the only thing that moderate and progressive Democrats can agree on these days is that inflation was the number one cause of Trump’s reelection. Cost-of-living issues continue to crowd out concern about climate change to this day.

But it isn’t just that inflation has sucked up all the political oxygen: The conditions that characterized the era of the climate hawk — stagnant energy demand, low interest rates, relative geopolitical tranquility, and the durability of Western center-left political leadership — no longer hold. Instead, the years after the passage of the IRA have been dominated by destabilizing events such as Russia’s protracted assault on Ukraine, Oct. 7, Israel’s violent response in Gaza, and the unprecedented boom in artificial intelligence.

As a result, many on the political left have even criticized the special priority Democrats gave to climate change during the Biden years. And it’s not just moderates.

As the socialist Syracuse University professor Matthew Huber argued in The New York Times earlier this year, “Democrats will surely continue to propose policies calling for jobs and public investment, but it’s not clear why climate should be at the center.” In March, the Sunrise Movement’s Aru Shiney-Ajay announced a “strategic pivot” for the organization, writing “climate action is impossible under authoritarianism.”

And as Heatmap executive editor and The Argument columnist Robinson Meyer observed recently, even many of the progressive climate movement’s erstwhile rising stars — like New York City’s Zohran Mamdani and Los Angeles mayoral hopeful Nithya Raman — have since shifted their focus to urban cost-of-living concerns.

All of this, according to die-hard climate obsessives, is climate hushing, and it’s unacceptable. “To think that any problem can be solved by not talking about it requires magical thinking,” wrote Covering Climate Now’s Mark Hertsgaard.

But how important is talking about climate change, really?

Declining emissions are an outcome achieved by affordable and scalable low-carbon alternatives to fossil fuels. That’s one reason why, historically, decarbonization was not strongly associated with aggressive climate politics. According to a Breakthrough Institute analysis, the fastest decarbonization rates in history actually occurred before the era of climate policy, mostly driven by national transitions to nuclear and hydroelectric power.

More recently, most of the emissions reductions projected after the IRA came from accelerated wind and solar deployment — deployment that was made possible mostly by massive price declines the technologies achieved over the course of decades, starting long before the rise of loud climate activism. It turns out that talking about climate change and making clean energy cheap are two different things.

This dynamic is not just limited to zero-carbon energy sources: When Democrats tried and failed to pass a carbon price in 2009, they hoped U.S. emissions would fall 17% by 2020, a target the country reached anyway. That decarbonization was largely driven by the fracking boom, which climate activists overwhelmingly opposed.

Even in our current era of backlash and “hushing,” most of the substantive climate policy gains of the Biden era have been entrenched, not eliminated. While Republicans in the current Congress significantly curtailed clean energy spending when they passed the One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA), they actually kept most of the IRA’s titles in place. When it comes to major sources of emissions reductions, they only fully repealed the tax credits for solar, wind, and electric vehicles — three of the most commercially mature technologies to benefit from IRA subsidies.

As a result of this selective repeal, about three-quarters of the clean energy capacity supported by the IRA is still expected to come online, according to estimates by the Columbia Center on Global Energy Policy’s Lily Bermel. And the more speculative technologies in greater need of policy support — like next-generation nuclear, carbon capture, and geothermal — now benefit from Republican as well as Democratic support.

As a recent Rhodium Group report projected, U.S. emissions are estimated to decline by between 27% and 41% below 2005 levels by 2040. Yes, that’s a slower decline than was projected immediately after the IRA’s passage — but that was also before the surge in new natural gas capacity needed to meet unexpected data center load growth.

While solar, wind, and battery markets continue to expand healthily, there’s a solidifying consensus that accelerating decarbonization and climate action will require substantial regulatory reforms — for everything from renewables permitting to transmission siting to advanced nuclear reactor licensing. These types of regulatory policy shifts cannot be accomplished by the congressional budget reconciliation maneuvers that led to both the IRA and OBBBA; in other words, they would require the kind of durable, bipartisan compromise for which loud climate activism is a hindrance, not a help.

This kind of cross-partisan policy brokering would mark a reversion to the political mean on energy policy, and not in a bad way. Most of the investments passed under the IRA were just enhancements to programs originally advanced by bipartisan coalitions in prior years and decades. It was these various clean energy programs, after all, that helped push emissions steadily downward, all while climate activists demanded emergency declarations and moratoriums on fracking and nuclear power.

So while loud activism certainly pushed the Biden administration to place a higher priority on climate policy, more of the same could cement polarization on matters of longstanding energy policy consensus. Quieter, sustained bipartisan collaboration on clean energy investment, permitting reform, and other climate imperatives will serve the cause of long-term decarbonization better than noisy, attention-grabbing climate activism.

Among the hushers

To be candid, my colleagues and I at the Breakthrough Institute are longtime climate hushers. As Breakthrough’s founder, Ted Nordhaus, wrote in 2019, “it is likely that a quieter and less sweeping approach to addressing climate change, one that disaggregates the costs of the policy and avoids becoming a rallying point for either climate advocates or their opponents, will prove more effective.”

But climate hushing is, above all, an emergent property of a world that has changed rapidly since the Biden years — and not just because Trump is back in office. Inflation, interest rates, and energy prices remain elevated, artificial intelligence is on the rise, and multiple geopolitical threats are mounting. Even progressives who were once laser-focused on climate change have moved on to focus on Gaza, oligarchy, ICE, or other issues.

That shift, ironically, includes the anti-hushers themselves. In a piece from earlier this summer, University of California Santa Barbara associate professor Leah Stokes argued that political candidates benefit from talking about climate change. But the evidence she cited showed that the climate response is dwarfed by the effects of talking about the economy and health care:

Stokes squared this circle by counseling Democrats to talk about climate change in the context of affordability, air pollution, and jobs. But this is just a tacit admission that public officials should avoid talking about climate change on an object level. The notion that climate progress is best achieved by connecting climate change to more salient, tractable priorities is simply what we climate pragmatists have been saying all along.

The danger lies in the anti-hushers reviving the expressive climate politics of the prior era, ratcheting up the volume on rhetoric without reckoning with the economic and political realities that made their maximalist policy agenda impossible. Simply defeating the hushers in an effort to convince a future Democratic White House to declare a climate emergency would do nothing to manage the climate problem and everything to worsen polarization on the issue.

By contrast, climate hushing can actually serve the cause of climate action. To the extent that climate goals will be achieved, it will largely be as co-benefits to other policy pursuits that even the anti-hushers understand make for better politics: decarbonization as a co-benefit of energy abundance and permitting reform, reduced natural disaster vulnerability as a co-benefit of infrastructure investment, and so on.

Whatever the case, the anti-hushing campaign is an implicit acknowledgment that climate change is no longer the North Star of Democratic politics — no longer the one issue to rule them all. Rather, climate change is one policy priority among many. That’s a positive development.

It will take many decades to build the abundant, low-carbon, resilient societies that climate action demands. Counting on climate change itself to remain priority No. 1 for that duration, amid expected and unexpected shifts in politics and human events, is foolish — no matter how loudly climate activists yell about it.
