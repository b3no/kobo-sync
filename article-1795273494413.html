Find the levers, and pull

Leaders should be in the business of making people’s lives better.

Halina Bennet
Nov 21
∙
Paid
 
READ IN APP
 

Everyone has a list of daily annoyances, recurring irritations that we mostly accept as the cost of life. Jets fly noisily in and out of airports situated near residential areas that were built before planes were invented. Headlights coming the other way on a two-lane road at night are momentarily blinding. I get about a dozen calls a day from unknown or “likely spam” numbers.

These daily annoyances feel somewhat out of the everyman’s control, so we treat them essentially like the weather — facts of life we all share.

It doesn’t have to be this way, though. Every annoyance I mentioned above exists because of a specific rule, agency, or gap in enforcement. And fortunately, each of them is currently in the crosshairs of elected officials who are doing the unglamorous work of finding (and pulling) the levers of change that actually make our lives better.

Robocalls, and filling enforcement gaps

North Carolina Attorney General Jeff Jackson’s office said residents received more than 2.3 billion robocalls in 2024 and that the number has continued to grow year over year.

In August 2025, Jackson launched Operation Robocall Roundup, an initiative of the Anti-Robocall Litigation Task Force — a bipartisan coalition of 51 of the country’s 56 state and territorial attorneys general — to crack down on the illegal routing of robocalls by telecom providers and entities responsible for connecting the automated voice calls to residents’ phone numbers, according to Jackson’s office.

The obvious but impossible enforcement strategy is to chase every person placing the call. That’s a losing game, of course, since the calls are abundant and often originate overseas.

But a robocall must, like any phone call, travel through phone networks to reach its target on the other line. It enters through a provider and gets passed along until it lands on a person’s telephone. The Federal Communications Commission (F.C.C.) requires voice providers (entities that provide voice communications connected to the public switched telephone network) to register in a Robocall Mitigation Database, file a plan for reducing illegal calls on their networks, and comply with government traceback requests. Providers that skip these steps are an open door for robocallers.

Jackson, leading the task force, sent warning letters to 37 voice providers that hadn’t complied with the F.C.C.’s rules. The task force sent notices to 99 downstream providers that accept traffic from those voice providers, putting them on notice of the alleged violations. The F.C.C. concurrently announced it would remove seven of the 37 from the database, effectively cutting them off from the network.

This month, more than a year into the operation, the task force is also urging the F.C.C. to strengthen its database, recommending several changes, including stronger verification and more transparency for consumers. More than 1,400 companies have been removed for noncompliance from the Robocall Mitigation Database since the beginning of 2025.

At What’s Next, a conference of Democrats held in Denver last week, Jackson spoke on the main stage about the tangible ways to deliver for the voters who’ve just loaned the party its trust. Unlike many of the conference’s speakers, Jackson took his time at the podium to give the lawmakers in the crowd a list of concrete issues to tackle, the major one being the need to stop the approximately two billion robocalls Americans get every month.

I, like the rest of the crowd cheering in the room, was enthusiastic about Jackson’s approach.

“Imagine if this next Congress was the one that finally destroyed the scourge of robocalls,” he said. “That is a direct and meaningful quality-of-life upgrade for all of us and keeps a ton of money in pockets of working people and elderly people.”

Airline noise, but who’s listening?

Representative Grace Meng’s constituents in Queens, N.Y., live beneath the departure paths from LaGuardia Airport. According to her office, the noise from air traffic increased significantly after the Federal Aviation Administration established new year-round flight patterns in 2012, one of which was the “TNNIS” climb that had been previously used during the U.S. Open to reduce noise over Arthur Ashe Stadium by rerouting planes over a densely populated residential part of Queens.

“Airplane noise pollution is a persistent quality-of-life issue that residents in Queens and communities across the country shouldn’t be forced to deal with day and night,” Representative Meng, a Democrat, said. “For years, constituents in my communities I represent have been calling for something to be done about the excessive noise they are experiencing.”

Meng reintroduced the Quiet Communities Act in September 2025 to give the responsibility of noise mitigation to the Environmental Protection Agency (E.P.A.). The bill has 21 cosponsors, including one Republican, and frames the issue as environmental, adding an incentive beyond noise, which the bill asserts is a public and physical health issue.

Meng’s lever of choice for mitigating noise is a structural shift. The F.A.A. is responsible for moving aircraft safely and efficiently, as well as for evaluating the effects of noise pollution, which is a built-in conflict of interest for the agency. It wasn’t always this way. The responsibility to evaluate and establish federal noise control efforts was granted to the E.P.A.’s Office of Noise Abatement and Control (ONAC) in 1972 by the Noise Control Act. ONAC was defunded in 1981 by the Reagan administration.

Chuck Elkins, who headed ONAC, is now on the Legal Advisory Council for Quiet Communities, the group advocating for Meng’s bill.

Headlight glare, and setting a standard

Some headlights seem impossibly bright. And by that, I mean that it is impossible to keep your eyes on the road when they are beaming directly at you in the dark.

Modern headlights are, in fact, far brighter than ones of past decades, with some LED lights producing 2,000 to 4,000 lumens compared with about 700 lumens from older halogens. Their light also tends to be cooler, which means that it appears harsher than the yellowy glow of older designs.

Adaptive and matrix lighting systems, “smart headlights,” which steer light away from oncoming traffic, only became legal in the United States in 2022 and are now being incorporated into new car models.

Vehicle lighting is governed by Federal Motor Vehicle Safety Standard 108, administered by the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration (N.H.T.S.A.), which does not set a flat cap on total light output (lumens). The N.H.T.S.A. has, as recently as this summer, acted on individual cases of headlight brightness. In July, it denied Tesla a petition to avoid a recall of thousands of its vehicles that the administration said violated federal brightness limits. Federal standards measure light intensity in candelas (light strength emitted at specific angles) rather than by setting a total lumen ceiling across the entire beam.

Representative Marie Gluesenkamp Perez introduced the LIGHT Safety Act (“Limiting Intense Glare for Highway and Two-lane Safety Act”) in March 2026. The legislation would require the transportation secretary to issue a final rule revising Standard 108 within one year of enactment, setting a maximum allowable brightness standard for low beam headlamps on motor vehicles “that is expressed in lumens or another appropriate (as determined by the Secretary) photometric measurement.”

What the three have in common

These three efforts are examples of the kinds of levers that leaders have found to deal with problems that otherwise suck up people’s time, money, and quality of life. None of these issues are partisan or part of the culture wars; the people harmed by them are numerous and easy to identify, and the solutions officials are pursuing use relatively dull mechanisms.

That dullness is a virtue.

It isn’t flashy to explain a provider registration database, a responsibility shift, or a regulatory standard, so these efforts cut through much of the politicking that voters perceive as distant from their own lives.

If problem-solving of this kind is rare, it’s partly because the incentives are aligned such that a sharp statement on a headline-driving issue is more attention-grabbing than sending a letter to more than three dozen telecom companies. But the people experiencing these annoyances are voters, and their lives will be improved by someone who fixes problems. Those someones can find levers like Jackson’s enforcement strategy, Meng’s agency re-evaluation, and Gluesenkamp Perez’s final rule. At this point, only Jackson’s efforts are showing results. But speaking directly to voters about the problems they face every day — and how they can be solved — is governing at its most basic and can provide tangible payoffs.
