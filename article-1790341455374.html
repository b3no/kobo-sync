Malcolm Gladwell Takes Aim at Guns

“The American Way of Killing” examines America’s extraordinary tolerance for violence.

Ron Charles
Sep 25
 
READ IN APP
 

When I was a child, a boy across the street shot himself in the head with a handgun.

About a decade later, a young man who lived on the street behind us shot himself in the head with a handgun.

In many countries, that would be an extraordinarily high acquaintance with gun deaths. But in many places in America, that’s mercifully low.

At the start of Malcolm Gladwell’s new book, The American Way of Killing, he talks with a Chicago doctor who’s lost 15 “close friends” to guns.

“This does not happen anywhere else in the developed world,” Gladwell writes.

Indeed, the numbers are so staggering — and so familiar — that we don’t really hear them anymore.

Gladwell notes that when he turned 12 in Canada, there were 701 murders in his entire country. That same year there were 1,645 just in New York City.

Why? he asks throughout this brisk, outraged book. Why is America so violent, so deadly, and so resistant to the most obvious solutions?

His answer is a particularly Gladwellian one: “Americans don’t see their problem clearly.”

And so, he sets out, in his typically engaging way, to explain what we’re missing. It’s a quick tour of American culture and history that feels more revelatory than it really is.

Provocatively, he starts by accusing gun control advocates of being “dishonest.” Their “single-minded focus” on mass shootings and certain types of assault rifles, he claims, ignores innumerable exceptions and the far deadlier toll of handguns. “This is not gun control. This is gun control theater.”

Sigh.

Well, yes and no. Rather than impugn the honor of these public safety advocates, why not consider their motives? After all, in a country enthralled by guns, it makes strategic sense to try to restrict some of the scariest and most newsworthy ones.

This glib overconfidence persists, which has the weird effect of making me object even when I agree with him, as I almost always do.

In the next chapter, for instance, Gladwell insists that the Second Amendment was passed to protect the right of Southern militias to keep enslaved people under control.

There’s strong evidence to support this interpretation, but how much stronger his argument would be if he engaged with the broader historical context, including concerns about standing armies and federal control of state militias. Instead, he concludes this chapter by repeating his italicized mantra: “A conversation becomes dishonest when either side avoids the truth because it is too difficult.”

Yes, but a conversation also becomes dishonest when one side avoids acknowledging the complexities of the truth.

I was genuinely fascinated by Gladwell’s discussion of what he calls “The Cornwell Effect.” This arises from the fact that American doctors — like Dr. Edward E. Cornwell III at Howard University — have more experience treating gunshot wounds than doctors elsewhere in the world. As a result, more Americans are surviving gunshot injuries than in the past.

This high survival rate, Gladwell says, creates a “textbook moral hazard” that allows us to discount what would otherwise be the full lethal effects of our gun culture. Why is there such a lack of urgency around tackling the problem of gun violence? “Maybe,” he suggests, “it’s because a very small band of surgeons and nurses and medics have been dutifully cleaning up the mess.”

But that neat conclusion sits uneasily beside the book’s point that America’s gun-death rate is already shockingly high. Would a country that blithely accepts 42,000 gun deaths a year really be more likely to act if it suffered, say, 62,000?

After Sandy Hook, haven’t we demonstrated that we’re inured to any level of violence?

Gladwell’s sharp analysis of Heller (2008) and Bruen (2022) — specious decisions that undermined a wide range of gun regulations — lays out how imperiled we are by a Supreme Court that relies on fanciful history and incompetent grammar to misread the Second Amendment.

And his argument that we focus too much on punishment instead of policing, too much on premeditated murder instead of impulsive violence is deeply persuasive.

But The American Way of Killing doesn’t ultimately get us much closer to understanding why the United States is so willing to tolerate this river of blood every year, year after year.

Gladwell behaves as though the answer lies in our failure to see something he can reveal to us with a flourish, like “The calls are coming from inside the house!” But I’m afraid the answers lie in a knot of political, cultural, and financial pathologies strangling this country. That situation, alas, doesn’t lend itself readily to Gladwell’s emblematic anecdotes or clever aphorisms. Shoot.◆

  • Long Day’s Journey Into Night, starring Jessica Lange and Ed Harris, opens today in select theaters (trailer). The movie is adapted from the autobiographical play by Nobel laureate Eugene O’Neill. While visiting his house in Danville, Calif., I was reminded that O’Neill completed the play in the early 1940s, but left instructions that it not be published or produced until 25 years after his death. His widow, Carlotta, waited three. The play won a Pulitzer Prize after its Broadway premiere in 1956.

A Greek Appetizer

Fans hungering for another novel from Madeline Miller must be satisfied with this amuse-bouche.

On Tuesday, the fabulously popular classicist who published The Song of Achilles and Circe will release a short story titled Mestra. At your local bookstore, you’re likely to see stacks of this tiny $15 hardback near the register along with bookmarks, novelty mugs, and enamel pins (I♥BOOKS).

Mestra is drawn from a creepy Greek myth about King Erysichthon, who’s cursed with insatiable hunger as punishment for chopping down Demeter’s sacred trees.

After consuming everything in his kingdom, Erysichthon finally sells off his own daughter, Mestra, for seven chickens.

But Poseidon takes pity on her and gives her the gift of infinite transformation. Unfortunately, King Erysichthon sees his daughter’s new skill as the basis for an endless series of grifts.

Miller uses the bones of this tale to flesh out a fairy tale of self-destructive devotion and shocking parental exploitation. Mestra gives and gives; her father takes and takes — and finally demands so much more.

Though packaged as a little gift book, Mestra reads like a grisly parable for the children of addicts of all kinds. It also resonates with the debate about adult children cutting off contact with their “toxic” parents.

“You kept him alive a long time,” a friend tells Mestra.

“Too long,” she thinks.

That hits the mark, but Miller’s extraordinary talents for developing characters, atmosphere, and thematic depth are much better suited to a bigger table. Serving this short story as a hardback book draws attention to what a tiny morsel it really is.◆

Underlined

A periodic feature highlighting a passage from something I’m reading.

The celebration of American masculinity at wartime also served to normalize and forgive behaviors that might have been unacceptable or worrying in other periods. After all, how could anyone deny troops a last flirtation before they were shipped off to combat zones? Film critic Pauline Kael, who was in her twenties during the war, remembered that troops internalized this expectation and even “used the techniques they saw in the movies” to flirt with women on the street. “If you were walking down the street and a guy in a uniform tried to stop you and you weren’t interested,” Kael recalled, the serviceman might remind a woman, pointedly, “That’s what we’re fighting for, that’s what we’re giving our lives for.”

From “Watching the Girls Go By: A History of Street Harassment in the United States,” by Molly M. Brookfield (The University of North Carolina Press).◆

Picture This

Despite how wily they are, cats have just one glorious life, not nine.

Young Cat, Old Cat, by Caldecott Honor winner Jillian Tamaki, provides a sweet celebration of the whole arc of feline life.

Each big page offers a short phrase that introduces the youngest readers to descriptive words:

baby cat
hopeful cat...
scaredy-cat

Remarkably, within the tight confines of that structure, Tamaki tells the story of a little girl who gets a new kitten. She watches it grow — and delights in its mercurial moods:

naughty cat
sleepy cat...
queenly cat

As must happen, we finally arrive at “old cat... oh-so-tired cat.” Tamaki’s pictures grow peaceful but never maudlin. A double-page spread of blues shows the girl waving toward the sky, “Goodbye cat.”

The soft images — cat among the stars — are open-ended enough to accommodate many different families’ discussions about death, though possibly not “He went to a farm.”

But the final page moves on: “brand-new cat!”◆

This Week’s Poem

Jill Bialosky is a rare literary figure who works on both sides of the publishing desk. As an executive editor at W.W. Norton, she’s nurtured the work of new and established authors.

But she’s also an accomplished novelist, memoirist, and poet. Her latest collection, Mock Heart, draws poems from her five earlier collections and includes about two dozen new poems, such as the one below.

I love the way this piece moves through Bialosky’s central concerns — family, motherhood, the passage of time — with her abiding poignancy and wit.

The Sprinkler Game

Sophia stands by the hose, barely
containing herself (I’m watching them from my bedroom window),
& crazily barks until my husband turns on the faucet
& waves the hose like a gleeful baton dance
across the grass. She seizes the water stream
with her mouth open like it’s one of the moles she digs up.
Earlier this morning on our daily walk,
our neighbor’s daughter, showing us her diamond ring,
tells us the story of her engagement,
sky luminous, sun beaming,
taking in the scent of wisteria,
still wearing her pajama bottoms, she stops
to pet Sophia smiling at her, tongue out, panting
from her excitement.
“She’s living her best day,” she says.
The diamond in the center of her ring is from her grandmother,
all he had to do, meaning her fiancé, was pick it up from the jeweler
after it was reset. We are living our best day,
I think, now in my bedroom
where the trinkets of the past shape-shift & shine in the daylight
like my neighbor’s daughter’s diamond’s weight of inheritance —
while Sophia yelps outside in her delirium.
But what I really want is to close the blinds,
to give in to melancholy, which even on a glorious summer day
has caught me like a bug. What is it? The spontaneity of youth,
the innocence of desire? — I feel — I feel it all, Wordsworth wrote,
he thought melancholy was a symptom
of the loss of innocence. I think it’s the sheen of an inner life.
And then the pup comes in with her wet face,
shakes her body as if she’s turned on a mixer
so that water drops spew from her coat into the air,
slumps down on my bedroom floor
with her singular humpf,
& wakes me out of my stupor.

From “Mock Heart: New and Selected Poems,” by Jill Bialosky. Copyright © 2026 by Jill Bialosky. Published by arrangement with Alfred A. Knopf, an imprint of The Knopf Doubleday Group, a division of Penguin Random House LLC.◆

Some Rise by Sin

In a previous century, I taught English at a small Christian college where students could be expelled for premarital sex. The 20 or so people in my Practical Criticism course could relate to Shakespeare’s Measure for Measure better than most students in America.

Last night, at the Folger Theatre, I was reminded what a strange, cruel play it is. The plot, remember, is about a puritanical leader who sentences a young man to death for premarital sex, but then secretly offers clemency if the condemned man’s sister will sleep with him.

When Measure for Measure originally appeared in the 1623 First Folio, it was placed among the comedies — it ends with four marriages, after all. But later critics have regarded it as one of Shakespeare’s “problem plays.”

The problem is that Measure for Measure resists any satisfying resolution.

Although everything is ultimately tied up without bloodshed, only one of those marriages is consensual; two are coerced, and the fourth is troublingly ambiguous. Wedding bouquets can’t waft away the scent of vice and hypocrisy.

The Folger production sets the action in the Jim Crow South, but we were sitting less than two miles from the White House. In a country ruled by a party that preaches family values while being led by a man notorious for sexual misconduct, that 1950s framing felt superfluous.◆
