Alaska’s ranked choice voting: Six years of experimenting and its biggest test yet

The system that remade the state’s politics is back on the ballot, and so is a candidate it has both carried and sunk.

Halina Bennet
Sep 9
∙

Alaska is in its sixth year — and third election — of an unusual ranked-choice-voting (R.C.V.) system that was created in a 2020 ballot measure that also included dark-money disclosure rules.

The campaign that boosted it, Alaskans for Better Elections, made three promises about what R.C.V. would deliver:

  1. Eliminate the spoiler effect — when a non-winning candidate pulls votes from another candidate, usually from the same party, causing them to lose an election they may have otherwise won.

  2. Reward candidates who build coalitions rather than cater to their base

  3. Open up primaries to the more than 60 percent of Alaskans who are unaffiliated voters and had been shut out of the former closed-primary system

The measure passed by 50.55 percent to 49.45 percent, a slim margin of 1.10 percentage points, and was first put into play in the 2022 elections — a big one for Senator Lisa Murkowski, but we will get there.

Under Alaska’s current system, most state and federal elections include a nonpartisan top-four primary with a single ballot in which the four highest vote-getters advance to a ranked-choice general election. If a single candidate does not clear 50 percent of the vote on the first tally of the general election, the candidate who placed last is dropped from the contest, and ballots cast for that candidate are redistributed according to voters’ next-ranked choices, with the process repeating until someone has a majority.

The first election under the new system, in 2022, was crucial for Democrats, even if Alaska Democrats had no shot at winning a Senate seat that year. Senator Murkowski — a moderate Republican — was running for a fourth full term after spending years opposing some of President Donald Trump’s moves during his first term and acting as one of the seven G.O.P. senators who voted to convict the then-former president in his second impeachment trial in 2021. In 2020 — just months before R.C.V. landed on the November ballot — Trump had pledged to endorse a Republican challenger to Murkowski in retaliation against her opposition to him during his first term. When it came time to run in 2022, Murkowski received the support of members of her own party, including then-Minority Leader Mitch McConnell, as well as Democrats, including Jeanne Shaheen, and Independent Angus King.

Murkowski’s main opponent was Trump’s pick, Kelly Tshibaka, who Trump called “MAGA all the way” in his endorsement. Tshibaka also gained the support of the Alaska Republican Party. Despite these boosts, Murkowski’s challenger could not overcome the coalition supporting the moderate incumbent. Murkowski won both the first and final rounds of R.C.V., receiving 43.4 percent of the vote to Tshibaka’s 42.6 percent in the first round before coming out in the end with 53.7 percent of the vote over Tshibaka’s 46.3 percent.

Under the old closed-primary system, it is conceivable that a well-funded primary challenger backed by Trump would beat a moderate incumbent Republican. And something like the all-party top-two primaries used in California and Washington would pose a different risk: Murkowski finishing in third place behind a conservative Republican and a conventional Democrat. The unique Alaska top-four system essentially guaranteed that Murkowski would make it to the R.C.V. round and then allowed her to win on the back of second-choice votes from Democrats. Murkowski openly leaned into this strategy, publicly endorsing the moderate Democrat Mary Peltola in her House race.

Peltola, who likewise endorsed Murkowski, pulled out her own win that election after Republicans split their vote between Sarah Palin and Nick Begich. Republicans read her win as spoiler chaos inherent to a top-four R.C.V. system. But that dynamic ran the other way in 2024, when Peltola trailed Begich after the first round of tabulation and couldn’t close the gap, even after redistribution of votes for the other two candidates — an Independent and a Democrat. Her success one cycle and failure in the next was a fast test of R.C.V.’s partisan neutrality.

Beyond one-off races, the state legislature has provided some evidence of the system’s success at creating functional government. After the first R.C.V. cycle in 2022, 17 of the state’s 20 senators formed a bipartisan majority coalition, and similar coalitions formed in both chambers after 2024. The critical thing here in practice is that the Alaska system allows moderate Republicans to avoid being squeezed out. G.O.P. State Senate President Cathy Giessel in 2024 said that though she opposed R.C.V. in 2020, the system has shifted candidates’ focus away from party pandering, which has resulted in more collaborative statewide governance.

As with other flavors of R.C.V., there is a concern about ballots getting thrown out via exhaustion: If all the candidates a voter ranked have been eliminated from the contest, then so, too, is the voter’s ballot. The ballot is then not counted in the final round. A paper published this year by researchers from the University of Illinois at Urbana-Champaign and Cornell Tech found that only four of 52 Alaska elections showed sensitivity to ballot exhaustion, and only one of those — a state House race decided by 0.1 percent — showed a substantial probability that completing the exhausted ballots would have flipped the result. The analysis found that practically speaking, while exhaustion is real, it is rarely outcome-determinative.

The system has been received with mixed popularity. In 2024, a ballot measure to repeal R.C.V. failed by 743 votes. Alaska Republican Party leaders said that repealing R.C.V. is one of their top goals in 2026, and a rematch is on this November’s ballot.

Also on the ballot again in November, of course, is Mary Peltola, who is running for U.S. Senate against three Republican challengers (two of whom, in case you are not aware, are named Dan Sullivan). In this case, R.C.V. could result in vote splitting if confused voters aiming to rank the incumbent Senator Dan Sullivan ahead of the insurgent Republican Dan Sullivan do the opposite. Those votes would still transfer to the intended candidate once the other is eliminated, as long as those voters rank him at all. In that case, exhaustion could take away from the incumbent’s tally.

R.C.V. may matter even more for Peltola’s chances than for incumbent Senator Sullivan’s. Her theory of success is roughly the same as Murkowski’s in 2022, and that bet is a likely reason why the race’s fourth-place candidate — a Democrat — dropped out of the race, presumably to consolidate Democratic support and further splinter the G.O.P. The same dynamic played out in the race for governor, which is currently between three Republicans and one Democrat after a second Democrat suspended his campaign.

Peltola has long highlighted her ability and willingness to work across party lines, including her support for oil and gas projects in the state, and is harnessing anti-Trump sentiment to bring Independents and non-MAGA Republicans into her camp. If those voters and Democrats rank Peltola and/or the other two non-Senator Sullivan candidates instead of the incumbent, the redistribution mechanism could very well result in one of the country’s most contentious races flipping this cycle.

Quick election updates:

  • In Tuesday’s New Hampshire Senate primary, Democratic Representative Chris Pappas and Republican former Senator John E. Sununu each secured their respective party nominations and will face each other in November. Sununu, who has Trump’s endorsement, is proving to be a formidable opponent for the Democrat.

    The state has not elected a Republican to the Senate since 2010, but the New York Times reports that the Democrats are panicking about their odds. Pappas sounded the alarms in a fundraising email sent out on Friday, simply writing, “We’re losing.”

  • In the race to represent Michigan’s Seventh Congressional District, William Lawrence, who co-founded the Sunrise Movement, won a three-way primary for a seat that is considered to be one of the Democrats’ most winnable pickup opportunities. In an example of post-primary moderation, Lawrence let his Democratic Socialists of America membership lapse in the month leading up to his August primary.

    Despite Lawrence’s efforts to distance himself from the label, the National Republican Congressional Committee is zeroing in on it: “Every embarrassing defeat leaves them with a radical socialist nominee, an emptier bank account, and another must-win seat off the board,” Mike Marinella, N.R.C.C. spokesman, told Fox after Lawrence’s victory.


    Read the New York Times coverage for more on the progressive’s race.

  • Speaking of the D.S.A., the Wall Street Journal wrote about the socialists’ “social” push to attract new members. The D.S.A., in other words, has figured out how to give an antisocial generation living in an antisocial moment the socialization they crave, bringing them to socialism along the way.

    “It’s a good way to be social,” one 25-year-old woman said of her activism in an interview with the Journal.

    The D.S.A. hosts coffee meetups, sports games, speed dating, picnics, barbecues, happy hours, and affinity-based meetups.

    “It really fulfills a social function that a church would,” said one longtime member.

  • The Congressional Black Caucus PAC endorsed Dr. Abdul El-Sayed for Senate in Michigan. During the primary, Black members of Congress and voters alike voiced concerns that the candidate was not sufficiently focused on or representative of them.

  • Trump’s super PAC, MAGA Inc., made its first general election contribution, spending $10 million in Texas to support State Attorney General Ken Paxton’s U.S. Senate race against State Representative James Talarico.

    Meanwhile, Talarico is ramping up his outreach to Black voters and finding unlikely allies like Paxton’s former top cop.

  • New data from Gallup found that Democrats have a ten-percentage-point advantage over Republicans in party affiliation, the New York Times reports. Forty-nine percent of Americans identify as or lean Democratic, versus 39 percent who identify as or lean Republican. This is a shift from a four-point Republican edge at the end of 2024.


    The last time Democrats held a comparable advantage was 2008, when Barack Obama won the presidency.
