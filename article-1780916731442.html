The neglected importance of boring methodological disputes

It’s annoyingly hard to find out what’s true.

Matthew Yglesias
Jun 8
∙
Paid
 
READ IN APP
 

If you consider yourself an intelligent person who thinks seriously about policy issues, you probably believe that your views about those issues should be grounded in the facts.

Values are important, obviously, but there’s a reason why policy arguments routinely start with an invocation of factual claims: Wages are stagnating. Inequality is soaring. Profits are on the rise.

These facts don’t prescribe particular solutions, but they orient us toward problems and areas of concern and at least point the way toward solutions.

But I think many people underestimate the extent to which a lot of seemingly straightforward factual issues hinge on weedsy methodological assumptions that are both technical and also subject to manipulation by biased researchers.

Even in situations where the methodological questions are relatively straightforward, a person acting in good faith can easily stumble into problems.

For example, FRED from the Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis is by far the most convenient place to find economic statistical data. And a person poking around on FRED could easily find this chart and therefore conclude that inflation-adjusted median wages were lower in 2014 than they were in 1979.

“Real wages fell slightly in the 35-year period between 1979 and 2014” does not necessarily imply any specific policy response. But it’s a dramatic finding that certainly has policy implications.

It’s not quite that simple, though.

This chart is deflating nominal wages using the Consumer Price Index, which is an unchained index.¹ One virtue of the unchained index is it can be calculated faster, so it continues to be the workhorse for Bureau of Labor Statistics efforts to provide timely information about inflation. But on longer time horizons, there is no advantage to speed, and the unchained nature of the index means that it will increasingly overstate inflation over time.

If you deflate nominal wages with a chained index like the Chained Consumer Price Index or the Personal Consumption Expenditures deflator, then you get a significantly rosier picture.

In this case, I don’t think you’ll find much disagreement among qualified professionals that the chained index is the right way to go. But as a basic approach to life, I don’t think “look up data on FRED” is a bad way to get answers to questions. It just turns out that this time, you might get the wrong answer because of annoying methodological reasons.

By contrast, people will disagree in good faith about Chained C.P.I. versus the P.C.E. deflator. And in this particular case, they say basically the same thing so it’s not important. But there are plenty of questions where the scope for reasonable disagreement is extremely significant.

Many important questions have unclear answers

Here’s a much more substantive issue. Inequality measures in the United States used to be constructed overwhelmingly out of the Census Bureau’s survey data. The way these surveys work, though, is that they are top-coded and don’t measure the actual incomes of very high-income people. So if you’re interested in exploring questions about inequality at the high end, you need to do what Thomas Piketty did and start looking at tax data.

A lot of what you hear about high-end inequality is downstream of the empirical work Piketty has done with Emmanuel Saez and Gabriel Zucman. That work, however, has been contested by Gerald Auten and David Splinter, who say that if analyzed properly, the tax data shows a much smaller rise in the top one percent share of national income — especially of after-tax income.

If you dig into the dispute, there are four areas of disagreement.

The most important is that we know that aggregate income is significantly under-reported to the I.R.S. Piketty and his co-authors estimate under-reporting from I.R.S. audits and then assign that income proportionately to reported income. Auten and Splinter say that’s empirically wrong and that under-reported income is more evenly distributed.

I am pretty sure the latter are correct about this point, but I’m much less confident that I agree with them on the other three, which have to do with allocating retained corporate earnings, attributing government spending, and how to account for the fact that higher-income tax units contain more married couples.

You see similar issues in other areas. As Noah Smith mentions in his recent reconsideration of the neo-Brandeisian movement in the United States, while these people are at heart deeply ideological, they gained credibility from a very real line of empirical research indicating that there had been a broad rise in market concentration and markups.

Probably the most influential paper in this literature is this one by Jan De Loecker and Jan Eeckhout, but there are many others.

After the initial wave of “omg concentration!” papers came out, though, there was a counter-wave of contrary empirical findings. In this case, it is deeply unclear to me which side is correct. As a political matter, I don’t think we should empower people like Barry Lynn and Matt Stoller who clearly don’t even care about the empirical evidence. But the empirical dispute itself is in fact important.

It’s also worth keeping in mind the structural incentives of academia. If concentration and markups aren’t increasing and nobody is saying that they are increasing, then an empirical paper saying “everything is fine” is going to be extremely boring. A smart academic isn’t going to waste time on that. Eventually someone comes along with an interesting finding and that gets published and then it creates a wave of followup papers documenting similar things in different ways. Once those papers are out there, suddenly debunking them becomes interesting. But you can’t really assess the state of knowledge on something like this until both sides of the academic incentive structure have played out.

Similarly, as I mentioned last week there is this ideologically freighted debate happening about trends in American versus European living standards that hinges on the details of how Purchasing Power Parities are calculated.

I think eventually someone will compile detailed evidence on consumption volumes — square feet of housing, number of cars, access to medical care, etc. — and maybe get us to clarity on this. In this case, I think you can probably say that even if Paul Krugman is right about the direct impact on living standards, Mario Draghi is still correct that it’s bad for Europe to not have a competitive high-tech sector for reasons of geopolitics. But it would still be nice to know who’s right!

Descriptive work is under-rated

A related issue is that high-quality descriptive work is pretty under-rated.

Eric Zwick and Owen Zidar have a book coming out this fall called “The Everywhere Millionaire: Who Is Really Rich in America and How They Got There” that I read in galleys and liked a lot.

It’s an unusual book because while it makes points that are policy-relevant, it’s not really a policy book. The main point is simply to document that while the very richest people in America tend to be founders of tech companies or star investors, there is a broader set of rich people who collectively hold a huge share of wealth and political power based on businesses that are pretty banal. They talk about owners of HVAC companies, successful dental practices, mid-sized restaurant groups, beer distributors, auto dealerships, and other boring companies.

You may have heard recently that corporate profit margins are at an all-time high.

Zwick and Zidar have a descriptive finding that doesn’t exactly debunk this but does somewhat change how I think about it.

They point out that a large fraction of rich Americans are like me: They own a small business and are also a key employee of the small business that they own. If you look at our tax returns, Kate and I earn pretty modest salaries. As a result, our employer enjoys incredible profit margins. But our employer is just us! This is tax optimization, not corporate overlords exploiting their workers. Zwick and Zidar say about a third of the fall in labor share is due to this kind of tax consideration rather than real change. So, again, not a debunking but a relevant consideration.

Another descriptive point that I think is underappreciated, even though people are constantly talking about housing, is Kevin Erdmann’s point that the growth in real estate wealth in the United States since 1980 is mostly scarcity-induced rent inflation rather than the result of new investment. We’re just bidding up the price of land because we’re not building enough housing and actually becoming poorer as a result.

There’s a lot of diffuse anxiety that actually taking decisive action to eliminate the scarcity would also eliminate a lot of paper wealth. But people would actually be richer on average.

A lot of trends are fake

One point here is just that it’s harder than it should be to know the truth about things. The internet has put more information than ever at everyone’s fingertips and made it really easy to look things up. But just looking stuff up is actually less useful than you might think. Obtaining knowledge is still difficult.

That’s in part because there are a lot of biases in the system toward publishing and propagating “interesting” findings.

Saloni Dattani had a good roundup recently of major media trends that it turned out were based on methodological issues. The huge rise in maternal mortality that sparked dozens of articles actually just reflected a change in how deaths are counted; nothing new actually happened. Dattani also alerted me to a study indicating that the collapse in interstate mobility in the United States — something I definitely believed was real — is mostly a methodological change.

Share

The belief that vaccines are responsible for the rise in autism in the United States has done a lot of damage to the country, and there is now the risk that R.F.K. Jr. blaming Tylenol for the rise in autism will do even more. What’s especially egregious about this is that there is no rise in autism to explain. It’s not just that the evidence of a link between vaccines and autism is weak — there is no phenomenon to explain at all. This is all about a change in diagnostic criteria.

The thing to keep in mind is that these kinds of errors are overwhelmingly likely to happen in one direction. “Hey, look, a trend!” is an inherently better story than “things are the same.” And there is massive negativity bias not just in what the media reports but crucially in what articles get clicked on and shared. You should expect to see methodological changes get mis-reported as trends primarily when the fake trend is bad.

And in these cases, where the methodological issues are genuinely murky, you should expect to see the negative interpretations receive more attention than the benign ones. Reality is almost always going to be more boring — usually in a good way — than media depictions of it suggest.
