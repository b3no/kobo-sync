The enigma of Trump

A midterm Mailbag

Matthew Yglesias
Nov 6

Well! Election season is a very busy time at our small shop, but reflecting back on the epic seven-hour livestream we did with GD Politics, Silver Bulletin, and The Argument, what really strikes me is the extent to which the wild inferences we drew right at the beginning of the evening from the early ballot drops in Indiana proved accurate.

We were all on there covering our asses and saying you can’t just assume that things that happen in Hamilton County will reflect national trends, but in this case, it turns out they did! It was a ton of fun, and I’m very grateful to everyone for letting me participate. Personally, though, I prefer writing to speaking live on camera, so I’m glad to be back doing election takes here in writing.

David W Lavoie: What is Trump’s play here? Is he really so shallow to say that as long as the border stays secure and the ballroom gets completed, Dems can do what they want?

My dominant answer here is that I have no idea. Donald Trump is such an incredibly overexposed figure that it’s easy to overlook how mysterious he is.

It’s relatively simple to understand, broadly speaking, what Paul Ryan thought was interesting and important about American politics. You could see which things he really emphasized and where he was more flexible. Trump sometimes seems like he doesn’t care about anything and is only interested in money and weird construction projects. But he sometimes seems borderline fanatical about tariffs and immigration. On the other hand, he rarely actually discusses his administration’s immigration policy in any detail. He gambled his presidency on a war with Iran but seems largely disengaged from it. And it’s not like there’s no reporting on this stuff; it’s just that the piling-on of information doesn’t really clarify the picture.

There were various points during Trump’s first term when it seemed like his indolence would lead him to be willing to do bipartisan deals with Democrats on reasonably favorable terms for the center-left. But as time went on, those deals always seemed to collapse and it seemed like he was a faker. But then the CARES Act did come together. And then he did a half-assed coup after he lost the election. But even there: Why? Was there really a lot of tangible upside to that? It was one of the most insane events of American political history, and the motivation behind it — especially given the shambolic nature of the plot — remains fairly obscure.

I did not spend the weeks leading up to the midterms doing dire warnings of Trumpian election theft. But it certainly occurred to me as something that might happen. When the results were in, though, he seemed almost indifferent.

So fair enough, what does Congress have to do with him? But he personally set in motion an incredible amount of redistricting drama in the House. He hand-picked disastrous Senate candidates in Texas and South Carolina. Why do this stuff if he doesn’t care?

I don’t know, and I don’t think we ever will know.

Spencer Roach: Early 2028 speculation: are Vance and Rubio still the most likely Republican POTUS nominees? On the one hand, the midterm results seem to discredit the Trump admin, making an outsider nominee more likely. On the other hand, most Republican primary voters will probably still be loyal to Trump, though it’s possible that the midterm result gives Trump loyalists permission to stop drinking the Kool-Aid.

I’ve said this before and I’ll say it again: My best guess is that a viable outsider candidacy won’t come from an “anti-Trump” figure but from someone I’d call a post-Trump figure.

This is someone who is basically aligned with the administration on domestic issues but says the war in Iran was a mistake, wasn’t personally involved with the administration, and makes some hand-waving claim like Republican turnout was bad because the party establishment abandoned the anti-abortion cause.

I’m not saying that outgoing Tennessee Governor Bill Lee would be the candidate to run that campaign, but it would be someone like Bill Lee — just some normal Republican Party politician who has been in no way “anti-Trump” but isn’t identified with Trump or especially well-known. Someone who will start popping up in New Hampshire diners and on podcasts talking about having implemented a broadly Trumpy (but more aggressive on abortion) agenda in a red state. Someone who doesn’t have a foreign policy record and who has a broadly more professional demeanor.

Weary Land: You mentioned examples of moderate preferences on policy proven at the ballot box. I’ll add that Question 3 in Wisconsin, which would ban anything remotely like affirmative action, passed by nearly 13 pp. That’s a notably higher margin than any statewide Democrat in Wisconsin (who seem to have mostly run behind the national environment). Do you think that elected Democrats “get it”? Do you think they’ll actually stay focused on affordability --- or is the siren’s call of unpopular left wing priorities too strong? Maybe an influx of candidates from swing districts will help?

One of the very first Slow Boring articles was about the failure of an affirmative action referendum in California in November of 2020.

This is one of those issues where I am genuinely uncertain what it is that Democrats think the political situation is. The information is plainly available for people who want to see it. Affirmative action performs terribly when it is placed on the ballot. It polls terribly in surveys. It appears to be the case that Democrats not only have access to this knowledge but that they are tacitly aware of it. I think this post from Bernie Sanders aide Warren Gunnels explaining that the whole progressive platform is supermajority popular is pretty dishonest, but obviously Gunnels is not so dishonest as to claim that affirmative action belongs on the list.

You could run this same play on the moderate side. Shake a moderate Democrat and he’ll tell you the party needs to focus like a laser on kitchen table issues and affordability and eschew politically toxic left-wing ideas like abolishing ICE. That’s fine as far as it goes, but few if any of them are daring enough to take this one on.

In research from David Broockman and Josh Kalla, moving to the center on race-based college admissions has a larger impact on electability than moving to the center on asylum claims or on trans girls playing on school sports teams. But none of the electability-minded moderates who’ve moved to the center on those issues want to move to the center on this other one that’s a bigger deal in electoral terms. And even stranger, none of the activists who are mad about Democrats moving to the center on the sports issue are advising them to pick something else — like affirmative action — that’s more potent instead. It’s basically the Moderation That Dare Not Speak Its Name. I once met a charismatic oyster farmer who was thinking about entering politics as a populist outsider, and I floated to him that this would be a great heterodox-but-populist stance that would be very popular with working-class voters in the whitest state in America, but he did not take me up on that idea and turned out to have some other flaws as a candidate.

Eric: Does this mean the AI build out is dead?

I doubt it. If you ignore all the A.I. aspects of A.I. policy and just focus on the literal aspects of data center construction, here’s my basic model of what’s going on:

You start out at Time One and there is in fact relatively little interest in building data centers. The vast majority of people aren’t thinking about data centers at all, but if you show them a picture of one, they don’t want it near their house. Most politicians are also not thinking about data centers, because nobody is talking about this. The people who are thinking about data centers are a handful of construction contracting companies and building-trades labor unions who like the idea of having jobs. They shop legislation around to give tax breaks for data center construction, arguing plausibly that even net of the tax breaks, these things are strongly positive in fiscal terms. Some states or counties pass laws like that, others don’t, and data centers get built in places where they are politically welcome.

But as demand to build data centers starts ramping up, the political dynamics suddenly change.

It was previously the case that most places didn’t want data centers but also that not that many data centers were getting built. The minority of places that did want them were bidding against each other for the privilege of hosting them. As demand suddenly and unexpectedly soared, the competition dynamic ought to have flipped. Instead of local jurisdictions bidding against each other to offer the best possible tax deal to data center developers, developers ought to have been bidding against each other to offer the best fiscal deal to local jurisdictions. If the ramp up had occurred gradually over a 20-year time span, that could have been a gentle recalibration of the dynamic. Instead, you’re seeing very sudden, earthquake-like shocks that cause some weird political disruptions.

At the end of the day, though, it’s just still the case that data centers — similar to warehouses or shopping malls or any other large non-residential structure — are potentially useful fiscal assets that have something concrete to offer their host jurisdiction. The reason they are getting built, meanwhile, is that there is a lot of money in A.I. It just stands to reason that there is some appropriate flow of that money to local governments and to utility ratepayers that is win-win for everyone and that it will get worked out one way or another.

JM: Do you have thoughts on Mike Johnson’s time as speaker of the house? My impression of the narrative when he became speaker was that he is in over his head and is going to be dog-walked by bomb-throwers in his caucus. But over the last 2 years I feel like we haven’t heard much about him despite the very thin majority which seems like a point in his favor.

My most niche conspiracy theory is that I don’t believe that Mike Johnson exists at all.

You may remember that the whole Republican caucus was in total disarray over leadership selection when suddenly out of nowhere this “Mike Johnson” character emerged as a dark-horse compromise candidate. I had never heard of him until the very moment that happened, and I think the reason is that he’s not a real person. In their moment of despair, Republicans just decided they needed some fake guy to serve as figurehead leader, so they came up with the most generic name imaginable — it’s not even a Louisiana-sounding name, just kind of off-the-shelf white guy. Then they used A.I. to gin up some photos of a generic-looking politician guy, and they’ve been snowing us ever since.

After all, I’ve never met Mike Johnson — have you? As you say, you basically never hear anything about him. There’s no juicy reporting on his inner circle, no fun anecdotes, no sense of how he feels about JD Vance versus Marco Rubio. He’s a total enigma, as if he doesn’t exist at all — because he doesn’t!

Paul G: This question may seem a little tone deaf given that it’s not election-related. But then again, maybe we all need some non-election content right now. One enduring beef I have with pundits is that many of them feel the need (whether personally or commercially) to opine on things they don’t really understand, resulting in weak, uninsightful, or just plain wrong analysis. Are there subjects you avoid writing about because they’re out of your depth, or areas you would like to write about but don’t because you don’t feel qualified to write about them?

Most foreign policy issues fall in this bucket.

I would love to do a piece about how the United States can constructively intervene in the conflict happening in the Tigray region of Ethiopia, but I have no idea. I’ve written a little bit about the civil war in Sudan, but I think my understanding of the U.A.E.’s interest in this is pretty superficial. I’ve read several articles about it, but I didn’t find the articles themselves to be very deep or probing, and I’ve never had the chance to really badger an expert about it and answer my questions to my own satisfaction.

But there’s not a ton of this stuff, because normally when I develop a strong desire to write about something, I set about to try to learn enough about it to write something.

When I first started writing about housing, for example, I had lived my whole life on the East Coast and had no idea about Western water issues. But these issues are very salient in Western housing policy, so I started to get questions about them. I tended to defer on the grounds that I didn’t really have a handle on that whole policy area, but it came up enough over time that I was motivated to learn about it and developed some conclusions.

Now I know that the real water-heads feel that my take on this is too simplistic. But I have the considered view that my take is actually good and correct and what’s happened here is that a community of expertise has grown up around a dumb system and has developed a kind of insular tunnel vision about how dumb it is.

Chris Granner: Matt—have you done an alt-history on “Bernie wins the nomination in 2016?”

I really want a Bernie fan to write one since I genuinely don’t understand what it is that they think would have happened. Like, say Bernie won the nomination and then beat Trump. And let’s be really generous and say this also flips the Senate elections in Wisconsin and Pennsylvania, so Bernie gets a Senate majority. This narrow majority confirms a Supreme Court justice to replace Antonin Scalia, which is a big deal. But what does the rest of the “political revolution” look like in a world where your Senate majority includes not just Joe Manchin but Joe Donnelly, Claire McCaskill, Heidi Heitkamp, and Jon Tester? Is Medicare for All happening?

Bernie’s 2016 campaign platform featured a carbon tax. Would that have happened?

I originally became a Twitter enemy of the Bernie faction mostly just because I spent the 2016 primary pointing out that obviously none of this was on the table and the whole perceived stakes of the primary were fake. It rubbed me the wrong way that the Bernie influencer community wouldn’t admit this even though it was obviously true, just like it rubs me the wrong way now that they won’t admit leftists underperform in electoral terms. In both cases, I don’t think the object-level factual dispute is the most important thing in the world. But these just are not difficult factual questions to judge — there was no path from “people vote differently in the 2016 Democratic Party primary” to “a single-payer health-care system in the style of Canada except with dental and vision benefits.” Beyond my annoyance, though, I would love for someone to spell out how the story of the Political Revolution doesn’t just end in a frustrating congressional term, a wipeout in 2018, and some very painful negotiations with Senate Republicans during Covid in 2020.
