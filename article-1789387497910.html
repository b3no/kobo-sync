Women’s Attraction is “Hardwired” Too

Why do men have a monopoly on being biologically programmed to prefer beautiful people?

Last week, I (as well as all of Twitter) came across a Spectator article called Love Can’t Be Lectured, a manifesto defending the immutable nature of male physical sexual preferences, written by sixty-three-year-old writer Sean Thomas. Credit where it’s due, he opens with a pre-paywall statement guaranteed to rake in the rage-traffic: he will not date women his own age because he is physically incapable of being attracted to them. His type is, quite explicitly, twenty-four-year-old Sadie Sink, who he says looks like a “Pre-Raphaelite nymph at the end of a Narnian winter.”

Anyway, his larger point is that men cannot be shamed out of their hardwired desires no matter how politically incorrect, inconvenient or “implausible for that particular man” they may be, and fair. I have no desire to push Sean Thomas towards any particular woman, his age or otherwise, as he believes body-positive advertisements for lotion are attempting to do. He says:

The advertising industry has spent 20 years trying to make us prefer women who are older, or fatter, or wrinklier – or black or brown and so on…Sexual desire is hard-wired, it is not a passing preference. And humans, especially males, are hard-wired to desire the young, the slim, and, yes, sometimes the fair. As men lust for them, women aspire to be them.

In general, I agree that most of our physical preferences are hardwired, or at the very least, out of our control (although I don’t believe there’s anything particularly biological about a male preference for skinny white women—many men across different cultures disagree on this—although other preferences are likely biological in nature) I also agree that attempting to lecture people out of those preferences is probably a waste of everyone’s time. If you are a man in his sixties, and not a particularly impressive-looking, famous or rich one, who is doomed to be repulsed by anyone over the age of thirty, you will probably suffer more than any woman who fails to trigger your lust. And yes, shaming will not change anything, although I disagree that this was the intention of Dove commercials. Only your own decision to compromise on some of your ideals—a decision almost every non-supermodel or non-billionaire makes when dating—will change your behaviors.

And yes, the world would be a better place if nobody had physical preferences at all, if everyone could simply fall in love with a soul and not care about the aesthetics of the casing. But alas, the preferences are there, and we must contend with them.

All that said, I can’t help but notice that the idea that physical preferences are hardwired—and if you don’t like it, get over it, that’s biology—seems to be limited to men. No matter how implausible these preferences are, no matter how mismatched they are with men’s own looks, they are taken for granted because men are “visual creatures.” Meanwhile, women’s physical preferences are viewed as up for debate, fleeting, frivolous, or perhaps nonexistent.

Of course, plenty of people seek to shame men away from preferring young and beautiful women (see: everyone mocking Sean Thomas) so I’m not arguing that men’s preferences are never questioned, only that they are assumed to be biological and immutable, which to some extent provides an armor against negotiation. Women, on the other hand, do not get to claim such things, with the exception of being hardwired to want a “good provider.” How convenient that our only acceptable hardwired preference is something mostly within men’s control!

I can only assume that this is a very convenient lie which makes everyone feel good about themselves: men can feel good about the fact that they can get any woman as long as they’re successful and have a good personality, never having to question how their own looks stack up to the looks they desire in a partner, while women can pat themselves on the back for being deep and looking past the physical.

But, galaxy brain time, I don’t think this is true at all, even if there are on-average differences between how men and women prioritize looks. Even if women are less visual than men, and evaluate more non-physical traits than men, we have all been fed a steady stream of propaganda to minimize the fact that women have “hardwired” physical preferences at all.

Attraction is amoral is one of the main red pill tenets with which I agree. And to be fair to the original red pill writers, they applied this to women’s attraction, essentially warning men that they could not weasel their way into women’s underpants just by being nice, if they were insufficiently sexually attractive. But as I wrote about in my genderslop review of the 2005 romcom Hitch, our society has been circulating a very appealing but untrue story: that women’s attraction isn’t very physical (or that women never find men physically attractive anyway so they just focus on personality and status) and that any woman who does have high physical standards (even if she’s very attractive herself) must be shamed out of them, unlike her attractive male counterparts (or even her not-so-attractive male counterparts) whose attraction to beautiful women is taken for granted. The entire premise of Hitch, in fact, is that dumpy men are allowed to prefer beautiful women (it’s only natural, after all!) but the aforementioned beautiful women must “have their eyes opened” to see the men’s beautiful souls. As I wrote in that article: why is the onus on women to see the soul?

In fact, up until the launch of Phoebe Maltz Bovy’s book, defending women’s right to be “frumpy but horny,” I have not heard anyone else defend the idea that women—including women who aren’t much to look at—are capable of being shallow just like men, and perhaps won’t get the man of their dreams, but cannot help how they feel. The only people who have come remotely close to this admission are blackpill incels who take it too far, declaring that all women find any man who doesn’t look like Jacob Elordi repulsive. (There is even a Reddit sub dedicated to this theory: r/WomenAreNotIntoMen. Perhaps fitting, they discussed Phoebe’s book.)

There is no need for the pendulum to swing that far, especially given that female attraction is no more a “black pill” than men’s attraction. Yes, women and men are both attracted to very good-looking people, but most of us are not quite that good-looking and will probably marry someone roughly at our own level. If you don’t believe me, just walk around Cleveland for a bit.

But, Phoebe and incels aside, why has everyone else been so eager to believe the myth that men’s physical attraction is an executive order, and women’s physical attraction is an unmoderated 4chan thread?

Perhaps it’s because some straight men cannot fathom women’s physical attraction at all. I suspect this may be a form of “performative heterosexuality,” wherein men claim not to know that objectively attractive men are good-looking, and simply throw their hands up and declare that all men are hideous (it would be gay to notice otherwise!) so clearly women are only interested in other, non-physical things. Straight women, on the other hand, can readily admit that Sydney Sweeney or Indie Navarrette is good-looking, without their heterosexuality coming under threat. Given that women may also feel it’s unseemly to gush about attractive men, we have a society where women and men agree that women can be beautiful, but nobody wants to say men can be handsome.

In fact, men—even handsome men—receive so few compliments on their looks that mentioning their handsomeness at all can be jarring. The 1964 play L'Apollon de Bellac follows a young and timid woman attempting to get ahead in her career, who receives the advice that all she must do is compliment men on their looks. Granted, this play operates under the assumption that men react so strongly to these compliments because they all secretly believe they are handsome. But I think it’s quite the opposite: a lot of moderately-handsome men actually have no idea, because nobody tells them! Male friends don’t gas up each other’s looks (too gay) and women don’t comment on men’s looks (unseemly, sends the wrong message, invites harassment.)

So, why would straight women be discouraged from appreciating good-looking men? Clearly not all straight women. We have Phoebe Maltz Bovy, and a few decades ago we had Salt-N-Pepa. Presumably, the list goes on. But in general, it seems more fashionable, as a straight woman, to say that you find women more attractive than men but oh, unfortunately I’m not into vaginas. Men are certainly discouraged from being predatory about attractive women, but women are discouraged from acknowledging the attractiveness of men at all, unless those men are especially unconventional. You can fawn over Stanley Tucci all you want, but people will side-eye you if you get horny for Glen Powell. In fact, during Brad Pitt’s heyday, the only thing I ever heard women say about him was that he was “too pretty” for them. Yeah, I’m sure you’d kick him out of bed.

So why are women participating in this mass delusion? This conditioning begins in childhood. Look no further than the stories on which children are raised. Both boys’ and girls’ stories depict romance, with a marriage to a desirable partner as the ultimate goal. At least there, we are aligned! But the message is very different depending on if the protagonist is male or female.

A romantically successful male protagonist will typically be some kind of underdog, who performs a great feat to win over a woman who is often both physically and socioeconomically out of his league. His identity as an unlikely hero is why we’re supposed to be on his side. We are never supposed to stop and wonder why he isn’t interested in all the average-looking peasant girls in his village, or why the princess is under any obligation to be interested in him when he’s not interested in his own equals. He is not usually hideous, but his looks are never really appraised at all. (I will give Disney’s Aladdin some credit here, in that Aladdin is poor but handsome enough to make middle-aged women blush in the market and call him “tasty,” even before he dons his Prince Ali fineries.) In any case, the takeaway is usually: be persistent, be brave, be impressive. All worthwhile lessons, of course, but none acknowledge that sometimes (most of the time, in fact) beautiful rich women just date beautiful rich men. And there is no guarantee that the beautiful, rich men won’t also be kind and smart!

Things are different when it comes to media aimed at little girls. The female protagonist is almost always beautiful (sometimes, but not always rich) and lots of men want to date her. Typically, her beauty is her one defining trait, aside from perhaps being able to speak to birds, who are probably also horny for her, because who wouldn’t be? Now, she doesn’t have to save a prince, or do anything in particular to impress a man, other than exist and be genetically blessed. However, her trial is that she must second-guess her own physical preferences in order to obtain a handsome partner. Essentially, she only “deserves” a completely looksmatched husband if she can prove she is willing to date ugly. She must kiss a frog and simply hope that he morphs into an attractive man. She must fall in love with a beast (who, at least at first, isn’t even that nice to her!) and maybe then he will transform into a cute guy. So while the boys are told to be persistent and go after the best women, women are told to put their revulsion aside and date only the kingdom’s most chopped, for the good of…something.

This mirrors the modern-day trope present in nearly every marriage announcement (often between two completely regular-looking people) wherein the man claims the woman was way out of his league and not even particularly interested in him, while he was persistent and eventually she was worn down or began dating him in a sleepwalking kind of way, believing their interactions to be friendly or professional until one day he proposes. Men and women are both complicit in this myth, which often is not really reflected in the physical appearance of the couple. Men enjoy feeling like they scored the princess, women enjoy feeling like they kissed the frog. Kids pick up on their parents and their relatives talking about this dynamic, even subtly. They see their dads gushing over their moms’ beauty (and perhaps the beauty of female celebrities) and their moms never once mentioning the appearance of any man. (No, if a man is attractive to her it’s because he’s “funny” or “kind.”) It becomes ingrained in us that it would be very weird if not morally wrong for a woman to care about looks at all, let alone have a strong preference for conventionally handsome men.

The only children’s movie that attempts to “subvert” this trope is Shrek, which doesn’t even do that. The big twist in Shrek is that Shrek, a grotesque but kindhearted ogre, believes he’s rescuing a princess when the princess is secretly an ogre herself. But in this case, the subversion only goes halfway. The worst-case scenario here for Shrek is dating a woman who is his stone cold looksmatch, not the equivalent of Belle falling for the Beast.

I understand Sean Thomas feels chided by body-positive commercials pushing heavier or older women onto him, but I’d like him to think about the fact that girls have been lectured about having any physical preferences at all, long before we even think about dating. Forget a sixty-three-year-old woman being lectured for only being into twenty-four-year-old male models—she’d be lectured for wanting a man her own age with hair.

It’s true that kids’ movies have been trending less in this direction as of late. One thing I liked about Frozen (2014) was that it contained a relatively conventional romantic plot line without making it all about a woman being forced to ignore her physical preferences or being trapped in any kind of coma (Yes, there is a handsome villain, but Princess Ana is clearly attracted to her love interest, who is just handsome in a different way.) Meanwhile, Elsa’s plot line is so unrelated to men that an entire fandom emerged, dedicated to the idea that she was a queer icon. Thanks, Obama!

But the same trope exists throughout romantic comedies and teen movies, especially those familiar to millennials, Gen X’ers and boomers. Even when the female protagonist is supposed to be shooting out of her league, we discover that actually, if not for her dorky glasses, she’s really hot, so it’s okay! The same metamorphosis does not exist for geeky male protagonists, whose looks are immaterial. When I first saw Austin Powers, I couldn’t help but notice that a female Austin Powers—a goofy-looking British woman with bad teeth and a paunch, wooing an uptight and handsome British spy many years younger—would never have existed, even as a comedy. The only person interested in writing such a movie would be, checks notes, Phoebe Maltz-Bovy.

(Credit to the American Pie series for having the awkward and fumbling Jimbo ultimately fall in love with an equally dorky, looksmatched woman after attempting to chase the sultry foreign exchange student. Again, there are exceptions.)

Many millennials were not raised on this new, more egalitarian, Frozen-esque media. For those of us who were born before the year 2000, we remember the vast majority of media aimed at little girls encouraging us to abandon all our physical preferences (if they even exist!) while never examining whether boys should abandon theirs—and in fact, encouraging them to shoot for the stars, forgetting that average-looking and unattractive girls exist at all. And how could boys abandon their preferences? They are hardwired, after all.

One important caveat here which is often ignored: whether for men or women, “hardwired” does not guarantee that everyone will like the same things. As much as Sean Thomas might insist that all men prefer variations on Sadie Sink, it’s clear that many men diverge in their tastes, and disagreement in taste is not necessarily “virtue signaling.” But, even then, we can make generalizations about the physical traits that men and women find physically attractive. At least when it comes to the body, a slim (but not underweight) physique with some natural curvature and low waist-hip ratio seems to win out on average for men. A similar pattern is generally true for women. Women disagree on things like chest hair or beards, and there truly are women who think Jack Black is the sexiest man alive, but when you pool the preferences of straight women, a slim body with some natural musculature is the majority preference. And while people are more forgiving of physical imperfections and a wider range of traits when they are not conventionally hot themselves, these ideals—slim but fit, slim with curves—are the top-rated for everyone, even overweight people and old people. (Examples from my study below, keep in mind this study was only capable of generating bodies that would exist without cosmetic enhancement or steroids.)

Then, speaking generally, there are some on-average hardwired physical preferences that are equally unfair and implausible: men are attracted to young women no matter how old they get (there are only so many young, attractive women to go around, especially if you are not especially impressive yourself) and women are attracted to tall men, even when they aren’t tall themselves (and the vast majority of men aren’t over six feet tall.)

So when it comes to women’s hardwired preferences, the one that gets the most condemnation is the common preference for tall men, presumably because it’s a standard that men simply cannot reach through effort, much the way a woman cannot will herself to be younger. I’ve even been guilty of this condemnation myself, as a woman who doesn’t care very much about height. I thought that caring a bit less about height was a good dating hack for me, and that maybe it would work for other average-height or short women. I made the critical mistake of assuming that a preference for tall men was chosen and frivolous, unlike a preference for fitness, which felt more “natural.” (This was, honestly, me pulling a Sean Thomas and assuming that only my preferences were hardwired. If I don’t care about height, why should anyone?) So I advised women to lower their height requirements. I thought I was giving good “advice.” After all, if you are only willing to date men over six feet, your dating prospects will dwindle significantly. Being open to average-height or short men—as I’ve always been—will make a big difference in your options!

However, I was ignoring that a preference other than my own (height) was just as valid as my own preferences (fitness.) Do I think it’s a good idea to limit your dating pool to very tall men, or women twenty years your junior, or any other in-demand and relatively rare category of people? No! But if these preferences are hardwired, we can’t shame people out of them, only assume that a desire to find a partner will eventually win out and force them to compromise on their more outlandish ideals. But if a man can be hardwired to only want a rare subset of women who are unlikely to fancy him, why is it impossible to imagine women who also suffer from this biological pathology? A woman’s preference for a tall and strong man makes about as much sense as a man’s preference for a young and gorgeous woman, biologically and evolutionarily speaking. Both are unfair and often not realistic. Yet only one is considered “hardwired.”

Some people engage with this topic in a prescriptive way instead of a descriptive way. They are more concerned with what preferences people “should” have, and less about the neutral reality that people just have physical preferences. And these people, when they are men, argue that men’s hardwired preferences are fine as they are because men ultimately aren’t that picky, but because women are so picky about non-physical traits, something’s gotta give. They see sexual preferences almost like character-building tokens in a video game, where men have pragmatically spent them all on good looks, and women are impractically trying to spend them on everything. They figure that if women have to reallocate their tokens, they may as well completely remove them from the “looks” category, and leave that to the men who have charitably decided to ignore women’s personalities.

But the simple logic of the dating world will sort this out. If a woman is able to find a man who is handsome, tall, fit, successful, kind and interesting, then she will probably date him. If she is unable to find a guy who hits all those checkboxes (more likely) she will either be alone or date someone who doesn’t. Most women will choose the latter. Women are painfully aware that as they get older, finding the perfect man becomes harder and despite Internet memes about picky yet overweight single moms, most women will adjust accordingly. A man refusing to consider a woman’s non-physical attributes doesn’t somehow make him “more reasonable,” but I also don’t think being the most reasonable is the point. If preferences are hardwired and amoral, then that’s that.

And, as it goes, most people wind up dating those who are similar to themselves anyway. Most couples are looksmatched and socioeconomically matched. Upper middle class people tend to date within the upper middle class, even if they were not consciously filtering for income. Beautiful people tend to date other beautiful people. Most married couples are within just a few years of each other’s ages. Mismatched couples happen, and we focus on them because they are so striking, but most people settled into a vague sense of their league and perhaps genuinely start becoming more attracted to people who appear “realistic” for them, no shaming required.

As for Sean Thomas, perhaps Sadie Sink will see the article and give him a call. And if she doesn’t, perhaps Hitch can help.
