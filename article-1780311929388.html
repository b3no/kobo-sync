James Talarico has an obligation to win

Ken Paxton is a terrible candidate, but don’t take anything for granted.

Matthew Yglesias
Jun 1
∙

Ken Paxton defeated incumbent John Cornyn last week to become the G.O.P. nominee for a Texas U.S. Senate seat.

This was one of those races where I wasn’t sure which outcome I was hoping for. Operatives at the Democratic Senatorial Campaign Committee and Senate Majority PAC could hardly contain their enthusiasm for the Paxton win since he is clearly the weaker candidate.

On the other hand, the two candidates are really not the same on the merits.

Cornyn is a conservative Republican. I don’t agree with him on public policy issues and I would not vote for him. But Texas is a conservative state, and it is what it is. Paxton, by contrast, is an election denier who tried to help Donald Trump stay in office after he lost in 2020. Beyond that, Paxton is an actual criminal. He simultaneously claimed three different homes as a primary residence on mortgage documents and, as we’ll see below, that’s not even the main financial scandal that got him in trouble. He’s the kind of Bible thumper who ended up in a messy divorce after it came out that he was cheating on his wife.

But the real scandal there wasn’t the affair so much as the fact that Paxton got his affair partner a job working for a guy named Nate Paul so that she could move to Austin. Paul and Paxton also jointly had a secret Uber account that was used for Paxton’s assignations. And Paul wasn’t doing that just because he’s a nice guy; he was doing it as part of a corrupt scheme with Paxton:

But in 2019, the F.B.I. and U.S. Department of Treasury agents raided Paul’s home and business offices. Additionally, Paul filed for at least 18 bankruptcies. Soon after, Paul’s complicated history with Paxton came to light.

Before the F.B.I. raid, Paul made a $25,000 political donation to Paxton in October 2018. Following the donation, multiple senior aides in Paxton’s office accused the attorney general of using his office to help Paul’s business interests, investigate Paul’s adversaries, and to help settle a lawsuit. In filings, the former aides described Paxton’s motivations as a “bizarre, obsessive use of power.”

If you’re a right-wing attorney general of a red state and your subordinates blow the whistle on you to the F.B.I. because you’ve been helping a criminal in exchange for him facilitating your affair, what’s supposed to happen is you leave office in disgrace.

That almost happened to Paxton, who was impeached by the Texas House of Representatives despite a strong Republican majority.

But Paxton was not only acquitted in the Senate trial: He somehow managed to parlay his scandals into upward political mobility and knock Cornyn off in a primary challenge. This history makes him a weaker opponent for Democrat James Talarico than Cornyn would have been.

The reason I’m not celebrating Cornyn’s defeat, though, is that Paxton’s odds of winning the race are still really good.

I’m worried that Paxton might be a weak candidate in the sense that Ted Cruz ran weak races in 2018 and 2024 against opponents who put up D+5.8 and D+6.6 Wins Above Replacement ratings while still losing. We heard a lot about that 2018 race because it was a strong national year for Democrats, so Beto O’Rourke’s 5.8 point overperformance made for a genuinely close election. Colin Allred’s run six years later was actually even more impressive, but it happened in a bad national political climate for Democrats so the final result wasn’t that close.

My point, though, is that I’m worried James Talarico will do what Beto did and run a good race in a solid year for Democrats and overperform by several points but lose narrowly anyway.

Looking at these kinds of Wins Above Replacement ratings is genuinely important if you want to draw valid conclusions about candidate quality. But this is also a “close only counts in horseshoes and hand grenades” situation. Paxton is genuinely terrible and the important thing about drawing him as an opponent isn’t “Talarico has a shot”; it’s “Talarico has a solemn obligation to the country to actually win this race.”

Texas is a very conservative state

What I mean by all of this is that there is a huge risk to the country of Talarico’s campaign, and the Senate ecosystem more broadly, becoming overly optimistic about beating Paxton just because he’s a corrupt scumbag.

Texas is a very large state. Advertising heavily across its many media markets costs a lot of money. So does vigorous in-person campaigning, which requires a ton of flying around and travel. Talarico has already proven himself to be a formidable grassroots fundraiser, which is great but can also be a trap. The kinds of people who make grassroots contributions to political campaigns are quite progressive — more progressive than the kinds of people who loyally vote for Democrats, who in turn are more progressive than the kinds of people who are swing voters.

And the problem of Texas is that as a Democrat, even if you win all the swing voters, you’re still going to lose the election.

Trump got 52 percent of the vote in Texas in 2020 back when he won less than 47 percent of the national popular vote.

To win, Talarico needs the votes of many, many people who voted for Trump twice and at least some from people who voted for him three times.

And it’s not like there’s an existing permission structure for this. Texas hasn’t elected a Democratic governor since 1990. They haven’t elected a Democratic senator since 1988. In red states, like Ohio and Iowa, Democratic contenders can hope to “win back” people who voted for Obama not all that long ago or to get the votes of young people who’ve never voted for a Democrat but whose parents are Democrats.

Texas does have “ancestral” Democrats like this — mostly Hispanics in South Texas — but it’s not clear that Talarico’s basic persona (white guy from Austin) is at all optimized to win them over. And even if he does win them over, that’s still not enough votes.

The point isn’t to give up hope but just to say that winning here is going to be really hard.

To win, Talarico is going to need to secure the votes of some people who voted for George P. Bush over Paxton in the 2022 attorney general primary and for Cornyn over Paxton earlier this year but have probably never voted for a Democrat in their lives.

Pulling that off will require making people think not only that Paxton sucks but that Talarico is a meaningfully “different kind of Democrat.” That means saying and doing some things that are not popular with small donors and Democratic staffers and elements of the base.

But I think Talarico should take seriously the idea that running a few points ahead of the fundamentals, losing, and having everyone say nice things about him is not meeting the moment.

Right now, Republicans are coming at him with a bunch of pretty lame attacks about how he’s secretly a vegan or something, and I have no doubt he can parry those and put up Beto/Allred numbers. But he can’t let this crook be the guy who puts Republicans over the top to secure a MAGA majority in the Senate. He’s got to try to win.

“The Joe Rogan Experience”

There are a couple of promising early signs. Talarico faced a vigorously contested primary, which meant limited scope for moving to the center. But since winning, he’s gone on Bobby Pulido’s podcast and talked about the importance of the oil and gas industry for Texas and for the United States of America. Oil and gas is a big deal in Texas, and this is also the kind of taboo-breaking that makes it clear to Texas voters that Talarico isn’t just a carbon copy of the Democrats they’ve been voting against their whole lives.

He’s also walked back some of his prior statements about God being nonbinary.

That’s good energy. It’s of course best to avoid the need to flip-flop. But whether it’s Donald Trump disavowing prior efforts to ban abortion or Zohran Mamdani disavowing prior support for defunding the police, flip-flopping is always the second best option. It won’t convince everyone, but it will convince some people. There’s also a bloc of voters who really just need to be convinced that you’re someone who cares about public opinion more than ideological purity and won’t do anything too crazy.

But the main thing that I want to remind everyone of is that the whole reason Talarico rose to the sort of prominence required to mount a Senate campaign was his well-received appearance on “The Joe Rogan Experience.”

Recall the years-long effort to get Democrats to say that Rogan’s views are so objectionable that one was morally obligated to shun his program and eschew his support. That was incredibly dumb. Talarico clearly knows that it’s dumb, and that it’s an opportunity to at some point say something broader about the importance of open-mindedness and how he differs from the more enthusiastic cancelers on the left.

The other thing, though, is that getting Rogan to say nice things about you is genuinely powerful politically. He has an audience that is both large and right-of-center in its political views, but his show also isn’t primarily about politics.

That’s a sweet spot for persuading the kinds of people you need to persuade in order to win in a red state. The specifics of Rogan have gotten collapsed into a kind of generic Democratic conversation about “podcasts” or “men,” but the issue genuinely is Rogan. If you want to get people who’ve never voted for a Democrat before to vote for a Democrat, it’s useful to get some prominent people who are not Democrats to say nice things about you.

Can Talarico get Rogan to do it again? Right after Paxton won, Talarico put out a statement saying that Cornyn supporters “have a place in our campaign.”

That’s a great gesture. The question is what meat can he put on these bones? What further Rogan-esque taboos can he break?

There’ll be a new set of Slow Boring campaign contribution recommendations one of these days, and in the meantime I’m puzzling over the Texas race. But for now, I would really encourage anyone donating to Talarico to do everything they can to make it clear to the campaign that they really want him to win and are going to be mad if he loses, but that they will absolutely not be mad about anything he says or does in pursuit of winning.

Texas in a good national political environment against a terrible opponent is clearly within reach.

But this is not a “terrible opponent” in the sense of totally incompetent. Paxton’s ability to climb the ladder of power while doing crimes is genuinely impressive in much the way that Trump’s ascent has been. Paxton is weak because he’s genuinely incredibly bad, which means it’s incredibly important to actually defeat him and not just beat the spread.
