Plus what happened to college and what’s wrong with Tucker Carlson

Before moving on to an A.I. question I did get for the Mailbag, I want to answer one I didn’t. Tyler Cowen has been challenging A.I. risk worriers to make some investable prognostications about the looming clouds of doom.

I forecast that he will not find this answer satisfactory, but I think the problem here is that the real-world investment advice I have received from safetyists is mostly bullish. Advice I have taken that has paid off well was stuff like “buy Nvidia.” Advice I have not taken that would have paid off well is stuff like “go get a job at Anthropic and enjoy some stock-based compensation.” I know big safety-heads who advise making large investments in out-of-the-money calls on index funds — i.e., bets that the stock market will increase rapidly. The basic safetyist thesis, after all, is:

  1. A.I. capabilities will accelerate in a transformative way.

  2. This has a nontrivial chance of leading to human extinction.

Item (2) is the basis for policy recommendations but in financial market terms (1) is the dominant consideration. Now of course, mathematically whether you think (2) amounts to a “5 percent chance” or an “85 percent chance” makes a difference. But unless you’re a financial professional, the safetyists and the super-optimists are saying the exact same thing — that A.I. is going to be an incredibly powerful, incredibly transformative technology and not just a boost to coders’ productivity or a new way for kids to cheat on their homework. From an investment viewpoint, the opposite of the safetyist view is “it’s all a bubble,” not “A.I. is going to be really good.”

I think it is a little embarrassing for the safetyist view that it is not more clearly distinguished from A.I. optimism. At the same time, I think the optimists have done the world a disservice by insisting on labeling everyone who has safety concerns a “doomer” and then, rather than understanding what the actual belief is, wondering why the “doomers” aren’t behaving like people who believe the world is doomed.

M_McQ: A lot of people are getting concerned about accelerating AI, including me. I think slowing things down with an international agreement is the only way to avoid a race between companies and nations, leading to a variety of bad scenarios. But even if we could convince the administration that this is a problem, won’t an AI slowdown just cause the AI bubble to burst, tanking the economy? Will political incentives just force us to keep racing and hope for the best?

Ignoring the substantive question about A.I. governance, something that I keep seeing in discussions of A.I. and the macroeconomy is a lot of partial equilibrium thinking, where people look at one change while holding the rest of the economy fixed.

Someone will observe that a very large share of recent G.D.P. growth has been data-center construction, do a little basic subtraction, and then say, “Without the data-center construction, G.D.P. growth would be way lower!” This appears to me to have been the logic behind the Trump administration’s politically bizarre decision to come out swinging as the industry’s greatest friend rather than offer people any kind of reassurances around electricity prices or anything else.

But this is not how a dynamic market economy works.

What’s happening is that right now, A.I.-related activities are the highest-reward use of financial capital, so tons of investment funds are flowing into them. That financial capital is being used to buy real resources — land, electricity, chips, skilled labor, etc. — which are being diverted from other purposes. If data-center construction slowed markedly, interest rates would decline. More apartment construction projects would pencil out. Electricians and HVAC technicians who are currently building data centers would build apartments instead. The price of memory and other computer components would go down and people would build more gaming P.C.s and more video game consoles.

It’s important not to make the reverse mistake and portray the economy as purely zero-sum. The reason so many resources are being sucked into A.I. is that investors believe this is the highest R.O.I. use of the financial capital and the real resources that stand behind it. If regulators block data centers, there will be an economic cost. If the investors turn out to be mistaken (a “burst bubble”), we will end up with a worse economic outcome than the one projected by their current optimism. But it’s an exaggeration to believe the resources will just go idle or the economy will tumble into recession.

I’m old enough to remember Paul Krugman and others spending a lot of time during the Great Recession trying to remind people of the opposite. That when inflation is below target and interest rates are low and the Federal Reserve is at the zero bound, resources really do just sit around idle. Under those circumstances, mobilizing unemployed people to go do something is on net beneficial even if the “something” they do is far from optimal. But we left that macroeconomic world behind us a long time ago. Today, inflation has been persistently above the Fed’s 2 percent target. And while interest rates still aren’t particularly high by historical standards, they are definitely higher than they’ve been in the recent past, and the Fed’s policy rate is not at zero.

This generates a lot of aggravation and annoyance and public distress about the cost of living. But it also means the economy has a natural robustness to demand shocks. Blows will be absorbed by interest rates and inflation and the economy will bounce back.

None of this answers the question of exactly what to do about A.I., but it’s just to say that the macroeconomic take on A.I. is that politicians should do the right thing and not worry too much about the short-term economic impact of slowing or regulating A.I. progress.

Then to delve a little bit more into A.I. while also being a bit more speculative, I think the current “race to superintelligence” dynamic at the leading A.I. companies is probably hurting productivity growth. I don’t think it’s widely understood that right now the marquee job in the industry is “use A.I. to build the next great foundation model.” Of course, plenty of people are trying to use the existing A.I. models to build economically useful software applications that they sell to people. But the top priority at Anthropic isn’t “use Claude to revolutionize the tax prep industry.” It’s “use Claude to build a better Claude,” and the same is true at Anthropic’s competitors. That’s the high prestige work. A disproportionate share of the top people and the hardware resources are going to that work.

If you waved a magic wand and made it impossible to ever make a core model that’s better than Astra or Fable, it’s not like A.I. would vanish or all progress on it would cease. But right now the perception among A.I. true believers is that eventually the models will be good enough to achieve recursive self-improvement and an intelligence explosion. If you believe that’s possible, then racing to achieve that outcome is very important, and you dedicate a lot of resources to it. If further progress in core models becomes impossible, then those resources get redeployed to more prosaic questions like how to use Astra to make money by solving economically significant problems.

Mark: I’m YIMBY, you’re YIMBY, but just how far would you go? Would you bulldoze the West Village or Brooklyn Heights if it meant you could build 500,000 units over 8 years? I just watched Ninotchka so....

I always want to be extremely clear about this. If I were in charge of land use in New York City, I would not bulldoze anything. I would not do a big urban-renewal program in underbuilt neighborhoods. There would not be eminent domain of brownstone Brooklyn.

What I would do is make it legal to voluntarily replace older/shorter buildings with taller more modern ones if the owner of the building wants to do that. If you applied a policy like that throughout the city, you’d expect a good number of parcels to get redeveloped. But you would not expect to see whole neighborhoods leveled. I don’t think we really know, ex ante, if a 5 percent or 10 percent increase in the New York City housing stock would satiate demand for living there or make demand to live there even more robust or what. But redevelopment would only continue as long as demand remained strong.

It has to be either that demand is satiated before there is wholesale redevelopment of the city or else that on balance people like the denser version of the city, which is why demand for even more housing remains robust.

Oliver: If you were entirely in charge of candidate selection as happens in European parties, how would you select congressional candidates? Assuming you could use assessment centres, exams, auditions or any tool you liked.

The main thing is I would give significant weight to prior electoral performance. The Democratic Party would run House candidates who were electoral over-performers in the state legislature. It would nominate House over-performers for statewide races. In the short term this would lead to modestly stronger nominees (Angie Craig rather than Peggy Flanagan), but in the medium term it would dramatically shift the incentive structure toward trying to put up strong electoral performances.

Testname: In general, I would be curious to hear if you have any thoughts on the Jason Arday affair (which has taken over my feed for a month now)

I am trying to be a little more mindful about not dipping into over-covered stories where I don’t have anything particularly novel to add or points that nobody else is making.

This story is of interest to people, though, because it touches on the broader question of the politicization of academia and/or the increasing political pressure on academia from the right. I had the privilege to read Neil Gross’s book “What Happened To College?” in galleys and blurb it. It’s now out for sale and I’d really recommend it to anyone who’s interested in the topic. Per the title, this is really about the undergraduate experience rather than faculty or research dynamics. But the thing he shows is that there’s a very significant and largely grassroots political polarization happening on campus. The minority of undergrads with conservative views are increasingly self-sorting into schools they perceive to be conservative-friendly, while progressives engage in the opposite sorting. The most prestigious schools tend to attract students who are both more progressive and more politically engaged than average. And what’s really unfortunate is that he has evidence that a lot of what’s driving the sorting is negative affective polarization — in other words, strong dislike of coexisting with people who you have political disagreements with.

He also shows that this sorting exists not only between campuses but also on a micro-level within majors and clubs. To quote his recent op-ed, “Fraternities and sororities, campus faith-based organizations, pre-professional clubs, and even a cappella groups can all become gathering points for students with broadly similar political outlooks.”

One upshot of this is that prestigious schools with left-wing student populations face nontrivial bottom-up pressure to become more politicized. This is partially balanced in an ecosystem sense by counter-signaling from conservative schools. But there actually isn’t a lot of demand for what you might consider an ideal of an apolitical university.

Separate from Gross’s findings, it strikes me that when I dip in to see Professor Bluesky, a lot of very smart people capable of doing high-level analytic work seem to turn that part of their brain off. Suppose that 98 percent of faculty do an excellent job of maintaining high standards and ideological neutrality in their teaching and research 98 percent of the time. That’s pretty good! These are human institutions and it’s not reasonable to expect flawless judgment in hiring decisions or totally flawless conduct from the typical employee. But then if 98 percent of the deviations from best practices all happen in a leftward direction, that’s actually a massive systemic bias that is going to look really glaring to outsiders. Even among solid researchers working in fields with high standards, I constantly see papers that would be improved by dumping the text into a good L.L.M., asking what a smart Republican would say about this, and then rewriting to at least try to address those concerns. People implicitly write for an audience, and if your implicit audience has no right-of-center people in it, you wind up addressing yourself in a weird way.

Matt Foreman: Do you think Tucker Carlson has real ideological convictions and is trying to win the fight for a nationalist Republican Party, or is he just trying to sabotage the Republican Party for fun? In his convo with Saagar Enjeti, their horseshoe agreement with AES on Israel was predictable. What surprised me was Saagar praising Zohran for “good governance” in disavowing defund, bringing down crime, improving sanitation and investing in infrastructure, with Tucker nodding along. Why compliment your adversary on issues where your party supposedly has the upper hand (unless you are just a wrecker)?

I can’t read Carlson’s mind, but I think it’s true that Mamdani is a controversial figure primarily because of Israel. He’s left wing on cultural issues in a way that Carlson obviously isn’t going to approve of but also in a way that any mayor of New York City would be. When Rudy Giuliani was mayor, he was pro-choice, favored gay rights, and was strongly pro-immigration and favorable to gun control. Michael Bloomberg, Bill de Blasio, and Eric Adams were all left wing in these exact ways and Andrew Cuomo would have been too if he’d beaten Mamdani.

So if you sympathize with Mamdani on Israel, then you have a guy with normal-for-N.Y.C. views on a bunch of stuff that conservatives don’t like who also seems to be leading a successful ongoing decline in crime and may be trying to do some helpful deregulation for small business. Why not say nice things about him, especially if your project is to try to realign the G.O.P. on Israel?

Sean O: How much does the choice of world map projections even matter? Can a map projection be racist and colonialist? And why is there this widespread belief that map projections are some grand, nefarious conspiracy?

It’s pretty clearly true that the Mercator projection gives people a misleading sense of the size of Africa and Greenland. Does this “matter”? Probably not. It’s hard to think of anything that’s taking place in the world that hinges on people’s gut intuitions about the size of Africa’s landmass. Anyone who is interested in geography has seen a globe. I don’t like reflexively calling everything racist or colonialist, and I think there was a lot of lazy content produced in this vein in the 2010s. But in a world where there is no canonically “correct” way to project a 3D Earth onto a 2D plane, the preference for one that makes Europe, the United States, Russia, and Canada look really large does seem plausibly influenced by colonial mentalities.

My eighth-grade geography class had a giant map on the wall with the south oriented up (I forget which projection it used) in order to deliberately disorient the students and remind us that mapping conventions are just conventions. I thought that was a useful pedagogical exercise. Of course, as a media professional I still present normal north-is-up maps because my goal when I present maps is to illustrate things clearly and not to disorient readers.

Joseph Lord-Lieutenant of ORD: Is the concept of a firewall against the far-right to keep it from ever governing still a workable idea? Secondly, given that in Germany the BSW agrees to govern with the AfD, does this serve to prove the horseshoe theory?

The German firewall policy does not make sense. I obviously don’t approve of AfD, the country’s far-right party, on a whole bunch of levels, but as an American the biggest problem here is that I really don’t want AfD suddenly taking over the federal government and radically reorienting German foreign policy in a pro-Russian direction.

At the same time, you can’t just wish AfD and its voters away. Fortunately, Germany has a federal system and normally has coalition governments. The healthiest development would have been to have some coalitions of AfD and C.D.U., the major center-right party, in the eastern states where AfD is stronger. There’s nothing Earth-shatteringly terrible that the junior partner in a coalition government in Mecklenburg-Vorpommern could accomplish. We have strong precedents from Finland, Denmark, and the Netherlands that giving far-right parties the opportunity to participate in coalition governments tends to take some of the anti-establishment sheen off them and induce splits inside the far-right movements. Of course, it doesn’t magically cause tough-on-crime or anti-immigration sentiment to vanish. But it does normalize the functioning of multi-party democracy, restore the normal tendency for the political pendulum to swing from left to right and back again, and avoid lurching around in crisis.

In Saxony-Anhalt there’s now a situation where AfD is going to be extremely empowered without ever having been tested by practical governing responsibility. I’m not really sure what to make of a potential coalition partnership between AfD and BSW, a left-populist party that could be the kingmaker. The two parties’ main points of alignment — anti-immigration and pro-Russian foreign policy — are really issues for the federal government, so, on its face, teaming up in a state government doesn’t make a lot of sense. But to an extent, these kinds of anti-system parties tend to be led by unprincipled adventurers who do weird stuff.
