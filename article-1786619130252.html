Medicare for All should be more like Medicare

A floor for health care, not a ceiling

Aug 13

Yesterday, I mentioned Abdul El-Sayed’s updated messaging on Medicare for All as a promising example of him trying to win over moderate voters. This is also the issue on which I think it makes the most sense for moderate Democrats to broadly embrace what the leftists are saying. But coming together around Medicare for All also means thinking more seriously about what Medicare for All means because you have to go from a slogan to an actual program.

“Medicare for All” is an appealing slogan for two related reasons.

One is that “Medicare” is the name of a program that exists and is already widely used and popular. Taking a popular and widely used thing and giving it to everyone has a kind of obvious appeal that’s lacking in many proposals for sweeping change.

The other is that while most people disagree with most of the values and worldview of secular cosmopolitan liberals, on health care specifically the mass public’s high-level ideological view is basically aligned with progressives’.

Health policy involves a lot of fussy details, and there are plenty of more specific left-wing policy ideas that may be unworkable or at odds with public opinion. But voters fundamentally reject the idea that whether or not sick people get care (or healthy people get preventative treatment) should be left up to the market or depend on ability to pay.

This broad principle, though, greatly under-determines what the policy should actually look like. In the real world, we can’t actually provide unlimited quantities of free on-demand health-care services to everybody, and the fact that this is an appealing moral vision doesn’t change the fact that we can’t do it.

So the question becomes how to instantiate these values in a way that is workable.

Different countries have come up with different answers, and to the extent that the specific proposal that American leftists call “Medicare for All” looks like any of those models, it seems derived from the health-care system of Canada. But going back to the fact that part of the appeal of the slogan is that people are in favor of Medicare, the actually existing health program for senior citizens, it would be smarter for “Medicare for All” to look a lot more like American Medicare.

Canadian Medicare is a ceiling not a floor

Just to keep things weird, the Canadian public health-care system is, in fact, broadly but informally known as “Medicare.”

Governed by the Canada Health Act and subject to meaningful province-by-province differences, it’s a single-payer health insurance system that gives Canadians access to a broad set of covered health services with no co-payments. It differs from “Medicare for All” mostly in that the list of covered services in Canada is narrower than what American leftists propose (no dental care, for example).

What’s most similar about Canadian Medicare and Medicare for All is that Canada has traditionally tried (though there have been some changes to this in the 21st century) to create not just a publicly financed floor on medical care but also a ceiling.

Creating a single-payer structure does not magically eliminate material scarcity, so in Canada you sometimes need to wait to get non-critical medical care.

And a critical feature of the Canadian system is you can’t just pay out of pocket to get covered services faster or to get a more deluxe version. As the Canadian Medical Association explains, that doesn’t mean there are no private clinics; it’s just that the private clinics are supposed to provide non-covered services.

But that still leaves plenty of ambiguities:

There are private clinics in Canada, but they usually focus on services outside of the hospitals / physician / diagnostics priorities set out by the Canada Health Act. For example, cosmetic procedures, physio and athletic therapy, nutrition services, and extra tests not on provincially recommended lists.

Some private clinics also offer a blend of public and private services, where the patient is only billed for the private services. It’s important for people to be aware that under the Canada Health Act, these clinics cannot require a patient to pay for a private service to be able to get a publicly funded service at the same clinic.

There’s still some grey area around which services private clinics are allowed to bill patients for — for example, virtual care provided by physicians. While physicians are part of the focus of the Canada Health Act, the Act was written before video chats and online medical services. Some private clinics say they’re allowed to provide private virtual medical calls and bill the patient or a third party.

If you think back to 2020 and the Democratic candidates standing on various stages being asked whether they want to ban private health insurance, this is what those questions were about.

A major premise of the Canadian health-care system is that people cannot cut the line to avoid the emergence of a two-tier health system. The idea is that if the public system has shortcomings, those shortcomings should be inflicted on everyone so that the whole of Canadian society is incentivized to work toward a collective solution.

This is an interesting idea, but the practical reality is that most Canadians live near the U.S. border, and there are lots of clinics in Detroit, Buffalo, and even Ogdensburg, N.Y., (“just 50 minutes south of Ottawa”) marketing medical services to out-of-pocket Canadian patients.

The existence of the “go to the States” loophole creates a sort of stable equilibrium around this, where Canadians get to uphold the super-egalitarian values of banning private care while in practice people can get private care. I will happily defer to Mark Carney’s political judgement about the wisdom of sticking with this as a pragmatic approach to Canadian problems. But I don’t think this is an attractive model for American health care.

The core progressive intuition about medical care is about floors rather than ceilings. The fact that America is a global outlier on health care reflects our lack of a solid floor; Canada is an outlier in the way it imposes a ceiling. And of course when most people hear “Medicare for All,” they think about American Medicare — a floor, not a ceiling — rather than a Canadian program with the same name.

American (or Australian) Medicare is a better model

American Medicare offers a broad list of medical services to elderly Americans.

Unlike Canadian Medicare, it does feature co-payments and deductibles to manage utilization without recourse to administrative rationing. But also unlike Canadian Medicare, there is no prohibition on providers offering supplemental or competing services. In large metro areas, it’s common to have upscale providers who refuse to accept Medicare’s relatively stringent reimbursement rates and subsist on rich people paying privately. There are also lots of hybrid situations where a provider is taking Medicare for their core services but also offering non-covered add-ons. There are also “Medigap” supplemental private insurance plans.

I won’t claim that every aspect of American Medicare program design is optimal. Some aspects are clearly messed up — for example, we should be adopting site-neutral payments and curbing over-subsidization of Medicare Advantage plans.

But, clearly, part of the premise of “Medicare for All” as a slogan is that people like Medicare. And Medicare is, structurally, intended to be a kind of universal minimum standard of care.

Looking at American Medicare — or any other country’s system for that matter — offers a reminder that there are no definitive answers or end points in running a publicly financed health-care program. There will always be pressure to spend more or be more generous in some areas, and there will always be countervailing pressure to spend less and reduce waste. New stuff is always being invented. Something like corrective vision surgery is not typically considered a core health service that needs to be covered. But if you can cure actual blindness, that is. And if it becomes normalized that we are curing the blind, I think it might come to seem unreasonable to exclude the merely nearsighted.

You could, in a technocratic fantasy, compose a comprehensive and fully accurate cost-benefit analysis list of every conceivable medical service and then agree that in principle, all of the covered services should have better cost-benefit than all of the non-covered services. But you still wouldn’t find a totally categorical dichotomy between “necessary” and “optional” care — there would be no unambiguously correct place to draw the line.

Allowing some overlap and parallelism between public and private systems is a good way of coping with the degree of arbitrariness that then attends these decisions.

Singapore, for example, has a heavily subsidized tier of health care that most people supplement with private coverage. Traditionally, access to an air-conditioned hospital room has been considered a luxury that is not covered by the subsidized tier. But Singapore is a rich (and hot) country, and the world is getting warmer, so there’s discussion about upgrading the subsidized tier to include air conditioning. This concept of the public system as a floor rather than a ceiling strikes me as a much better expression of reasonable pluralism about what our basic values really commit us to.

I also think it’s important to acknowledge that for all the Canadian anxiety about line-cutting, they really are in practice relying on medical tourism to the United States as a pressure release valve.

Australia is another country with a similar culture and a health-care program called “Medicare,” but it’s on the other end of the geographical isolation spectrum. In Australia, Medicare is a floor not a ceiling. About half of the population has private supplemental insurance, and it’s common for providers to be dual-track participants in both the public and private systems.

Canadians worry that if they allow this, providers will exit the public system and it will collapse.

Australia shows that this is a manageable issue. You do need to fiddle with the payment schemes over time to keep things working, but all health-care systems involve fiddling. At its best, private spending by the affluent drives investment in facilities and infrastructure that can then be used by the broader public in time. This is especially important because one function of the American private health market is that our rich people tend to serve as the “consumers of first resort” for innovative medical products that later filter down to the global public.

Prioritization is the biggest issue

This is worth a post of its own, but my strong feeling is that a lot of the mod/prog debates about health care are much less significant than debates about priority-setting.

The 2020 primary featured furious debates about Medicare for All, but then in 2021 it turned out that both progressives and moderates were committed to elevating climate spending over health changes, so that’s what ended up happening when they gained power.

Nobody really talked much about Medicare for All until the contested 2026 primaries, at which point it came up again. I think that Medicare for All is a good factional issue for leftists — or at least a good slogan — for roughly the same reason that health care in general is a good issue for Democrats. This is the major topic on which mass opinion is most progressive. I am also one of the people out there in the public who has doubts about progressive ideas in a lot of areas but who’s pretty aligned with the left on health care.

The moral of all of this is that it would be nice if Democrats in general and progressive Democrats in particular actually prioritized this issue.

But it seems to me that instead, Democrats are often debating which slogans to embrace (“Green New Deal,” etc.) rather than which policies to prioritize. Whether or not you want to label a major ICE-reform initiative “abolishing ICE” is much less consequential than whether you actually want to pursue major ICE reform or would rather focus on an ambitious child-care program or on restoring the zero-carbon-energy tax credits that were created in the Inflation Reduction Act and repealed in the One Big Beautiful Bill Act. Similarly, whether you call your big expansion of publicly provided health insurance “Medicare for All” matters less than whether you pursue a big expansion of public health insurance programs or decide this other stuff is more important. I don’t want to say it’s knowable in advance how much can be achieved in 2029 because it depends on the election outcomes and the macroeconomic situation, but we know there will probably be limits to how much gets done, so the ordering of priorities will matter.

Once you have a priority, though, you also need a specific policy goal. And here I think the floor-not-ceiling and Australia-not-Canada principles are the way to go. For starters, they remove the biggest scare point, which is the fear that Medicare for All involves taking away something people already have. They also better align the proposal with actually existing floor-not-ceiling American Medicare, a program people already like. Last but definitely not least, I think they align with the core moral intuition driving people toward progressive conclusions on health care, which is the idea that there are highly beneficial medical services that everyone should be able to get regardless of ability to pay.
