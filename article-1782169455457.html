When Trump’s influence is no match for money’s

Plus D.C. goes D.S.A. and why ranked-choice voting took so long in Maine

Halina Bennet
Jun 22
∙

Donald Trump’s influence in Republican primaries is considerable, but last Tuesday’s results out of Georgia are a reminder that it’s not absolute — especially when a rival candidate is spending at record-breaking rates. Georgia Republicans had a chance to nominate the president’s pick for governor, Lieutenant Governor Burt Jones, yet they chose health care executive Rick Jackson. Despite Jones’s backing from the president’s political operation, voters went in the other direction after Jackson spent $100 million (much of it his own money) convincing voters that while he might not have Trump’s endorsement, he is a MAGA champion.

Jackson will face former Atlanta Mayor Keisha Lance Bottoms in November.

The Senate primary broke the other way. Trump waited until the closing days to back Representative Mike Collins over Derek Dooley — a former football coach who had the support of term-limited Governor Brian Kemp — and Collins won on Tuesday with about 56 percent of the vote. He will face incumbent Senator Jon Ossoff in one of the marquee races of the cycle; Ossoff’s campaign says it pulled in more than $1 million the day after Collins’s victory.

Maine’s results are in

After 10 days, Maine has sorted out its three highest-profile ranked-choice primaries. The state is unusual both for its embrace of ranked choice and for how slowly it counts ballots, but those two things are only loosely connected. The ranked-choice math itself goes quickly — it’s just a matter of running numbers through a spreadsheet once all the ballot data is in. The delay, then, is almost entirely due to the logistics of collecting the ballots.

Maine counts centrally, meaning that ballots and memory sticks from each one of the state’s 487 towns have to be physically driven to Augusta, and only law enforcement personnel — like state troopers and game wardens — are allowed to transport them. About 150 towns still don’t have on-site scanners, so their paper ballots get scanned by hand once they arrive at the counting center. Towns with electronic tabulators, by contrast, transfer their results using encrypted flash drives. According to the Bangor Daily News, simply compiling results from every town takes several days before tabulation even begins.

On top of that, officials manually cross-check every town’s totals against what was reported on election night, and mismatches are routine. In this primary, mislabeled or mixed-up memory sticks from four towns added another day of waiting.

But once the tabulations concluded, the results locked in a House race that is sure to be closely watched. Former State Auditor Matt Dunlap won the Democratic nomination in the Second Congressional District, edging out the establishment-backed Joe Baldacci. Dunlap will now face former Governor Paul LePage for the seat Representative Jared Golden is vacating. The Democratic nominee’s win was secured after three rounds of ranked-choice voting, CBS News reports.

Trump carried that district by nearly 10 points in 2024, so Republicans have good reason to like their odds.

In the gubernatorial race, former State Representative Hannah Pingree will face Republican Bobby Charles, with independent Rick Bennett also on the ballot. Local strategists think Bennett’s presence could scramble the usual partisan math, especially since donor attention and money are flowing instead to the Senate contest between Susan Collins and Graham Platner, WGME reports.

D.C. picks a mayor — and a fight with the White House

Janeese Lewis George won the D.C. mayoral primary by a wide margin over Kenyan McDuffie, giving democratic socialists control of two major U.S. mayoralties following Zohran Mamdani’s victory in New York last year. Similar candidates are now competing elsewhere, including Los Angeles.

Lewis George’s contrast with outgoing D.C. Mayor Muriel Bowser, who tried to keep relations with the Trump administration workable, is sharp. Trump told reporters before the vote that he’d consider moving to take over governance of D.C. if Lewis George won, and her campaign leaned into the threat rather than away from it, the Washington Post reports.

Whether that confrontation comes to pass once Lewis George is in office is a different question, but it could have a massive effect on D.C.’s budget and home rule.

A poll buried in Michigan

Politico reported last week that pollster Steve Mitchell ran a survey of the U.S. Senate primary in Michigan that, by his account, showed State Senator Mallory McMorrow collapsing to just 6 percent support. That would put her far behind Abdul El-Sayed at 42 percent and U.S. Representative Haley Stevens at 33 percent. The outlet that had commissioned the poll, Michigan Information & Research Service (MIRS), opted not to publish it after the McMorrow campaign objected. Her team says that’s because the poll was a mess, not because they successfully pressured anyone into burying bad news.

Their complaint is that the poll was distributed through an open link rather than a controlled sample. This means that anyone who got the link could theoretically fill it out repeatedly or pass it to allies, which is a real methodological problem if true. The MIRS editor who killed the poll said other pollsters he consulted shared the same concern, according to Politico.

Regardless of whose account of the poll’s validity is accurate, the underlying trend in the race seems directionally reasonable, given that published polls have also shown McMorrow trailing.

State legislatures are building a defense

Five Democratic-led states — Washington, California, Colorado, Connecticut, and Maryland — have now passed laws meant to insulate their election systems from interference that the Trump administration has been threatening.

The legislation takes different forms in different states — restricting who can access voter rolls and voting equipment, in California’s case, or creating buffer zones around drop boxes and granting emergency powers to the governor, in Colorado’s, CNN reports.

Election law experts quoted by CNN said these laws are likely to hold up better than challenges to outright federal actions would, because they’re regulating state officials’ own conduct rather than trying to constrain Washington directly.

The numbers, briefly

Pennsylvania’s Franklin & Marshall poll, which was conducted in the first half of this month, has Governor Josh Shapiro leading Stacy Garrity 50 percent to 28 percent in next year’s governor’s race. Trump’s approval in the state has fallen to 29 percent positive — down from 39 percent back in March. Democrats hold a 12-point edge on the generic ballot among Pennsylvania voters in that same poll.

In Iowa, Democratic nominee for Senate Josh Turek has a slight lead over G.O.P. nominee Ashley Hinson in a Global Strategy Group poll that was taken earlier this month among 1,000 likely general election voters. While the poll shows that Republicans have a 10-point advantage in voter registration, Trump’s favorability is underwater, and Turek, who leads Hinson by two points, could prevail amid deepening dissatisfaction.

Nationally, the major generic-ballot trackers, including Silver Bulletin, Morning Consult, and RealClearPolling, are still showing Democrats with leads in the mid-to-high single digits, broadly in the range of the 2018 wave.
