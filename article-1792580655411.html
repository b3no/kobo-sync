Trouble man: A diplomat’s life in hot spots

Ben Rowswell’s career took him from Somalia to Iraq, Afghanistan and Venezuela. Some like it hot

Paul Wells
Oct 21
 
READ IN APP
 

Ben Rowswell’s out in the private sector these days, working at Andrew Bevan’s new consulting firm Catalyze4. But before that, he had a 25-year career in Canada’s foreign service. Not an ordinary career.

This is my first long conversation with Rowswell, though we’ve known each other to say Hi for many years. I became aware of him when he was the RoCK, or Representative of Canada in Kandahar. His assignment was to lead the civilian projects and services that complemented Canada’s military effort in the evolving Afghanistan conflict. It required extended presence on the ground, often in body armour. Later I learned that Afghanistan wasn’t his first tough gig, and later still that it wasn’t his last.

Rowswell studied international relations in Washington, DC, where statecraft is a toolkit of widely varying utility rather than a spectator sport. He wanted to know how wars begin and end, and what happens after that. Even before joining Canada’s Foreign Service, he found himself working for a UN aviation contractor at Mogadishu airport, in the middle of Somalia’s civil war.

He learned Arabic to boost his chances of being posted to conflict zones. When the Americans invaded Iraq, Canada steered clear, mostly: Rowswell volunteered to be Canada’s eyes and ears in Baghdad. He served as Canada’s ambassador to Venezuela during the governments of both Stephen Harper and Justin Trudeau, while its democracy collapsed.

He comes away from it all skeptical of military solutions but not of diplomacy. "Major conflicts tend to end through politics and not through... The military victory of one over the other,” he told me. And he hopes that as Canada scales up its military capability to hit NATO targets, it will increase its political presence through its network of Canadian diplomats, not only through the frenzied jet-setting of one prime minister. “I think we do ourselves a disservice by assuming that Canada is weak,” he told me.

“We are more powerful than we think.”
