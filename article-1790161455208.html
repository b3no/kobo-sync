Jill Lepore on AI and democracy

The Pulitzer-winning historian on how to keep the Artificial State from winning

Paul Wells
Sep 23
 
READ IN APP
 

Where are the public intellectuals? Here’s one.

I’m thrilled to bring you this interview with Jill Lepore, Harvard historian, prolific New Yorker staff writer — her author page lists 15 articles this year alone — and newly-crowned Pulitzer Prize winner in history for her book We The People: A History of the U.S. Constitution, a 700-page argument for her country’s Constitution as a document that was made to be amended, should be amended, and lately hasn’t.

Lepore loves to tell stories about cultural movements, titans and oddballs, but sometimes events lead her to make a more pointed and personal argument. That’s the case with The Rise and the Fall of the Artificial State, her latest bestseller and the subject of this interview.

Here Lepore traces parallel histories: the long story of human attempts to automate democracy and governance, and the parallel — and sometimes dangerously unconnected — history of attempts to think through the implications of such a notion. “Few grand transformations have been more precipitate or more heedless” than the rise of what could soon be “government without consent, even government without humans.”

This is one of those interviews that actually became more timely during the period between recording and release. The world has spent weeks in the grip of an AI panic, fuelled by the concerns of AI executives themselves. And Lepore’s book describes how that usually goes, and how it seems to be going again: a new technology is embraced; some of its heralds get serious second thoughts; and the most careless among them dismiss the concerns and go barrelling ahead.

“That sense of powerlessness around this cascade of invention seems to be accelerating,” she told me. “Very little of it feels chosen.”

And then, a little later: “The tools are tremendous. But the political ideas of these people almost all harken back to the worst and most discredited ideas of the 1930s.”

Share

But she is not here peddling despair. She believes the old-fashioned state, the one that’s run by humans and coughs up at least occasionally appropriate leaders, can make a comeback, if people only have faith in institutions and in themselves. “It is important to go to these meetings,” she says. “To show up at the city council meeting.”

Yeah, I’m not sure that’s true either. But it was good to hear Lepore make her case.
