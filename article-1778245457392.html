The Most Exciting Room in Washington

At the Library of Congress, research becomes an adventure.

Ron Charles
May 8
 
READ IN APP
 

Let’s get the bad news over with first.

We’ve grown used to book banners going after novels like Beloved, 1984, and The Handmaid’s Tale. But now the censors are coming for nonfiction.

According to a report released yesterday by PEN America, attacks on nonfiction books are surging.

In the 2024-25 school year, nearly a third of the titles banned were works of nonfiction — more than double the previous year’s share.

Reflecting the Trump administration’s hostility to facts and scholarship, book banners have grown increasingly intolerant of titles about science, health, history, political activism, social movements, economic inequality, and even classical art.

This strain of politically activated anti-intellectualism “represents something new and distinctive about the trajectory of book bans in public schools,” the PEN America researchers write.

“At a time when faith in public institutions is in jeopardy, doubt in expertise and education is on the rise, and our literacy rates are on a decline, we should be making it easier to access literature that is both relatable and educational in schools.”

Library of Congress, that’s your cue.

Tomorrow, the world’s largest library will open a dazzling exhibit to introduce young people to the power of research.

The Source: Where Curiosity Sparks Discovery encourages kids ages 8 to 15 to dig into the Library’s vast collection of texts, images, sounds, and films. This is the first time the 226-year-old Library has offered a permanent, experiential learning space designed in consultation with and for children and teens.

The 4,000-square-foot gallery — seven years in the making — is like nothing I’ve seen before: an audacious effort to yank library research from the dank stacks of the public imagination and show kids how exciting and rewarding the quest for information can be.

At the start, visitors come upon an enormous circular card catalog. Pull out some of the drawers and you’ll see drafts of Spider-Man, Mickey Mouse, and the Gettysburg Address. Motion-activated sensors launch enchanting light shows on the cabinet. Other drawers hold an early copy of The Wonderful Wizard of Oz and an LP of Take Me Out to the Ball Game.

It’s an homage to the old card catalog, transformed into a dynamic introduction to the Library’s 180 million items.

Walk farther into the exhibit under a ceiling dancing with posters, books, and musical instruments, and you’ll find video kiosks where researchers explain their work on basketball, cartoons, and WWII.

In the other room, kids are encouraged to roll up their sleeves, sharpen their curiosity, and dig into the collection just like real researchers.

In four cleverly demarcated zones, visitors can find and handle replicas of presidential papers, historical newspapers, maps, and posters.

Throughout, electronically tagged records and reels let children call up broadcasts by Amelia Earhart, recordings of baseball games, films of historic elections, and much more. Antique typewriters, stereoscopes, cameras, phonographs, and microfilm machines showcase what technology came before today’s research tools.

In a pre-opening presentation for the media, Acting Librarian of Congress Robert Newlen said, “This new gallery is especially important at a moment when students need to develop the ability to navigate and evaluate information. The Source helps young visitors hone their research and media literacy skills early so they can be critical thinkers and informed citizens.”

College student Caleb Sinnwell was on hand to explain how he used the Library of Congress’s online resources when he was in 7th grade to learn about the WWII Ghost Army.

“Researching doesn’t need to be boring. It can be creative, collaborative, and fun,” Caleb said. “When you approach it with curiosity, it becomes less about finding the right answer and more about exploring possibilities.”

His work was instrumental in getting Congress to honor Ghost Army veterans in 2022.

“History isn’t just something we study,” he said. “It’s something that can inspire us to act and even make history ourselves.”

The Library of Congress may have created a future librarian. Or senator.

How effectively The Source engages typical children will be up to the kids who visit, of course. Awesome technology and flashy interactives don’t necessarily inspire the qualities of good research. But it doesn’t hurt to clear away the cobwebs. As Emily Dickinson writes, “Had but the Tale a warbling Teller — / All the Boys would come.”

A few students from nearby Saint Peter School were getting an early preview along with journalists pestering them for reactions. The kids agreed The Source was “cool.”

Lizzy told me, “You would think of the word ‘library’ as being a place with a bunch of books, but this place has a bunch of other things than books, and it teaches you about sound and film and researching.”

Speaking of research: Ironically, the pre-opening presentation made no reference to former Librarian of Congress Dr. Carla Hayden, who was fired by Trump exactly one year ago today. She launched the Source after she received a letter from an 8-year-old boy who lamented that he couldn’t get a Reader Card to the Library until he was 16.

That curious little boy is now old enough to apply for his own Reader Card, but millions of other kids might just get the research bug at The Source.◆

Books Go to the Movies

  • By all reports, The Sheep Detectives is not baaaad. This comic mystery, starring Hugh Jackman, is based on German author Leonie Swann’s 2005 novel, Three Bags Full, about a flock of sheep determined to solve their shepherd’s murder.

  • Remarkably Bright Creatures, starring Sally Field and Lewis Pullman, begins stretching its tentacles across Netflix today (trailer). The movie is based on Shelby Van Pelt’s 2022 novel about a cleaning woman who befriends an octopus at the aquarium where she works. Like Swann’s sheep, this octopus is on a mission to solve a mystery that humans can’t.

  • It must be universally acknowledged that The Other Bennet Sister is streaming on BritBox (trailer). The 10-part BBC drama stars Ella Bruccoleri as Mary, Lizzie Bennet’s plain sister. Based on Janice Hadlow’s 2020 novel inspired by Pride and Prejudice, the series gives a full life to Austen’s studious and overlooked young woman who “had neither genius nor taste.”◆

What’s in a Name?

Sure, you might attain immortality through good living, but why take that risk? For the right bid, your name could persist forever as a character in a popular novel.

Jean Hanff Korelitz, Jennifer Egan, and Rebecca Makkai are just a few of the bestselling authors selling off naming rights to characters in their next books (details).

They’re part of PEN America’s Character Naming Auction, which runs through May 15.

Can you think of a more thrilling Father’s Day present than putting Dad in David Baldacci’s upcoming thriller? (Winning bidders also get a signed copy of the book.)

Money raised by the auction will fund PEN America’s Author Safety Program, which helps protect writers facing harassment, threats, and intimidation.

As of this morning, the top bid — $1,000 — is for a character in a future novel by Jodi Picoult.

Who will the winner be in her next book?

“The truth is I can’t tell you yet if the bidder will be a villain or a hero,” Jodi says in response to my inquiry. “The last time I did this, the winner was Gunther Frankenstein — and I made him a medical examiner in Nineteen Minutes. But I’m happy to offer this up to support PEN’s Author Safety program, which provided a lifeline to me last year when I had to use it!”◆

It’s About Time

Five years ago, I spotted the most novel thing I’d ever seen: a clock that tells the time — minute by minute — with quotations from works of literature.

At 6:45: “‘Gregor,’ somebody called — it was his mother — ‘it’s quarter to seven. Didn’t you want to go somewhere?’”

At 9:00: “At nine o’clock, one morning late in July, Gatsby’s gorgeous car lurched up the rocky drive to my door and gave out a burst of melody from its three-noted horn.”

You get the idea: a different quotation for each of the day’s 1,440 minutes.

The Author Clock enjoyed more than its 15 minutes of fame on Kickstarter, but spent years getting units made and shipped to customers.

It was worth it. This elegant device sports an oak frame and a brass dial. (There are two sizes, 4.3 inches or 7.8 inches, for $209 or $369.) The quotations — more than 13,000 from more than 2,500 famous authors — appear crisp and clear on the e-paper screen.

Now, a competitor is challenging the Author Clock tick for tock.

NovellaMate comes in one 7-inch size, along with many more features and a larger literary database. It sports a touch screen that can switch from time quotations to literary quotations keyed to your local weather conditions. During a rainstorm, for instance, it might display: “She was thoroughly soaked before she realized it was raining and then only because one of the shopping bags split” from Toni Morrison’s Song of Solomon.

Another mode shows inspirational quotations.

You can set an alarm, and, if you want, you can choose your favorite authors and themes.

From a software point of view, NovellaMate is far superior, and with a touch, the clock will read the quotation aloud in a stately British voice.

Yes, the brown plastic casing looks like it came from the BlackBerry era, but at $199, it’s also cheaper than the least expensive, much smaller Author Clock.

My only complaint is that the NovellaMate’s backlit LED screen is not quite crisp enough. And the author names are too faint.

So there you have it: Eleven forty-five or quarter till noon. Surely it’s only a matter of time before someone combines the Author Clock’s elegance with the NovellaMate’s versatility.◆

Literary Prizes and Honors

  • Wednesday night in Washington, Mahreen Sohail accepted the PEN/Faulkner Award for Fiction for Small Scale Sinners, a story collection about women in Pakistan. Sohail noted that she’d worked on a novel for more than six years but couldn’t find a publisher. In desperation, she turned to the stories she’d been writing on the side simply as a creative release. “I’d written a whole other book,” she said. “Art can be surprising. It is one of the many joys of making it, how it can reveal yourself to you.”

  • Daniel Kraus’s Angel Down, a surreal World War I novel written in a single breathless sentence, won the Pulitzer Prize for Fiction. (Book clubs, take note: Angel Down will be released in paperback on Tuesday.) We the People: A History of the U.S. Constitution, by New Yorker writer Jill Lepore, won the Pulitzer for History (full list of winners).

  • Erika Krouse won the Joyce Carol Oates Prize sponsored by New Literary Project. The award, worth $50,000, honors “a mid-career author of fiction who advances the vision and mission of NewLit — to drive social change and unleash artistic power across the generations and the nation.” Krouse’s most recent book is a collection of stories called Save Me, Stranger.◆

Picture This

Our younger daughter’s first word was “whatisit?” and she employed it no more than 18 million times a day. Once, insisting that I couldn’t possibly help, she screamed and screamed for her mother. When Dawn finally came downstairs, our daughter pointed to a pancake and demanded, “Whatisit?”

Dan Ojari and his 8-year-old son Finn have published a deceptively simple rhyming book that sweeps me back to those precious days of tireless identification.

Is This a Plum? is a peek-through picture book that presents a delightful series of tricky images.

Each die-cut page shows what looks like a plum or a cat, but when you turn the page and see the full image beneath, it’s obvious that plum is a hippo’s bum; that cat’s a bat.

Is this a GOOSE?

No — it’s a MOOSE!

Is This a Plum? strikes that rare balance of wit and cuteness. It’s also a brilliant (unspoken) lesson for kids about the importance of questioning first impressions. After all, a limited perspective can skew one’s conclusions disastrously.

Is this a CAKE?

No — it’s a SNAKE!◆

This Week’s Poem

On Monday, Ars Poeticas, by writer and scholar Juliana Spahr, won the Pulitzer Prize for Poetry.

The collection’s seven poems consider the role of poetry during a time of environmental destruction and political crisis.

“I thought that poetry / could be apart from the nation still,” Spahr writes, before acknowledging that she wants her work to engage closely with the world.

This excerpt is one section of a longer poem called “Bison”:

When the bison wallows,
it is not just that the dust rises up.
The soil compacts, hair and oil mix in it,
eventually there is a deep impression,
one that fills with water when the rains come.
Then an impossible art results,
the bison way of making things right
for the feathery leaves of the yarrow,
the purple- or pink-tinted stalks of the field milk vetch,
the stalkless flowers of the cushion phlox,
and the wispy pink plumes of the prairie smoke.
Tall bluebells too, pendant bell-shaped,
next to the simple yellow flowers of the alpine arnica.
The bison way of making things right
for the wooly gray leaves of the fringed sage,
right too for the sage’s small and unremarkable fruits.
Right for the roots of the Jerusalem artichokes,
the prairie turnip and the prairie parsley.
Right for the prairie chicken.
The bison way of making things right for the male
to come hooting, dancing, tail up,
neck sacks inflated all the while.
Right for the western chorus frogs
that come out at night to chorus and feed.
Right for the high jumping northern cricket frog.
Right for the small invertebrates and arthropods
upon which they feed. The sap-feeding herbivores
with their beak mouthpart sucking on plant fluid.
The chewing herbivores with their
mandibulate mouthparts. And those that
carnivorously feed on a variety of prey.
Right also for the grasshopper sparrow, also feeding,
and then creating a well-concealed open cup
on the ground under the tall stalks of the big bluestem,
the shorter ones of the little bluestem,
the switchgrass and the Indiangrass too.

Excerpted from “Ars Poeticas.” Copyright © 2025 by Juliana Spahr. Used with permission of the publisher, Wesleyan University Press. All rights reserved.

P.S. Marianne Boruch won the $100,000 Jackson Poetry Prize sponsored by Poets & Writers. Her most recent collection, Bestiary Dark, inspired by her studies in Australia, addresses the natural world and environmental destruction.◆

Literary Adventures

Last weekend, I went to Austin, Texas, for my favorite niece’s wedding. Before the ceremony, I had a chance to wander around the city for the first time.

At BookPeople, Texas’ largest independent bookstore, we found a terrific selection of titles on towering bookcases arranged in an irresistible maze of major and quirky subjects.

Dawn and I were astonished by the Austin Public Library, a modern six-story palace of glass and limestone overlooking Lady Bird Lake. Completed in 2017, the library features a breathtaking atrium intersected by floating stairwells. On one wall hangs a 37-foot-tall cuckoo-clock-inspired sculpture of red crows. In addition to the library’s vast collection of fiction and nonfiction, there’s a playful children’s section, a dedicated room for older kids, and special spaces for crafts, including 3D printers, which were buzzing away.

And just two blocks from our hotel, we found the O. Henry Museum, a quaint tribute to William Sydney Porter, who wrote “The Gift of the Magi” and hundreds of other short stories that few people know today. The museum — a late 19th-century Queen Anne-style house — has been lovingly restored and decorated with pieces of furniture once owned by the Porters, such as their Horace Waters piano.

It’s not a large museum. The young docent was able to give us the entire tour without moving her feet. But the outlandish details of Porter’s life, which include a conviction for bank fraud, an escape to Honduras, and several years in prison, make for an exciting tale.

In a twist of fate worthy of O. Henry himself, we arrived a week too early to see — or enter — the 49th Annual O. Henry Museum Pun-Off World Championship, which takes place tomorrow (details). Next time, we’ll try to be more punctual.◆
