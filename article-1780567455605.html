Elizabeth Warren’s “new“ child care push doesn’t make sense

A poorly designed idea that nobody is asking for

For many years now, advocates of an expanded role for the federal government in the provision of child care have been angling to “go next” in Democratic Party priorities. And we’re already seeing things like a $50 million push not just to help Democrats win races in the midterms, but specifically to get their campaigns to center child care issues.

Elizabeth Warren, a critically important thought-leader in the party, said at the Center for American Progress’s IDEAS Conference in May that “It would be political malpractice for Democrats not to be talking about child care every chance we get, going into the midterms and beyond.”

But of course, she doesn’t just want to talk about child care in the abstract. She wants to talk specifically about her Child Care for Every Community Act, which was introduced last year with Mikie Sherrill as House co-sponsor, a role that has been taken up by Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez now that Sherrill is governor of New Jersey.

The link between calling on Democrats to campaign on an issue and pushing specific policy measures relates to a very dysfunctional norm in the Democratic Party where everyone more or less agrees that the way to win a substantive argument about policy prioritization is to first win an argument about messaging. There’s a similar push underway, led by Sheldon Whitehouse, to get Democrats to talk more about climate change.

Of course, for senators like Warren and Whitehouse with safe seats in New England, there’s no downside to just talking about both.

But Democrats need to win races in Iowa and Alaska and Texas and Ohio if they’re going to do anything at all. And to do that, it helps for the national party to have accurate information about what voters care about. But if progressive issue advocates believe that the only way to secure prioritization for their topic is to deceive party elites about the voters’ priorities, they’re incentivized to engage in spreading misinformation.

I would note that you don’t actually need to conduct politics this way. In 2001 and then again in 2003 and again in 2017 and again in 2025, newly elected Republican presidents prioritized regressive tax cuts that conservatives believe are morally important. But Republicans have never made “we want to cut rich people’s taxes” the centerpiece of a national political campaign. They don’t even make it the centerpiece of their pitch for the regressive tax bills they promote. They simply keep in mind that the thing they most care about is not the same as the thing that persuadable voters most care about.

And just as persuadable voters don’t really care about climate change, they don’t seem to care very much about child care either.

I don’t want to get the people at Navigator Research in trouble, because they’re trying to be good Democrats and progressive allies. But their latest battleground polling presentation (which to be clear is full of good news for Dems) also makes it clear that the child care issue is a niche topic.

This should not be a huge surprise. Child care costs are a huge topic for dual-income couples with kids under five, especially if they live in expensive metro areas. But while people like that are very well-represented in the Democratic Party staffer class, they’re just not a very large share of the population.

Interest in subsidized day care centers is limited

One issue here is just that while the general topic of child care is highly salient to anyone whose kids are too young for public school, that’s just not that many people.

As I wrote back during the 2020 primary, Democrats were looking at this issue through a very particular lens. The kinds of people who become members of Congress or who run for president or who work on political campaigns or in the media or for policy advocacy organizations tend to be really into their jobs. These are lines of work where people typically earn less money than someone with their skills or credentials could earn in the corporate sector. And because this work has a lot of intrinsic appeal to a highly motivated subset of the population, the jobs are difficult to obtain even though they don’t pay very well.

People like that who are also politically progressive — especially if they are politically progressive women — have a very specific concern about their careers getting derailed by family responsibilities. They also tend to have nine-to-five, Monday-Friday jobs rather than working odd hours in service industries.

If that’s you, then formal center-based child care (i.e., things like day care centers and preschools) is a good solution, especially if that means high-quality facilities with trained staff. It is, however, very expensive, especially in generally high-cost metro areas. But even though it can seem like “everyone” is worried about this, it’s actually a somewhat idiosyncratic preoccupation of media and political professionals in D.C. and New York.

New America, a center-left organization, recently surveyed parents with kids under five, and they found that desire to spend more time with kids is much more common than desire to spend more time at work.

I think there is probably some social desirability bias at work here — who’s going to say no to “quality time” with your kids? But the fact that paid work ranks below things like “household tasks” and “personal time” is a reminder that way more people have jobs than careers. They’re working for money, not self-actualization.

I don’t want to pose too stark a dichotomy here and make it out to be the case that those of us who are career-oriented don’t love our children.

But it’s definitely true that I sometimes say “yes” to doing work-related things that take me away from my family that are not strictly necessary to making a living. I find my work really interesting, and I would keep inflicting my takes on the world even if someone gave me a billion dollars. I don’t think the typical waitress or medical assistant or housecleaner has the same relationship with their work. Relatively few mothers of young kids say full-time employment is their ideal lifestyle.

By the same token, aspiring tradwives are also pretty rare. Most mothers don’t want to be completely economically dependent on their husbands and are attuned to the problems of boredom and anomie that Betty Friedan famously documented. But kids reach school age after just a few years, and most mothers of young kids would ideally prefer to keep a foot in the workplace and also spend a lot of time with their children. Preferences for child care arrangements are all over the map.

The complexity of real life and the stark divides that people have in terms of their preferences in this regard are a reminder that this is a very tricky policy area.

Most people are not parents of young kids, so any proposal to spend lots of money specifically on parents of young kids is something of an uphill climb. The particular version of center-based care with standards modeled on Head Start and military child care programs that Elizabeth Warren and Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez have landed on compounds the problem because it offers something most parents of young kids don’t particularly want.

Program design details matter

Somewhat separate from the politics, it’s also worth paying attention to what exactly this proposed program does. Building on proposals from the 2020 presidential campaigns and early iterations of Build Back Better, the Warren bill says that “higher-income families would pay no more than 7 percent of their income, while lower-income families making less than 75 percent of their state median income would be fully subsidized.”

This kind of sliding-scale rather than universal approach helps keep down the headline cost of the program.

But it does also mean that as designed, the bill does the least for the kind of upscale married professional women who are the target constituency for full-time center-based care.

Warren’s program would give a much larger boost to working-class parents, many of whom would probably prefer to use additional financial resources to cut back on working hours. There’s a big risk that by subsidizing a lot of families who are not currently in the center-based child care market (because they’re relying on more informal arrangements) to enter it, you wind up pushing the unsubsidized price up quite a bit and somewhat backfiring on the people who’d be most inclined to like this idea.

This is exacerbated by the fact that the program is, of course, a bit of an everything bagel that “ensures parity by requiring that wages and benefits for child care workers be comparable to those of similarly credentialed local public-school teachers.”

I will confess that I do not completely understand what that means, since most child-care-center workers have different credentials from local public-school teachers. But the American Federation of State, County and Municipal Employees — which has endorsed the legislation — has an initiative where the owners of subsidized day care centers in California unionize to collectively bargain for higher reimbursement rates from the state.

That, I think, helps explain where the demand for the particular emphasis on formal child care centers as the way to help parents comes from. Child care is not a salient issue for the mass public, and parents of young kids are divided on the extent to which this is their preferred child care arrangement. But unlike cash assistance, it has a specific interest-group coalition behind it.

Look for smaller ways to help

Getting back to where we started, Democrats should try to run in 2026 and 2028 on messages that appeal to voters’ actual priorities rather than letting issue advocates gaslight them into thinking that climate change or child care is really driving the electorate.

Separately from that, Democrats at an elite level need to try to do some priority-setting based on what they actually think is important.

If you’re going to spend a bunch of money on something child-related that the voters aren’t particularly clamoring for, I think it makes a lot more sense to focus on expanding the Child Tax Credit (perhaps specifically for kids too young for kindergarten) than on this interest-group slop. Parents could use the money to pay for child care at day care centers, but they could also use it to help out in other ways.

It’s worth emphasizing here that family expenses don’t fall into neat little buckets.

There are good ideas to reduce housing costs that focus on land use, mortgage regulation, and other regulatory barriers to supply and that don’t cost any money. If housing were cheaper, that would make raising a family more affordable, even if those reforms aren’t specifically targeted at child care costs. The easiest thing Democrats can do to make center-based child care specifically more affordable is to look at regulatory issues. Devon Gorry and Diana Weinert Thomas find that the lower day care staffing ratios in blue states raise costs despite no clear evidence of improving quality.

On an even more low-profile level, one of the few immigration restrictionist measures undertaken by the Biden administration was tighter regulation of the au pair program, which the Trump administration has also handicapped by screwing around with visa administration.

It seems like trying to encourage orderly and relatively uncontroversial forms of immigration like au pairs is a much better idea than going wobbly on border enforcement or deportations of people who get arrested.

But this all hinges on actually prioritizing the interests of consumers of child care services rather than viewing subsidies to the child care sector as a jobs program.
