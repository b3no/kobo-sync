A market full of paradoxes

Buyers have leverage they can’t use, but developers have a socialist mayor they can. Americans love housing and hate data centers, but the country seems to feel the opposite. Everything’s weird.

Halina Bennet
Sep 21
∙

Housing this week is a tale of contradictions. Buyers have more leverage than they have had in more than a decade, but they aren’t feeling it. New York City’s socialist mayor is winning over developers, while the G.O.P. is flailing with its own housing message. And just as America began to invest in the housing supply, the country is, for the first time, spending more on building data centers than on building homes.

An unlikely friendship

New York City is facing its tightest rental market in more than 50 years, with 1.6 percent of housing in Manhattan available as of July, according to data from the Corcoran Group, The Economist reports.

That shortage, along with the city’s subsequent affordability crisis, helped put Mayor Zohran Mamdani in office. Research from the Searchlight Institute from November 2025, just after Mamdani’s general-election victory, found that affordability messaging appeared in 78 percent of his campaign content, and 45 percent of voters cited “affordability and working-class policies” as a main reason to vote for him.

When Mamdani was running with this message, real estate developers were running in the opposite direction.

An email that circulated among homebuilders last year warned that “the financial capital of the world risks being handed over to a socialist ... We cannot — and will not — let that happen,” The Economist reports. But during his first year in office, the mayor courted the industry that first opposed him, and its tone changed:

  • Early on, Mamdani tasked teams with figuring out why affordable housing production has slowed and identifying which city-owned land and buildings could be converted into housing.

  • In February, he lobbied President Donald Trump for $21 billion in federal funding for a housing development in Queens that Representative Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez opposed in 2019 on the grounds that it displaced communities and handed public land to private developers.

  • Come May, he unveiled his Block by Block plan to build 200,000 affordable units as well as preserve an existing 200,000 and offer incentives to developers willing to renovate public housing.

  • Last month, three major developers met with Mamdani to discuss the city’s agenda for housing growth.

Meanwhile, Politico reports, the G.O.P. cannot seem to get its own housing message together, threatening the party’s success in the midterm elections.

Rates are climbing, and the market can’t keep up

The average 30-year fixed mortgage rate exceeds 7 percent for the first time since January 2025, driven primarily by the war in Iran pushing Treasury yields higher — the 10-year Treasury yield climbed above 5 percent last Monday, reaching its highest level since 2023. Freddie Mac put the average 30-year rate at 6.76 percent the week before; daily readings since have crossed 7 percent, the Wall Street Journal reports.

Home sales, accordingly, have taken a blow.

Existing home sales fell 2 percent month-over-month in August, landing at a seasonally adjusted annual rate of 3.98 million units. That is the lowest reading since June of last year; Zillow economists had forecast a 4.3 percent increase, according to the Journal. Michael Fratantoni, chief economist at the Mortgage Bankers Association, told the Journal he expects the rate increase to push even more buyers to pause on applying for loans or signing contracts, further stagnating a market that has been on hold for four years straight.

Colorado, which was ranked in May as the third most expensive state for housing in a report from its chamber of commerce, is a clear example of the dynamic at hand. In mid-September, statewide mortgage rates hit a two-year high of around 7.12 percent, up from a three-year low in February, when rates dipped below 6 percent. As rates continue to rise, sales from eastern Colorado to the Western Slope are falling, the Craig Daily Press reports.

Buyers don’t feel like the market is theirs, even if it is

Even if mortgage rates are high, by nearly every structural account, the market is tilted toward buyers. About 80 percent of major U.S. metros are considered buyer-favorable, and sellers continue to outnumber buyers nationally. Still, CNN reports, sentiment isn’t aligned with the math. High rates and prices are preventing buyers from taking advantage of the negotiating power they theoretically have.

With listings at a five-year high, buyer demand sluggish, and homes priced too high, sellers are trying to sweeten the deal. They offered concessions in 44.7 percent of nationwide home sales in August — the highest August share since at least 2020, up from 42.6 percent a year earlier, according to data from Redfin.

Those concessions do vary based on local market conditions, with the highest rates in the Sun Belt, according to analysis from Redfin. In Atlanta, Ga., sellers offered concessions in 72.8 percent of home sales, while San Jose, Calif., saw a share of 4.2 percent.

Concessions from sellers come in the form of repair credits, closing-cost support, and mortgage-rate buydowns, Business Insider reports, the last of which real estate experts say often result in more savings than a price cut.

Emily Peck of Axios reads all of this as evidence that perceived wealth is propping up consumer spending. Existing home sales have collapsed from a 2021 peak, but the economy itself has not.

Peck cites investor Bob Elliott, who believes that homeowners are not forced to learn their home’s value until they try to sell it. “Most owners are locked in with a perception of high value, without actually being forced to turn it into liquidity,” he writes. “It’s that lack of transacted mark-to-market which creates the fragility of the whole market.”

There is some evidence that the perception is beginning to unravel. Almost 6 percent of home listings were taken off the market in July without a sale, following a trend that has been growing over the past year, Axios reports, with prices beginning to decline in Seattle, Texas, and Florida. Redfin Chief Economist Daryl Fairweather told Axios that it makes sense that more homes would enter the market this year, five years after Covid and its related housing boom. Homeowners often try to sell five years after they buy, she said, “especially if they bought a home that they thought was going to be a starter.”

Even landlords are drowning in debt

Renters, not just homebuyers, are having difficulty finding affordable housing, even when many buildings are vacant. At the same time, apartment landlords across the United States face about $2 trillion in aggregate debt, as interest-rate caps purchased during Covid, when rates were unprecedentedly low, begin to expire, forcing building owners to refinance at today’s swollen rates, the Wall Street Journal reports.

Prices are rising across the board for insurance, taxes, and utilities, further compounding the pressure and diminishing income even in buildings where units are filled and rents are going up.

America is spending more on data centers than on housing

Inflation-adjusted spending on information-processing equipment such as data centers and computer hardware now exceeds real private residential fixed investment — essentially, spending on home construction and improvements — for the first time, Fortune reports.

The Bureau of Economic Analysis found that real private residential fixed investment totaled $748 billion in the second quarter of 2026, down 18 percent from its 2021 peak, Fortune writes. Meanwhile, spending on information-processing equipment rose 51 percent over that same period to $752 billion.

This dynamic might hurt pro-data center candidates hoping to win the midterm elections. Read more from Fortune.
