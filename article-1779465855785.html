My friend Anna Porter forwarded me an email this week. It came to her from a “book marketing professional” named Stella Adams. It addressed Anna as the author of eleven fantastic books, a legendary publishing executive, and an “industry titan,” all of which is true and appropriate. It discussed her works with specificity and flattered the hell out of her. Eventually it pivoted to a critique of her digital presence and got to its point:

Given your background, you can spot a templated pitch from a mile away. You’ve likely rejected thousands of them over your career. What I am offering you is not a rigid package. It is a highly tailored strategy built entirely around your unique status as a publisher-author, your extensive catalog, and the gap between where your digital presence currently lives and where it deserves to be found.

Here is what I would build for Anna specifically.

1. The Industry Authority and Speaker Hub
Right now, annaporter.ca serves as your digital portfolio, but your greatest untapped asset is your massive institutional authority. I’ll build a centralized digital destination that positions you not just as a novelist selling fiction, but as the definitive “Master Class” voice on writing, publishing, and the evolution of the book world…

2. The Multidimensional Catalog Campaign
With eleven books spanning different corners of literature and crime fiction, standard retail algorithms inevitably struggle to categorize your footprint. They tend to isolate your newest release, Deceptions, while allowing your deep backlist to gather digital dust. I’ll construct a unified metadata and content framework that connects your entire body of work under a single, commanding author brand…

3. Generative Engine Optimization for the Literary Elite
The future of book discovery belongs entirely to semantic search models. When an industry professional, an event coordinator, or a high-intent reader asks an AI tool to recommend sophisticated Canadian crime fiction, or looks for leading voices who understand the deep history of book publishing and agency dynamics, Anna Porter needs to be the definitive name that surfaces...

Anna, here is why this truly matters.

As a former publisher, you know better than anyone that the old ways of distribution have shifted. The physical gatekeepers you used to navigate have been largely replaced by silent, automated, and often indifferent retail algorithms. If a catalog as rich as yours isn’t backed by an equally sophisticated technical infrastructure, it risks being quietly buried on overcrowded digital shelves. The incredible energy you’ve poured into eleven books—and your lifelong commitment to the world of literature—deserves a digital architecture that fights back with the same intelligence you brought to the printing press. The publishing process doesn’t end when the book hits the shelf. It ends when your entire legacy is rendered unmissable to the modern world.

It’s a classic book marketing scam, albeit better informed than most. The main giveaway was that the sender had a Gmail address rather than a company address (and one that didn’t match the sender’s name, at that).

Three weeks ago, Don Levers, author of our forthcoming The (Almost) Perfect Heist: The Incredible True Story of the Vancouver Safety Deposit Robbery (order here), received an email from the “institutional content outreach team in partnership with Bloomsbury Publishing.” It said nice things about his book, which it couldn’t possibly have read, and claimed to be considering it for international distribution across its network. The sender signed off “Kind regards, Caledonia, Institutional Content Outreach, Bloomsbury Publishing Partnership Team.” Caledonia, too, had a Gmail address.

The publishing world is swimming in scams at the moment. There are Amazon scams where authors’ names are appropriated to sell AI-generated books. There are different Amazon scams where the contents of authors’ books are summarized by AI to create low-cost competitors to the originals. There are any number of fly-by-night shops that will promise to publish your book for a lot of money and deliver squat. But by far the most prevalent scams are of the marketing variety.

As we’ve discussed several times here, millions of books are published in North America every year and the vast majority of them fail to break through. That leaves writers frustrated and vulnerable to marketing scams. They land in an author’s inbox offering welcome praise and lament that said author’s book hasn’t received the attention it deserves. They offer to rectify that grave injustice.

It’s an ingenious strategy: you’ll live a long time before you meet an author who thinks their book got the attention it deserved. Writers all over the world are forwarding these fake emails to their publishers saying “See—this person gets it! Why didn’t you do all this?”

Inevitably, the promises of marketing breakthroughs involve a fee, often several thousand dollars. Victoria Strauss, co-founder of Writer Beware, has done some excellent reporting on these scams. She describes what happened to someone who took the bait:

They reported spending over £2,000 on services, including a video trailer, social media posts, email blasts, and a follower-building effort. Amelia [the scammer] really did deliver those items, taking over the writer’s social media accounts to do so—but they yielded little engagement, and the writer later discovered that most of the follower accounts were fake or inactive, and the email blast recipients consisted primarily of random addresses with no relevance to the writer’s book. The writer wound up having to delete their social media accounts.

More advanced versions of the scams begin with an email purporting to be from a famous author. You think, “wow, Stephen King read my novel and wants to chat!” So you chat, and Stephen wants to introduce you to his agent, who pretends to read your sample chapter and likes it but thinks it needs a little work and has a professional editor in mind who can help, for a fee.

Other variations involve famous authors writing with offers to promote your book themselves. Or random people emailing to urge you to submit to an agent who is right now looking for writing exactly like yours. Some scams contact writers through their Authors Guild member profiles to give them a veneer of authenticity.

There’s an international ghostwriting scam run by an outfit calling itself Harper Book Writers. It targets self-publishing authors, charging them about $4,000 for editing and publishing services, which it never delivers. Apparently it upcharges for Barnes & Noble store placement, which no publisher can guarantee.

The so-called Hachette Book Group scam also preys on the self-published. Authors receive fake emails from Hachette, a real publisher, claiming to have been impressed by their book on Amazon and offering a traditional publishing deal, for a fee. Hachette, according to the Authors Guild, has been impersonated in a number of frauds:

Tactics include fake job interviews and offers using Hachette’s name and logo, fraudulent publishing contract offers from fake agents that promise a large advance from Hachette and involve fabricated documents or claims of working with Hachette employees, and phone calls posing as Hachette staff demanding fees before entering into fake publishing agreements.

We’ve seen a fake literary festival courting authors for appearances. Fake book club invitations are common, usually involving a hosting fee. Another approach is the book-to-screen adaptation fraud, where a few thousand dollars will get your book into the Netflix pipeline. There are fake award scams, fake review scams, fake workshop scams, and fake podcast interview scams.

That’s a staggering amount of grift trained on an industry renowned for its low stakes. But then many of these operations are run out of Nigeria, where a few thousand dollars is a good annual salary. You have to admire the hustle. I’d read a novel set in a literary scam factory in Abuja.

Last week, I got hit myself.

There are a whole bunch of clues in that email, starting with the Gmail address, which I overlooked on first pass. I was busy luxuriating in the praise for my work. It took me a beat too long to think “Rodale does health and wellness—this makes no sense.” I googled Marine Cochran and landed at a site that sells shore power systems to cruise ships. That’s when I noticed that the sender had scraped Marnie Cochran’s email signature and misspelled her name in the body of the message, and sent it to me from a Gmail account.

It’s bittersweet to be scammed by someone with impeccable taste.

The one rule of scam emails is never respond. I told Marine I had a few ideas that I’d be happy to discuss.

How else was I going to determine the nature of the scam? The reply:

So she’s not interested in discussing my ideas. She seems interested in finding me representation, which sounds like some version of the agent gambit. It made me curious as to why Marine couldn’t have done a simple search to find out if I had representation. So I googled “Kenneth Whyte’s literary agent.”

I knew the late Dean Cooke and quite liked him, but we never did business.

Quite a mess we’ve made of our communications environment.
