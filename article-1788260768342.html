Republicans have a big midterm cash advantage

Democrats have alienated billionaires without winning back the working class.

Matthew Yglesias
Sep 1
∙
Paid
 
READ IN APP
 

The 2004 election was the first cycle that I covered professionally. John Kerry was badly outspent by George W. Bush that year, and there was nothing remarkable about this.

Kerry was a Democrat and Bush was a Republican. Republicans were the party of business and of the rich, so of course they were better at raising campaign funds. An incumbent Democratic member of Congress would often be able to outraise a Republican opponent. But Dem challengers and Dems running in competitive open races typically faced major campaign-finance headwinds. That’s just how the world worked.

Then a whole bunch of things happened at once.

The 2006 and 2008 cycles were really bad for Republicans, which hurt their fundraising and put wind at the backs of Democrats. The party coalitions were also starting to shift, with Democrats attracting more and more college-educated professionals who weren’t necessarily “rich” but certainly capable of putting a few hundred bucks behind candidates they liked. And the relatively new ability to raise money online meant that it was suddenly feasible for a popular politician to raise significant amounts of money from those smaller donors.

This trend was reinforced by individual politicians. Barack Obama was well liked in Silicon Valley at a time when those companies were increasingly becoming the dominant force in the American economy. And then came Donald Trump, who in his first race in particular alienated a lot of traditional Republican Party donor types.

As a result of this kind of Long Realignment of campaign spending, we now have a large cohort — people a bit younger than me who weren’t engaged with the 2004 campaign — who don’t remember what it’s like for the center-left party to be at a financial disadvantage. In particular, a lot of progressives love to rant about billionaires and oligarchy and taking on the power of big money, but they usually do it in a way that treats Democratic Party donors as the enemy to overcome without much thought to the financial balance in the general election.

But I think it’s worth noting that things have changed, again.

Democrats’ current midterm polling feels almost too good to be true, and there’s a theory that summer polls are producing an illusory result. That may be the case.

But rather than posit polling error, I would like to raise the concern that, while James Talarico and Mary Peltola really would win if the election were held tomorrow, between Labor Day and Election Day every Dem running in a “reach” seat is gonna get hit with a barrage of ads aiming to amp up polarization and keep these seats red, all powered by a resurgence in Republican fundraising.

If your mentality is “well of course there’s always a lot of ads but who cares,” you may not be prepared for how much the financial balance of power has shifted.

Republican committees have a big — and new — money edge

Back in July, Jessica Piper at Politico went through everything in the latest round of financial reports and showed that Republicans have a systematic cash advantage. The Republican Senate committee has more than its Democratic counterpart and so does the House committee. So do their aligned super PACs. The R.N.C. is walloping the D.N.C. at fundraising. And then there’s this mysterious $400 million Trump super PAC known as MAGA Inc.

And that’s not even all the money that’s out there.

Elon Musk says he’ll invest some unspecified huge sum in a G.O.P.-base mobilization operation.

As Piper notes, this is a meaningful change from the quite recent past. At this point in the 2024 cycle, Democratic Senate orgs had more money than their G.O.P. counterparts, and the House fundraising gap was just $22 million. The D.N.C. significantly outraised the R.N.C. in 2024.

And 2024 itself represented a major narrowing of the financial advantages that Democrats had enjoyed earlier in the Trump era. Some of that is Musk personally, who is just so rich he can single-handedly shift the balance of power.

But a lot of it is a broader realignment among tech donors.

Marc Andreessen has played a prominent but somewhat confusing role in this. Andreessen himself is a longtime Republican who was a major bundler for Mitt Romney at a time when this was unfashionable in Silicon Valley. Nothing he says about his gripes with the Biden administration explains anything at all about why he is a big G.O.P. moneyman. What changed is that Andreessen’s longstanding argument that tech sector executives should enthusiastically support Republicans started to convince people, notably founders and executives.

I think everyone should be careful with how they talk about this, though, because as this David Shor scatterplot shows, most of the workers in the tech industry who donate — even at Palantir and SpaceX and Anduril — donate to Democrats.

What’s changed is that the dollar-weighted contributions from a lot of these companies now favor Republicans because the richest guys at the top are Republicans, and they donate a lot.

This is, in many ways, sort of the natural order of things. The center-left party is more popular with rank-and-file workers, and the bosses support the center-right.

But because the image of “tech” in the minds of people who don’t work in tech is so dominated by high-profile executives spouting off on Twitter and podcasts, I’m worried everyone on the left is convincing themselves that “tech bros” are all right wing and that this risks polarizing these rank-and-file tech workers against the Democratic Party. And I find it concerning that so many progressives talk as if having a private-sector job per se is corrupt and the only ethical way to make money is to be a nonprofit lifer, an heir, or a divorcée.

Money and its limits

Of course, if campaign spending won elections, then Jaime Harrison would be a United States senator today. On some level, I think the Trump-era reversal of the parties’ traditional financial situations was responsible for some unhealthy developments on the Democratic side — most notably, a lot of public messaging that was primarily geared toward firing up online donors rather than working to persuade swing voters. The financial reversal also led to a lot of money being wasted on candidates who ran as conventional progressives in red states.

On the other hand, it’s a mistake to conclude that money doesn’t matter.

The Wisconsin gubernatorial primary provided a good illustration of this. David Crowley ran a bunch of late-in-the-game television ads, but he didn’t run them in the handful of Wisconsin counties that get their TV from out-of-state stations. Francesca Hong did notably better in that northwest corner of the state than in other similar places.

I think this is probably part of the story of the Michigan Senate primary, too.

There’s a lot of discourse about polling error in that race, and I’m sure there were some sampling problems since primary electorates are hard to model. But there was also a ton of late pro-Haley Stevens ad spending, and I think it probably genuinely swayed a lot of people.

It can be hard for political obsessives to internalize this, but to voters who don’t care very much about Israel/Palestine and don’t follow factional conflicts closely (which is to say, most voters), Stevens and Abdul El-Sayed are not that distinct. Ads that are just like “here’s Stevens working with Obama, here’s El-Sayed saying mean things about Kamala Harris” can shift their opinions.

You’d expect this to be less effective in a general election, though.

In a general election context, just knowing which party the candidates belong to conveys a lot of information. But I’d say that goes to show that the context and the strategy matter a lot. If you look back at the failed Jaime Harrison campaign, no matter how much money he spent, almost none of it seemed like it was designed to appeal to the median voter in South Carolina, who is quite conservative compared with the national average.

Contrast that with this Josh Turek ad. I haven’t seen any testing or metrics on this, so I can’t tell you whether the ad is effective or ineffective. But the theory of the ad is obvious. Turek is running in a Trump-Trump-Trump state, and he thinks that Iowa voters find Democrats too soft on immigration, so he’s running an ad to inform them that he is not soft on immigration, and he’s backing that up by citing specific things he did in the state legislature.

Will it work? I don’t know. But the way it works, if it does work, is that the ad spending complements the moderate position-taking. It doesn’t substitute for it. Advertising is a good way to make sure voters hear about something. But you still need the thing they hear to be worthwhile.

I suspect Democrats’ 2026 fundraising woes stem in part from burnout following the incredible sums spent on Kamala Harris’s 2024 campaign. But I don’t think “ads don’t work” or “money doesn’t matter” is the right inference to draw from her defeat.

The heavily targeted swing states, after all, did shift right by much less than the national average. The problem for the campaign was that the reality of Harris’s record didn’t give the ad makers much to work with in terms of “Hey, I bet you didn’t know X.”

By the same token, whatever hand-wringing I do in this post, the most important fact about the 2026 election is that a large swath of people who voted for Trump in 2024 are angry about his broken promises on affordability. Places that Trump won by one or two or three percentage points will swing, and Democrats will win the House. But the second most important fact is that the map is still structurally difficult for Democrats: because of gerrymandering, there aren’t very many seats Trump won by just a few points, and because of the distribution of Senate seats, Dems need to win several reach races to win a majority there.

Everything is tradeoffs

Just as Turek’s advertising serves as a force multiplier for his moderate position-taking, the big risk for Democrats is that Republicans’ financial edge serves as a major force multiplier for the favorable maps.

When you’re talking about an election in a state like Alaska or Ohio or Texas or Iowa, there’s an almost bottomless pile of facts that amount to “I’m a Republican and my opponent is a Democrat.” Of course, that argument doesn’t work as well with Trump’s national approval rating as low as it is. And in Iowa and Ohio, at least, there’s a reservoir of Trump voters who voted for Democrats earlier in their lives and may not be totally opposed to doing so again.

But it’s still true that Turek and James Talarico and Mary Peltola and Sherrod Brown are all Democrats and most of the voters in their states are not.

When you’re running uphill like that, it’s really helpful to have the campaign cash. Money alone won’t win the race, but if you take smart positions and say smart things, then money can make sure people hear about all of that and also ensure that they hear all your attacks on your opponent and also hear that you have conventional popular Dem messaging on things like health care.

In other words, I think that the kind of financial edge that Democrats had in 2020 combined with the much smarter position-taking on issues that many of them are doing in 2026 could make these reach races winnable.

The good news for Democrats is that despite lagging badly in committee fundraising, most of their key Senate candidates are beating their respective opponents in individual donations. Turek in Iowa is a notable exception, which is why he’s Slow Boring’s top Senate donation recommendation.

The other candidate I would flag here, though, is Abdul El-Sayed, who has a very narrow lead in individual contributions over Mike Rogers and is going to face a torrent of AIPAC money in the general election.

If the Democratic committees were more flush than their G.O.P. counterparts, they could try to come in to compensate. But they’re not. So basically every target Senate race is going to end up feeling like they’re not getting enough support from the national party because they genuinely won’t be getting enough support from the national party because there’s not enough money to go around.

I think those on the left, having gotten the nominee they wanted in this race, need to spend less time getting in fights about Hasan Piker and more time raising money among their own people for El-Sayed.

Way to Win and Data for Progress are for some reason touting Democrats’ odds in the Mississippi Senate race, which seems like crazy overreach in a world of resource scarcity. I don’t particularly feel like urging my readers to reach into their pockets for El-Sayed, because I’m not the one who said it was a good idea to nominate a leftist in a swing state. I think Turek and Peltola and Don Davis and Bobby Pulido and Johnny Garcia and the other candidates are where I’d prefer to send scarce dollars.

But if you’re a progressive organization that doesn’t want to raise money for moderates, then get your money to the progressive in a winnable race in Michigan!

More broadly, I think Democrats have shifted back into their historic role as financial underdogs in a somewhat unconsidered way. They haven’t, for example, done this while regaining their historic role as the party of working-class voters, because accomplishing that requires more than just pressing the “economic populism” button. The financial picture should be good enough to win the House in 2026, but I think it’s going to be an additional headwind in the battle for Senate control.

And it’s worth thinking ahead to 2028 and asking how Democrats can stop being both the party with the richer coalition and the party that gets outspent.
