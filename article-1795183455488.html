Book Is a Four-Letter Word

Are you even an American if you can’t swear?

Ron Charles
Nov 20
 
READ IN APP
 

When I was growing up, nobody in our house could swear except my mother, who never ventured beyond theologically tinged words like damn and hell.

Once when I was 12 or 13, I overheard my father use the word bastard while talking to someone at the office on the phone, and I thought I might lose consciousness.

At my Christian prep school, we children of God were warned that using profanity was intellectually lazy, but even at the risk of singeing my wings, I didn’t believe that. There was nothing lazy about sinning with your tongue — the possibility was unspeakably thrilling.

Still, as a grown-ass man, I can’t swear worth a damn. Profanity, like any language, is hard to pick up later in life. A colleague once told me there was something deeply unnerving about hearing me swear, like watching a dog use a stethoscope.

I fantasize about eviscerating some insufferable fool with the verve of Peter Capaldi in The Thick of It — “He’s as useless as a marzipan dildo” — but I haven’t the linguistic finesse or the sexual experience.

My ambivalence, it turns out, is deeply American. As Steven H. Jaffe explains in his new book, Four-Letter Nation: The Long, Dirty History of American Profanity, we’ve always reviled and relished bad words.

According to Jaffe’s engaging history, English settlers arrived in the New World with shipfuls of shocking phrases and a determination to snuff them out. In 1664, John Porter Jr. denounced his parents in such creatively obscene terms that a Massachusetts court almost sentenced him to death.

And it wasn’t just naughty Johnny. The list of saucy insults that Jaffe ferrets out from Puritan records would make Richard Pryor blush.

Prohibitions against profanity gradually shifted from matters of religion to standards of class, but the attraction to the nasty side never waned. It was built right into America’s founding. As much as profanity might shock and disgust, it could, when employed effectively, also establish one’s rebellious, virile spirit. Let prissy John Adams clutch his pearls; Washington and Franklin knew when to let a curse or a blue word fly.

And Honest Abe — ladies, cover your ears! Lincoln’s colorful tale of a young man’s amorous relationship with his cat once caused members of the fair sex to faint.

It was the terror and boredom of battle, though, that really brought salty language into common parlance. You didn’t hear it from Ken Burns’s Civil War documentary, but for the lexicographer pursuing the evolution of American obscenity, soldiers’ diaries and letters are a veritable treasure chest of smut, profanity, and scatological humor. (Even teenage Thomas Edison, the future Wizard of Menlo Park, exchanged “bawdy jokes in Morse code with other young key tappers across the Union.”)

By the end of the Civil War, despite the efforts of self-appointed word police, it was Damn the torpedoes! Full speed ahead!

“Profanity, it seemed, did not necessarily undermine patriotism, self-sacrifice, and courage,” Jaffe writes. “Maybe, in the heat of battle, profanity even fortified such sterling qualities.”

After all, he continues, “Profanity was manly,” and middle-class America, with its boring office work and stultifying suburbs, didn’t leave many avenues for scratching one’s crotch. A generation of earnest young men learned how to sneer and swear from Hemingway.

As Four-Letter Nation sweeps through the 20th century, Jaffe examines the way baseball stadiums, jazz clubs, the Great Migration, and the tumult of the Sixties corroded the “verbal rules of genteel middle-class convention.” Dirty words were already pawing at Beaver Cleaver’s windows.

Nixon’s Watergate tapes, feminism’s rejection of Victorian decorum, and gay rights’ sexual candor would soon break the panes.

Now we have so few truly forbidden words left — Jaffe counts three — that profanity feels like it’s going to hell in a handbasket. And Donald Trump’s barbaric yawp would seem to have removed all limits — and thrills.

But don’t despair, little potty mouths.

“It’s always been a mistake to bet against the survival of profanity,” Jaffe writes. “That there have to be unspeakable and hence powerful words seems a timeless human trait.”

What shocks us will continue to slip around, but we’ll always crave the meat of transgression.

Pick another protein? Sheeeit. I’d rather pick another sentence instead.◆

Books Go to the Movies

  • In 1983, when Roald Dahl was finishing up work on his next bestseller, The Witches, he made some shockingly antisemitic remarks. Giant, a gripping play by Mark Rosenblatt, imagines a conversation between Dahl and a representative of his American publisher sent to coax a public apology from the prickly author. A filmed performance of John Lithgow’s award-winning portrayal of Dahl is playing this weekend at select theaters (trailer). Don’t miss it. Dawn and I saw the play in London a couple of years ago and were blown away.

  • Amy Poehler and Hugh Laurie star in a wacky sitcom called Dig about an archaeological project in Greece (trailer). The 10-episode series, which starts streaming on Peacock Nov. 23, is based on Kate Myers’s 2023 comic novel, Excavations, about a discovery that could overturn ancient history.

  • Scottish actor Ian Charleson, best known for Chariots of Fire, played Hamlet at London’s National Theatre in 1989 while he was dying of AIDS. Elsinore, starring Andrew Scott as Charleson and Olivia Colman as his doctor, tells that remarkable story of physical endurance and artistic triumph (trailer). The movie, which opens today in select theaters, is already inspiring enthusiastic Oscar talk.◆

You’ve Got Mail Essays

Until a few days ago, my favorite holiday card showed a puddle with the message, “Have a Merry Christmas. Frosty would have wanted it that way.”

But now I’m ready to up my holiday card game.

Biblioasis, an indie publisher in Ontario, has just released the most delicious thing since mini marshmallows. For the price of a high-end greeting card ($7.95), you get a nicely bound essay with space for your holiday message, plus an envelope. It’s a new series of mailable chapbooks called “Greetings, Chap!”

Best of all is the first essay they’ve chosen: “I Don’t Even Really Like Christmas,” by Richard Kelly Kemick (order).

“I can’t remember not wanting a miniature Christmas village,” Kemick begins. “It’s like how I can’t remember when I first realized I have bad posture; there are some parts to yourself you never have to learn but just have to accept.”

And up he goes, like Dasher and Dancer, delivering the funniest Christmas confession since David Sedaris donned elf ears.

What does it mean for an otherwise normal adult man to be off his eggnog about an ever-expanding, increasingly expensive Christmas village?

Kemick knows this hobby borders on madness — he’s secretly selling his wife’s clothes to buy tiny wreaths and sleigh bells — but what’s Santa if not a little crazy?

Aside from a brisk sleigh ride through the culture of miniature Christmas villages, “I Don’t Even Really Like Christmas” is a brilliant reflection on the impossibility of explaining the manias that define us.

Kemick threads the garland with just the right berries of wit and pathos. His village, inhabited by some 60 little people, sports 18 buildings, including a bakery and a chocolate shop, a hockey rink and a wee theater.

But this is no Miracle on 34th Street. “Mine is a city that understands the true weight of mortality,” he notes. Look closely and you’ll see “a man feeding ducks, forlorn because he’s in love with his brother-in-law. There’s a chimney sweep who looks like shit and should’ve unionized years ago but instead drinks his paycheque at the tavern and goes home to a wife who doesn’t love him and kids who don’t respect him.”

What might Kemick tell the 14-year-old kid he hears about just one town over who’s on his way to assembling the largest miniature Christmas village in Canada? Should he encourage this rival or knock over his snowmen?

Even if you’ve long said “Bah, humbug!” to holiday cards, I guarantee you have friends who would love to receive this essay from you.◆

No sooner had the music from the fancy dress balls of his second inauguration faded than President Trump began a merciless dismantling of American international food aid, the domestic safety net, and the USDA programs encouraging the nation’s family farmers to grow as much as they could. The catalytic momentum of innovations to end hunger sparked during the pandemic was bluntly deactivated. What remained, though, were the lines.

As Thanksgiving of 2025 neared, residents of Hale and Greene Counties in Alabama rose before the sun did to make their way to the Greensboro recreation center. The traffic backed up for more than a half mile, beyond where the paved road gave way to a dirt path. Within two hours, 500 turkeys and 500 hams, along with bags stuffed with collard greens, sweet potatoes, and cans of corn and green beans, were gone.

From “Abiding Hunger: An American Paradox,” by Roger Thurow (Agate Surrey).◆

Picture This

When Dawn and I attended college in Southern Illinois, the only restaurant we could walk to was Elsah Landing. It was an excruciatingly quaint eatery in an old wooden building so close to the Mississippi River that sometimes flooding made it unreachable.

I learned a lot about literature in college, but I learned a lot more about pie at Elsah Landing. Helen Crafton, wife of the college’s football coach, introduced me to Cranberry Raisin Pie, Scintillating Lemon Pie, Chocolate Cherry Pie, California Strawberry Pie, Mocha Toffee Pie, and so many other lifelong friends.

To this day, the best thing Dawn makes is a Black Bottom Pie, described in her yellowed copy of The Elsah Landing Restaurant Cookbook, which long ago lost its plastic spine. (If you see a used copy online, buy it.)

Amy Hest clearly understands the special status of pie. Her lovely new picture book, The Thing About Pie, follows a child waking up early to make a blueberry pie.

“The thing about baking is the smell,” Hest writes. “Oh, that glorious smell when your pie is in the oven. You with your nose in the air, sniffing left like this — and right like this — that smell getting more and more glorious.”

Hest’s genius is her restraint. She has a delicate, incantatory style, but she’s more than willing to stay quiet and let Christopher Thornock’s gentle illustrations of the house, city, and countryside carry parts of this story.

A little drama interrupts the idyll — “BUMP! And there goes your pie” — but never fear. Grampa will get to share this treat.◆

This Week’s Poem

It’s been only five weeks since I excerpted a poem from Brenda Hillman’s Still House in the Desert, but Wednesday night she won the National Book Award for Poetry, and her publisher has kindly allowed me to share another piece from this remarkable collection about memory, family, and the environment.

In her acceptance speech, Hillman thanked her husband, the poet Robert Hass, who won a National Book Award for Poetry in 2007. “We have a matching set,” she said, holding up her heavy new trophy. “It’ll be nice to have one next to his.”

The Edge of the Almost There

The coyote has left a dawn trail in the ditch
& the mouse will return to the granary.

A whiptail lizard races through the patio;
it has laid mostly female eggs;
there will be many brides from now on.

Your mother’s house is empty but welcoming.

Clouds make a seersucker jet stream

over bricks where the grapefruit tree was.

The childhood phone number falls from a wire
with the voice of the boy who died
& one who managed more than he could bear.

You stand at the slightly stuck sliding glass door
as she used to stand in her threadbare shirt
regarding her garden, both pleased & displeased,

a soul between incarnations,
a cricket resting on the carapace it has shed.

From “Still House in the Desert: An eco-contemplation,” by Brenda Hillman. Copyright © 2026 by Brenda Hillman. Published by Wesleyan University Press. Used by permission.◆

A Man Is Known by the Books He Wears

How many times have you wanted to bring along a novel but didn’t have any way to carry it? Your purse is too small. Your bag is already too heavy. It won’t fit in your coat.

That’s exactly the challenge that inspired the new Dust Jacket.

Tim Trayhurn wanted to take his dogs on a long walk so they’d be tired enough to let him read by the lake. But he couldn’t fit a book in his pocket.

“I was amused by the irony of it,” he tells me from his home in Norwich, England, “because I’d done a lit degree, and it’s almost de rigueur for style-conscious men to have a French-style chore coat. If you went to the English faculty library, it was just like a parade of hipsters.”

But you can’t fit a copy of David Foster Wallace’s Infinite Jest into those stylish pockets.

As Jack Kerouac or someone once said, Irony is no match for a man with a needle and thread.

Determined to solve his sartorial dilemma, Trayhurn sewed big pockets onto his own coat. The Dust Jacket was born.

“Casting my mind back to my university days, I’m thinking, ‘Okay, what would I take out with me to the library or to the cafe or wherever it is that I’m studying that day?’ I wanted these capacious pockets so that I could have all my reading accoutrements.”

With a background in marketing, Trayhurn found a manufacturer in Portugal and turned full-time to selling heavy cotton jackets with multiple expanding pockets that can carry around a shelf of books. (Starting at $156.)

“The idea of a jacket just for books is so decadent and over the top,” he says, “so it needs to be really fantastically useful.”

(Running up against Southwest’s carry-on restrictions? Load up your Dust Jacket and board the plane!)

“The crossover between people who are interested in clothes and people who are interested in books seems quite natural,” he says while I’m wearing a Lands’ End T-shirt from the Reagan administration and cargo shorts so threadworn I don’t dare turn around in public.

As a teenager, Trayhurn read Fahrenheit 451 and became convinced of “the virtues of reading literature,” but in his early 30s he already feels time’s winged chariot hurrying near.

“You go into a big bookstore,” he says, “and there is a pang of existential dread about all the books you’ll never read.”

Consequently, you’ll find the pockets of his Dust Jacket packed with the classics. “They’ve been remembered for a reason,” he says. “They’ve been passed down to me for a reason.”

The same might be said someday of his ingenious jacket, which comes in ecru and a variety of bold colors, including some that only bees can see.

If my modeling career takes off, this could be my last newsletter.
