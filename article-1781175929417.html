Can we retire “public safety”?

Just talk about crime.

Matthew Yglesias
Jun 11
∙
Paid
 
READ IN APP
 

In the Los Angeles mayoral race, Nithya Raman — a left-YIMBY candidate who was in the Democratic Socialists of America but didn’t get its nomination and wasn’t supported by any of the D.S.A. members of the City Council — made it into a runoff with incumbent Mayor Karen Bass, an icon of the failed status quo in an American city that isn’t living up to its strong underlying fundamentals.

The third wheel in this race, Spencer Pratt, got a lot of attention from conservatives who don’t live in L.A. and raised some good points about problems in the city. But he fundamentally failed to even try to connect with actual public opinion in a place that is heavily Democratic.

Marc Novicoff (who longtime subscribers will remember from his days as Slow Boring’s very first research assistant) wrote a great piece about this fairly depressing three-sided race.

Now that it’s down to two sides, though, there are basically two ways it could break.

One is that Bass could mobilize fear of Raman’s more out there left-wing takes to reach Pratt supporters and the kind of low-engagement part of the electorate that doesn’t vote in primaries. The other is that Raman could make good on (1) the promise of big tent YIMBYism and (2) the reality that she doesn’t actually owe anything to the institutional forces of either the status quo or the far-left and put together a unifying reform message.

I don’t know her, or the city, or the different elite actors in Southern California well enough to know exactly how she’d pull that off.

I’d probably start by saying nice things about Dan Lurie in San Francisco, since he’s really the mayor who tapped into what’s good about Pratt (normie anguish about West Coast cities letting their downtowns turn into shitholes) but not into what’s bad about him (being a Republican who doesn’t actually seem like he could administer a city).

But a really quick piece of advice I’d offer to her, or to anyone looking to moderate their image, would be to run through all their communications materials and purge mentions of “public safety” in favor of the word “crime.”

By the same token, I was chatting with some people the other day about why Baltimore Mayor Brandon Scott doesn’t get more credit as an anti-crime hero. That’s an interesting question, and in response I’ll probably write a post detailing the successes of the Charm City’s focused deterrence approach and what other places could learn from it. But I don’t think it helps Scott’s credit-taking efforts that he describes what he’s doing as combating “gun violence,” as if the end goal is to have Baltimoreans stabbing each other.

In general, I think purely linguistic questions tend to be a bit overrated in politics. What matters most is what elected officials do and the substantive positions that they take.

But crime really is a topic where most jurisdictions’ outcomes are now improving and most politicians have walked away from the substance of bad ideas. That’s all great, but it also means that it makes sense to try to talk like a normal person. All of the ideas Raman embraces under the heading of “public safety” are about crime. These are not proposals about traffic calming or coyote attacks or mudslides or anvils falling out of the sky in “Looney Tunes.” If Raman wants to build a broad anti-Bass coalition, she needs to talk like a person who has some sympathy for the substance of Pratt voters’ concerns. And that means moving beyond the euphemisms coined in the progressive NGO complex.

A euphemism that’s outlived its usefulness

Many conservatives are excessively hung up on stuff that happened six years ago relative to egregious conduct by the current ruling coalition. But in Democratic Party circles, nobody wants to explicitly talk about how crazy things got in 2020.

One aspect of this is that it became conventional wisdom, briefly, that anyone talking about crime as a problem was just doing racism.

This became an issue almost immediately for politicians, often themselves African-American, who represented relatively high-crime neighborhoods and whose constituents very much did worry about crime.

As crime started rising in 2020, it was untenable to be totally indifferent to these concerns. And so “public safety” arose as a constructive intervention in the discourse. A person could talk about “public safety” to signal that they weren’t one of those bad tough-on-crime racists, but also weren’t one of those LARPing gentrifiers or out-of-touch suburbanites who had no idea what was going on.

Similarly, even when progressives stopped talking about crime, they didn’t stop supporting gun control. So they needed a way to express the idea that it’s bad when people shoot each other, and “gun violence” stepped into the fray.

In the particular circumstances of 2020, this all worked out well enough. Joe Biden and mainstream Democrats did not come out for defunding the police. Neither did the mayors of most large cities. And while the reaction to the 2020 crime jump was too slow, it wasn’t like the 1970s and 1980s when we had a generational increase in crime. Most jurisdictions pretty quickly decided to take “public safety” seriously, and in 2022 the numbers started turning around.

But even if we accept “public safety” as a useful rhetorical tool, this is like a ladder that we now need to kick away.

Raman doesn’t need to avoid cancellation from the progressive NGO borg. She needs to persuade Angelenos hungry for change that her version of a pro-housing L.A. government doesn’t mean every neighborhood will turn into Skid Row. That calls for being specific and addressing voters’ full range of concerns.

Lots of kinds of crime are bad

Murder is both the worst crime and also the one that is measured most precisely, so people rightly pay a lot of attention to statistics around murder and related crimes of violence.

But part of the utility of the word “crime” is that it speaks to a broader set of concerns than just violence, but also something more specific than “safety.” When I stayed in Downtown L.A. over Christmas a few years ago, I witnessed more than one person peeing on the sidewalk. This is illegal (I assume) but while I’m sure it would make some people feel unsafe, I have enough experience with urban living that I wasn’t particularly frightened by street urinators.

Nonetheless, like most people, I prefer not to walk around on streets that smell like urine. There’s a lot of cool stuff in D.T.L.A. and a lot of opportunity to convert more surface parking lots into apartment buildings and shops and make it a place with even more cool stuff. But the street urinators are bad for business over and above any objective threat to “safety.” The guys camped out in Pershing Square make the park less useful for its main purpose as a location for recreation. The guy I saw passed out in front of the Dunkin’ who was adjacent to a pool of (presumably his own) vomit wasn’t physically threatening; he was incapacitated. But those scenes are not a good advertisement for mixed-use transit-oriented developments.

More broadly, while progressives are interested in linkages between crime and poverty, I think they mostly get the direction of causation wrong.

I’m rich, so while I’m annoyed that I’ve had two e-bikes stolen in D.C., it’s not the end of the world. But property theft can be devastating if you’re poor. If your car gets stolen and you can’t get to work on time and you lose your job, that’s a disaster. If a small business needs to invest in extensive anti-theft measures, that means it’s harder for them to expand. If a neighborhood’s stores suffer from extensive shoplifting, then stores are less likely to open, so residents have fewer job opportunities. The McDonald’s by my house has removed all the chairs from its dining room because they had been so frequently occupied by vagrants and it became impossible for the restaurant to enforce the rules against loitering.

I can survive without a sit-down McDonald’s experience, but it does deprive people in the area of what’s probably the cheapest “dining out with the kids” experience.

All the way on the other end of the spectrum, there’s a broad terrain of Rich Guy Crimes that Republicans don’t care about. But large-scale tax evasion is a serious problem. The only way to pull this off, really, is to own a business, so G.O.P. indulgence of tax cheats ultimately shifts more and more fiscal burden onto the shoulders of normal people with normal jobs. That’s bad even if it doesn’t impair “public safety.”

We just have a lot of rules in society that make things functional — you need to pay your taxes, you can’t pee on the street — even if the individual instances are not as serious as murder.

Something we’ve seen in recent years with shoplifting is that there are tipping points: Once word got out on social media that chain store employees weren’t going to physically intervene to stop people from stealing, the formerly minor problem of small-scale retail theft became a bigger problem.

It’s why crime, broadly, is bad. If you don’t think something should be a crime, then that’s fine — make it legal to smoke marijuana. But if there’s a fare to ride the bus, then people who don’t pay the fare should be punished. Don’t commit crimes!

Who are you listening to?

Of course, some on the left are still loud-and-proud police de-funders or prison abolitionists. But for candidates who aren’t, and especially for candidates who hopped on the anti-enforcement bandwagon but want to convince voters that they’ve seen the error of their ways, I think the very utility of the “public safety” euphemism is why it’s now obsolete.

What we’re talking about, essentially, is a verbal formula designed to ward off criticism from progressive groups and nonprofits.

But what someone in Raman’s position wants to do is ward off concerns that she’ll be excessively responsive to those groups. When Zohran Mamdani said he no longer believed the things he’d said about crime and policing in 2020, enough people believed him to win an election in New York City, but lots of people didn’t believe him.

Then he retained the former administration’s police commissioner, crime continued to fall in his first months in office, and the N.Y.P.D. grew in size. This has now become a topic that is beyond the level of speculating about what he “really” believes and whether he’s “really” changed his mind — a politician is what he does, and what Mamdani is doing is acting like a person who cares more about reducing crime than he does about avoiding criticism from anti-enforcement leftists.

There was a bizarre piece in Gothamist recently about extra officers getting deployed to high-crime neighborhoods in the Bronx that takes until the 29th paragraph — after many quotations of left-wing professors and activists — to share the perspective of a guy who lives in the area and is excited about the idea of more cops on the beat.

The key thing is that Mamdani clearly does not care at this point if a reasonable tactical decision draws unreasonable criticism from unreasonable people.

And politicians need to use rhetoric that conveys their lack of interest in these unreasonable people.

Back in the summer of 2020, when I was saying that policing reduces crime at a time when that was an unfashionable take, Alec Karakatsanis rebutted me with an article arguing that the whole idea of “crime” is a mistaken framing.

There’s a certain intellectual consistency to that. The abolitionists sincerely reject the whole ordinary language construct of “crime.” Most people, though, do think it’s a real thing and want politicians to talk about it plainly, which is a reason that saying that police reduce crime by arresting criminals and deterring others from committing crimes is a powerful formula.

Of course, there are other things besides punishment and deterrence that you can and should do to help people in troubled situations. But we in fact know that people from all walks of life commit crimes for financial gain when they think they can get away with it, and purely focusing on social service delivery will no more stop shoplifting rings or drug crews than it will stop tax evasion or Medicare fraud.

Fight crime! It’s a perfectly good word.
