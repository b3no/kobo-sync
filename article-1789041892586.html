She Didn't Pick Chad--She Picked the Sheet Mask

Meeting or dating new people is full of uncertainty and unknowns--and for many young singles, that fails the ROI test.

There are two pretty standard genderslop takes about the dating landscape for today’s singles, depending on your gender or “team.” Some men claim that dating apps have inflated women’s egos and given them all the belief that they should settle for nothing less than Christian Grey himself, spinning up fiction about the increasingly ridiculous height preferences (at one point, it was supposedly six feet, now I’m hearing it’s 6’5”.) Then, to add insult to injury, apparently these women are engaging in nonstop sexual debauchery with said giant-scale Chads while being “flown to Miami.” (Of course the 8’9” Chad is flying you to Miami—he’s too tall to fit in an airplane himself!) Meanwhile, plenty of women will push back and say that actually, the bar for men is “on the floor,” and if men weren’t all depraved, porn-addicted, misogynistic, cheating jerks, women would be perfectly happy to date them. Some have taken the stance that even straight women never particularly liked men, and it’s only Gen Z women who are emboldened to admit it.

Either way, clearly something isn’t working, and in both cases the assumption is that, for whatever reason, modern-day women are not interested in dating most men.

That conclusion is not entirely unfounded if you look at the current state of affairs. Over half of Gen Z is single, and many of those people are not actively dating, while 78% still want marriage someday, indicating that there is a discrepancy between current behaviors and desired outcome. But the issue is likely not quite as gendersloppy as “women all want Chad” or “all men are losers.” While attractive playboys exist and always have, they are not hoovering up all the women and engaging in nonstop orgies. A lot of women just aren’t having sex at all. Zoomer women are having less sex than their millennial predecessors, to the point that sex positivity, casual sex and hookup culture are seen as painfully passé. And while losers have always existed, there are plenty of decent men and women who want partners, but can’t seem to find them. (When I ran a qualitative survey for all singles, both men and women complained at length about all their dates being flaky or ghosting.)

In fact, the dating crisis probably mirrors the general loneliness crisis in one particular way: a lot of people may theoretically want a serious romantic partner someday, and may not harbor any hateful feelings toward any gender, but cannot find the motivation to engage in the unpredictable, sometimes disappointing, embarrassing, boring or awkward world of dating and relationships—especially when they have so many other things they could be doing instead.

A new article in The Times was just published the other day, titled, Why My Gen Z Mates Don’t Have Boyfriends. And, well, the answer appears to be “because they’d rather be doing something else.” In one memorable story from a friend of the author, the validation from having an attractive man hit on her is more than enough to scratch her romantic itch, and not compelling enough to warrant dating him. (According to her, there was nothing wrong with him either, she just wasn’t sure she wanted to risk the possibility of bad sex—ignoring the fact that she does not have to have sex just because she went on a date!) And this isn’t even an issue of terminal phone addiction. The women in the article appear to have very full lives—they’re part of hobby clubs, they see friends, they are not stuck on the couch all day. But they cannot muster the enthusiasm to put effort into dating, and are quick to find excuses to get the ick. The author admits, in a very Seinfeld-esque way, that she stopped seeing a man after seeing him make an error when using an ATM machine.

It all reminds me of the childfree people who claim they would want children, if not for some horrible barrier. They would have children, except climate change. They would have children, except they need a combined income of $200K to even consider it. They would want children, but what if their son turns out to be the next Jeffrey Epstein? It gets to a point where you have to wonder if they really “would” want kids, or if perhaps they just don’t see parenthood as being worth giving up their current degree of freedom and leisure time.

Granted, this article was not drawing on a large sample size of women—just the author and her friends, who may be posturing for the sake of getting an article published. Plenty of writers have attempted to create big statements about social trends which clearly aren’t happening on any grand scale, such as the idea that millennial women are increasingly dating Gen Z men, (courtesy of Vogue). But most of the stories from the Times article have one thing in common: even when these women are attracted to a man, they don’t seem to actually want a relationship badly enough to risk the effort, uncertainty, and potential embarrassment or boredom of getting there.

These women are not passing over swaths of normal, decent men in order to share a small segment of top-tier men. Often, they are passing over men entirely to just do things that they are guaranteed to enjoy—things that don’t involve men. Unlike time spent with familiar friends or alone, dating is full of the potential for blunders, embarrassment, bad conversation, and other mishaps, even if nothing rises to the level of violence or trauma. This is not an article about women who are scared of men, or even women who dislike men—just women who would rather be doing literally anything else. One woman was happy to report that a man would lose out against her face mask, not just if he wasn’t impressive enough, but every time.

I don’t want to extrapolate too much from one particular woman, because it’s entirely possible that she truly doesn’t want a boyfriend and will continue to feel that way. If so, that’s her prerogative! But the way she speaks about predictably enjoyable, familiar activities beating out the unknown every single time reminds me of how many other people talk about socializing with new people at all (romantic or platonic) all while lamenting the loneliness epidemic. If there is any risk of being bored, feeling awkward or embarrassed, or not having fun, socializing becomes “not worth it.” The only problem is that when many people behave this way about meeting new people, people become lonelier, often without realizing the role they played in this outcome.

So, are women uniquely uninterested in dating men, or is everyone less open to new experiences? It’s a mix. Young single women definitely feel wary of the current crop of single men, in part because of things like porn addiction and manosphere content consumption (and this was confirmed via an earlier study I ran, where women were far more likely than men to express trepidation about the opposite sex.) But women also are just more likely to have fuller platonic social lives than men to begin with. From my recent survey of almost 4,000 respondents, when filtering for single people in their twenties, women were in a slightly better place socially than their male peers across the board. They were more likely to have friends from work, more likely to have seen friends in the past week, and more confident in their ability to throw a party with ten or twenty attendees. Men were slightly more likely than women to have gotten zero party invitations in the past month.

This is not as stark a difference as one might expect, given concepts like “mankeeping,” or the emotional labor of women being the sole provider of socialization for their lonely and friendless male partners. But men do have fewer friends than women. This could be, in part, why women may not need a boyfriend the same way men need a girlfriend, if it’s safe to generalize. Part of why a woman would want a boyfriend is for companionship, and if she’s already getting that from a good group of friends, the man becomes less important.

But of course, the sex. Unless she is interested in women (and as far as I can tell, the women in the article are straight) she cannot acquire sex from her female friends. And yet, this is another area where men appear to have a stronger hunger than women. In part, this could be because men are just hornier than women, but there’s another important difference: men’s orgasms are nearly guaranteed from vaginal sex, but women’s aren’t, and women’s likelihood of orgasming is directly related to how serious the relationship is. Per my other survey on respondents’ recent sexual experience, you can see the orgasm gap persists no matter the type of relationship, but orgasms are far more common within marriage or serious relationships than other pairings. This is true for men too, but in casual encounters, the gap between men and women is also wider.

Big problem if you want to have that fulfilling marital sex: you need to have sex with someone for the first time before any of that happens! And it might actually suck!

Now, a woman can enjoy sex without orgasm. Many women who have casual sex, even if they are not frequently climaxing, report enjoying it and wanting to do it again. I believe them! But I think for many of them, the thrill comes from the pleasure of feeling validated and desired. If a woman is already getting that validation from dating apps, casual sex also becomes less appealing, which could explain why hookup culture feels so retro now. In a separate survey I ran, over 20% of single women under thirty reported using dating apps just to flirt, with zero intention of ever meeting up. If they get companionship from friends and sexual validation from men they’ll never meet, you can see why the urge to brave the awkwardness of dating and find a boyfriend might just not be very strong. Perhaps she wants a boyfriend or a husband someday, but getting there seems like a whole lot of work for potentially little to no gain.

Plus, while most of this article has pertained to women, I’ve already covered that social anxiety and fear of rejection push men into social inertia, and that many men who might see themselves as “incels” are doing so presumptuously, never having attempted to date in the first place.

The aversion to the unknown is really my main concern here, not individual women being inadequately desperate for a husband, or individual men being afraid to hit on women. This aversion is also prominent in conversations around platonic socialization, because unlike finding a partner and getting married, platonic socialization is a bit more open-ended, with unlimited spots one could potentially fill. Even among Gen Z, I think it’s safe to say that most people have quite a bit more urgency around finding a romantic partner than making new friends, especially if they already have friends. And if people are too concerned about the effort and uncertainty around finding a partner, what on Earth is motivating them to meet new platonic friends? Possibly: nothing at all.

As many of you know, I’ve written about my struggles getting good party turnout. I was also on a Search Engine episode about my predicament, and much of the aftermath concluded my problem was that I was simply too annoying. Many such cases! True as that may have been, especially during my unfortunate “musical impressions” era, some of the commentary about the episode had me wondering if perhaps many people—men and women—had an aversion to doing anything social that was not guaranteed to be enjoyable. And meeting new people, whether for dating or for friendship, includes no such guarantee. In fact, there will be more misses than hits. It’s easy to see why someone can recede into the path of least resistance (and ultimately loneliness) if their mentality is, “I can’t do anything if I don’t know for a fact that it’ll be good ROI.” Plus, if they have other things on which to fall back—not just existing friends, but social media, porn, or other forms of nonstop entertainment, it’s much harder to push themselves to make an effort for something that might not even be fun every single time!

A couple of comments that came off the episode were (and I’m paraphrasing/combining here:)

  • I can’t believe she threw parties where people didn’t know each other. Going to a party where I didn’t already know most of the attendees would be my personal hell! What if other people were boring?

  • Why would I bother to get dressed up for a cocktail party if I don’t even know if I’m going to have a good time? Why would I bother to get dressed up for anything at all when I don’t even want to leave my house in the first place?

  • I already have friends who I can hang out with casually, why would I bother going to the effort to attend a party for someone I don’t know that well?

While I’m sure not everyone feels this way, I got the sense that many people are calculating the choice to interact socially by attempting to eliminate as much risk as possible. And by “risk” I don’t mean the risk of anything actually dangerous happening, but just the risk of not having a good time, not liking people, being rejected, feeling awkward, or being bored.

As much as I want to reassure these people that they shouldn’t worry about those things, I have to admit that all of those possibilities are guaranteed to happen many times over if you are dating or making new friends. I can only try to convince them that these things are, ultimately, “worth it” for having a fuller social life, more friends, and hopefully a serious partner at some point. But like many long-term goals, this requires ignoring your short-term urge to follow the path of least resistance and stay in your comfort zone.

I would rather people deprioritize dating or making new friends because they already have friends and hobbies, if the alternative is deprioritizing new people and situations over scrolling TikTok. But I find it hard to believe that anyone’s social life or romantic life can ever be fulfilling if they are completely closed off to new experiences. Yes, even if those new experiences carry the risk of not being pleasant every single time. And even if there’s a really good face mask at home.
