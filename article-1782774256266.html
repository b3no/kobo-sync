New York’s insurgent left sent ripples across America

How big is the Mamdani tsunami?

Halina Bennet
Jun 29
∙
Paid
 
READ IN APP
 

There are just over four months until Election Day, and the fundamentals haven’t moved much: Democrats still hold a generic-ballot edge, President Donald Trump’s approval rating is still underwater by historic margins, and the party’s biggest fight is with itself over how far left it should lean. This week offered a useful stress test of all three issues.

The big numbers

Nate Silver’s generic ballot average sits at D+6.1 as of Saturday, down slightly from a D+7 peak earlier in the month but still in solidly positive territory for the out-of-power party. Democrats’ position is comparable to where they stood at this point in 2018, when the party achieved a 41-seat House pickup, Silver writes. Individual polls vary, but Democrats hold a lead across the board.

The more important number for Republicans is Trump’s approval, which Silver’s average puts at net -17.8 as of Sunday, roughly flat after a steep slide that took place from March through May. A Marist poll out this month found just a third of the country approves of his handling of the economy — his worst mark yet in that survey — which is more significant than the top-line number, since affordability is still the primary issue for voters.

Mamdani’s slate sweeps New York, and the ripples reach Wisconsin

Tuesday’s New York congressional primaries were a loud electoral signal, even if their impact beyond New York City is yet to be seen. All three congressional candidates endorsed by Mayor Zohran Mamdani won: Brad Lander beat Representative Dan Goldman in NY-10 by more than 30 points, Claire Valdez won the open NY-7 seat, and Darializa Avila Chevalier knocked off longtime Representative Adriano Espaillat in NY-13 after a nasty final stretch fought largely over Israel. Notably, Valdez and Avila Chevalier won against candidates backed by the party establishment, which is the detail that is rattling Democratic elders and operatives. Turnout was well below that of last year’s mayoral primary, so it’s worth being cautious about reading too much of a mandate into the results.

Trump used the results almost immediately, warning of “communists” coming into Congress in a sign the G.O.P. will likely make democratic socialism a national midterm theme regardless of how many seats are at stake or how many socialists are running.

The next legitimate test of whether this momentum travels outside blue cities and can manifest in statewide races comes in Wisconsin, where State Representative Francesca Hong’s gubernatorial bid is picking up steam ahead of the August 11 primary. Hong is running against a more traditional electability lane occupied by Lieutenant Governor Sara Rodriguez, and Wisconsin is about as good a control group as you could ask for. It is a true swing state — not a one-party city — and there will be a competitive general election on the other side of the primary. If Hong wins, there is likely to be a cascade of articles asking whether the Mamdani model scales. If she comes up short, expect the opposite.

Election-integrity fights escalate

As the primaries continue and November 3 inches closer, election security discussions are becoming increasingly urgent. Senate Democrats under Chuck Schumer and Alex Padilla launched a new Election Observer Program on Thursday. The first-of-its-kind program will train congressional staff to monitor polling places and to document voter intimidation, threats to poll workers, and misinformation. It will focus on the states holding Senate elections this fall and is modeled on a similar program the House has run for years. That announcement came the day before more than 20 Senate Democrats sent a letter demanding that the Department of Homeland Security, the Office of the Director of National Intelligence, the Department of Defense, and the Department of Justice preserve all election-related records. The senators warned agency heads not to delete or destroy documents that could be relevant to future election disputes or investigations.

Both moves are a clear response to a broader pattern of election interference overseen by the Trump administration. F.B.I. agents earlier this month searched the Cleveland offices of the Ohio Organizing Collaborative, a voter-registration group, seizing computers and questioning staff as part of a Justice Department inquiry into its registration work. New York University’s Brennan Center for Justice has flagged this search as being part of a wider trend of federal investigations that have targeted voting infrastructure ahead of November. Whatever you make of the underlying inquiries, the political read is that Democrats believe contesting the legitimacy of the election, rather than just the outcome, is now part of the G.O.P.’s approach, and they are building a documentation trail accordingly.

State races to watch

South Carolina: Attorney General Alan Wilson won Tuesday’s Republican gubernatorial runoff over Lieutenant Governor Pamela Evette. The race was muddied by Trump, who initially endorsed Evette before the first primary and then co-endorsed both candidates ahead of the runoff. Wilson will now face Democratic State Representative Jermaine Johnson in November. South Carolina hasn’t elected a Democratic governor since 1998, so this one’s a long shot for Democrats regardless.

Maine: Senator Susan Collins remains in a competitive race against oysterman Graham Platner despite coverage of his past texts and relationship allegations that dominated the news cycle earlier in June. Betting markets slightly favor Platner based on recent polling that shows him with a narrow edge in head-to-head matchups.

Kentucky: Governor Andy Beshear is spending more time on the trail campaigning for fellow Democrats this cycle. The New York Times reads this as 2028 positioning more than midterm help.

Texas: Former G.O.P. Senator Jeff Flake said this week that he’d vote for State Representative James Talarico over Attorney General Ken Paxton for Senate if he lived in Texas. His statement underscores just how unpopular Paxton remains within the party’s moderate wing, especially after Trump endorsed the Texas A.G. over sitting Senator John Cornyn. Flake was careful to frame his remark as just short of an endorsement, noting Talarico is well to his left on policy. But he did argue character should outweigh party loyalty, pointing to past instances of the G.O.P. cutting ties with figures like former Senate candidate Roy Moore. Flake’s comments are not a one-off. Earlier this month, he made nearly identical arguments about Maine, urging Republicans there not to back Platner. Given Flake’s track record of crossing the aisle (he served as Biden’s ambassador to Turkey and endorsed Harris in 2024), this is not shocking. Still, in a race already rated a toss-up, any erosion of Republican unity is good for Talarico.

Gas prices are driving the midterms

The price of gas continues to be the connective tissue between the war in Iran and the midterms. Trump told the Justice Department on Wednesday to investigate oil companies for “gouging,” arguing that they were not lowering gasoline prices fast enough as crude prices fell. The directive is itself a sign the White House is worried enough about politics to go looking for a villain rather than spinning something positive.

Prices did dip below $4 a gallon this month for the first time since March, with crude closing just a few dollars above its pre-war level. But the recovery is still slow and uneven: Prices remain up nearly 40 percent since the war began, several states are still at or near $5 a gallon, and energy analysts aren’t expecting full normalization until sometime in 2027 — well past the midterms.
