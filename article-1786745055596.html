What is college worth?

An enrollment cliff combined with high sticker prices no one is paying has some private colleges rethinking their tuition.

Halina Bennet
Aug 14
∙

The University of Tulsa announced earlier this week that it would cut annual undergraduate tuition and fees from $54,000 to $25,000, starting in fall 2027. Current students will see their current financial aid package readjusted so that effective immediately, their payment will not exceed the new cap, the Tulsa Flyer reports.

While a 54 percent price cut at a private university might look like a recession indicator, TU’s move is part of a growing recognition among schools that they have to address the collision between the exploding cost of American higher education and what most students are willing to pay.

TU President Stacy Leeds, who has been in the job for just six weeks, said that people “tune out” when they see the high cost of attendance, and that she aims to give them a number they can process. More than just cutting prices, she explained, the move is an attempt to align the sticker price more closely with what most students were already paying with scholarships and aid, the Flyer reports.

In other words, TU is conceding that few students were paying full freight to begin with, and by being more realistic with its pricing, the university is making a bet that more students will be inclined to attend.

A broader trend

Tulsa isn’t an outlier. Private nonprofit colleges reported an average tuition discount rate of 57.1 percent for first-time undergraduates during the 2025-2026 academic year, according to the National Association of College and University Business Officers. That rate is the highest on record, Forbes reports. What this means is that for every dollar of tuition, the average school is collecting 43 cents. With fewer people enrolling in colleges as sticker prices climb, some schools are cutting the price to match the one that most students are paying in an effort to recapture the students who have been priced out or who have decided that no undergraduate degree is worth $200,000.

In fall of 2024, two years before TU made its cut, Bethel University, a private Baptist university in Minnesota roughly the same size as TU, lowered its tuition from $44,050 to $25,990 starting in fall 2025. The move was framed explicitly around “transparency and clarity around the true price of attending Bethel.” Prescott College in Arizona announced last year that it was introducing a $15,000 flat-rate tuition, down from $33,960, to begin this fall.

Don’t get me wrong — these universities are unlikely to be slashing their prices solely out of the goodness of their hearts. I reckon that it has to do at some level with financial distress. TU had a $31 million deficit in its 2026 budget and has gone through layoffs and a hiring freeze, the Chronicle of Higher Education reports. Forbes reports that the tuition cut is made possible by donor gifts as part of a $350 million scholarship fundraising campaign that allowed the distressed institution to experiment with the pricing reset.

This is not a recession indicator

Despite the sense that tuition has grown astronomically, the cost has actually been slowly falling in real terms.

Brookings economist Phillip Levine’s net-price analysis found that inflation-adjusted net tuition fell across every income bracket at every kind of four-year institution between 2019 and 2026. The steepest decline was among private universities with large endowments, where net tuition for students from families earning $85,000 fell 30.8 percent, to $10,800, Higher Ed Dive reports.

Meanwhile, published sticker prices have continued to climb, with average private nonprofit tuition and fees hitting $45,000 in the 2025-26 academic year, up four percent from the year before, according to College Board. The combination of rising prices and rising discount rates has created the cavern into which reality has fallen. The result is a race in which colleges maintain high list prices for prestige and negotiation, while using that wiggle room to discount individual students’ tuition further in an effort to fill seats.

The demand-side pressure is largely demographic.

The number of 18-year-olds in the U.S. has been falling since 2008, when birth rates dropped. The schools that are forced to confront this reality right now are those that are tuition-dependent, which don’t have the massive endowments or state subsidies that let the top elite private and flagship universities absorb the hit of the enrollment cliff. They are the schools that cannot afford to be need blind, to dole out financial aid as grants instead of loans, and who cannot absorb the financial hit of declining enrollment. Tulsa’s own numbers make this point, where enrollment “has been in decline for years,” and Leeds says the goal of the tuition cut is to eventually rebuild toward the school’s enrollment peak, the Flyer reports.

Certainly, with inflation and all the rest, Americans are feeling a very real financial squeeze that makes the high cost of college untenable for most people. Total U.S. student loan debt has climbed to $1.866 trillion ($1.69 trillion in federal loan debt and $176 billion in total private student loan debt), with 42.8 million federal borrowers. But on top of that, the ongoing conversation about whether traditional four-year college is worth it, combined with the dwindling number of college-aged people, is shrinking an applicant pool chasing too many small private colleges.

This structural trend has been building slowly for quite some time, as evidenced by the National Association of College and University Business Officers’s discount-rate data. It’s not a reaction to a specific economic downturn or a spike in unemployment, but when high prices and tuition cuts are combined with low enrollment on the backdrop of a moment of real financial anxiety, it sure looks like some kind of recession indicator.

I expect that more small-to-medium, mid-tier private colleges in shrinking or stagnant metro areas will make announcements similar to TU’s and Bethel’s in the coming months and years. But I won’t take them as evidence that it’s time to panic about the economy. For now, this reads to me as evidence that a pricing system that was designed poorly has finally met its moment of reckoning.
