What does permitting reform have to do with housing?

BAAJA’s housing effects, explained

Halina Bennet
Nov 23
∙
Paid
 
READ IN APP
 

On Thursday in a 62-33 vote, the Senate passed the Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act (BAAJA), which has been debated primarily in terms of energy and climate policy but whose provisions implicate a broad range of different kinds of construction projects.

Notably, tucked inside it are changes that would help reduce barriers to building housing while facilitating federal financial assistance for certain housing projects.

What the bill changes

In general, privately funded market-rate housing does not face federal environmental- and historic-preservation reviews — these rules overwhelmingly constrain public- rather than private-sector activities.

But federally assisted projects are often subject to National Environmental Policy Act (NEPA) and National Historic Preservation Act (N.H.P.A.) reviews, which lead to significant development delays, according to a coalition of pro-housing organizations that wrote a letter of support for the bill.

The bill would exempt projects that are only linked to the federal government through financial assistance from N.H.P.A. Section 106 historic review, unless that money is directed primarily for historic preservation. If BAAJA is enacted, many housing projects supported financially by the U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development (HUD) and the Department of Agriculture (U.S.D.A.) would no longer require this kind of review, reducing NIMBYs’ litigation options and allowing projects to proceed more quickly.

Environmental review would still apply, but the bill would expand categorical exclusions and make them easier to use. It also would set review deadlines and allow qualifying state or tribal reviews to substitute for NEPA reviews, streamlining the process.

A provision changing Clean Water Act (C.W.A.) reviews would make a difference for private developers, since those reviews apply regardless of federal funding. BAAJA would impose a 150-day window for legal challenges to C.W.A. fill permits — Section 404 permits required before placing materials like rock, dirt, or sand into water or wetlands for projects like road construction, utility crossings, and land development — and prevent judges from interrupting active construction while agencies fix review errors. According to the coalition letter, streamlining C.W.A. permits could save up to an estimated $425,000 in permitting costs per project. The bill also restricts state and tribal water-quality reviews and limits certain Environmental Protection Agency (E.P.A.) interventions and legal challenges, such as the agency’s power to intervene retroactively or outside of a narrow window after a permit application is complete. Currently, under C.W.A. Section 404, the E.P.A. can veto the use of a location for dredged or fill material at any point in the permitting process.

Despite the bill’s focus on energy and infrastructure, the housing coalition writes that BAAJA’s reforms could have broad impacts on housing programs, estimating the bill would affect about $10 billion a year in housing assistance through HUD. According to the letter, BAAJA’s changes to historic preservation and environmental review would have implications for many of HUD’s programs, including the Community Development Block Grant program, the HOME Investment Partnerships Program, public housing assistance, Economic Development Initiatives, and loan programs. These implications would grow larger if a future Congress attempted, as many progressives have suggested, to expand federal investment in social housing or repeal the Faircloth Amendment — which caps the number of public housing units that HUD and local public housing authorities can fund and operate at 1999 levels — and increase federal funding of public housing construction.

BAAJA is the second large bipartisan reform bill to make real headway this Congress, following the 21st Century ROAD to Housing Act, which was enacted in July. ROAD was almost entirely housing-focused, and it included some review reforms — including for U.S.D.A. infill projects. BAAJA, if enacted, would expand those permitting reforms to include a wider set of programs.

What it doesn’t change

BAAJA, unlike ROAD, is not primarily a housing bill.

In fact, its housing effects are to some degree incidental — downstream consequences of efforts to change review rules for energy projects. BAAJA doesn’t make any changes to local zoning, density limits, or approval processes, which are the primary barriers for most housing projects (and also for data centers, which have been widely discussed in the BAAJA debate). Instead, its effects are concentrated on the minority of housing projects that are federally assisted or require wetland permits.

Wetlands cover about 5.5 percent of the land area in the 48 contiguous states, according to the E.P.A. What’s more, the 2023 Sackett v. Environmental Protection Agency Supreme Court decision narrowed C.W.A. jurisdiction over wetlands with a “continuous surface connection” to relatively permanent bodies of water connected to interstate navigable waters. The E.P.A. estimated that after that ruling, developers would no longer need a federal permit to fill or dredge more than 60 percent of the nation’s wetlands, making the wetland permitting reforms applicable to relatively few projects.

The opposition

YIMBY groups are predictably supportive of a bill that cuts down on regulations that extend wait times and create hurdles for housing and infrastructure projects. But some groups — specifically environmental groups and many tribes — are wary of how the changes will threaten land protections.

Tribal organizations and Earthjustice argue that by weakening N.H.P.A., the federal government will no longer have to work with tribal nations and states to determine impacts on an area’s cultural and historical significance. In the case of tribes, the change allows agencies to bypass consultation, which Earthjustice says infringes on tribal sovereignty.

The bill now goes to the House, where it is likely to face a closer vote than in the Senate.
