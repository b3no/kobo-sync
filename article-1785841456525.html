"First Date Uber" is "Rainbow Sex Party" for 2026 Adults

How many women are *actually* demanding an uber for a first date?

Many millennials probably remember a particular urban legend which plagued our parents and educators in the 2000s—namely, the idea that teens were hanging out after hours and having “rainbow sex parties.” Although I was not the kind of kid who would ever be invited to such a thing even if they existed, I had major doubts that they were real at all. And I say this as a teenager who attended a private school which has been described by my subscribers as “horny Gossip Girl.” Don’t get me wrong, plenty of people were having sex, but there were no rainbow-oriented trinkets involved.

Let’s back up for those of you who don’t remember this urban legend. Rainbow Sex Parties often involved teens paying each other for sexual favors with a large variety of different-colored wristbands. How were teenagers acquiring this many different colored wristbands in a time before Amazon? Nobody knows. I was supposed to believe that teenagers who were impulsive enough to trade bracelets for sex at a drunken house party were also good enough event organizers and project managers that they would be running around to different stores days ahead of time to find seven separate colors of wristbands, clearly communicating which sexual favors correlated with each color, and then orchestrating the exchange of wristbands at the party?

Some versions of Rainbow Sex Parties involved an act that everyone would realize was impossible if you had ever given or received a blowjob even once. In 2002, conservative pediatrician Meg Meeker wrote a book called, Epidemic: How Teen Sex Is Killing Our Kids. In it, she includes a story from an anonymous 14-year-old who claimed that she attended a variant of a Rainbow Sex Party, wherein girls were given different shades of lipstick and asked to each perform oral sex on a boy so the lipstick shades would, presumably, accumulate in a rainbow along the length of his shaft. And then Saddam Hussein nuked the entire party, which was summarily swarmed by killer bees and washed down with acid rain.

Look, perhaps I’m not enough of a blowjob expert, but my understanding of the mechanics is that, how do I say this, nobody would be able to see a clear band of lipstick on a penis once you’re done, let alone multiple bands in different colors left by multiple people. Perhaps I’m taking it too literally and it wasn’t really a true blowjob but rather a one-time “stamping” of sorts, but I’ll be honest: I don’t think this happened, let alone on any grand scale, although it’s possible a teenage boy made it up as an Inbetweeners-type story.

But people were convinced this was a widespread epidemic, probably because the media just loves a scandalous story that terrifies parents and feeds into the zeitgeist. The Oprah Winfrey Show even did an episode about this underground “teen phenomenon” in 2005.

It’s easy to laugh about what was certainly a Y2K-era urban legend now, but a new one has emerged, except this time it’s about adults dating in 2026—and it makes sense, given that the current cultural anxiety is about the loneliness epidemic and birth rate, and less about out-of-control randy teens. Basically, according to today’s legend, we are supposed to believe that women are, en masse, demanding that men send them Ubers or black cars to pick them up for the first date and are cancelling dates if this doesn’t happen.

I’m sure this has happened before. But is it something that is putting men and women “at war?” Not even close. Even when filtering for the exact type of person who is most likely to engage in this behavior, is it simply not common enough for the outrage.

The first time I read about this phenomenon was in a buzzy New York Post article where the tribulations of “Big Apple lifestyle influencer” Savannah Pagnozzi were generalized as a problem that was affecting most, or at least a significant portion, of Gen Z singles in urban centers. Basically, Pagnozzi canceled a date after a guy didn’t send her an Uber to pick her up, and this was apparently happening all over the place.

Just one problem. Pagnozzi is not just a “lifestyle influencer.” She currently describes herself as a comedian, and the vast majority of her jokes (at least as far as her recent Instagram posts go) are about scrounging around the streets of Manhattan looking for rich men. She has also explicitly admitted to being a sugar baby, although it’s unclear if this is a current or just previous profession for her. In a recent clip, she advocates “telling men you’re materialistic” as a good dating strategy. Moreover, she has endorsed Luxy, a dating app specifically designed to meet rich men.

This is an especially odd person to choose to make a point about “Gen Z singles.” Most Gen Z singles aren’t influencers at all, let alone comedians whose main bit is “what if a woman was a gold digger” and former sugar babies.

I’m sure some naysayers will tell me that Pagnozzi actually is representative of all Gen Z single women, because actually, most women demand these things and Pagnozzi is just being more honest about her motivations than other women. Devil’s advocate time. Let’s assume that requiring a man to send you an Uber or black car for a first date is not necessarily something every single woman is doing, but it’s very prevalent among attractive, young women living in urban centers where people aren’t driving their own cars that often. In order to test this theory, you’d need a survey which filtered for those factors. Luckily, only one person is pedantic enough to actually run it.

I ran a survey of a little over 1,000 people. And, well, it turns out that receiving an Uber or black car for a first date (or sending it) happens less than 5% of the time, even for young singles in urban locations. In other words, this behavior is not the norm, it’s a rounding error. And remember, even with these relatively low percentages, this is strictly limited to urban singles, the exact group of people who would be likely to experience this behavior.

Of course, if this behavior is most common among age-gap couples (also not the norm or average situation, more on that in a second) then perhaps the statistics are obfuscated by limiting the age of the men to 39 and below. I didn’t have a lot of responses from single men over 40, but I was able to poll men on what they’ve ever done, so if currently-partnered men have ever sent an Uber or black car to a date, it would show up in that dataset. As you can see, not only is “requiring an Uber for a date” not the way most young, urban women are behaving, but it’s an extreme minority position. Also, to avoid sampling bias, I did not say what the topic of the survey was when I promoted it, because I did not want strong feelings about the first date Uber “phenomenon” to drive the likelihood that someone would respond or not.

So what happened? What is extremely uncommon behavior being presented as the latest iteration of Rainbow Parties—the scandal tearing apart Gen Z singles?

What likely happened is that a custom from sex worker culture (specifically that of sugar babies—a pretty big online community, albeit a small minority of the real-world population) has leaked into the discourse via social media, and a small proportion of “civilian” women adopted it. Most people, however, either haven’t heard of it this or know about it and think it’s silly.

There’s another reason this behavior might not be common among women: they don’t want to give their home address to a guy they don’t know yet!

But I’ve noticed that two groups of men seem to think this behavior is widespread: young men whose main interactions with women are by watching short form clips of the Whatever podcast, and slightly older men (ie: my age and older) who exclusively date younger women.

The first group is exposed to so much rage bait starring sex workers that they begin to believe every woman is a sex worker, or sex worker adjacent. They begin to believe, for example, that young women don’t date men their own age because they fail to provide adequate “resources,” despite the fact that most women actually are just dating guys their age. Not only has average age at first marriage been extremely consistent with the man just a few years older than the woman, dating as far back as the 1500s, but even dating relationships are typically within five years. To examine more casual relationships (ie: just dating, not marriage) I ran a different survey. When asked about their most recent (including current) non-married relationship, the average gap was still somewhere between 0-5 years (still broader than the average gaps for married couples). Hardly enough of a gap to make the claim that most women in their twenties are dating much older men for their money, or being “flown out” anywhere. Unless we assume a bunch of twenty-six-year-old men are flying women out to Miami. In a separate survey, when I asked people who they typically dated, those who had dated consistently enough to notice a pattern almost always said that they dated people close to their age.

I understand why older men who exclusively date younger women believe that all modern women are engaging in some form of sugar baby behavior whether they call it that or not. If these men leverage their money to find younger women, then money is the main appeal of these men. Therefore, an unspoken (or spoken) sugar baby dynamic is their main experience dating younger women. I don’t say that to judge or moralize if everyone is a consenting adult, but it would be the equivalent of a dominatrix dating exclusively in the BDSM community, and then confidently declaring that all men love to be whipped.

When you look at the actual data, most people are marrying those within a couple years of their age, dating those still pretty close to their age but with a slightly wider gap on average, and generally not crossing thresholds in socioeconomic status either. Per the Institute of Family Studies, which indicates that gaps in income are more pronounced in lower-income brackets. All this to say: the trophy wives, or the 22-year old girl who gets flown out to go on a boat in Miami certainly exist but are not the norm, and if you are exclusively encountering women like this it’s probably because you’re the type of man who dates them. Which, again, is fine, but is not the majority experience.

So, I think we can put this issue to bed. The Uber issue is not the latest trend putting single men and women at war, nor is it splitting Gen Z. Even among the exact people most likely to engage in this behavior—urban singles, especially twenty-something urban single women—it’s extremely uncommon.

Personally, I think this was an op to distract us from the real problem: out of control teens hosting Rainbow Parties.
