Can liberalism survive the algorithm?

Learning to live together online

Today, staff writer Jeremiah Johnson takes on the problem of living together online. So much of our politics, culture, and daily life happens on the internet, and it’s tearing us apart. What can we learn from the healthy communities of the internet? Are some platforms healthier than others? I put these questions to Jeremiah, and his answers offer some grounds for hope.

The internet’s toxicity isn’t good for anyone, but it’s especially bad for liberals. Liberalism is a social technology that allows people with profoundly different value systems to live together. It doesn’t require you to agree with your opponents or even to respect their views all that much, but it does require you to accept that they have a right to express those views and live their lives in accordance with their own values.

These habits can be difficult to practice offline, but they’re even more difficult online. And that only helps extremist political ideologies.

Social media rewards moral clarity rather than ambiguity. Explaining the complicated reasons a specific policy is misguided takes time and effort, and doing so asks the audience to hold several ideas at once. “The system is rigged and shadowy elite forces are screwing you” is emotionally legible in the blink of an eye.

Share

Simplistic good-versus-evil narratives are easier to communicate quickly, and they’re more likely to provoke the behaviors that platforms measure: clicking, liking, sharing, and replying. Illiberal politics thrives in this kind of environment, where the distinction between an opponent and an enemy is collapsed.

If we want people to be able to disagree online without becoming enemies, we’ll have to defend the corners of the internet that reward something other than conflict.

What’s wrong with the modern internet?

It’s important to start with a diagnosis of the problem, and I think that the main problem with the modern internet is that we are simultaneously seeing too much of each other and too little. We can interact with almost any person on the globe, but the part of them we see is limited to the content incentivized by algorithms. And the incentives that govern human behavior are very different on social media than they are in real life.

Your neighbor is someone you see often. Your kids might play together. Maybe she attends your church or plays in your softball league. You know things about her separately from her opinion on hot-button political issues. Maybe you clear her driveway of snow in the winter. Perhaps she brought you soup when you were sick. If you have a political disagreement, it exists inside a grounded, real-world relationship.

On the internet, none of that grounding exists. In real life, people understand that you should act differently at a bar than you would at work or at home or at a funeral. But the internet and social media have destroyed many of these distinctions. Instead, billions of people are pulled into one global conversation, where distinct local practices and norms melt away and are replaced by universal rules. Media theorists call this context collapse — the flattening of multiple audiences or situations into a single, global context.

When someone spouts a bad opinion on social media, that’s all you see them as — a bad opinion, stripped of context. You don’t know anything else about them, and, in most cases, you’ll never have another interaction with this particular anonymous person.

Offline, you build trusted relationships that are maintained over years of repeated interactions. Online, you build an audience.

Social media encourages us to think of ourselves as authors, as broadcasters narrating a point of view rather than as normal people embedded in a community. Thinking of ourselves this way changes how we interact with everyone around us. Your incentive isn’t to maintain a harmonious relationship with a neighbor you have a long-standing rapport with. It’s to put on a performance for your followers.

Opinions become content. Rather than asking how our actions might impact those around us, we instead think about how those actions look to the invisible crowd. Anonymity supercharges this dynamic — nameless avatars face essentially no repercussions for insulting, boorish behavior.

The way we have built our social media networks makes these conflicts even worse. Extreme views draw more reactions and engagement, which means algorithms are more likely to push them to the top of your feed. For the same reasons, conflict is inherently more viral than agreement. We all end up with a timeline full of loud, angry people shouting extreme viewpoints at each other, fighting like cats and dogs.

The first step in learning how to live together online is figuring out which social media spaces are still functioning well.

Operate at a more human scale

I’m a member of a small Discord server centered on movies and TV fandom. It only has a few hundred active members, but that’s part of what makes it worth coming back to. I recognize the usernames every time I log on. I know which users are funny, which users like to argue, and which users have encyclopedic knowledge of the Game of Thrones universe. I also know which folks just got a new job, whose spouse got sick last month, and who can be relied on if I were to suddenly need a favor.

Smaller communities like my Discord give participants enough context to interpret one another charitably. They’re certainly not immune to petty squabbling and cliquish behavior. But more localized online communities recreate the conditions that make offline conversations work: repeated interactions, common ground, and consequences for being a jerk. When you interact with the same folks every day, you’re more likely to give them some grace and be granted grace in return.

One of the reasons so many other parts of social media feel so toxic is that you’re effectively speaking to a global audience. Even if you’re a small account, your Twitter comments, TikTok videos, and Tumblr posts can be seen by anyone online. If you say the right thing (or the wrong thing), you could go viral, getting millions of impressions and thousands of responses from total strangers.

Consider the case of the “30-50 feral hogs.” Amid a larger conversation about gun control, William McNabb asked how he was supposed to contend with feral hogs on his property without the use of assault weapons:

Willie McNabb 🐗
@WillieMcNabb
@JasonIsbell Legit question for rural Americans - How do I kill the 30-50 feral hogs that run into my yard within 3-5 mins while my small kids play?
4:01 PM · Aug 4, 2019
3.84K Replies · 6.94K Reposts · 31.8K Likes

Social media had a field day making fun of McNabb and his wildly implausible scenario. His sincerely posed question was interpreted in the harshest way possible by scores of people trying to win points in a larger political fight that had nothing to do with him.

It wasn’t until mainstream sources began to report that feral hogs are actually a massive problem that people realized McNabb had a point. This instance of context collapse eventually became so infamous that it led to an entire segment of Last Week Tonight with John Oliver discussing how the feral hogs guy was vindicated.

Luckily, those seeking smaller communities have several thriving options:

  • Reddit is now one of the largest social media platforms in the world, and there are niche subreddits on nearly any topic imaginable.

  • Discord has seen strong growth over the past few years.

  • People are increasingly treating global social media as a form of entertainment and retreating back into WhatsApp chats or Facebook groups to actually discuss things with smaller communities.

Heavy moderation

One of the most interesting communities on Reddit is the /r/stopdrinking subreddit. As you may have guessed, it’s a community to support those who want to stop drinking. It has almost 300,000 weekly visitors and, on most days, more than 1,000 people comment in the Daily Check-In thread. It’s an incredibly supportive community, but it couldn’t exist without strong moderation and community norms.

It imposes strict rules. Comments are expected to be kind and not engage in “tough love.” Posting while drunk is banned. Users are asked to post from the “I” perspective, sharing what works for them but not mandating what others should do.

The community has also built a shared culture that includes badges denoting how long users have been sober, the aforementioned daily check-ins, and the community’s motto of IWNDWYT — I Will Not Drink With You Today. It’s a wonderful little corner of the internet, and it’s helped countless people struggling to become sober. What’s important about /r/stopdrinking is that it works even though it’s anonymous and entirely without a formal organization or funding backing it.

Downvotes everywhere

Virtually every form of social media has a like button of some kind. It might be a thumbs up, a heart, or an upvote arrow, but there’s almost always a way to signal that you agree with a particular comment or post. But only a few platforms give you a way to express disapproval. TikTok, Twitter, and Instagram don’t have downvotes, and that’s part of what makes them more toxic than they should be.

Downvotes are a form of distributed moderation, a way for communities to enforce strong norms without requiring active moderation at all times. It may seem counterintuitive to advocate for downvotes as a way to build healthier online communities. But subreddits like /r/WhatIsThisThing, where users post pictures of mysterious objects and ask for help identifying them, show why they matter.

Lots of users take guesses. Some are wild speculation, some are jokes, and some are well-intentioned but wrong. But the downvote system helps stop the incorrect and joke answers from dominating the results, pushing them further down where they’re less likely to be seen.

Reddit is the only major social media site to incorporate downvotes into how it ranks content on your feed, and it’s no coincidence that it has the strongest reputation for quality answers. Reddit’s reputation for quality is so strong that it’s become common knowledge that Google searches work better if you append “Reddit” to whatever you’re searching for.

In addition to increasing quality, downvotes also reduce toxicity on most platforms. Controversial or toxic posts tend to be more viral because they draw lots of outraged engagement. But on Reddit, the most controversial posts are sunk by downvotes to the bottom of comment sections and feeds.

This type of distributed moderation is especially important when you consider the 90-9-1 rule of online communities. This stylized rule of thumb says that 90% of all users are lurkers who never post or comment, 9% are commenters, and 1% are power users who create most of the content in the community. If a small chunk of users wants to dive deep into the comments to bitterly argue with the most noxious commenters, that’s fine. But the vast majority of casual users who rarely, if ever, comment won’t have to see them, instead seeing only the most upvoted, constructive comments and posts.

Hang on to text-based platforms

Before the modern internet came into being, people were using text-based bulletin boards, Usenet, and other patched-together systems to build community, discuss, and debate.

Today, more and more social media comes in the form of short-form vertical video. When a friend tells you they lost a few hours of sleep doomscrolling last night, it’s becoming less likely they were having a conversation and more likely they were watching TikTok or Instagram Reels.

Video’s increasing dominance of social media degrades our ability to truly talk to one another. It makes social media less of a platform for person-to-person conversation and more like a one-to-many broadcast platform where millions of viewers watch a small handful of famous creators.

Sure, there are comments on Instagram Reels and TikTok videos. But when was the last time anything actually productive was said there?

It may mean fighting against the tide, but liberals who care about the quality of our communities online should stubbornly hang on to text-based platforms like Reddit or Twitter (or the Substack comments sections of liberal magazines!). They may not be perfect, but they’re far more interactive and likely to provide challenging discussions than video-focused social media.

I recognize this is a tall order. In many ways, video-based social media like YouTube and TikTok barely qualify as “social” at all. They don’t require friends, conversation, or community — the algorithm doesn’t need any of those to keep serving you an endless supply of performers.

Rather than bringing people together, such platforms often have the effect of isolating us. And they’re only getting more popular. TikTok is the fastest-growing social network for news, and YouTube recently surpassed Netflix in average daily viewing.

There isn’t one simple trick that will make this trend disappear. But we can, at the very least, refuse to surrender every space online to the march of short-form video.

Liberals should work on making sure they keep investing in threaded, text-based social media that allows for deeper conversations. We should push for more places to incorporate strong moderation tools and downvotes to reduce toxicity. And we should keep building human-scale communities that incentivize us to be neighbors and friends rather than influencers and audiences.

Doing this work won’t fix the toxicity of the rest of social media. But in that toxic landscape, we can build our own oases where people are supportive, cooperative, and tolerant. We can build communities where people with different worldviews can disagree without hating each other.

And maybe, when outsiders compare what they see on the rest of the internet with what they see in our spaces, they’ll decide they would rather hang out in the less toxic space. Maybe our cohort of liberals will grow. And bit by bit, we can make progress against the tide.

I don’t know if it’s possible to defeat TikTok, but we can at least make sure TikTok doesn’t defeat everything else.
