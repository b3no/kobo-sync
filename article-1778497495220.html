"Are we kind of being pricks?"

Marblehead’s housing fight reveals the problem with local democracy.

Jerusalem Demsas
May 11
∙
Paid
 
READ IN APP
 

Before David Modica confronted the Town Meeting of Marblehead, Massachusetts, with the question that would make him viral, he already knew the answer.

“Are we kind of being pricks?”

Marblehead is a small coastal Massachusetts town (pop. 20,576) with a median home value approaching $1 million. Almost 80% of residents 25 and over have a bachelor’s degree and more than 93% are white. Modica was questioning his exclusive town’s opposition to a state housing law. And yes, they are being pricks.

Five years ago, Republican Gov. Charlie Baker signed a bill that passed the House 143-4 and 40-0 in the Senate. The Massachusetts Bay Transportation Authority (MBTA) Communities Act requires cities and towns served by the state transportation system to do what experts formally call the bare minimum.

The law required that these communities provide just one district “of reasonable size” where multifamily housing could be built.

If a locality refused to comply, it would no longer be eligible for a variety of state funding sources. But for wealthy suburbs such as Marblehead, that’s not a very strong incentive.

While Modica’s charming irreverence went viral, my favorite part of his remarks was when he asked, “We’re trying to make sure we build no houses? People live in houses.”

Here he had struck on the core problem: The problem with more houses was the potential to bring more people to town..

Perhaps the cleanest articulation of this view came from resident Claudette Mason: “You keep mentioning minimal impact — no impact on our town,” Mason said to the Planning Board in 2024. “But we’re talking about another 3,000-plus people in town. Those streets are already parking lots, so there is an impact on our streets.”

There were also concerns that local schools were overtaxed as it was despite the fact that Marblehead Public Schools enrollment has fallen 24% over the past decade.

But Modica is skeptical that rational arguments will sway his fellow Marbleheaders. Instead, he’d like to use shame:

“We have a lot more Black Lives Matter signs than Black people,” he told me on the phone yesterday morning. “You saw the whole post-George Floyd thing, you can shame white people into a lot of stuff.”

Modica’s overarching argument is that in a progressive town like Marblehead (Harris earned a near 40-point lead in the last presidential election) he wants to use shame to get more people to accept the need to build more housing:

“They’re doing marches for peace and bake sales for Gaza and that’s great but if you do all that shit you can’t then be like ‘we gotta conserve all this shit.’”

There is a burgeoning left-YIMBY synthesis heralded most clearly by New York City Mayor Zohran Mamdani but also would-be Los Angeles Mayor Nithya Raman. But I’m not sure shame is enough to outweigh local government’s inordinate power over local land use and housing decisions.

Marblehead isn’t unique, as The Globe has detailed thoroughly:

Wellesley counted a mostly built apartment complex far from transit as part of its multifamily housing requirement.

Chelmsford picked already-developed areas of Route 110 while openly discussing how they were going to flout the requirements: Planning Board member Annita Tanini defiantly argued, “if the state is telling me to do something, I’m going to comply, but that doesn’t mean I’m going to make it easy to develop.” She continued: “It’s making as many barriers to entry as possible.”

At the same town meeting, board member Chris Lavallee told residents they were specifically looking “at areas that were already highly developed…because the likelihood of them being developed in the short term is extremely low.”

Norwood planner Sarah Dixon told her town’s Select Board: ““We’ve strategically placed these districts in areas that have multifamily housing…The market incentive to tear down [an apartment complex] and rebuild isn’t there.”

Do you have the courage to hate the voters?

Marblehead didn’t start out being “pricks,” that was the result of a (somewhat) democratic process.

In May 2024, town planners proposed a plan to upzone three separate streets for a total of 899 potential new homes. This plan was rejected at a Town Meeting by a 377-410 vote.

An entire year later, after the Massachusetts Supreme Judicial Court had upheld the law, the Town Meeting approved basically the same plan it had rejected, this time by a 951-759 vote.

Two lawyers (it’s always lawyers) discovered a mechanism that had sat dormant for 71 years. The 1954 Special Act—unique to Marblehead—allowed a citizen petition to send Town Meeting decisions to a townwide ballot. As such, the approved plan went to the voters who rejected it 3,642 to 3,297.

Which brings us to last week, when the town of Marblehead decided that a golf course, which, of course, is extremely unlikely to become housing of any kind, would be a great place to pretend to plan for new housing. Under questioning from Modica about the farce, a Planning Board member wearily admitted that “we tried the other way and it was rejected.”

Stop fetishizing local democracy

Federal policymakers in particular have absurdly romantic notions of what goes on in local government. They point to polls showing that people have more faith in local government and glamorize the simple life of a local elected official who knows all their constituents by name.

But while the image of the local town selectman looms large in our national imagination, the vast majority of people don’t engage much in local politics at all. What does it mean to approve of your local government if you never vote, petition, or show up to a local meeting? Most people can’t even name their mayors or city councilmen let alone their county councilmen or hyperlocal elected officials.

Whenever local democracy is actually called on to mediate important disputes, its structural limitations become clear:

B.R. Ambedkar, the most influential Indian statesman of the 20th century, when presenting the Draft Constitution in 1948 argued that “village republics have been the ruination of India…What is the village, but a sink of localism, a den of ignorance, narrow mindedness and communalism.”

Ambedkar scorned those who fetishized the village: “The love of the intellectual Indians for the village community is of course infinite if not pathetic.” Instead, he argued, rights should go to individuals, not communities.

It wasn’t that Indian villages were uniquely bad, it was that the local is a place where exit is costly, everyone knows everyone, outside arbitration is absent or intermittent, and dominant local hierarchies are reproduced with maximal efficiency.

The very same people who make up a locality also make up a state and a national polity, but local government is exclusionary by nature. Unlike national politics, local politics does not ask you to conceive of yourself as part of a broad group of diverse peoples, it taps into your most parochial tendencies, feeding the narcissism of small differences.

This is why the same people, when asked whether they want to see more housing built, will answer very differently when asked whether their state or nation should build more housing and when asked whether their town or neighborhood should. The very phenomenon of Not-In-My-Backyardism is predicated upon a parochial, localist self and a broad-minded self capable of seeing the forest for the trees.

From California and Utah to Colorado and Minnesota, everywhere I have traveled to report on the housing crisis I am met with the same argument against state preemption of local zoning powers: You don’t understand, our state has a special tradition of local control.

Of course, the only reason we’re all in this mess—and by that I mean a nationwide housing crisis—is because of the national special tradition of local control which has resulted in a shortage of anywhere from 4.7 million to 10 million homes.

And in each one of these states, attempts to give local governments wide latitude in implementing housing policy has resulted in the type of trickery on display in Massachusetts today.

In California, the most famous case is that of the accessory dwelling unit (ADU) which took 30 years of legislation to finally produce significant numbers of homes because localities kept tacking on absurd requirements such as “you have to reserve parking spots for each ADU” or “ADUs can only be built on lots larger than 15,000 square feet.”

As I’ve written before, this is called the “thousand paper cuts” response: “In other words, most California cities appeared to comply with the state mandate by amending their zoning rules to permit ADUs, but they imbedded many costly regulatory requirements within the ‘authorization’ that dramatically curtail the likelihood that ADUs will actually be developed.”

The MBTA Communities Act is the most chill piece of YIMBY legislation I can imagine. It has barely any mandates. Marblehead and other communities like it will likely be in compliance despite likely producing zero new homes as a result of the bill. And yet it sparked a multiyear battle.

There is no path to solving the housing crisis or any of our major collective-action problems like climate change and energy scarcity without moving power over land-use decisions away from local government.
