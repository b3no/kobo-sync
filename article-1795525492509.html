You Can’t Control Your Kids All the Time

The value of picking your battles

One of the best pieces of parenting advice I ever saw was when I scrolled a Twitter thread (yes, really) asking older moms of 5+ kids what their biggest lesson from their parenting experience was. One seasoned mom said, “Almost nothing is as big of a deal as you think it is.”

At the time, I was deep in Mommy Instagram Psychosis, where every single decision I made, every single interaction with my kids, and every “failure” felt like a huge deal. I lived in fear of either accidentally traumatizing my kids, or being too permissive and accidentally raising a future serial killer. My youngest was still a baby, but my older child was a very stubborn preschooler. He would get fixated on inconsequential things and throw tantrums about them all day (I still remember the day-long tantrum over a missing balloon pump) demand unreasonable things (he once went on a hunger strike and his primary objective was to obtain a chocolate-only diet, which we did not provide) and spin up drama over some of the silliest things imaginable (see: his insistence on bringing a backpack full of genie lamps to school.)

He’s now six, so tantrums have been replaced with more-manageable lawyering. But at his wildest, we often couldn’t “control” him, despite everyone online demanding that parents “control their damn kids,” like it’s as simple as just telling them what to do and having them listen to you. This isn’t coming from a place of a gentle parent who thinks any form of consequence is child abuse, but sometimes he would (and occasionally still does) blow through all reasonable consequences in about five minutes and simply not care.

I’m obviously still learning, and I may look back on this era of my parenting and say I was wrong, but for now I feel reasonably confident in the fact that picking your battles is an extremely important part of discipline.

I thought about this topic again after hearing the news that a child (age unclear) had accidentally led to a beloved hippo’s death at the Cincinnati Zoo via an increasingly bizarre chain of events that otherwise sound concocted by the worst of my OCD intrusive thoughts: he or she flung a knit hat into the air, the hat hit a ceiling fan, the ceiling fan hurled the hat into a one-foot-wide gap in the hippo’s enclosure, the hippo ate the hat, and the hat caused a fatal bowel obstruction.

The Twitter reaction was that any halfway decent parent should have known that this kind of thing was bound to happen, and should have already been well-versed enough in hippo anatomy to know how thin and fragile their gastrointestinal tracts are. And even then, throwing your hat in the air is annoying, so even if a parent didn’t know it could spark danger, they should have put an end to it immediately. More broadly: parents today can’t control their damn kids.

Now, if my kid was flinging a hat around, I’d probably take the hat away because clearly he didn’t want to wear it anyway, and I wouldn’t want to risk losing the hat or having it hit someone in the face. But there are plenty of other “annoying” things kids can do, where making a big deal of the behavior could provide them a bit of a thrill (yes, some kids really thrive on negative attention) and then you’re stuck dying on a really stupid hill.

First, let’s talk about discipline. The easiest disciplinary tactic is just to distract your kids so you don’t have to “put your foot down” in the first place at all. This is easier to do with some kids than others. Despite what the gentle parenting influencers claim, my son was never able to be redirected. It works for some kids, though. My daughter was upset about not being able to eat a popsicle for lunch, but I quickly distracted her with an art project and she snapped out of it.

Then, there are “natural consequences,” which almost anyone would agree are an ideal first line of defense if distraction isn’t an option. Some examples here: if your kid refuses to wear a coat when it’s cold outside, take them outside and let them feel how cold it is, and usually they’ll opt for the coat without you having to push it on them. If your kid uses a toy as a weapon against their sibling, the toy is taken away. If your kid is playing too rough with a toy and it breaks, they learn not to play rough. Sometimes, natural consequences are a bit less convenient. For example, if your kid is acting out an amusement park, the natural consequence is that they have to leave. But that’s not realistic—you paid a lot of money to go there, and you just arrived, plus the well-behaved sibling is having a nice time. And, as I’ve learned, leaving a place because a kid is misbehaving only works if they want to be there, and kids are more likely to misbehave in places they find boring, where going home is actually the desired outcome.

Then, there are delayed, imposed consequences. These are not ideal for toddlers and preschoolers because they likely don’t understand them. A good example would be “taking away TV.” If my three-year-old is doing something naughty at 11 AM, and I tell her she’ll lose TV (and she’s only allowed to watch TV after dinner anyway) she will absolutely forget about this by the time the consequence is realized. Even my six-year-old son will sometimes ask me if he’s lost TV as evening arrives, because he vaguely remembers doing something naughty earlier that day but can’t remember if TV was revoked.

This all makes modern parenting pretty difficult because if you’re in my bubble of parents who believe it’s bad to scare, routinely scream at, or hit your kids, you just don’t have that many options for “controlling” them. This issue is made worse by the fact that this group of parents often don’t have that many privileges to revoke. If you don’t let your kids eat sugar, watch television, or play video games, what exactly do you “take away” when they’re being naughty? “You better behave yourself, Leo, or you’ll lose your nondescript piece of wood.”

The problem is that, if you subscribe to this type of parenting (even a less cartoonish kind) you don’t have that many tools in the arsenal for controlling a particularly defiant kid, but the world around you still expects you to adhere to this form of parenting and have perfectly-behaved children. This works if your kid is naturally complaint and easy, like my second. It does not work if your kid is like my first. And while I’m not opposed to laying down the law, I don’t believe I can fully “control” my first born without terrifying him into submission. If I have to pick between controlling my kid (while scaring him) and picking my battles, I will absolutely pick my battles and focus only on behavior that rises beyond the level of “annoying.”

This also means that when your kids are misbehaving in public, someone will judge you. They will judge you for having kids that are misbehaving at all, even if they’re toddler-age. They will judge you for not doing enough to control them. And if you attempt to correct their behavior in any way that seems “too harsh” (for example, if you raise your voice) they will judge you for not being able to control them with less disruptive measures or for being mean. And of course, if your kids are compliant because they have a screen, you may as well just go to Parent Jail. While it’s trendy these days to proclaim that “being bored” is an essential skill for children to learn, adults are incapable of actually tolerating the way bored kids behave in public, which, even if not destructive, is often very annoying.

So, what’s wrong with kids these days? I argue: nothing. Kids are kids and will do annoying things sometimes, and you should probably save the big guns for times when their behavior is actually a real problem and worth the fight. But if we behaved better than our own kids (a declaration poisoned by the fact that most of us don’t remember being four) it’s probably because most of us were scared of our parents, but are intentionally choosing to raise kids who are not scared of us. The gentle parenting influencers will insist that children are better-behaved when they aren’t actively punished or intimidated. This is simply not true! Kids snap into shape when they’re scared of their parents, and this is not necessarily a good thing, so if you want your kids to not be scared of you, you should accept that sometimes they’ll do annoying kid stuff.

Many millennials reading this probably grew up scared of their parents, even if their parents weren’t remotely abusive. I absolutely grew up terrified of my parents, not because screaming at me was always their first choice, but because I blew through all other options with my notorious stubbornness. One example: At four, I was expelled from ballet school for spitting on the mirrors and teaching the other girls how to spit on the mirrors. In fact, other parents had called for my removal. I was given the option to still perform in the year-end recital, but I bragged to my mom that I had refused to learn the moves anyway and planned to crash the recital stomping around and making loud noises. Points for baby CHH for honesty, I guess.

As I recall, my mom yelled at me for this, and I cried. But it worked: I don’t think I was re-enrolled in ballet school, but never again did I misbehave quite like that in any other school. But importantly, she screamed at me because nothing else worked. The natural consequence of being expelled or excluded from the recital meant nothing to me, because I thought it was stupid anyway. There were basically no imposed consequences that could be leveraged because at that age, I didn’t have a whole lot of TV or sugar to take away. So, she yelled at me.

There were many times that I wanted to do something naughty as a child, but quickly I remembered that doing so might get me screamed at. Kids really hate being screamed at, and it might actually work to make them listen to you. Fear is, of course, a great motivator. But to what end? My parents have both apologized for yelling at me too much, and while I completely forgive them (I was, in fact, kind of a dick) I agree that it was a mistake and I try never to yell at my kids. I’ve only ever yelled at my kids briefly, in the form of, “Oh, are you KIDDING me?!” or “GET OFF THAT RIGHT NOW!” (for example, when my son tried to jump on the baggage carousel at the airport.) But I’ve never thought to deliberately sit them down to scream at them until they felt adequately scared and sorry. And yet, these kinds of lectures were pretty common a few decades back! Yelling at your kids wasn’t something you did only because you snapped suddenly in a moment of weakness or to avert a danger, but a deliberate and legitimate strategy for punishment.

Maybe you have a naturally well-behaved kid, and you’re able to discipline them without ever raising your voice. Maybe natural consequences are all you need for them to be perfectly compliant and for nobody to ever think you’ve failed to manager your kids. But other people have kids who are spirited enough to put their parents in a bind: do you accept some degree of naughty, age-appropriate behavior, or do you terrify them into submission? I pick the former, because as that veteran mom said so long ago, most of this is just not a big deal.
