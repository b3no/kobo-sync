The New Fashion Snobs: Anti-Capitalists

It's not about money anymore--it's about "taste." Which costs money.

Apr 29
∙
Paid
 
READ IN APP
 

When you think of the fashion industry, and Fashion People who are “in the know” you probably think of rich people. Or at the very least, young, artsy cosmopolitan people who are upper class enough to be adjacent to wealth even if they don’t actually have lots of money. Anna Wintour is famously not a woman of the people.

But there’s a new brand of fashion snob in town—okay, by “town” I mean “the Internet,” but bear with me—and their trademark is mixing the fashion snobbery of the cattiest editor at Vogue with seemingly-discordant far-left politics and a hatred of “capitalism.”

I’ve been noticing this archetype for a while now, where certain leftists have nominally divorced the concept of taste from money, and therefore feel it’s politically consistent to mock women who are conservative, or just seem vaguely uncultured and normie-coded, for their “tasteless” clothes—often clothes which appear inoffensive to 99% of the population.

The latest viral target: Erika Kirk (although it’s hardly the first time she’s been skewered for her fashion) who had the gall to wear a Saks Fifth Avenue dress from the “clearance section” and a Gucci clutch that peaked in the late 2010s. This was tweeted by a self-proclaimed socialist, by the way:

I’m not going to focus on the author of the tweet—enough people have already dragged her, with many going way too far for the stakes of the situation. But this isn’t even about her, nor do I want to talk about her. She is just one example of a relatively vocal group of fashion snobs on the Internet who act like a cattier version of Janice Dickinson on America’s Next Top Model, but with a “it’s okay, because this person is conservative or at the very least, too normie and not visibly edgy or leftist, so they’re fair game.” This also happened when this group dragged Lauren Sanchez for her “tasteless” wedding dress, and then mocked the dress’s defenders for being too low-class and uncultured themselves to understand what was wrong with it.

Obviously, critiquing celebrity red carpet outfits is just a normal thing to do, and most people who enjoy fashion have done it at one point or another. But there are a few unspoken rules. First of all, this criticism mostly just applies to people in Hollywood who are being dressed by teams of strategists, not regular people or political figures (it would be odd, to say the least, to criticize the wardrobe of Elizabeth Warren.) Melania Trump’s fashion should be juxtaposed against that of Jill Biden, not Hunter Schafer.

Second, the substance of the celebrity fashion critique should always be about the outfit, not the person. And because these critiques are generally limited to red carpet events, it’s understood that all the outfits are from high-end designers and chosen by a team of people, so critiques aren’t about the class status or “taste” of the individual wearing the clothes.

So no, this cohort of fashion critics goes beyond typical red carpet yays and nays. The criticism feels personal, often politically-motivated, and is directed toward people who never claimed to be trendsetters, who shouldn’t be held to that standard.

I had a run-in with this cohort myself not too long ago, despite not being remotely conservative or really famous at all. After sewing deep into the night, I stupidly logged onto Twitter and expressed a sleep-deprived degree of confusion about a silver bodycon Gucci dress in a runway show that, at first glance, read “fast fashion” more than “avant garde.” Suddenly, I received an onslaught of quote tweets from sardonic leftist women about how I was a frumpy middle-aged mom who needed to keep her nose out of Fashion World because I wasn’t a “Fashion Person.” In particular, I was dragged for saying the dress probably cost “over $1,000” because it actually probably cost more than $5,000 (this is, I repeat, over $1,000) and if I couldn’t tell the difference between a $1,000 dress and a $5,000 dress I was a broke loser.

Your leftists, sir.

But…they also suggested the dress wasn’t out of reach for people without $5,000 to spare—convenient, isn’t it?—because “secret industry gays” would hook them up with the dress “backstage at the show.” Um, okay. I have yet to procure a secret industry gay who gives me free Gucci dresses at runway shows that I would, presumably, attend between putting my children to bed and doing the laundry. I will continue my quest to enter this echelon of society—which I’m sure has nothing to do with class.

I know I’m sort of crashing out over this because these people were mean to me personally, but you know what? Fuck you, it’s my Substack. Anyway, back to my article. My big, beautiful article.

You would think that engaging in any type of class-oriented snobbery is antithetical to being a leftist, let alone a critique as frivolous as “this person wore a dress from last season—on the clearance rack!” But, see, they justify it by claiming that this is an issue of taste, not money—and that Erika Kirk and Lauren Sanchez have plenty of money, but no taste. If they simply had taste, they would have known where to go to find something that was actually stylish, and they could have spent less money on these secret, classy items (this was the actual explanation given by Erika Kirk’s critic.) But of course, they won’t tell you what those places are, or how to find these coveted stylish items because if all the middle-class centrist losers from flyover country knew their secrets, they wouldn’t get to lord it over everyone else. Sometimes, they’ll just say “vintage.”

In fact, mocking wealthy, conservative or just “not clearly leftist and cool” women for their bad attire is an easy way for them to keep this belief system running. If Karoline Leavitt looks terrible, and Karoline Leavitt is extremely privileged, that means fashion has nothing to do with class and money, and therefore it’s okay to be a fashion snob, the same way you can safely be a leftist and mock someone for writing shitty poetry. The only problem is, writing poetry is free. But clothes of any kind—unless you are stealing them, which is a whole other issue—still cost money.

When the Erika Kirk drama happened, a particularly interesting dichotomy showed up in the discourse. A fight erupted between leftist women who were so cultured, high class and refined that they could spot cheap stitching from a photo taken thirty feet away, and conservative women one-upping each other for the coveted title of Biggest Spendthrift. This culminated in one woman, the final boss of the “wore duct tape dress to the prom” archetype, who claimed to have pilfered her own wedding dress from “a barn.” (I have never before seen anyone shop from “Dress Barn” so literally.) It was bizarre, to say the least, to see frugality associated with conservatives (let alone conservatives proudly championing off-brand Chinese goods) and luxury—sorry, ahem, taste—associated with leftists.

This particular dichotomy was on full display during episode 2 of this season of Euphoria. In a particularly memorable scene, Maddy visits Cassie at the pool and gives her advice on how to monetize her influencer career, sardonically implying she has no taste or originality. We are, I suppose, meant to cheer for Maddy here, since Cassie wronged her in the previous season, and that was how many women on Twitter reacted to the scene. Cassie is also in a more financially privileged position than Maddy, at least at the time of the episode. I think a lot of leftist fashion snobs on Twitter see themselves in Maddy—Maddy is edgy, left-coded, camp, femme fatale, whatever. Cassie is MAGA, normie, unironic, Mar-A-Lago. True to the narrative, Maddy witheringly tells Cassie she has no “taste.”

But there is a big difference between the Maddy/Cassie dynamic and these women on Twitter. First of all, Cassie and Maddy know each other and have beef. Also, per my insistence that not everything depicted on Euphoria is meant to be endorsed, maybe we’re supposed to think Maddy is being kind of a bitch. But I think this particular interaction sums up the archetypes that certain people have playing out in their heads. Fashion is irrelevant to money, it’s all about taste, and conservative or inadequately edgy people (moms, normies, Sydney Sweeney adjacent people, whatever) have “bad taste.” Ergo, it’s okay to mock them in a way that would typically be considered “snobby.”

But the snobbery is the point. It brings me back to the concept of temporarily-embarrassed high school bullies, and the fact that people who don’t feel they have much power or privilege (whether that’s true or just a self-imposed narrative) feel justified in “punching up,” often in a far more aggressive and rude way than would ever be necessary. And certainly, most of us have done this to some extent. I joked myself about “MAGA dresses,” although I was the butt of the joke because I wore one. But yes, throwing these people a bone, there is plenty of truth to the fact that high fashion as an art form is antithetical to traditional, safe, heteronormative (not the assumption that most people are straight, but the assumption that gender roles should be rigid and heterosexuality should be the only accepted orientation) aesthetics. Put another way, I can see why leftist fashion snobs don’t think they’re being inconsistent. If your ideal of fashion is not simply wearing flattering and pretty clothes, but making a big statement and taking risks, you just won’t see a lot of that at CPAC, except for the spare person wearing a tinfoil hat and guzzling colloidal silver.

But as much as we can claim “taste” and “money” aren’t the same thing, there’s obviously a lot of overlap. Clothes cost money, as evidence by the fact that every “fashion guide” on Substack features $900 Khaite shoes (Telling a bunch of writers to spend $900 on shoes? Really?) And no, I do not believe that everyone has a “secret industry gay who hooks them up with free designer deadstock.” Girl, what? Do you think the “median American” or even someone in the top 5%, has a secret “industry gay?” Come on now. Money or not, this is an issue of class, and if you’re not willing to be honest and admit that being upper class (even if you aren’t materially wealthy) gives you more proximity to the type of clothes that signal “taste,” regardless of what cool hookup gets you a discount, then you’ve lost the plot.

But these people, despite claiming to be proletariat revolutionaries, enjoy the exclusivity of it all. Telling everyone where to find clothes with “taste” at low prices would simply ruin the game. If you know, you know. Oh well. No ethical consumption under capitalism, right? Although, I suppose you could come pretty close by…uhhh…sewing almost all your own clothing.
