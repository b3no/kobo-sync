Slow Boring candidates won the midterms

A look back at our fundraising slate and lessons for next time

Matthew Yglesias
Nov 25
∙
Paid
 
READ IN APP
 

With Thanksgiving tomorrow, I want to express my appreciation for everyone who contributed to this year’s Slow Boring slate of candidates and offer an update on how we did. I also wanted to call out and thank Ethan Winter. He’s a friend whose day job is at Mind the Gap, an organization that provides research-driven guidance to donors on the most cost-effective strategies for protecting and supporting American democracy. He’s done a ton of behind-the-scenes work on these Slow Boring slates and finally received permission from his bosses to take a public bow.

Here’s a nice summary of our slate of candidates. They, thanks to what was obviously a very strong election overall for Democrats, mostly won. When looking at this chart for the purposes of assessing our judgment, it’s worth recalling that not only was the national climate very blue, but it was also a bit more than four points bluer than the publicly available information suggested. I obviously would love to have private information about the real state of public opinion, but even though polling error is real, nobody has unearthed a reliable way of out-guessing the polls. So when you look at Slow Boring money that went into races like the Senate contests in Iowa and Alaska, those are the ones that a reasonable person would have assumed were on the knife’s edge.

In light of what we now know about the results in South Carolina, Mississippi, and Louisiana, it seems like some reassessment of electoral dynamics in the Deep South is warranted. Some of this is probably differential turnout effects that wouldn’t be present in a presidential election year, but it’s definitely something I’ll be thinking about as we head into the next cycle.

That being said, the point of the Slow Boring slate — somewhat in contrast to Mind the Gap’s mainline work — isn’t just to identify high-leverage races but to help build a bench and a pipeline for pragmatic, culturally moderate, pro-growth Democrats who can change the trajectory of the party and the country. And I think we made great progress on those fronts. For paid subscribers, we dig into individual races and lessons learned below.

Two who lost

I’m sad about Bale Dalton, who was always a reach pick in a tough race (though he won a vigorously contested primary against two opponents who wouldn’t have been able to make it close). When a race gets this close, there’s a lot of woulda coulda shoulda stuff, starting with the fact that a cleared field would have been helpful here.

I think the Democratic Party needs to re-evaluate the whole Florida situation in a more serious way, though. Lakshya Jain thinks Democrats may have lost the Florida Senate race on the basis of candidate quality. A lot of people have noted, though, that Angie Nixon only ran a bit behind David Jolly.

The problem is that as Michael Grunwald warned way back in June, Jolly — despite being an ex-Republican — wasn’t really doing anything to convey the idea that he was a different kind of Democrat. My intuition is that these kinds of linkages across the ticket can be important. In Iowa, both statewide candidates were running smart, differentiated campaigns that made life easier for House candidates downballot. In Texas, Democrats seem to have really pulled together up and down the ballot in a cooperative way. Florida, though, is a mess.

The other candidate from our slate who lost is Bill Hill, who ran as an independent for Alaska’s at-large House seat. Given that Democrats running as Democrats won the gubernatorial and Senate elections in Alaska, it’s hard to make the case that it’s impossible to win there with a D after your name.

I thought the Osborn Gambit — backing independent candidates in red states rather than running Democrats — was worth trying in 2024, and the results were good enough that I thought attempting to expand it in 2026 wasn’t crazy, even though it was never my favorite idea. But between Hill losing, the vote-splitting in Montana, and the fact that the shine came off Dan Osborn in Nebraska (he received a smaller share of the vote this year than in 2024), I think it’s time to pronounce this idea a failure. Adam Hamilton and Josh Turek (and, for that matter, Dalton) all show that it is possible for moderates to win contested primaries. You just have to try.

Senate champions

We consistently championed Turek going back to his primary against the more liberal Zach Wahls, and I think Slow Boring is one of the only national outlets to have given him any measure of air cover against activist criticism for his moves to the center.

Pre-election polling suggested this would be a nail-biter, and while I’m glad he won relatively comfortably, I am especially glad that Iowa has a senator who should be capable of winning re-election even in a less favorable environment. Turek is tough as hell, he’s smart, he has an amazing personal story, and his win should open up space for more pragmatic candidates in the future. We also backed Lindsay James downballot in Iowa, and as I said above, I think she was lifted up not only by our money but by the fact that Turek and Rob Sand ran great statewide campaigns.

Mary Peltola’s prior career as a House member for Alaska was the key proof point for my thesis that a return to all-of-the-above energy policies would be possible and useful for Democrats. She paved the way for James Talarico (who was only not on our list because his fundraising was so amazing) and other Texas Democrats to flip the whole state. I think the implications of Democratic Party success in energy states have not yet really sunk in for much of the party, but we understand.

Of course, with perfect information these two races wouldn’t have seemed quite as pivotal, but given the fog of war, I think these were good choices. I also think these candidates are the two most battle-proven of the Democratic Party seat-flippers.

Unlike some of the other candidates who won, Peltola and Turek had to run against reasonably good opponents. Dan Sullivan and Ashley Hinson are party-line Trump-era Republicans, but they’re not ravaged by scandal and didn’t commit egregious gaffes or otherwise manifest severe vulnerabilities. They just got beaten fair and square by quality candidates.

Building the bench

Ben McAdams (UT-01) originally got on our radar when Utah’s map looked different and this was going to be a red-leaning seat that only a moderate could win. I believe in the moderate electability advantage, but I also just prefer moderates, so once it got redrawn as a safe seat, I still supported him. The upside of his thumping victory is that unlike a conventional progressive, he’s now set up to challenge Senator Mike Lee or otherwise run statewide.

Similarly, Bobby Pulido (TX-15) crushed his opponent so badly that in retrospect he probably didn’t need our money. But I’m a Bobby Pulido hipster, and I believe we were the earliest advocates in the media for taking his race and Johnny Garcia’s (TX-35) seriously.

I also don’t really know to what extent it’s right to say that these two rode the Hispanic wave to victory versus to what extent they helped create it.

Bernie Sanders did not invent economic populism, and guys like Pulido and Garcia — who refers to himself as an “old school Democrat” — helped revive the brand among ancestral Democrats in South Texas by reminding them of that. The whole 2026 achievement in Texas is amazing and multifaceted, but I think these two guys are an important part of the story and that Ted Cruz should be looking over his shoulder in the Senate.

Rebecca Cooke (WI-03) was the first of several House candidates this cycle to qualify as a “Bernie Dog,” endorsed by both Sanders and the Blue Dog caucus. This is theoretically the most tractable path forward for winning both primaries and special elections. I’m not 100 percent sure how she and her two new colleagues Sam Forstag (MT-01) and Bob Brooks (PA-07) are going to make this work in practice, but she’s very smart, and I hope she figures it out.

Don Davis (NC-01) took on a very difficult reassignment in running for re-election after he was subject to a brutal gerrymander. He’d overperformed in the past, he ran to the center in this race, and he won. I’d say he’s an unquestioned favorite for a future statewide run, but North Carolina has an embarrassment of riches among Davis, Governor Josh Stein, and Attorney General Jeff Jackson.

Manny Rutinel (CO-08) ran left in his House primary but is very pragmatic and indicated a clear desire to move to the center for the general. He’s also a great champion on housing and artificial intelligence issues, and I’m looking forward to him carrying that work forward.

Stuff in the states

Here’s where my minor regrets in light of the scale of the overall blue wave reside.

Anita Earls and our three state-legislature candidates were selected as part of longer-term strategies related to beating Republican gerrymandering, but given the scale of Democratic victories, these races ended up being not so pivotal. Kris Mayes, similarly, will prevent an election denier from enforcing election laws in a critical state, but this ended up being an easy victory.

The Keisha Lance Bottoms race for Georgia governor I honestly just find weird. This was a really, really important election with big implications for Medicaid, redistricting, and the basic integrity of the 2028 election, but major national donors seemed content to let her get badly outspent despite massive enthusiasm for Stacey Abrams in earlier races.

She won comfortably enough in the end that I suppose it was fine, but there wasn’t a ton of polling in this election, and I was nervous about it all cycle. I wish I understood why there was so little interest in this race. Given a palpable lack of enthusiasm for Bottoms, I wish national figures had tried harder to push Geoff Duncan as the nominee because that would have sent a cool message nationally. It all worked out, though! And I stubbornly think that I was right that this was an incredibly important non-federal race that merited investment.

Lessons learned

Thanks once again to everyone who contributed. Slow Boring’s readership stands out for its generosity, and your efforts make a real difference in the world. I heard over the course of the campaign season from many of the candidates on this list, who were all very grateful, and that gratitude of course belongs primarily to you. It’s rare to have money in politics that’s explicitly pegged to political pragmatism and designed to create better incentives for the future, and that’s why I think this is so important.

My mantra for the next two years is about agency in politics. The blue wave took some good luck and a lot of craftsmanship from the people who make ads, but it also required intelligent, tough-minded, and occasionally courageous choices from people seeking jobs that are very important and very difficult and also not especially lucrative.

Going forward, I’m proclaiming the independent experiment dead — it’s a manifestation of a lack of agency. I also need to be more agentic myself at updating the list more dynamically in light of a shifting national environment. I honestly found it difficult to believe that the polls were just as likely to be underestimating Democrats as overestimating them, and that left me too conservative in our final recommendations.

I think we all did a great job this cycle, but nothing is perfect, and we need to be constantly learning from evidence and experience and doing better in the future.
