The futile quest to define class in America

The economy is way more complicated than workers vs. owners.

The implosion of Graham Platner’s Senate campaign has spurred an interesting discussion of class in America. I think there are two reasons this episode is revealing, both of which stem from the Platner campaign’s decision to characterize him as a working-class candidate with working-class appeal.

One reason is that while Platner is unambiguously “working class” in the sense that he “does not have a college degree,” he’s an odd fit for the role in a lot of other ways. He’s a small-business owner whose mom also owns a small business and whose dad is a successful country lawyer. He went to private high schools and to George Washington University in D.C. Yes, he dropped out of G.W. and thus qualifies as a non-college person — which really is how political demographers characterize the working class — but does this actually make sense?

When I first started working in political journalism, I found the idea of a definition of working class that included Bill Gates (at the time the richest man in the world) somewhat absurd. But I was beaten down by convention and have generally used the term the way everyone else does.

The Platner experience, though, puts this question back on the table.

The other reason is that while Platner had a good chance of beating Susan Collins before being derailed by scandal, there was no evidence that he was beating benchmarks in appealing to non-college voters.

Zohran Mamdani’s strength in the New York City mayoral race was with younger and better-educated voters. In Michigan, Haley Stevens is winning with non-college voters while Abdul El-Sayed is dominating with better-educated ones. You even see this internal to labor unions. The current leadership of the United Auto Workers is a left-wing faction within the union that was able to prevail in the most recent internal election largely thanks to the strength of unionized graduate-student instructors rather than people who work in the auto industry. It’s just clearly true that the audience for left-wing politics is primarily college graduates.

One way of squaring both of these circles is to adopt a line that Platner himself used in an interview: The working class is anyone who works for a living.

“My definition of working class these days is essentially anybody who makes money from wages,” he told the New York Times in May. “If you work for a living and you go out and put in hours and you pay taxes just like everyone else, I think that’s quite fair.”

When I first saw he said that, I thought it was idiosyncratic. But over the past couple of months, it’s become clear this definition appeals to a lot of people. It helpfully squares the circle — the kinds of left-wing people who are most inclined to talk about working-class politics tend to be college-educated professionals, and they naturally want to define the adherents of their own movement as working class. And it makes semantic sense. What is the “working” in “working class” if not a reference to the fact that people are working for a living?

This definition of working class is so expansive that it’s not actually useful, though.

The idea of “class” is an important part of our social and cultural legacy. It’s obviously a critical concept in Karl Marx’s work, and Marx exerts a large influence over all kinds of left-wing social analysis.

It’s also a real and prominent feature of 18th- and 19th-century British and European societies, and all kinds of non-leftist literary and historical works that constitute our shared heritage invoke it all the time. But I think the more you try to make these ideas work in characterizing the contemporary United States, the more it looks like a mirage. We have a lot of inequality in this country, but we don’t have much in the way of sharply discontinuous social classes.

Traditional “class” analysis is discontinuous

If you open a book by Jane Austen or a novel such as George Eliot’s “Middlemarch,” you’ll find portrayals of English social class in the first half of the 19th century.

And a key feature of the society depicted in these works is that while there is a degree of social mobility, both upward and downward, there are also real boundary conditions around class. England had a titled aristocracy with formal membership rules. You’re either in that class or you’re not! It also had a gentry class, composed of the owners of large estates who made a living by renting out land to tenant farmers. The tenant farmers were a different class — lumped together with the servants who’d be employed by the gentry. Between the tenant farmers and the gentry were a “middle class” of professionals and also a group of yeoman farmers who owned their own land.

And over the course of the 19th century, these arrangements were perturbed by the Industrial Revolution. A new class of rich people owned factories and a new “working class” of urban laborers worked in the factories. Importantly, though, as your English teachers would no doubt be at pains to point out, while money was very important, class was still somewhat distinct in these settings. Making a lot of money in trade or manufacturing did not make you a gentleman. And affluent tradespeople would often try to buy a country estate and have their kids or grandkids assimilate into gentry status.

This is just to say that while it’s not that you couldn’t cross the boundaries from one social class to another, there was a bit of a taboo around it. There was a difference between being a real country squire and a guy who bought an estate yesterday. And people looked at least a little bit askance at folks who worked their way up the ladder.

You clearly see in the Platner story, though, that there’s just nothing like that in America.

When people say that Elon Musk’s dad owned an emerald mine, that’s meant as a knock on Musk. In America, we diminish people’s achievements by saying they came from affluent families who gave them a leg up. And it’s normal to accuse people of painting an exaggerated portrait of hardscrabble origins.

Michael Kinsley wrote a great piece 25 years ago about the prominence of reverse snobbery in American life that has, if anything, only become more prevalent. We just don’t have discontinuous social classes the way you’d find in Austen books or while watching or reading “Game of Thrones.” It does not make sense in our society for a kid with rich parents to try to make himself look better by obscuring the fact that his parents earned their money rather than inherited it.

But the Marxist idea that how people relate to the means of production is important still makes a lot of sense. It’s just that here, too, we don’t have a sharp categorical divide.

America’s mixed bourgeois society

The Marxist idea was that industrial capitalism would end up looking a lot like feudal agriculture. Instead of landlords and tenants (or nobles and peasants), you’d have bourgeois people who own the means of production and a proletarian class of people who work for a living. And clearly this dynamic does exist in the United States today.

Walmart is the largest private-sector employer in America, and seven of the 400 richest people in America are heirs to the Walmart fortune. Walmart, of course, employs all kinds of people, but the largest segment of its workforce is rank-and-file folks making low wages. It’s not exactly the same as a factory in 1876, but the dynamic is broadly similar: You have people who own shares of stock in the company, and you have people working for the company.

But while these poles exist, they exist in a context in which most people are somewhat between them.

I’m sure the guy who actually runs Walmart, C.E.O. John Furner, owns many shares of Walmart stock. But he didn’t inherit them. He started out stocking shelves at a Walmart retail location in Bentonville, went to the University of Arkansas, moved on to the company’s management track after graduating, and apparently did well and became C.E.O.

That’s obviously an unusual life trajectory. But it’s true that most established companies are run by a set of executives whose financial interests are aligned with shareholders via stock options but who are also definitely “working for a living.”

And while coming all the way up from stocking shelves is unusual, you do normally get to be C.E.O. by doing a lot of lower-status jobs first. Tim Cook worked for 12 years at IBM, became C.O.O. of the computer reselling division of a company called Intelligent Electronics, and then was an executive at Compaq when Steve Jobs hired him in 1998 to become Apple’s senior vice president of worldwide operations. From there he was promoted two or three more times and eventually became C.E.O. of the world’s most valuable company.

It’s not just that there’s something illogical about characterizing rich executives like Furner and Cook as “working class” — it defies the actual economics of the situation. The executives are definitely working for a living, but they are receiving all this stock-based compensation to make sure their economic interests are fully aligned with the owners.

But who are the owners?

With Walmart, the Walton family is by far the largest shareholder. But the largest shareholders in Apple are just investment funds like Vanguard and BlackRock. About 65 percent of the company’s shares are owned by E.T.F.s and mutual funds.

Which is to say it’s not even like there’s some Richie Rich owner pulling the strings behind Cook. He, as C.E.O., is the pointy end of the spear of the interests of capital.

But capital in this case is largely random retirement accounts. Most very rich people work for their daily bread, and most working people own at least some capital. More than 60 percent of Americans own shares of stock, and a comparable share of households own a home. These are also partially life-cycle effects. You have young people who own neither stock nor homes but who realistically will later in life — only 44 percent of people under 30 own stock, for example.

There’s also a big education gradient. More than 80 percent of college graduates own shares, as do 87 percent of people with incomes over $100,000. People with lower earnings and less education are less likely to own stock.

It’s not that you would look at Jane, who works in the accounting department and has a 401(k), and John, the C.F.O., and say, “They’re just the same. They both have jobs and they both own stock.” These are different economic situations. But the American economy is a spectrum, to say nothing of the very large share of people who don’t work for for-profit enterprises.

Workers without capitalists

This is, I think, a very underrated divide in terms of Americans’ relationships to the means of production. About 15 percent of the labor force works for some level of the government. Another 10 percent work for nonprofits, mostly in health care.

These jobs feature distinctions of pay, job function, and status, but they don’t feature literal clashes between workers and owners because there are no owners.

One reason this distinction is important is that labor unions, which are very important in the political rhetoric of class, are much more prevalent in the government sector than in private business. Concurrently, a large share of recent labor-union organizing has been happening in the nonprofit sector — especially in universities and nonprofits with progressive missions.

Noah Smith recently accused graduate-student labor-union activists of effectively LARPing as working class. I’m not sure I would put it that way.

But I do think it’s relevant that they aren’t organizing against the interests of a capital-owning class — they’re fighting an internecine battle about the distribution of resources inside the university. These different stakeholders are then aligned with each other when you turn to a topic like whether there should be more subsidies to universities, something that all different kinds of university employees can agree on.

I think that one reason the kind of rich-versus-poor politics that progressives want to meme into being struggles to get off the ground is that many policy conflicts pit one economic sector against another rather than the poor against the rich.

It’s normal to have conflicts in any workplace between management and rank-and-file workers (though most Americans like their boss), but when Ford and G.M. object to regulations requiring electric vehicles, the U.A.W. is on their side.

Not only is the American labor market more complicated than “workers versus owners,” but within the category of “workers,” most people have at least some valuable specialized skills — they’re not just an undifferentiated mass of labor. People generally want the specific enterprise they work for to do well because if it expands, that gives them a shot at upward mobility, while if it does layoffs, they will find some of their specific experience devalued. And they definitely want their occupation to prosper.

Inequality without dichotomies

That’s all just to say that the American economy is a land of contrasts. Or, it’s complicated. The point, I think, is that if you’re looking for a modern-day equivalent that will track the divide between gentry and peasants, you’re not going to find it.

Marx’s observations about the difference between people who own the means of production and the people who hire out their labor to the owners of capital goods resonate somewhat. But relatively few people fall on one side or the other of a stark divide. Most people own at least a little slice of the financial capital in the United States, and an even larger share of the rich and powerful corporate decision-makers in America are in fact working. Lots of people aren’t working for capitalist business enterprises at all. Beyond the government and nonprofit workers we talked about, lots of people are self-employed or work for small shops. Most poor people don’t have full-time jobs, and most people living off investment income are retired people who spent their prime years working.

The college/non-college divide is useful for political analysis because it probably tracks some underlying psychological traits that are politically relevant. But there are big, well-known gaps within the education system. There’s a huge difference between attending Yale and attending Southern Connecticut State University, which is just two miles to the north. In particular, as college attendance has expanded, merely having a degree from somewhere or other has become less and less an elite credential.

I don’t think any of this is particularly surprising or controversial.

But it does mean that all statements of the form “the real class divide in America is such-and-such” are going to fail. I don’t want to say something like “there is no class in America” because that would sound like I’m denying there is inequality in America when you can easily look it up and see that there’s a lot. You can look at the top 1 percent or top 10 percent or top 0.001 percent or you can compare the median to the bottom 10 percent, and these are all very different measures and they all show an unequal society.

I’m just saying the modern economy is too large and complicated to characterize as a simple workers-versus-owners dichotomy. And in terms of social attitudes, it’s not shameful or suspect to be upwardly mobile or cross class lines.

College versus non-college does track a big difference in political behavior, but my guess is that’s because it tracks psychological attitudes rather than specific economic roles. When you take a weird edge case like Platner, who got into a selective school but then dropped out, you get a guy with normal millennial college-graduate political views and a standard base of electoral support for someone with those views.
