Mamdani’s not the first to try to bring cheap groceries to N.Y.C.

Walmart versus the stationary bandits

Matthew Yglesias
Aug 4
∙
Paid
 
READ IN APP
 

As Zohran Mamdani inches forward with his plan to introduce low-cost government-run grocery stores in New York City, I think it’s worth noting that he’s far from the first person to have noticed that the city’s supermarkets are unusually expensive or to have thought it might be a good idea to try to bring in some lower-cost options.

The most prominent attempt to do this was not by a democratic socialist or even a mayor, but rather a businessman working out of the obscure town of Bentonville, Ark.

His name was H. Lee Scott, and he was the chief executive officer of Walmart back in 2004 when they announced the opening of their first New York City location in Rego Park in Queens.

Rego Park is suburban-ish by New York City standards — in true Real America style, it’s right by a freeway exit — but very dense by U.S. standards, and it’s also got a subway line. So it was a logical spot for an urban Walmart experiment. And Walmart, by all accounts, had ambitions beyond this one neighborhood in Queens. New York City is an enormous market, and a number of significantly smaller cities share N.Y.C.’s traditional urban layout. So if Walmart could make it work in Rego Park, they’d expand to other N.Y.C. locations, and that would offer proof of concept for a broader urban strategy.

This wasn’t a totally natural fit for Walmart’s existing business, but when you’re already the largest retailer in the country, you take your opportunities where you find them.

Their logic essentially tracks what Mamdani is trying to do.

Scholars Stefano DellaVigna and Matthew Gentzkow have found that chain retailers very rarely practice location-specific price variation. So while groceries in N.Y.C. are more expensive than the national average, the dominant cause is that the mix of stores operating in the city is different from what you see on average nationally.

Thus the Mamdani strategy of trying to open cheaper stores makes sense. Emek Basker found in 2005 that when Walmart enters a new market, average retail prices of grocery-type products fall by between 6 and 12 percent. Basker’s later research with Michael Noel finds that this is due to a small impact of competition (existing stores drop prices by maybe 1 percent) and a large direct impact of Walmart being cheap (its prices are 10 percent lower on average). A separate paper looking at Walmart’s entry into Mexico has a similar finding: large welfare gains driven mostly by Walmart itself being cheap with a small secondary effect from competition.

This is especially important for low-income New Yorkers because, as Jessie Handbury’s research finds, poor people in affluent cities can experience significant welfare loss from being exposed to a retail mix that’s optimized for rich customers.

Bringing in a store like Walmart, which is broad in its offerings but really does specialize in serving a thrifty client base, could be a huge help.

So what happened?

The long war on Walmart

This Queens episode played out during the heyday of a national coordinated campaign against Walmart, led by labor unions but especially by the United Food and Commercial Workers union, which represents employees of many supermarkets.

The goal of this campaign was to somehow coerce Walmart into becoming less hostile to union organizing or else (more realistically) halt the expansion of Walmart and thus protect U.F.C.W.-represented stores.

In my experience, the coordinated campaign concept is not very well understood, and this led to a lot of Walmart-related confusion in the early 21st century.

But the basic idea of a coordinated campaign is that once you’ve decided on a target, you throw the kitchen sink at it. So the reason U.F.C.W. was mad at Walmart was that they were undercutting already-organized stores on prices, which tended to put downward pressure on the union in negotiations. But there’s no law of politics that says you need to explicate your real reasons or even be logically coherent in your arguments when you’re going after an enemy.

So a lot of anti-Walmart rhetoric of this era would emphasize the threat Walmart posed to small businesses, for example. This was in fact logically contradictory to the specific union goal — Walmart, much more so than a small retail business, in principle could be unionized, and U.F.C.W. would never want to help small businesses gain protection against Safeway or Kroger. We also got takes about how Walmart is bad for the environment or even that Walmart costs the government money because the welfare state exists and it employs a lot of members of low-income households.

Barry Lynn, the intellectual father of the neo-Brandeisian antitrust movement, argued that Walmart was a monopoly just a few years before Amazon became an alleged monopoly.

The point is, a whole political infrastructure was built around stopping new Walmarts by any means possible.

And this infrastructure set its sights on the Rego Park project. That Walmart location was slated to be part of a larger development by Vornado Realty Trust, and opponents killed the store by pressuring the city council to push the developer to go in a different direction. Walmart then made a go at opening a store in Staten Island where they had the support of the borough president but dropped the plans when faced with environmental cleanup costs.

Having won there, Walmart opponents led by U.F.C.W. and a lobbyist named Richard Lipsky (who also worked to kill the Bloomberg-era congestion pricing plan and would later go to prison for bribery) worked through the Neighborhood Retail Alliance to preemptively mobilize to keep Walmart out of the Bronx. This successfully got C.E.O. H. Lee Scott to give up on bringing Walmart to N.Y.C. and was surely good for Lipsky’s “more than 10,000 city supermarkets, bodegas, greengrocers and restaurants” but bad for low-income New Yorkers.

In 2010–11, Walmart made a fresh push to open a store in Brooklyn but was blocked by political opponents. By 2014, the city council was demanding that Walmart stop giving to New York charities because they saw the philanthropy as a stealthy lobbying effort.

In 2017, Walmart threw in the towel.

As a result, this whole Walmart saga had been largely forgotten by 2021–22 when inflation made grocery prices a salient issue and was dead and gone by the 2025 mayoral campaign. But the fact remains that before Mamdani wanted to pilot the idea of low-cost groceries in New York, Scott had the same idea. And it was blocked by the political power of the entrenched incumbents who didn’t want to compete on price.

Stationary banditry runs amok in New York City

My story, in which New York needs less rent-seeking and regulatory protectionism, is obviously different from the leftist narrative promoted by the Mamdani fandom.

But you can sort of align these things.

As I’ve said before, the best way to understand the political economy of New York is in terms of Mancur Olson’s theory of stationary bandits: Mobile bandits raid a farming community and run off with the loot, but stationary bandits conquer the farming community and set themselves up as rulers who tax the farmers and build themselves palaces.

One difference between a stationary bandit and a roving bandit is that even though a stationary bandit is happy to rob and exploit, he doesn’t want to actually kill the workforce or wreck the productivity of the farmland. Part of what’s special about N.Y.C. is that its economic engine, Wall Street, involves a relatively small number of people. Across most of America, the broad national rent-seeking anti-Walmart coalition couldn’t successfully block the expansion of cheap groceries because having an efficient and thriving retail economy is too important to local tax bases. But in N.Y.C., it’s very possible to extract tremendous rents from workaday New Yorkers even while maintaining the city as an affluent place that lots of people want to live. Grocery competition was excluded, prices stayed high, ordinary people suffered, but the city itself continued to thrive.

And a lot of the city is like that.

Billionaires don’t care whether or not there’s a Walmart in Rego Park. They don’t even really care if the subway fare is unnecessarily high due to overstaffing or that service can’t be expanded because per-mile construction costs are absurd. The billionaires pay high taxes, but it’s evidently worth it to them for the city’s lifestyle. The question for middle-class New Yorkers is why they aren’t enjoying the best public school system in the country in exchange for all the money the billionaires put into it.

You can’t actually address these problems, Mamdani-style, purely by trying to turn the left flank.

I’ve read a lot of skeptics arguing that the government-run cheap stores are doomed to failure or just dependent on subsidies that could never scale. I think the more interesting question is: What if they’re not a failure?

In an abstract ideological sense, “legalize Walmart” and “open a publicly owned grocery store” are not the same idea. But they endanger the exact same coalition of stationary bandits for the exact same reason. You could maybe buy off the union local by letting them unionize the store, but rival retailers still don’t want to be undercut on price (especially from a subsidized project). Even if the public stores are unionized, that’s cold comfort to the existing unionized grocery workforce. Mamdani’s plan is attracting ineffectual conservative snark rather than the kind of project-killing large-scale opposition that crushed Walmart precisely because the people invested in the outcome don’t think it will work. But if it were to work as a pilot, then momentum would build to expand it, and you’d have to take on the exact same bandits.

And if you do take on the bandits, the government-run stores are probably unnecessary. I focused on the specifics of the Walmart saga because the parallels are so striking, but Stephen Smith had a great Vital City piece running the whole gamut of dumb impediments to creating a highly competitive, highly efficient grocery sector in the city.

There is plenty of work to do. And it’s hard work. But if you can do the work, you’ll set the stage for success. Anything else will inevitably just be tinkering at the edges.
