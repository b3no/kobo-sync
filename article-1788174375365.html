When "economic liberty" means central planning

Mark Carney is not an autocrat.

Matthew Yglesias
Aug 31
∙
Paid

Canadian Prime Minister Mark Carney is not just the head of his country’s Liberal Party, but something of an icon of liberalism globally. At a bleak moment for center-left parties worldwide, he won his election, and has remained popular as he feuds with an increasingly indefensible American president.

So it’s remarkable to hear a prominent voice on the American left describe the Canadian leader as a “dangerous autocrat” who is not just worse but “far worse than Trump.”

This was not a one-off outburst. Matt Stoller, research director at the American Economic Liberties Project, has previously called Carney a “dangerous authoritarian.”

Why? Well, this latest outburst was because Carney is “a former Brookfield and Goldman guy” who apparently favors airport privatization.

To take Stoller’s argument at face value: No, Carney is not far worse than Trump; he’s far better. Trump’s view is that Canada is weaker and smaller than the United States, and that while a rupture in the commercial relationship is bad for people on both sides of the border it will be worse for Canada so it should act like a supplicant and kowtow to his demands. This is not the most salient aspect of Trump’s malgovernment, but it is probably the clearest expression of his authoritarian heart — he’s a man who’s fundamentally more comfortable treating North Korea’s dictator as a peer than the elected leader of a fellow democracy.

Carney, on the other hand, has stood up for the rights of smaller nations and for economic sanity. Americans outside the MAGA camp ought to be signaling loud and clear that we will end this trade war after we break Trump’s monopoly on political power.

The good news is that very few people of any ideological persuasion agree with Stoller about Carney. The bad news is that Stoller is not just an influential figure on the populist wing of Democratic Party economic policy, he’s in many ways just a guy who’s imprudent enough to follow the post-liberal left’s logic to its natural conclusion.

The tweet matters less as an assessment of Carney than as a glimpse of a larger ideological inversion on the populist and post-liberal left. A movement that speaks in the language of “open markets” and “economic liberty” has come to treat every deviation from absolutely perfect competition as a warrant for central planning. As a result, its adherents regard traditional market liberals as enemies more profound than a corrupt authoritarian who is currently wielding state power. Many on the left claim to view politics as a remorseless twilight struggle against an ill-defined force known as “corporate power,” and Stoller has the courage of these convictions: Carney is a more alarming authoritarian than Trump precisely because he respects liberal limits on the state, and Trump does not.

The post-liberal left’s position on aviation, a sector in which competition is always imperfect and government is always involved, makes this ideological inversion unusually easy to see.

Aviation as a microcosm

The notion that airport privatization is a more egregious threat to democracy than January 6 is a reminder that the larger post-liberal economic camp to which Stoller belongs is full of strange ideas about air travel.

For four decades, the federal government’s Civil Aeronautics Board (CAB) regulated U.S. airfares and directly controlled which routes airlines could fly. Because the CAB wanted to subsidize certain markets with weak demand, this meant raising prices for long-haul flights to compensate. “Concerned that such practices caused inefficiencies and inhibited the growth of air transportation, the Congress deregulated the industry,” explains a 1996 GAO report. Airlines were allowed to set their own prices and pick what routes they wanted to travel. This resulted in cheaper airfare but fewer nonstop destinations and smaller planes in places with weak demand.

The idea that disbanding the CAB turned U.S. air travel into a miserable hellscape is an article of faith on the post-liberal left. Ganesh Sitaraman, director of the Vanderbilt Policy Accelerator for Political Economy and Regulation, published a 2023 book titled Why Flying is Miserable (the book is long, but the answer is airline deregulation) and followed up in 2026 by proclaiming that Spirit Airlines’ bankruptcy proved that competition in aviation has failed and we need to return to comprehensive regulation of the industry.

This whole narrative makes absolutely no sense.

American air travel volumes keep setting new records, and the share of American adults who have flown in the past year or over the course of their lives has risen throughout the deregulation era. This is because airfares were growing slower than the overall rate of price inflation until Trump started a war with Iran (another example of him being worse than Carney) that spiked jet fuel prices. Americans fly more than ever for the simple reason that flying is much more affordable than it used to be.

If you are not happy with the quality of the service provided by today’s cheaper-than-they-used-to-be fares, the industry invites you to spring for an economy-plus seat or a lounge pass or any of a million other add-ons that can buy you a better experience. If, alternatively, you think flying is basically fine despite the various annoyances involved, you can enjoy the fact that it is dramatically cheaper today than it was before Jimmy Carter deregulated the industry.

It’s also worth being clear on what “deregulation” means in this context. There are, obviously, a lot of regulations still governing air travel. What was unique about the old system was not the regulation of pilot training and aircraft safety, but that of entry into and exit from different markets. Today, if one airline is making a killing flying from Tucson to Nashville, a rival carrier is allowed to enter the market and undercut it on price. The essence of the old system was to prohibit this. If you wanted to enter a market and offer lower fares, you’d have to go to the CAB and beg for permission.

Way back in 2012, Phil Longman, now the policy director at the Open Markets Institute, and Lina Khan, who would later become an AELP-backed antitrust policy celebrity, castigated airline deregulation for the Washington Monthly. The essence of the Longman/Khan argument was that airline deregulation, by allowing airlines to exit unprofitable markets, precipitated heartland decline by depriving cities such as Cincinnati and St. Louis of the volume of airline service they once enjoyed. Left totally unacknowledged is the converse phenomenon whereby cities that have gained rather than lost population — including Austin, Phoenix, Charlotte, and Nashville — now enjoy much more service than they used to have.

All this squabbling about the aviation industry can seem incredibly picayune, but what’s telling is the populist position’s complete dismissal of the liberal values of competition, freedom, choice, and flexibility.

Airport privatization is fine

Stoller’s broadsides against Carney are centered on the Canadian leader’s openness to airport privatization.

Canada’s major airports are already neither conventionally public nor conventionally private. Ottawa owns the land, but since the 1990s it has leased most large airports to self-financing nonprofit authorities that operate and develop them while paying rent to the feds. Carney’s government is now considering longer leases, more private investment, and “alternative models of ownership” in order to lower costs and attract private capital for expansion.

It is this fairly obscure policy debate that has Stoller dubbing Carney a “dangerous autocrat” who is “far worse than Trump.” Even in Stoller’s worst nightmare, the risk is that a private buyer of an airport would jack up prices for consumers. Since he considers monopolies “mini-dictatorship[s]” that’s presumably how he lands on the conclusion Mark Carney is an autocrat for considering leveraging private investment to expand airports.

Of course, for sane individuals with a more literal understanding of the word “autocrat” — defined as “a ruler with unlimited power, or someone who demands that people completely obey them” — even if Carney granted a private investor control of an airport, it would be absurd to analogize it to despotism.

But Stoller is also wrong on the merits. Airport privatization isn’t some moral or practical red line for liberals.

It’s not a huge surprise that there is a tradition of airports being state-owned and state-managed facilities: A large share of airports originated as military bases, and aviation historically was closely tied to public-sector functions such as mail delivery. Aviation is also (still) a heavily regulated industry for safety purposes, and airports are major infrastructure projects that are hard to build without state involvement of some kind.

That said, the primary purpose of airports is to serve as operational bases for for-profit airlines that serve paying customers. These are not “public goods” in the technical sense — airport space is both rivalrous and excludable — and government-owned airports are not operated as free services for the public.

Under the circumstances, it’s natural that some countries have experimented either with private ownership or with contracting airport management out to private firms. This practice is fairly widespread in Europe. A company called Mundys, for example, operates both of Rome’s airports. Skavsta Airport, a secondary airport for Stockholm serving low-cost airlines, is private. So is London’s Gatwick Airport.

There are potential downsides to this arrangement, but to see the upside of privatization, look no further than Trump’s own airport schemes.

The president, like many of us, finds traveling out of Dulles International Airport in the Washington, D.C., suburbs to be a subpar experience. and has backed a $19.9 billion renovation plan that United Airlines, the main user of the airport by far, is enthusiastic about. In theory, these investments will make the airport more attractive to fliers as well as create more commercial opportunities for restaurant and shop owners. The increased revenue deriving from those streams will pay the interest on the bonds that will be issued to finance the renovation, and everyone ends up better off for it.

But what if the extra travelers don’t materialize? Then the airport authority will have to raise per-enplanement fees to pay the bonds, which will make the airport a less attractive destination for service, leading to fewer flights and even higher fees on the remaining flights. Alternatively, though in theory the airport authority’s bonds are backed exclusively by airport revenue with no taxpayer guarantee, it is plausible that the state of Virginia and/or the federal government will step in with a partial bailout to prevent the airport from entering a death spiral.

As a United frequent flier who lives in the District of Columbia, I personally welcome the opportunity for investment that improves my flying experience with the Virginia taxpayer left holding the downside risk. But this is an objectively dubious proposition.

It’s also fundamentally weird to leave the scope of a project like this up to the whims of the president.

In hypercapitalist Europe, the way they would handle this is by seeing if any private airport companies want to invest in Dulles. If nobody wants to put private capital at risk in the airport, that’s probably a good sign that there is little reason to think it would generate real economic value. If somebody does want to invest in renovating the airport in exchange for control over future fees, then that investor would be left holding the downside if the anticipated extra passengers don’t materialize.

By transferring the risk to the private sector, you also get properly aligned incentives to evaluate the renovation plan. It seems likely that some amount of sprucing up really would increase traffic to Dulles. But just because the first $5 or $10 billion is a worthwhile investment doesn’t mean the last $5 or $10 billion is.

What’s the right figure? I have no idea. And neither do you, and neither does Donald Trump. That is the beauty of outsourcing this kind of decision to a company like Brookfield or Goldman Sachs, which employs people at high salaries to do the technical work of analyzing investment opportunities and trying to understand what makes sense. They might make a mistake — companies often do — but taxpayers don’t need to be the ones holding the bag.

The statist approach used in the United States is not a huge practical problem, but the president’s interest in playing amateur airport architect is emblematic of the flaws of the system — and of the very real extent to which he is a more dangerous authoritarian than Carney.

Another fox in the henhouse

In December, I wrote about the rise of illiberal left-wing identity politics (what we are now calling “woke 1,” I suppose) under the guise of liberal commitments to cosmopolitanism and equality.

I deemed this the fox in liberalism’s henhouse. But a similar dynamic applies on the economic front. Note that not only do left-wing post-liberals want to reduce our reliance on competition, markets, and economic freedom — they do so while insisting that they want to do the reverse! Their flagship institutions are named Open Markets and Economic Liberties, but their advocacy agenda is for more central planning. And they’ve convinced many elected officials and nonspecialist journalists that theirs is just a movement of people passionate about antitrust enforcement.

Antitrust enforcement is, indeed, an important regulatory function of the government.

But the signature move of the post-liberal left is not an obsessive focus on promoting competition. It’s an obsessive focus on noting that very few real-world markets meet the full textbook definition of perfect competition — and then leaping to the assumption that comprehensive regulation would be better. The truth is that, in most industries that face some deficits of competition, you could take policy action outside the antitrust space to make them more competitive. Certainly that’s the case in aviation, which is burdened by restrictions on foreign investment in airlines and by serious bottlenecks at the airport level — bottlenecks that could be relieved by privatization rather than by leaving this infrastructure in the hands of state monopolists.

Most of the players in this movement are savvy enough to avoid blurting out, Stoller-style, the reality that they see traditional liberals as worse than Trump. But he deserves to be commended for his honesty on this score.

To the fanatical planner, Trump is genuinely bad but also a figure of grudging admiration, since he is showing many potential paths for government meddling in private business affairs. The left populists just want to do it better, on behalf of causes they deem more worthy. Someone like Carney, trying to uphold a more traditional order, earns their true contempt.

But millions of progressive-minded people who are not deeply invested in the minutiae of regulatory policy can look at this standoff and see clearly the void between a deranged bully and a patriot standing up for his country and common sense. With the battle lines drawn this starkly, there is no better time than the present to start pulling away from this dabbling in illiberal populist economics and return to the true path of market competition as the central engine of the economy.
