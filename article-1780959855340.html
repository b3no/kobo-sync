Trump and Schumer test their endorsement power

Competitive Senate and gubernatorial races are taking shape.

Halina Bennet
Jun 8
∙
Paid
 
READ IN APP
 

Last week’s Iowa primary was perhaps the most encouraging for Democrats so far this cycle, with two key races yielding clear takeaways. In the G.O.P. gubernatorial race, Trump-backed Representative Randy Feenstra lost to Zach Lahn, a farmer and businessman who — boldly for the Corn Belt — criticized big agriculture, in what became the week’s most notable rebuke of Trump’s endorsement power.

Feenstra lost despite being widely considered the frontrunner after receiving a last-minute endorsement from Trump.

State Auditor Rob Sand, the only statewide-elected Democratic official, will face Lahn in November after running unopposed. This will be the first open-seat gubernatorial race in Iowa since 2006.

On the Senate side, both parties now have their nominees for the open seat left by retiring Senator Joni Ernst.

Democratic State Representative Josh Turek, a two-time Paralympic basketball gold medalist and the choice of Senate Minority Leader Chuck Schumer (and Matt), defeated State Senator Zach Wahls in the Democratic primary. Wahls, who had been outspoken in his criticism of Schumer, had the backing of Elizabeth Warren. Republican Representative Ashley Hinson, who was endorsed by the president, advanced on the G.O.P. side without much serious opposition.

Democrats are optimistic about flipping the seat after Turek, who represents a district that Trump carried three times, won by 26 points with a message that zeroed in on his record of bipartisanship.

Iowa, previously a longshot for Democrats, is shaping up as a legitimate battleground for both the Senate and governor’s mansion.

California is still counting

California’s gubernatorial primary unfolded on June 2 in a scramble to take one of the top two positions, and one of those placements still isn’t set. Democrat Xavier Becerra and Trump-endorsed Republican commentator Steve Hilton most effectively separated themselves from a large field that included Democrats Tom Steyer, Katie Porter, Antonio Villaraigosa, and Tony Thurmond.

Becerra, who is — as of Monday morning — the only candidate officially advancing to the general, has won about 27 percent of the counted vote so far, with Hilton coming in at roughly 26 percent, NBC News reports. A Becerra vs. Hilton matchup in the general election looks like the most likely scenario, but there are still votes to count and Steyer, who is in third place right now, has not given up hope.

Regardless of whether the general becomes a race between Becerra and Hilton or Becerra and Steyer, it is sure to be expensive. Were he to win the second spot, a Trump-aligned Republican would likely receive millions from the G.O.P. in California and across the country. That would force Democrats to spend heavily in a state they should win comfortably just to ensure their nominee would not be overwhelmed by Republican spending. But if billionaire Tom Steyer were in the race, he would be likely to spend tons of his own money, forcing Becerra to counter. It is hard to know which is preferable, but either way the amount of money spent is sure to be astonishing.

Like the gubernatorial race, the Los Angeles mayoral primary produced a clear frontrunner but a messy fight for the second runoff slot. Incumbent Mayor Karen Bass held a lead on Sunday night with nearly 35 percent of the vote, while Nithya Raman overtook Spencer Pratt for second place as ballots continued to be counted — the two separated by only a few thousand votes with 83 percent of the expected vote already in, NBC Los Angeles reports. Bass has been the target of major criticism for her handling of the 2025 wildfires, with Pratt — a registered Republican whose home burned in the Palisades Fire — challenging from the right, and Raman, a D.S.A.-aligned city councilmember, challenging from the left.

Maine is only getting messier

Is there ever a week when the race for Maine’s U.S. Senate seat isn’t fighting for national attention? If so, that certainly wasn’t the case last week. The state’s Senate primary election is tomorrow, but the two candidates who will duke it out in the general are already decided. Who is leading in that race, though, is less obvious.

A UMass Lowell/YouGov poll conducted in May found Democratic challenger Graham Platner leading incumbent Republican Susan Collins 48 to 43 percent among likely Maine voters, with 6 percent undecided. The poll showed a significant gender gap. Fifty-four percent of women supported Platner, compared to 42 percent of men, while Collins led among men with 51 percent support.

That’s a nice look for Platner, but there is one massive caveat: The poll was conducted before news outlets reported allegations of sexually explicit text messages involving Platner and before the New York Times reported allegations of “unsettling” behavior by his former girlfriends.

But the controversy may have actually energized Platner’s donor base in the short term. His campaign reported that after the N.Y.T. article was published, it raised more than $200,000 in a single day, marking its best fundraising day since former Governor Janet Mills dropped out.

In some 2028 news, several Democrats who are running for governor in Maine’s primary on Tuesday — including Secretary of State Shenna Bellows, Nirav Shah, Hannah Pingree, and Troy Jackson — signaled they would support moving Maine to a winner-take-all Electoral College system for presidential elections if Nebraska Republicans did the same, Axios reports. Nebraska Republicans under Governor Jim Pillen have previously pushed for a winner-take-all system, though efforts fell flat last year and Nebraska Democrats say the votes aren’t there. Every other state and Washington D.C. vote this way — Maine and Nebraska are the only holdouts.

Brown leads in Ohio’s very expensive race

A Fox News poll released June 3 found Democrat Sherrod Brown leading appointed Republican incumbent Jon Husted 53 to 45 percent in the Senate race, which is outside the margin of error. Trump’s net favorability in Ohio had fallen by more than 20 points since the 2024 election — from +6 to -15 — a shift the pollsters suggested was hurting G.O.P. candidates.

In the governor’s race, Democrat Amy Acton narrowly leads Republican Vivek Ramaswamy 50 to 49 percent, essentially a tossup. Ohio is shaping up as one of the most expensive states in the country, with two competitive statewide races and roughly $120 million pledged by outside sources.

Michigan: The only state messier than Maine

I honestly don’t have much to report on Michigan, where it feels like every day the numbers, the fights, and the support shifts in a Senate primary that remains the most contested intra-Democratic Party fight of the cycle. Dr. Abdul El-Sayed, the progressive insurgent in the race, has been consistently drumming up enthusiasm among the left, while U.S. Representative Haley Stevens and State Senator Mallory McMorrow split the more moderate vote.

The bigger news is that the New York Times reports Senate Minority Leader Chuck Schumer has privately been pushing major donors toward Haley Stevens, describing her as the more electable moderate in the general election. Schumer is officially neutral but has been making calls behind the scenes — a pattern that’s become standard practice for party leadership trying to shape primaries without causing further fracturing within the Democratic coalition.

Democrats are down on the House

An Axios analysis published June 7 found that Republican redistricting efforts have shifted the House map enough that Democrats now need to outperform Kamala Harris’s 2024 national margins by 4.9 points to win a majority — up from 3.1 points before the redistricting war sent the math topsy-turvy.

Democrats currently hold an almost six-point advantage on the generic congressional ballot, so Democrats are theoretically positioned to win the majority, according to Axios, but the margin for error is much, much thinner than it would have been before Republicans had their map victories.
