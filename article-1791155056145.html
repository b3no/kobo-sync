The Verdict, by Kobe Yank-Jacobs

According to both President Donald Trump and Vice President JD Vance, what the U.S. military is doing in Iran is not a war. That said, as of Friday, the U.S. is moving an additional 10,000 troops and a third aircraft carrier to the region, raising concerns that this suspiciously warlike “conflict” could escalate.

By all accounts, Americans are sick of the conflict, which was started unilaterally by the president. According to new data, they are also ready for Congress to rein it in.

Sixty-six percent of registered voters in The Argument’s latest poll, fielded nationally between September 16th and 22nd, believe that the president should be required to get Congressional approval before sending armed forces into action — and only 21% oppose. Almost a majority would go even further: 50% of voters believe that Congress should defund a military action when the president moves ahead without Congressional approval. (Only 26% of respondents oppose this.) Historically, the main enabler of unilateral presidential warmaking has been legislators’ skittishness about reigning in conflicts after they’ve started, but our survey suggests that this is a step that voters feel they are entitled to take.

The public wants Congress to approve military action
By margins of 46 and 24 points respectively, voters want Congress to approve military action or rescind funding if presidents act unilaterally.

Now, it would be great if this result was motivated by high-minded respect for the Constitution, which is very clear that Congress is the branch with the power “to declare war,”¹ but a closer look at the results suggests the results are heavily influenced by current events. For one, the current war in Iran — initiated unilaterally by President Trump — is the most unpopular war since Vietnam, when compared to Afghanistan, Iraq, and World War II. More voters think that George W. Bush’s invasion of Iraq was justified than the current conflict.

There is also a strong partisan slant to our poll results: 94% of people who voted for Kamala Harris in 2024 said they think that Congress should approve war, while only 40% of Trump voters said the same. That said, 44% of Trump voters ages 30 to 44 preferred requiring Congressional approval, suggesting younger generations may be closer to consensus on the need for Congressional approval.²

But even if our results reflect anxieties specific to this moment in time, the overwhelming support for Congressional signoff is still a rebuke of what’s become business-as-usual in Washington.

The United States has not actually declared war since World War II. Beginning with the Korean War in 1950, presidents have either ordered troops into combat unilaterally, secured a different form of wartime “authorization” (as George W. Bush did to invade Afghanistan), or plainly abused past authorizations to make war elsewhere. (The Afghanistan authorization was used to justify drone strikes in Yemen and Somalia.) The result is that presidents functionally make war wherever and whenever they want.

In 1991, when asked what he would do if Congress declined to authorize the Gulf War, President George H.W. Bush told a reporter, “I still feel that I have the constitutional authority” — a sentiment later echoed by presidents from Barack Obama to Donald Trump.³

Thankfully, we don’t necessarily need high-minded Constitutionalism to change how things are done. Some of the most substantial reforms to presidential authority have come in response to specific presidents violating a norm or law, including term limits (after FDR), anti-nepotism laws (after JFK), and restrictions on withholding congressionally appropriated funds (after Nixon).

Congressional capitulation

The fact that half of registered voters are open to Congress taking action even after a president moves ahead with a war unilaterally could be the key to reigning in the executive here.

Once presidents take the initiative to launch a combat operation, it’s hard for politicians in Congress to take a vote opposing or reversing it. There is a fundamental asymmetry between a single person with the ability to handle threats quickly and a large deliberative body, lumbering along in its approval, each politician fearful of being seen as unpatriotic. That’s how war powers got out of whack in the first place: The history of U.S. warmaking is the president using dramatic incidents — real or provoked or lied about — to cow Congress into going along.

As early as 1846, James Polk ordered troops into disputed territory between the Nueces River and the Rio Grande to provoke the start of the Mexican-American War. To stir up support for the Spanish-American war in 1898, President William McKinley pointed to the explosion of a ship off the coast of Havana, despite inconclusive evidence about the cause of the explosion. (Later evidence has suggested it may have been an internal issue, not an attack).

In the summer of 1964, the Johnson administration claimed that its ships were innocently patrolling international waters when struck by the North Vietnamese, but they were really 12 miles off the Vietnamese coast, likely trying to draw fire. (The second of the alleged strikes also never happened.)

The modern era has made this dynamic — presidents making war a fait accompli and daring Congress to pull them back — much worse. In 1973, in the waning days of the Vietnam War, Congress tried to restrain presidents by passing the War Powers Resolution. The law generally required presidents to get congressional approval within 60 days or end hostilities, with up to 30 additional days if necessary for a safe withdrawal. But presidents have used that deadline to justify acting unilaterally.

Unfortunately, the law has had the opposite of its intended effect. On paper, it cemented the requirement for Congressional signoff. In practice, it gave presidents the green light for monthslong deployments that sitting members of Congress would end up hesitant to rein in.

The real significance of The Argument’s finding, even if it was heavily motivated by the current war on Iran, may be that the public believes Congress can act forcefully after a president has initiated combat, which is where we most need Congress to be muscular. If the issue is that legislators find it politically untenable to go against a war once it’s started, we now know that, in the abstract at least, the public is on their side.

🌟Abundance Wins of the Week🌟

  • Top Senate Democrats and Republicans released the long-awaited text of a massive reform to the federal permitting regime: the Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act of 2026 (BAJAA). If passed, the package would make it much easier to build energy projects of all types — but likely with particularly pronounced benefits for clean energy — plus the long-distance transmission needed to efficiently move energy across the country.

  • In other good climate news, the federal Nuclear Regulatory Commission (NRC) issued a construction permit for the Tennessee Valley Authority (TVA) to build America’s first commercial small modular nuclear reactor. It’s the NRC’s second approval of construction for a new reactor this year, and both reviews were completed months ahead of schedule. Small modular reactors promise much simpler and faster paths to carbon-free nuclear energy than traditional reactors. (TVA will still need a separate operating license before it can run the reactor.)

  • New York City just unveiled 12 “Fast Track Districts” where it will streamline the review process for qualifying affordable housing projects. The districts — which include Manhattan’s Upper East and Upper West Sides, as well as large swaths of South Brooklyn, Staten Island, and Queens — were selected because they have produced the smallest share of the city’s affordable housing over the past five years: just 1% combined. The fast track process, which was approved by the city’s voters last year, is projected to reduce review times from seven months to three.

The Argument recommends, by Milan Singh

It’s officially October, and I’m ready for it: no more humidity, layering of outfits, assorted pumpkin spice foods and beverages. What can I say? I’m a bit basic.

To celebrate the arrival of autumn, I asked my esteemed colleagues for their fall-themed recommendations.

“I have no justification for how this is fall themed, but a friend found me in a Little Free Library The Floating Admiral, a detective novel collaboratively written by Agatha Christie, GK Chesterton, and twenty of their Golden Age detective novelist friends,” said staff writer Kelsey Piper. “It was great fun, and now I’m suffering immensely from the fact no one else I know has read it so that I can complain about it with them, so if you have read it, hit me up!”

Kelsey also read John LeCarre’s The Constant Gardener, but “while I love John LeCarre for being the most cynical everything-is-bad-because-of-human-nature-and-always-will-be spy novelist of them all, it hits differently when he’s being cynical about post-colonial Kenya instead of declining imperial Britain, so it was mostly just kind of depressing.”

Gender and culture fellow Maibritt Henkel recommended Julia Jacklin’s new album The Gem, which she said was “perfect for October — think Australian Phoebe Bridgers.” Operations manager Angela Tracy overcame some initial difficulty in narrowing the recommendations down (because “fall is the best time of year,” which is clearly true) but eventually settled on an unconventional-yet-simple dessert: a brûléed squash or sweet potato, riffing on the traditional Japanese winter snack.

The rest of the office was feeling a bit laconic, so I provided some additional incentive by telling them that if they didn’t give me a recommendation, I would have AI do it for them. That seemed to do the trick.

“This might make me sound like a narcissist but I randomly came across this book called Jerusalem the Golden by Margaret Drabble and immediately picked it up for obvious reasons,” said editor Jerusalem Demsas. “It’s a coming-of-age novel about a girl trying to break out of a provincial and constrained upbringing — but she finds out that no matter how far you run you can never escape your mother.”

“AI recommended that I recommend The Heart is a Lonely Hunter and I do, in fact, recommend it,” said senior editor John Thomason of Carson McCullers’s 1940 debut novel. “Alongside Ross McElwee’s 1986 film Sherman’s March, it’s probably the best piece of narrative art ever produced at least partially in (and, I would argue, at least partially by) Charlotte, North Carolina, a city with a well-known reputation for philistinism.”⁴

Video producer Justin Zuckerman also agreed with AI’s suggested recommendation: Ken Burns’ Baseball, which he’s about halfway through. “It’s around 18 hours of baseball history and lore, with folksy music and even folksier characters! It’s a great way to get excited about the postseason and also get your baseball fix during the winter months.”

Staff Writer Jeremiah Johnson said that he and his wife enjoyed watching “spoopy” — as in, “a mix of spooky and cute,” like The Nightmare Before Christmas — in the lead-up to Halloween.

“All horror media is implicitly fall-themed,” copy editor and fact checker Eli Richman noted, “and since he was robbed in The New York Times' top TV show list, I'll go ahead and recommend the works of Mike Flanagan. All of them…. The Haunting of Hill House gets all the love, and it's hard to argue it isn't deserved. But I'd reserve my biggest praise for Midnight Mass and Before I Wake.”
