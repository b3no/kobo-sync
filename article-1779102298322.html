Trump’s China policy is a disaster

Surrendering American advantages in an effort to score a big soybean sale

Matthew Yglesias
May 18
∙
Paid
 
READ IN APP
 

One way that smart people sometimes like to demonstrate their broad-mindedness is to give Donald Trump credit for something that he supposedly got right. And a common talking point among this crowd (here’s Jamie Dimon) is that Trump was “right about China.”

But as his trip to Beijing unfolded last week, I was struck once again by how deeply untrue that is.

It is clearly true that the circa-1999 optimism that trade with China would lead to domestic political reform in the People’s Republic has proven false. Some people, of whom Nancy Pelosi strikes me as the most politically prominent, were genuinely right about this all along.

The views of others evolved over time in response to events: The Great Firewall was erected in 2006, power gradually shifted from Hu Jintao to Xi Jinping between 2008 and 2012, and the Umbrella Revolution in Hong Kong was crushed in 2014, which was also when the government stepped up repression of the Uyghurs. Xi had entrenched himself as a personalistic dictator by 2018, and the last whiff of freedom was wiped out in Hong Kong in 2019-2020.

By Barack Obama’s second term, the old consensus was dead and discredited, and the White House was pitching a vision that combined sound understanding of the economics of trade with a new skepticism of the geopolitical aspects of intensive economic integration with China.

That was the Trans-Pacific Partnership.

Trump, because he has insane ideas about the economics of trade, was always skeptical of trade with China.

But he’s also skeptical of trade with Canada, and he was definitely skeptical of forming a large integrated trade zone featuring Japan and Vietnam and Australia and other Pacific Rim countries. He’s incapable of approaching the economic relationship with China in a way that makes sense because he doesn’t understand any of the relevant issues. He’s incapable of positioning the United States as the leader of a coalition of free nations to defend the value of liberal democracy because he does not believe in the value of liberal democracy.

There have been plenty of efforts to backfill his dumb ideas with better ones. If the president is inexplicably fond of Vladimir Putin, his defenders might say, “Maybe he’s hesitant to make generous financial contributions to Ukraine’s defense because he wants to conserve resources for the Pacific.”

Maybe!

But in practice he promotes the domestic political fortunes of pro-Russian political parties in NATO member states and runs down our munitions stockpiles in Iran.

Obama’s grand strategy of pivoting to Asia and confronting China was very much open to criticism. The Biden administration had a somewhat different view of these issues, and I think there are reasonable alternatives to both the Obama and Biden views.

But Trump is not doing either of those things.

In his first term, Trump spent the critical early months of the Covid pandemic lavishing praise on China’s handling of the situation because he was hoping to score a major soybean sale.

That was dumb. But this time around, he is once again touting agreements to export soybeans and energy commodities to China. He also brought Jensen Huang with him on the trip and keeps handing favorable decisions to Nvidia on export controls.

These are the actions of a man in the grips of a sick obsession with bilateral trade balances, not someone serious about geopolitical competition.

If not free trade, then what?

As a basic conceptual point, I think it’s good to start with the fact that “free trade” is basically one idea, while “not free trade” is a bunch of different ideas.

One major locus of resistance to free trade is labor unions that represent American manufacturing workers. What this locus wants is tariff protection for the specific industries that they have organized.

On this view, importing Chinese-made iPhones is basically fine (there is no American iPhone factory and if there were, it would be built in a right-to-work state), but importing Chinese steel is very bad. But also on this view the problem with the steel is not that it’s Chinese — it’s that it’s not made by U.S.W. members. Importing Japanese steel is no better.

From a strategic competition standpoint, steel probably matters more than iPhones since you need primary steel to build tanks. But also, the Chinese part of the Chinese steel is really important. The United States is, despite my best efforts, a smaller country than China, so we’re not going to be able to make more stuff than China. What we have is a much larger and richer set of allies, so depending on allies for critical goods is fine. It’s good, in fact.

A third view — Trump’s view — is to take the naive mercantilist approach and decide that a trade deficit is per se bad.

On this view, it doesn’t really matter whether you’re importing smartphones or steel, or whether the import comes from China or Japan. The point is that imports are bad. Now, if you block all imports from Japan, Japan might block all imports of American goods, which would also be bad. So to the mercantilist, everything in trade policy is a series of confrontations and strategic bargains ultimately resolved via “deals.”

If you’re Trump, that can sometimes mean policy concessions outside the trade domain, such as threatening tariffs on Mexico to try to change its approach to drug trafficking. But it also can mean trade-specific concessions, bullying other countries into buying more American stuff.

Of these three views, the labor protection one is a fact-based political concern that needs to be navigated in a pragmatic way. The geopolitical competition issue is a genuinely substantive concern.

And the mercantilist hangup about bilateral deficits is total nonsense.

Navigating between one and two is hard enough to do in a reasonable way, but Trump makes it completely impossible thanks to three.

Critically, he doesn’t understand that China gives up nothing to us when it agrees to buy commodities like soybeans and oil from the United States. Xi is aware that the United States has more natural resources per capita than China and thus is well-positioned to be a supplier of said resources to the world’s leading industrial power. That’s his vision of how the relationship should work!

Competition is about retaining and improving America’s ability to make complicated goods and stay at the cutting edge of world technology. That’s why, again, even though letting Nvidia export more chips is good for a short-term boost to the S&P 500, it’s terrible strategic competition. The goal shouldn’t be just to export stuff, but to maintain an edge in artificial intelligence.

Trump’s mercantilist nonsense isn’t limited to countries with whom the United States has strained relationships, though. He’s also constantly raising trade barriers with friendly countries in ways that make it harder to compete.

Trump is a diplomatic disaster

The whole idea of provoking major diplomatic crises with Canada and Denmark is so stupid that it’s hard to believe it really happened, but Trump really did — just for laughs, I guess — threaten to attack two perfectly friendly countries for no good reason.

I guess this enhanced his credibility as a bully when he decided to actually deploy military force to decapitate the regime in Venezuela. But ultimately, all that generated was hubris and a quagmire in Iran.

Speaking of Iran, one accurate point that Trump makes about this war is that as furious as American voters are about the negative impact on their living standards, the consequences are much worse for people living in Europe or Japan or South Korea.

His view is that this means these friendly countries ought to somehow bail him out of this jam. But for extremely understandable reasons, they are not inclined to do that — and it’s not even clear that they could if they were.

What Trump never seems to consider is that making allies resent the United States weakens his hand rather than strengthening it.

Americans are mad enough about the consequences of this course of action, but he’s also gone and made a whole bunch of other people around the world even madder. And while partisanship puts a floor under the number of Americans who’ll become enraged at Trump for hurting them economically, there is no such floor in other democracies. He’s gone and made America a villain in the eyes of millions of people around the world.

One of Trump’s diplomatic “wins” from the summit was getting China to agree that the Strait of Hormuz should be demilitarized and un-tolled long-term. That’s something no prior president would even have considered asking for in a diplomatic agreement because it went without saying.

I think the Obama plan of working with allies to reach a deal with Iran on curtailing their nuclear program in order to pivot American resources out of the Middle East was the right way to handle this issue. But the hawk counterpoint to Obama — forceful military intervention to achieve regime change in Iran — at least makes sense.

Trump opted for a dead zone third alternative in which he assumed he could achieve regime change at low cost, and when that proved to be wrong, he went into a state of paralysis that’s harming our economy and our relationship with every country around the world and weakening our hand vis-a-vis China.

Losing the energy race

One reason that preserving America’s edge on access to chips is so important is that on a level chips playing field, the key to A.I. supremacy will be access to energy to power data centers.

Here we retain a lead over China in per capita terms, but China has four times our population and thus more total electricity available.

More to the point, though, the trend line is not favorable to the United States. China is adding hundreds of gigawatts of solar per year. In the United States, it took 15 years for the Vogtle 3 and 4 nuclear reactors to get built, timeline delays so severe that they made the whole thing insanely expensive.

These were the AP1000 reactor design, which is supposed to be the state of the art of the American nuclear industry. Several of those reactors were also built in China, which allowed Chinese companies to develop their own domestic version, the CAP1000, and China is now rolling out new reactors with a 56-month timeline.

Trump’s main idea about how to address this is to talk about coal and let coal plants put more mercury into the water.

Back in 2024, there were bipartisan talks on a permitting reform deal that would facilitate both renewables and natural gas pipelines. Democratic leadership dragged their feet on it for long enough that Republicans eventually decided they could kill the bill and get a better deal after the election. They won the election, which did shift the bargaining framework in their favor, but even though at least some Democrats have been willing to sign on to a more G.O.P.-skewing deal, it’s been impossible to move forward because the administration is using every tool in the toolbox to kill as many wind and solar projects as possible.

Under the circumstances, Democrats are very worried about the credibility of any deal they might reach and nothing is happening.

As is often the case, the Trump administration’s public communication is so unclear and propagandistic that I genuinely don’t understand what their position is. Interior Secretary Doug Burgum, when pressed on this recently, just reiterated that solar panels don’t produce electricity when the sun isn’t shining.

This is a genuine impediment to building a 100-percent-renewable electrical grid. But in the real world, states with large amounts of utility-scale wind and solar just burn gas when they need to and save money by not burning gas when they don’t need to. Batteries are getting cheaper and will further address intermittency. But Burgum knows this perfectly well and frequently celebrated new wind installations when he was governor. Republican-led Texas has built lots of solar power.

This is all just Trump in a nutshell.

Intermittency is a good reason not to ban fossil fuels, but it’s a terrible reason to ban renewables. Strategic competition with China is a valid reason to restrict some imports from China, but it’s a terrible reason to restrict imports from friendly countries. Burden-sharing with Europe is a plausible reason to limit aid to Ukraine, but it’s a terrible reason to be promoting pro-Russian political parties across the continent.

Trump cheerleads for the American A.I. industry, but he keeps surrendering our advantage on chips. And there’s very little debate or scrutiny over any of this because of his vague brand as a guy who is tough and likes making deals.
