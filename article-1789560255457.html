NATO’s James Appathurai on zombie viruses and sabotage

My podcast returns with a Torontonian who lives in Brussels, imagining the very worst that could happen

Paul Wells
Sep 16

We’re going to have a party at the National Arts Centre next Wednesday to celebrate — Yes, celebrate! — the return of Parliament, and to record a live episode of this podcast with The Panel. Details at the bottom of this post.

The podcast is back. I am rusty. I know you’ll bear with me. We begin Season 4 of The Paul Wells Show with an absolute corker of an episode.

This is my favourite kind of PW Show episode, featuring somebody you might not know but will be glad to meet. James Appathurai is Toronto-born, a University of Toronto grad. He’s been at NATO headquarters in Brussels since the late 1990s. He was the Alliance’s chief spokesperson during much of the Afghanistan war in the 00’s. That’s where I met him, during one of my brief stints as a sort of war correspondent. Since then he’s moved to from one key position to another.

These days he is styled the Deputy Assistant Secretary General for Innovation, Hybrid, and Cyber. The remit of the division he leads actually extends even further: his official NATO biography mentions “risks to energy security, the security impacts of climate change… securing critical undersea infrastructure… artificial intelligence, quantum computing, and biotechnology.”

It’s basically a catch-all for all the things a military alliance should worry about besides fighting and shooting. That was already a lot of things when Appathurai received this assignment in 2022. It’s far more now. At one point I was going to draw a distinction between these “new conflicts” like hybrid warfare, info ops and quantum code-breaking on one hand; and “traditional conflicts” like the Russia-Ukraine war on the other. It’s a worse than useless distinction: the Russia-Ukraine war, like all the deadliest wars, has been a monstrous innovation lab with the highest possible stakes. Which is why Canada is now learning about drones from embattled Ukraine.

James is a superb communicator who’s been thinking about this stuff most of his adult life. When I ran into him in July he asked to come on the podcast, which didn’t then yet exist, because he wants Canadians to be up on the latest from an Alliance we helped found. I’m glad we didn’t make him wait.

Highlights:

There’s a gigantic armory in Rzeszów in southeast Poland, where all the weapons from Ukraine’s allies are stockpiled on their way in. “The Russians don’t touch it,” Appathurai said, “because they don’t want a force-on-force fight with NATO. Because they know they’ll lose it. So the proof of NATO deterrence sits right there.”

But after we spoke, the Russians did strike a train station on the Ukrainian side, as Western VIPs were returning from an annual conference in Kyiv. Proof of Appathurai’s point that the Russians don’t attack a NATO member state directly, but also that they’re pushing.

“The Russian risk appetite has grown,” Appathurai says. “Our intelligence chiefs [and] political leaders... are kind of worried that the Russians are now going to try something small with regard to NATO” — some kind of limited, but unmistakable attack.”

A precision strike, from a regime not noted for its precision. “What concerns me is that the Russians miscalculate. They miscalculated on Ukraine. They thought it was over in four days... They didn’t think Sweden and Finland would join NATO... They can push too hard, cross a line they didn’t know was there, and trigger a big reaction.”

Soon he’s rattling off a Pandora’s box of threats. Malware attacks that could “shred our cyber defenses.” Or, if you prefer, “weaponization of artificial intelligence to make new biological or chemical weapons.”

You need “a certain kind of positive spirit and robust constitution to work for a long time in this place,” Appathurai admits. At dinner the other night, somebody started using the word “obliteration” before the salad course. Someone else said: “The permafrost is melting in Russia and cities are starting to sink. It’s releasing anthrax,” she said. I knew that, Appathurai said. “It’s also releasing zombie viruses.” So after this episode, you’ll know what those are. You’re welcome.
