The dream of Blue Texas

Plus my “Moby Dick” limited series and the problem with primaries

Before I get to this week’s Mailbag, I’ve gotten some questions about whether or not we have an updated list of donation recommendations since the slate we published back in July. And the answer is — no, I think those recommendations hold up.

To be clear, though, the presumption of the recommendation is that you are a normal person interested in making small- to medium-sized hard-money contributions to candidates. We identified a list of people based on a balance of how pivotal/strategic their race is, how much extra money is actually needed, and how aligned the candidate is with Slow Boring’s priorities and worldview. To make a list like this useful, I think it’s important to keep the list short, and that means that plenty of perfectly good candidates running in important races end up off the list.

If you’re a billionaire who can easily afford to give to more candidates, you should by all means do that. I feel bad to not be recommending Scott Wiener (who’s been on our list in the past) in his runoff against Connie Chan, for example. Jonathan Kreiss-Tomkins, who’s running for governor of Alaska, is amazing. I’m very intrigued by what Adam Hamilton is cooking up in Kansas. There’s a wide range of worthy House challengers associated with the Blue Dogs and Welcome PAC who I like a lot. I wouldn’t want anyone to assume that the failure to include someone who seems broadly Slow Boring-aligned reflects a negative judgment on them as candidates. It’s just that I think the list is most effective when it’s really selective and reflects judgment not just about the candidates but about where your hard money is most likely to make a difference.

Speaking of which, we’ve raised nearly $1 million for the slate so far. That’s a ton in a cycle when fundraising has generally been a problem for Dems. Thanks for everything you do!

Greg S: There are many ways to gauge influence but one is access to powerful people. How many US Senators would take your phone call directly?

I have the phone numbers of exactly three senators. I don’t know that they’d pick up if I called out of the blue, but I try to text judiciously.

Neil Mehrotra: Can Gina Hinojosa really win Texas governor’s race? Her profile as a state legislator would not have seemed to position her to win statewide, but she seems to have successfully positioned herself as a moderate. Is this just an example of the Abbott campaign being slow to respond?

The polls show her within striking distance but definitely behind. For her to win you’d need there to be a meaningful polling error in Democrats’ favor (which is conceptually possible but I wouldn’t bank on it) or for something new to happen in the race that deals a further blow to Abbott. The betting odds heavily favor Greg Abbott, and I don’t see any reason to doubt that.

If the final result ends up being like the polls — which is to say close-ish but not exactly a razor-thin margin — I think people will think of it as a missed opportunity on two levels.

One is that while Hinojosa has run an extremely smart campaign with some very based campaign ads touting her law enforcement endorsements, featuring a January 6 participant who’s supporting her, and portraying herself as an advocate of low taxes, she’s not really someone who has a pre-campaign reputation as a moderate. The ideal profile for a statewide Democratic candidate in Texas would be a Hispanic moderate from South Texas. Vicente Gonzalez or some equivalent type of guy in the state legislature or former Representative Filemon Vela would be my top choices.

The other is just that while Democrats had a big financial edge in the early Trump election cycles, the money now favors Republicans. If this were the 2018 or 2020 fundraising environment, I think there’d be a strong case for investing a lot of money in Hinojosa’s gubernatorial run. She could conceivably win, and a win there would be very high value. Spending on her campaign would be complementary to spending on a key Senate race and a couple of key House races. It’s not a crazy idea. It’s just hard to make the case for it in a world of financial scarcity where Democrats are at a financial disadvantage all across the board.

Why is it competitive at all despite a non-ideal nominee and scarce money? Well, for one, again, Hinojosa doesn’t have a perfect profile but she’s running a smart campaign. Another factor that she (like Rob Sand in Iowa and Amy Acton in Ohio) has going for her is that when it comes to wrong-party gubernatorial candidates, you have a built-in margin of comfort. That is, if you’re sick of Abbott and you’d like to see Texas public policy get a little bit less right-wing but also don’t trust progressives, you don’t need to believe that Hinojosa is a moderate deep in her heart to vote for her. You know that it’s just the case that the policy status quo in Texas is very right-wing, that the legislature is very right-wing, that other statewide elected officials will be right-wing, that all kinds of commissions will be full of legacy Abbott appointees, etc. There is zero chance of Texas state government becoming an off-the-rails progressive policy zoo. And her actual agenda is fairly limited. Hinojosa is promising to roll back the voucherization of the high-performing public-school system that George W. Bush and Rick Perry built. She’s going to be more skeptical of data centers. She’ll try to expand Medicaid. It will be an uphill struggle for her to achieve even these goals, so if you like that agenda, you can comfortably vote for her and not worry too much about the risk that she may secretly harbor further-left views.

Beyond that, Abbott’s problem is that he’s been in office a long time and he’s the third Republican governor in a row and there’s been a G.O.P. trifecta for a long time and it’s just hard to govern for a really long time without eventually overreaching and pissing people off. The longer any party coalition governs, the more scandals accumulate and the more undisciplined its leaders become. It gets easier for voters to take for granted whatever they like about them and harder for aligned interest groups to take “no” for an answer about their most unsound ideas. You see a version of this in the relatively tight polling for the Oregon governor’s race. I think Tina Kotek will probably pull it off, but if there were a Democrat in the White House, she’d be in real trouble.

Paul: Is a jury of your peers really the best way to achieve just outcomes?

Probably not. But it does seem good at achieving what I think it is meant to achieve, namely making it difficult to abuse criminal prosecutions for political purposes.

Kirby: What do you think of sortition systems? It seems like the average voter is more civically minded than the median voter, if that makes sense.

I am very interested in sortition, which is basically jury duty for legislative functions.

A common American form of municipal government, for example, is the council-manager system. I wonder if it wouldn’t make more sense in most of those cases to have a council selected by sortition. The actual governing of the city would be done by a professional city manager, but the manager would need to present an annual budget that he talks through with a randomly selected council of residents and those residents would also have the power to fire the city manager and hire a new one.

Mechanical Buttons: If you were extremely wealthy and had decision- making power at one of the streaming services, what show would you have made? Assume you don’t need to recoup the costs.

The answer is that I would like to see someone make a “Moby Dick” limited series.

For starters, C.G.I. has progressed to the point where it’s probably cost-effective to do the whale-hunting action stuff in a way that looks decent in a television context. Beyond that, though, this classic American novel is really something that doesn’t work as a movie. “Moby Dick” is long, it’s digressive, and more to the point the digressions are integral to what the book is. Trying to chop it down into a streamlined adventure story doesn’t make sense.

The way to do it is to take advantage of the fact that television as a medium allows you to have episode-to-episode inconsistency in the style and tone of the program. It’s common for modern shows to be shot like a very long movie with a lot of stylistic continuity. But think about how “WandaVision” would deliberately play with the format. Or how “Buffy” had a musical episode. Or how “Star Trek: The Next Generation” would sometimes be a costume drama inside the holodeck. “Moby Dick,” in particular, should just have certain segments that are mockumentaries about whales or the whaling industry.

Some other thoughts:

  • The documentary segments should be narrated by John Hodgman and done in the tone of “The Areas of My Expertise.”

  • The iconic line “Call me Ishmael” should be spoken dialogue, not voiceover. It should be the narrator trying to make conversation with an indifferent fellow traveler on a boat to New Bedford.

  • The theme music should be “Gigantic” by the Pixies.

Last but not least, they should try to cast Americans in the American roles rather than the HBO thing of constantly casting British people (I love Kelly MacDonald, but that was an insane accent in “Lanterns” for a Midwestern sheriff from Gotham City).

But just in case any executives are out there reading, I don’t think this is some kind of crazy “if I had a billion dollars and money was no object” scheme. I think this is actually a good idea and it would get a lot of attention and people would watch. There is just honestly not that much equivalently iconic intellectual property out there waiting for someone to grab. Obviously there is a history of people attempting to do adaptations of “Moby Dick,” but none of them are well-regarded, and I think the fundamentals of “really long story that involves whale hunts” have just basically not been realizable until the modern era. Under today’s conditions, though, I don’t see why you couldn’t do it.

Tom Scheinfeldt: Swim dad here. Age group swimming can be pretty intense, swim parents especially. How’s everyone in the Yglesias household holding up?

Our kid is on what counts as a relatively low-key swim team by D.C. area standards (which still means four practices per week, some of them very early), so I haven’t seen any unhinged parental behavior. It’s a slightly weird sport as a spectator because it’s such a high ratio of sitting around to actually watching your kid compete, but two things I like about it are that it’s extremely healthy with minimal injury risk and that the basically objective nature of swim times means that they can’t lie to you about your kid’s prospects.

Bloodknight: What was the point of the Alliance to Restore the Republic? In both timelines the New Republic fails in roughly thirty years either because of the Star Tek villains that are the Vong or to the clear mishandling of the post-Imperial transition under Mon Mothma. Does the continuation of Coruscant as the seat of government matter? Absent the out of nowhere Vong invasion does Leia outperform the Canon timeline version of Mon Mothma as head of the Republic?

George Lucas originally described the Rebel Alliance as inspired by the Viet Cong (worth keeping in mind for complaints that later iterations of the franchise got too woke), which was a highly disciplined and ideologically unified movement. More recent depictions have shown what was actually a quite fractious set of disparate opposition forces.

In particular, the prequels show a strong axis of conflict around centralization versus decentralization operating within the context of the Old Republic. Palpatine himself originally gains power as an advocate of centralization within the context of republican institutions, only later seizing power by transforming the Republic into a personalistic dictatorship. So you always have a tension inside the rebel movement between the idea of reclaiming power for the Senate rather than an emperor and the idea of decentralizing authority. This is closer in spirit maybe to the situation in Afghanistan, where both the mujahideen resistance to the Soviet Union and the later alliance against the Taliban were mishmashes of groups with competing ideas that were unified against a common enemy but not really pursuing a common goal. That sort of rebellion is prone to collapsing after winning.

cp6: I got into an argument with some relatives recently. I said that privatizing federally owned ranchland would be a good idea, and they claimed that this would lead to all the ranches being sold to developers and converted to suburbia. I tried to tell them that this would only happen to a small number because it wouldn’t make economic sense for most of them, but they simply didn’t believe me and insisted that I didn’t know what I was talking about and all the ranches would definitely get developed.

If they were talking to you, what would you say to them?

The right way to think about privatizing non-park federal land is that it would make other western states look more like Texas, where there is very little federally owned land. It is true that the existence of vast stretches of privately owned ranch land in Texas facilitates suburban sprawl because where housing demand exists, there is a strong tendency to sell the land off to housing developers. This is also, as I said on Tuesday, a major reason there is a lot of renewables development in Texas.

That being said, it’s not like there are no ranches in Texas. Outside of the Northeast Corridor and California, most land in the United States is not particularly valuable, and its highest and best use is in fact as some kind of agriculture. So it’s both true that privatizing more western land would facilitate more suburb-building and also that the land mostly would not be turned into housing subdivisions.

In general, I think people underrate the benefits of land use flexibility. I think you should be allowed to construct gigantic Billionaires’ Row-style supertall apartments on all the residential land in the United States. Obviously, though, there is almost nowhere that someone would actually want to do that. Most people do not want to live in supertall buildings, the construction cost per square foot of such buildings is high, and most land is not that expensive. It’s just that the deadweight loss of quantity restrictions on housing is very large, the question of where demand exists is not entirely predictable in advance, it’s not the worst thing in the world for the occasional investor to go bust via overbuilding, and patterns of demand change and we all ought to be flexible. But when you say “unlimited density on all residential land,” there’s a tendency to imagine every parcel being suddenly converted into Hong Kong or something. Even in literal New York City this mostly wouldn’t happen, just as Texas mostly isn’t suburbs of Dallas. You just want maximum flexibility to build the housing that there is demand for, rather than the current dynamic of constraints on all sides.

Eliot: In your interview with Jon Favreau, you argued that moderates need to be “looser” to attract attention in a Dem presidential primary, citing the appeal of Platner’s unpredictability and Gallego as a mod who might pull it off. But don’t Platner’s unraveling and the emerging view that there’s something “shady” about Gallego illustrate the risks of that approach?

To be clear, yes. Primary elections are bad. The nature of the primary is to hand inordinate power to advocacy groups and media figures who lack any kind of accountability and responsibility, and everything that is helpful to winning a primary is anti-correlated with attributes that are useful for winning general elections and governing effectively. That’s my whole point. General election voters love candidates who are both boring and moderate.

Primary electorates are skeptical of moderates, but beyond that, the only way to win a primary is to get a lot of attention. I was arguing that a good way to get attention and be “exciting” without adopting a ton of dumb issue positions is to be looser and less scripted as a public figure (see Donald Trump). Literally none of this is a good idea, though. The best way to pick a 2028 nominee would be to run a boring-but-popular red- or purple-state governor. The issue is that we don’t pick nominees that way. Given the system that we have, you have to try to come up with the least-damaging approach. What Steve Bullock and Amy Klobuchar tried in 2020 — just be an obviously strong electability choice and hope that electability-minded voters will vote for you — doesn’t work. You need to come up with some kind of razzle-dazzle.

Now I do think it’s true that in a world where politicians are advised to be very cautious, there is going to be a certain correlation between a loose media style and an overall lack of discipline. But that’s a contingent relationship. If the conventional wisdom about ideal media strategies shifts, then people who are buttoned-up in other aspects of their life can be more interesting and get more attention.
