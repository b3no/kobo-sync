In 2005, when I was sixteen, I noticed that sexual mores appeared to be getting looser and looser, with seemingly no end in sight. This had at least something to do with the fact that I attended a school which, after describing it to my subscribers, was dubbed a “a hornier Gossip Girl.”

My feminist baby boomer mom and I had a very open dialogue about sex, and I couldn’t help but notice that stories of her teen dating life seemed much more restrained, even in the free love era of the 1970s. She told me that when she was in high school, boys and girls typically dated for a while before having sex, even if they weren’t saving sex for marriage. At my high school, however, the standard was that girls started relationships by being “friends with benefits,” and to expect anything more was the behavior of a “stage-five clinger.” Strangely, this expectation did not seem unique to cool or popular boys who had options to burn. I almost dated—but ultimately broke ties with—a geeky, ninety-pound jazz band member, who wore a retainer 24/7, because he was hellbent on “FWB only” and didn’t want to settle down. In his case, the alternative to casual hookups was just not dating anyone, because there weren’t many takers. Still, casual sex or bust. In his case, bust, presumably into a sock.

In a moment that now feels a bit prophetic, I said to my mom, “One day, everyone is going to walk around town with a diamond over their head like in Sims and it’ll turn green when they’re attracted to you and if you both have green diamonds, then you’ll find some place to have sex.” (Of course, the green diamond thing has yet to happen, but dating apps basically work this way.) My mom, newly single and confused about which men would be into her, laughed and said, “Well, at least it would eliminate a lot of guesswork.” Then, I added, “But what comes after that? Would people just walk around naked all the time, having sex in public? I don’t see how things can go any further than where they are today.”

Then, it was my mom’s turn to play the soothsayer: “You’d be surprised. At some point the pendulum will swing and all this casual sex is actually going to be really, really uncool. Future generations will look at what your friends are doing and think they’re the lamest people ever.”

And—what do you know—as “millennial” has become a code word for something that has reached the peak of dated, dorky, cringe, whatever…millennials, in our supreme cringeyness, have also claimed sex positivity, casual sex, and perhaps, all heterosexual sex, in the generational divorce, as Gen Z radical feminists deride sex positive millennial feminists as “male-centered cumrags” across social media, instead touting sex strikes and swearing off men entirely. Having too much sex, especially with too many people, is cringe now. Millennials really can kill anything.

I remember the first time I saw someone identify as “sex negative.” It was at some point in the early 2010s, on some blogging forum. I remember chuckling and saying to myself, “Sex…negative? Like…anti-sex?” The concept seemed absurd to me. It’s one thing to be against coercive sex or sexual assault but against the concept of sex? I knew asexuals existed, but I figured they just weren’t interested in sex, not against it in an ideological way. Who were these strange creatures?

Of course, I went down a rabbit hole and discovered nuanced critical opinions on sex work and porn, along with galaxy brain takes such as “all heterosexual sex is rape,” or “straight women could simply choose to be gay and end all their suffering.” And today, I see more of that than ever, especially among Gen Z, as The New York Times informs us they are “ditching sex.” But as far as I can tell, the difference between the sex negativity I saw in the 2010s and the sex negativity among Gen Z women today is that the Gen Z sex-negativity (or at least the terminally online version of it) seems less directed at men and the patriarchy, and more directed at the women who seem too easygoing about sex—millennial women.

(Before you tell me that “nobody is saying that,” just trust me that people are saying that, and I’d rather not creak open the rusty doors of the asylum and copy and paste every tweet I’ve seen on the topic—I’ll save that for Many Such Takes. But let’s just say that over the past week, millennial sex-positive feminists were rebranded as “Ghislennials” for saying it was unlikely that straight women would be happy to abstain from sex forever. Make of that what you will.)

Allow me to be pedantic for a moment: arguably the peak of hookup culture (in the pockets where it was a thing) existed with Gen X even more than with millennials. Just look at the most popular media of the nineties—the characters in Seinfeld are consistently having sex with people they just met (although not well enough to rival the pleasure of the risotto), all the women on Sex in the City belonged to Gen X, and even sappy classic romcoms like When Harry Met Sally exist against a backdrop that casual sex was an expectation among college-educated city dwellers (and they called it “making it,” which I think should make a comeback.) But “millennial feminism,” where “millennial” is basically code for “too dated to be cool and not old enough to be retro,” just like Aztec-print waterfall sweaters, has become the enemy of Gen Z sex negativity.

And, despite hand-wringing warnings from people—mostly gullible right-wing men— whose only exposure to Gen Z has been TikTok videos made by undercover sex workers, who they we are living in a state of unprecedented debauchery, hookup culture and casual sex are really just not popular with Gen Z. Of course, they coined the term “situationship,” so clearly casual sex is not entirely off the table, but generally, there is a lot more sexlessness among Gen Z than among millennials or Gen X’ers at the same age:

But it’s not just sexlessness—lots of sex things that might have been rebellious or countercultural seem to be old-coded now, the way a Marilyn Manson inspired mall Goth might have a different aura in 1995 versus today. Several years ago, a younger friend of mine—the kind of person who will try everything once just for the story—went to a swingers’ club but left upon discovering that everyone there was old enough to be her parent. A polyamory dating site reports that almost all their members are millennial or Gen X, with zoomers making up less than 6% of their userbase. Surprisingly, Gen Z and millennials are roughly equally as likely to report kinky fantasies, although I’ve anecdotally seen BDSM satirized as a “quirk chungus Reddit” millennial thing to do—and, much like Judaism, one can be kinky in a non-practicing sense.

Perhaps we’ve simply Sexed too hard and too much. We normalized too many things, we made the goofy cartoons about muppets fisting each other that never should have seen the light of day, we gave our food trucks and restaurants saucy pun titles, we had indie sleaze music, we had Tucker Max, we wore too many Wet Seal bodycon dresses with mesh cutouts and our skinny jeans showed too much camel toe and now our successors have entered the Giant Clown Pants Contest. Perhaps anything can become uncool if it’s in-your-face enough for long enough, and maybe it was really bad timing to have the early days of social media ruled by young people who just started having sex and were really excited to tell everyone about it via the results of their OKCupid Slut Tests.

But I want to be careful not to frame the rising tide of sex negativity as nothing more than a swinging pendulum declaring something dorky because it’s done by the Olds. I do think this is a part of it, but I want to examine some other motivations too.

Consider that more than half of teens in 2023 reported seeing porn before the age of thirteen. Meanwhile, millennials (especially the elder millennials who were victim to dial-up Internet) often had to just use their imaginations or jack off to R-rated movies and lingerie catalogs. If you are traumatized by hardcore or violent porn (or by the people who watch it) at a young age, it makes total sense that you might become a bit reactionary about it. And, in fact, both young women and men are more likely to support restrictions on pornography. If young men’s first ideas of sex come from hardcore pornography (especially when they’re way too young to be watching it) their first sexual experiences with women might be particularly awkward and porn-inspired, if not downright unpleasant for the woman. It’s notable, for example, that while Gen Z is having less sex overall, the sex they do have is far more likely to involve choking, which I’ve even seen described as a “standard” part of “vanilla” sex.

There is also the fact that sex-positive millennial feminists may have overplayed their hands when it came to casual sex and the rhetoric around it. The “defund the police” of sex, if you will. Officially, if you ask anyone who once fell into this category, they will say that they never encouraged anyone to have casual sex, let alone pressured them into it. Their only quest, they’ll say, was to fight back against slut-shaming and patriarchal standards of purity. But the reality was that because the normalization of casual sex was pretty darn great for men, dating became very difficult for young women who did not to partake in hookup culture, assuming they were in one of the regions and cultures where it was the norm. Some millennial women had casual sex because they enjoyed it, but many others did it because it was the only way to secure commitment (boys only wanted cool girls, after all) and it often didn’t work anyway. Many others did it because they were drunk, everyone was doing it, and it was expected of them in a way that became coercive. Other women have stories that began in such a setting, and ended with sexual assault because the casual sex era peaked before the affirmative consent era was born. It’s no surprise that #MeToo came right off the heels of this!

When feminists my age defend casual sex, they’re mostly defending the right for consenting adults to do what they want in the bedroom and not be judged. And yeah, fair. But during the sex positivity heyday, you simply could not make the argument that casual sex was only enjoyable to a subset of women, or that most women, on average, had better and more fulfilling sex within monogamy (which is just a fact.) And God forbid examining the fact that men and women might process sex differently and require different levels of emotional intimacy to have a good time, or worse—that many women were engaging in casual sex in the hopes of securing a relationship! You may as well have been interning for Rick Santorum.

I can see why Gen Z women feel alienated from that conversation, especially if they’ve witnessed or experienced casual sex “gone wrong,” even if the “wrong” did not culminate in anything illegal. In many ways, I might agree with them. That said, I still don’t think millennial women are “sex trafficking” them by extolling the virtues of sex positivity on Twitter. You are not being trafficked. You are twenty-six and reading a generalized tweet on a subjetct.

So perhaps unpleasant formative experiences have driven some of these people away from sex and sex positivity and into a form of voluntary celibacy or extreme sex negativity. But the anti-sex-brigade goes beyond the reactionary nineteen-year-old radical feminists of Twitter who will call older women whores for even having sex with their own husbands and who ask, incredulously, what a man can offer that a vibrator can’t. This is happening with young men too—not all, just as not all young women are calling for 4B sex strikes, but enough to make an impact on the culture.

In fact, whenever I think about how sex has been rebranded as uncool, I think about an interview between Piers Morgan and Nick Fuentes. In this interview, Morgan grills Fuentes on the fact that he’s a virgin (supposedly for religious and ideological reasons, although many have, shall we say, doubts) under the impression that informing him, “You’ve never been laid” will be humiliating and tank his cred with his groyper supporters. After all, what could be more embarrassing for a right-wing man than being a virgin? But Morgan did not realize that Fuentes’ supporters are fully aware that he’s a virgin, and they like it—which is why he was unashamed to agree with this statement during the interview.

Part of why Fuentes’ celibacy is considered cool (among his crew, anyway) is that his followers have horseshoed around beyond frat bro misogyny and into being so misogynistic that they dislike women too much to want to have sex with them. (I’m not sure if he’s since backed off this position—not a big Fuentes watcher myself.) And while these men may not have been Fuentes fans, this week’s edition of Many Such Takes covered some young right-wing Catholic men who believed that it was sinful to be attracted to your wife, considered marriage and sex a “necessary evil,” and one guy who said that women were so “irredeemable” that he hoped for a sexless future wherein men could reproduce via IVF and enter sexless marriages with each other. (There’s a zero percent chance that last guy was a Catholic but he somehow slipped into the conversation.)

The motivations for voluntary sexlessness or sex negativity among the radfems and the reactionary tradcaths are clearly different on many levels, and by mentioning some underlying connecting themes I am not implying that they are equally harmful, something of which commenters are always keen to accuse me. But these groups both show notes of highfivesexuality, a term I coined to describe same-sex platonic bonding that occurs by being performatively antagonistic toward the opposite sex. As I mentioned in my article on the topic, highfivesexuality is the default state for many middle school kids, who eventually get over it once they grow up and decide they actually want to have sex, and maybe boys/girls don’t have cooties after all. While I can’t generalize all of Gen Z this way (Plenty are, in fact, dating! Even marrying!) I wouldn’t be surprised if social isolation and excessive screen time has plunged some zoomers into a state of arrested development where highfivesexuality persists long after it’s appropriate.

And perhaps I was wrong when, at sixteen, I figured that the only way people could rebel against their elders was by becoming more sexual. I viewed history through the lens of increasing sexual openness. We had the repressed 1800s, then we had the roaring twenties, then we had the swinging sixties, then we had hookup culture, and next…? But in reality, the pendulum has swung back and forth repeatedly, with several instances in history where the younger generation has rebelled with prudishness, not with libertine sexual mores.

In fact, the Victorian era in England was a backlash to the far more sexually open Regency era, per The Origins of Sex. And before the Victorian era, there was another backlash to the bawdy and lewd culture of Restoration England in the late 1600s—see the poetry of the controversial Earl of Rochester (portrayed by Johnny Depp in The Libertine) including his famous poem, “Signior Dildo.” So while it’s baffling, at least at first, to see sex derided as cringe and embarrassing, the domain of the chopped uncs, there is historical precdent.

Perhaps Gen Z has some valid reasons to rebel against the sex positivity that ruled our youth, while others are just being maladapted weirdos about it. Or maybe we were just far too open about it, left no room for imagination, made too many corny jokes, and now we have quite literally ruined sex for the next generation, entering the same dustbin as diamonds, golf and malls.
