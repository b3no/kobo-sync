We’re all progressives until the next election

The Verdict by Jerusalem Demsas

The Democrats’ overwhelming midterm victory seems to have ended what I like to call the interwoke period.

After two years of corporations, universities, and other cultural institutions racing to distance themselves from DEI, as well as tech CEOs falling all over themselves to glaze Donald Trump — remember the gold plaque?? — the desperate turn many of these same actors are now making to the left is pretty funny.

Palantir CEO Alex Karp went viral this week for calling himself, his company, or his worldview “progressive” eight times in the span of a 22-minute episode of Politico’s The Playbook Podcast. “I know more about being a progressive than almost anybody on the planet,” Karp proclaimed.

Methinks the lady doth protest too much.

Share

While it’s technically true that Karp has been calling himself a progressive since at least 2018, he’s clearly an adherent to the words-don’t-mean-things school of thought. I do not know Karp, nor have I ever spoken to him, so what is in his heart is unknowable to me. But I do not personally find it progressive to build ICE software that helps agents find and arrest immigrants as part of a brutal, unpopular, and un-American deportation policy supported by a negligible number of left-leaning voters.

Plus, Karp’s donation history makes very clear the bet he made on right-wing politics: He donated $360,000 to a Biden-Harris fundraising committee in 2023 before donating $1 million to MAGA Inc. the following year. Now that Democrats are set to control Congress, he’s eager to explain that all of Palantir’s products serve their interests: “I absolutely believe Palantir — all of our products are essentially progressive,” Karp told Playbook.

Karp’s blatant opportunism at least has the virtue of self-awareness. At one point in the interview, when pressed about his giving to MAGA Inc., he exclaimed that he’s not “the village idiot”: “Of course I give to everybody — what, you expect me to be stupid? — like, that’s just how I roll.”

Karp wants you to know that he’s in on the gag. I see that same self-awareness in progressives joking about people putting pronouns back in their bios and email signatures, turning data centers into Criterion closets, and converting the Trump ballroom into a COVID-conscious lesbian bar.

What’s interesting about these jokes is that nobody seems to believe that they’ve actually won the culture wars. The joke is that the people who abandoned progressive cultural signifiers the second they became politically inconvenient will be back performing them as soon as the incentives shift. An election can change how people act — and who they genuflect to — without proving that anyone's mind has actually been changed.

After Trump’s reelection, the commentary treated his win as evidence that the culture itself had made a lasting shift right: toward bro-influencer politics and away from “woke feminization.”

By early 2026, The Washington Post was describing young Trump supporters who saw his victory as a “cultural realignment”; an Axios article about young Trump voters framed their opinions on masculinity and religion as a “sharp cultural turn” that could reshape politics for years.

But, according to early evidence, young men swung back toward Democrats in the 2026 midterms. Yet we have not seen any extended hullabaloo about how young men are obviously all cultural Marxists now. The coverage has been provisional; The Post questions whether men will stick with Democrats and quotes a researcher saying that young men were swing voters.

I don’t think this is a story about media bias in a particular political direction — after all, the 2016 Democratic primary also spawned a thousand takes about the rise of Bernie bros and the dirtbag left. The Guardian asked why there were “suddenly millions of socialists in America” and argued that Sanders hadn’t made young people socialists; he had just revealed them. And The Post reported that Sanders was “profoundly changing” how millennials thought about politics.

But the 2016 result didn’t mean that voters hated women, the 2024 result didn’t mean young men had become conservatives, and the 2026 result doesn’t mean the whole country is now ready to join the Democratic Socialists of America. The temptation to treat each big swing as a revelation of voters’ actual views is divorced from reality. An election might just reveal what voters are angry about, or what coalition they currently see as closer to their views, without proving that the country has permanently changed its mind.

I don’t know why everyone is drawing different lessons from 2026, but I know why I do: Since 2016, I’ve become intimately familiar with the concept of no permanent victories.

Winning an election can make a set of ideas feel dominant or inevitable — the long arc of history is bending my way! — and losing one can make your cause feel dead — we live in hell — but neither feeling holds up in the long sweep of history.

Many progressives lived through Obama’s supposedly durable coalition, Trump’s first victory, the great “racial reckoning” of 2020, Biden’s election, the corporate DEI backlash, the return of a seemingly-more-popular-than-ever Trump — and now, the great repudiation of the Orange Man. Progressives and liberals know better than to take even this, the biggest partisan election victory in at least half a century, as a sign of things to come.

The “no permanent victories” idea was best articulated by T.S. Eliot in a 1927 essay in which he argued, “If we take the widest and wisest view of a Cause, there is no such thing as a Lost Cause because there is no such thing as a Gained Cause. We fight for lost causes because we know that our defeat and dismay may be the preface to our successors’ victory, though that victory itself will be temporary; we fight rather to keep something alive than in the expectation that anything will triumph.”

The right may now be learning this same lesson. It is facing a country it thought would cheer a cruel deportation policy and hostility to non-white immigrants and realizing that, actually, people mostly just want eggs and gas to be cheaper.

And the left, I think, is embracing irony in victory because it knows the Karps of the world are not on its side forever, and that the people reaching for progressive language may not truly share the same commitments.

The Road to 2028 with Lakshya Jain

Lakshya Jain, our director of political data, gives us his update on the race to become the first president of the post-Trump era:

Everyone seems to have agreed that cost of living is the No. 1 issue for swing voters. But what I think pundits are missing is that it’s also the top issue for Democrats: In our polling, 62% of Kamala Harris voters graded this as a top-two concern. This is, after all, what catapulted New York City Mayor Zohran Mamdani to his win over more well-known candidates.

But I don’t know that any of the Democrats trying to run for president have really shown an ability to engage with productive solutions on this issue that they can sell to a Democratic primary audience just yet. The primary winner won’t be the candidate who can outflank everyone else on Palestine, but rather the candidate who most effectively channels economic discontent.

My guess is that Georgia Sen. Jon Ossoff could do it. But if he falls short, I would think the next most likely candidate is Rep. Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez. Neither former Vice President Kamala Harris nor Gov. Gavin Newsom has shown any inclination or past history of being able to recognize this problem, and I don’t expect them to start now.

On the Republican side, it’s still Vice President JD Vance’s race to lose. You might think that's because Vance is trying to put a bit of early distance between himself and the fantastically unpopular Iran War. But really it’s because no other Republican wants to run in this environment; even Secretary of State Marco Rubio has signaled he has no interest. The chorus calling for him to throw his hat in the ring will only grow louder, though.

Life of the Mind with Kelsey Piper

Kelsey Piper, The Argument’s staff writer reporting on AI, tech, science, education, and politics, tells us what she’s seeing in the world this week:

As my colleague Jeremiah Johnson summarized yesterday, Jensen Huang’s ouster as Nvidia CEO came after revelations that the company interfered with a Department of Commerce investigation into export control violations by a related company. That company was controlled by a woman with whom Huang was having an affair, and one that was suspected of routing Nvidia’s advanced AI chips into China.

An intelligence community memo leaked to The New York Times argued that Huang’s paramour had ties to China’s spy agency. The lurid affair-related details are new, but concerns that Nvidia flouted export controls have been around for a while: At a $1-million-per-head dinner at Mar-a-Lago, Huang pressed President Trump to allow more chip sales to China.

I don’t know if the export controls are good policy or not. But having them and not enforcing them got us the worst of both worlds: infuriating China without actually securing leverage for a bilateral agreement to not chase dangerous superintelligence. Hopefully the policy that comes out of this shock will be better.

🌟Abundance Win of the Week🌟

Maibritt Henkel, one of The Argument’s writing fellows, gives us her pick for this week’s biggest win in the wide world of abundance:

The Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act (BAAJA) cleared a key procedural vote in the Senate on Monday, the first of many legislative hurdles. If passed, the bill promises to significantly speed up the review process for major infrastructure projects, ease the build-out of high-voltage transmission lines, and ensure that technology companies shoulder data-center-related spikes in grid infrastructure costs.

But while BAAJA would likely streamline the construction of wind and solar energy projects, it would do so partly by limiting the power of outside parties to sue to block projects, angering some environmental groups and tribal leaders. While 73 senators voted to advance the legislation — prominent holdouts included Sen. Bernie Sanders and Sen. Jeff Merkley — BAAJA’s passage is far from assured in a closely divided House of Representatives where majority control is soon to change hands.

On Tuesday, the Congressional Progressive Caucus announced its official opposition “unless significant improvements are made.” A final Senate vote could happen as early as next week.
