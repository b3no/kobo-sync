Why Amazon is eating books

The second-hand book business, for decades, has been plagued by an oversupply of inventory. Hundreds of millions of books sit on retail shelves and in warehouses in vain hopes of being read again. The used book site BookFinder.com alone advertises 150 million listings. Every year, hundreds of millions more books are printed, adding to the problem. There’s been no relief in sight. Until this year.

The used book market has been hot lately. Weirdly hot. Suspiciously hot. Enormous orders have been made—booksellers are talking about an historic spike in sales.

The independent journalism outlet 404 Media noted the spike and began investigating. It spoke to a bookseller with a specialized inventory who, in a good week, used to sell about twenty books, mostly to schools and libraries. His business had been shrinking because school and library budgets have been shrinking. He noticed an uptick in April. He now routinely sells hundreds of books a week. “There’s no rhyme or reason to it,” says the seller. “Also, there’s a total disregard for the price of the book. I’ve had some books that sold … that were … greatly overpriced.”

Other booksellers discussed similar experiences in an online forum hosted by Alibris, a popular used book marketplace. They’d seen huge volumes of orders using AutoBuy, a bulk ordering feature: buyers uploaded lists of ISBNs (individual book identifiers); Alibris searched its inventory and delivered. The buyers, who appeared to be working from massive spreadsheets of ISBNs, wanted one copy of each book. They never ordered older books that don’t have ISBNs, and they never haggled over price.

The booksellers believed AI companies to be behind the purchases. AI companies have famously trained their models on literature for years. A frontier or cutting-edge AI model needs tokens, or small chunks of text. Meta says its Llama 3/3.1 model was trained on 15 trillion tokens. An 80,000-word book has about 100,000 to 120,000 tokens, so you’d need roughly 125 million to 150 million books to get close to 15 trillion tokens. That’s a lot of books. Back in 2010, when Google was trying to scan every book for its own purposes, it estimated there were 130 million unduplicated titles in the world. So models are also trained on web pages and public documents, but books are especially valuable because their text tends to be long, coherent, and written by humans—premium tokens.

Anthropic settled a copyright infringement case for $1.5 billion after it trained its Claude chatbot on roughly half a million pirated ebooks. The settlement worked out to about $3,000 a book. The average price of a legally obtained used book is maybe $8 to $10. A federal judge has ruled that converting lawfully purchased physical books into digital copies can constitute “fair use” under copyright laws so long as the physical originals are destroyed and no duplicates are made. You can see how buying, scanning. and destroying used books would be cheaper than settling class-action suits.

The Anthropic lawsuit revealed the company’s plan, Project Panama, to do just that. The destruction serves two purposes: it keeps you on the right side of the law, and it’s far easier and faster to scan a book if you guillotine its spine and feed the pages into a high-speed scanner. Anthropic apparently had a deal with a supplier to scan and destroy between 500,000 and 2 million books in a six-month period.

Given their appetite for tokens and what AI companies are spending on chips, compute, data centers, and class-action settlements, dropping $100 or $200 million to buy 10 or 20 million books is a bargain. Why would they slow down to haggle over price?

The journalists at 404 couldn’t prove that AI companies were behind the recent spike in used book sales, but last month they found a company called ISBNdb that claims to have the world’s largest book database. It offers what 404 calls “high-volume book acquisition services,” helping AI labs “source bulk printed book purchases of between 1,000 to 1 million books per order. ISBNdb’s data makes it easier for AI companies to methodically acquire, scan, and turn printed books into training data while avoiding duplication.”

More recently, the 404 gang cleverly arranged for an Apple AirTag to be included in a shipment of about 1,000 books purchased on Biblio, another used book marketplace. The AirTag flew out of California to Milwaukee, where it was delivered to a specialized distribution company called Trifinity. It sat there for two weeks before heading west by truck and eventually arriving at Amazon warehouse LAS8, 5801 Nicco Way, Las Vegas, NV.

Amazon, among many other things, is an AI company. It’s building a suite of models under its Nova brand to compete with Anthropic, OpenAI, Google, etc. These are frontier models requiring gigantic feeds of training data, or human text.

Employees at Amazon warehouse LAS8 work on a team called VGT3. The team has a logo, described by 404 as “a dinosaur, brandishing its teeth and with a book in its hands.” This blatant ripoff of the Toronto Raptors logo is consistent with AI industry attitudes toward intellectual property.

Employees at VGT3, which shares its premises with LAS8, an Amazon print-on-demand operation, told 404 they do nothing but scan books. Shipments come in on trucks. They get unloaded, put on pallets, and moved to receiving where their barcodes are scanned, presumably to record the ISBN and avoid duplication. The books are then fed into a machine that shaves off their spines. The pages are stacked on library carts and delivered to one of more than twenty high-speed scanners. The work is repetitive and boring, but employee forums suggest these Amazon workers are relatively happy with their lot—no reports of walking 15 to 20 miles on a 12-hour shift, anyway.

An employee interviewed by 404 described their initiation at VGT3: “We have to walk past everything to get to the back of the warehouse. That’s where I first saw all the scanners, and I was really wondering what was going on. I remember I even asked someone what they were doing and they said they’re not sure even the people who work there know what they’re doing. Of course, once I got over there and started putting two and two together, I kind of understood. And you know, I think people who work there probably know what was going on over there, but probably just didn’t want to talk about it. We also saw them cutting the spines off the books and everything.”

The employee said all manner of books in various languages are being scanned: “When we first started a lot of them were brand new. Some of them are used as well. Like you could tell, they were liquidated from a library or something like that. We were even getting boxes of stuff from London … There was even … stapled together papers that said that they were presented to Parliament on the behalf of Her Royal Majesty the Queen.”

The employee is purportedly unhappy that the books are being destroyed: “There’s so much knowledge and so much stuff in them and some of them look like they might be rare, and I’ve heard that they order rare books, and that’s why I say some of these are so obscure.”

The 404 guys got a statement out of Amazon, although it wouldn’t say how many facilities of this kind it has around the world, and it wouldn’t respond to criticisms of its wholesale destruction of books: “Amazon purchases books through commercial channels to help develop and improve the products and services our customers use.”

404’s reporting inspired the Wall Street Journal to do some similar work last weekend. A serious share of what passes for book industry reporting at both the WSJ and the New York Times these days is rewrites of stories developed by independent media or book influencers. The Journal spoke to a bookseller who reported large orders of obscure titles—Home Long-Term Oxygen Treatment in Italy and Marriage Litigation in Medieval England—from buyers with fake-sounding names who never balked at the prices. Another admitted to enjoying the windfall while worrying he’s selling shovels to gravediggers: “These books probably have a purpose that will be totally contradictory to the longevity of my business.”

Many people in chat rooms and Reddit forums are upset about large-scale book destruction. I feel their pain, although I’m not sure there’s anything inherently wrong with the practice. The book industry itself destroys tens of millions of books a year. Landfills and recycling facilities brim with books people have discarded. We have an oversupply of used books.

The potential destruction of irreplaceable volumes seems to be the most genuine concern, although there’s not much evidence that’s happening. The buyers are targeting single copies of books between 1970 (the start of the ISBN era) and 2022 (pre-AI). Pretty much everything published has already been scanned by the likes of Google or Internet Archive. Publishers in Canada, the US, and the UK are required to send archival copies of their books for preservation in government libraries. The odds that a tech company will buy and destroy the last copy of any book are negligible.

Still. Mass demolition of books is unseemly. “Even if you love the technology,” as someone said on Reddit, “you can admit that the concept of an AI literally eating books to become more powerful is pretty dystopian.”

Some booksellers report holding back special books and signed editions from suspected AI orders. Elon Musk, of all people, has instructed his book scanning team to preserve any rare books that come into his facilities and scan them the hard way with spine intact. It’s unclear if he realizes that failing to destroy could change the copyright analysis. Regardless, good for him.

I don't suppose anything will stop this practice. In the meantime, used booksellers need to triple or quadruple their prices. If the machines are going to eat the world's books, they should pay dearly for the meal.
