Democrats should seize the chance to end the filibuster

A national voter ID law is nothing to fear.

Matthew Yglesias
Aug 3
∙
Paid
 
READ IN APP
 

There’s a weird bit of slightly under-the-radar drama playing out in Congress right now. Donald Trump keeps demanding that Republicans scrap the legislative filibuster and pass the SAVE America Act, while Republican leaders keep insisting this won’t happen and that Trump should get off their backs.

The latest escalation is that Trump wants Senate Republicans to cancel the August recess so they can talk about SAVE, but John Thune doesn’t want to do this because it’s a huge waste of time, which in turn is leading Trump to rage against Thune.

This hasn’t gotten that much attention since I guess it’s hard to get people to read stories about things that aren’t happening. But there are five points I think deserve emphasizing, the last of which is, I think, the most significant:

  1. This fight illustrates that even Trump can’t actually pull off the “pound the table harder” approach to getting bills passed that progressives often fantasize that a sufficiently committed Democrat could use.

  2. Democrats are holding firm even though the core SAVE concept of requiring photo ID to vote is extremely popular and it might be wiser to get to “yes” on this.

  3. Republicans have written a bill that pairs a popular idea that they claim to think is important (photo ID) with a bunch of dumb chaff that makes it impossible to actually enact the thing they’re saying is important.

  4. Both sides continue to wildly overstate the practical stakes of this kind of legislation and in fact are probably backwards in terms of which side would receive the (modest) electoral benefit from making it harder to vote.

  5. And finally, progressives who are nominally committed to getting rid of the filibuster seem to have no interest in the question of whether the best way to achieve this is to actually work with Trump on it while he’s hot and bothered.

The single biggest topic on which I am aligned with progressives against moderates is getting rid of the filibuster.

I think frontline Democrats’ affection for the filibuster is emblematic of the basic weakness of center-left factionalism at the moment. You have a bunch of senators who want to have it both ways, telling the groups that they agree with them about XYZ while knowing it’s cheap talk because the filibuster makes it impossible. I think it would be a lot healthier on a number of levels to let bills pass the Senate by majority rule, for frontline Democrats to admit that they have a limited appetite for progressive ideas, and for progressive Democrats to admit that the thing they actually need is clearer priorities, not more big ideas.

But the frontliners’ opportunism is grounded in real facts of self-interest. They like being able to have it both ways and don’t want to scrap the filibuster, which is exactly why lots of Republican senators don’t want to scrap the filibuster for the sake of the SAVE America Act.

So if you want to scrap the filibuster, the time to do it is now, when Trump and Mike Lee and others are committing to doing it.

Progressive senators should work with right-wing ones to scrap the filibuster, and then moderate senators should work with moderate Republicans to try to modify SAVE into a more reasonable voter ID bill.

What the SAVE America Act actually does

Voter ID policy varies a great deal state by state, with many states having no requirement that you show a photo ID to vote. More conservative states, however, tend to require photo ID. Those ID laws, in turn, are classified by election law nerds as “strict” (Ohio, Wisconsin, North Carolina) or “non-strict” (Texas, Florida, South Carolina).

In a non-strict state, if you show up to vote without your ID, you’re allowed to vote if you do something like sign a sworn affidavit, whereas in a strict state, it’s truly no ID, no vote.

The spirit of the non-strict requirements is that anyone who is factually a United States citizen and a registered voter ought to have the right to vote. The ID rules are meant to be precautions against fraud, but they’re also trying to safeguard against errors in the other direction.

Important for the larger conversation here is that the strictest laws are not in the most right-wing states. They’re in states where Republicans have majorities in the state legislature but where they’re worried about losing competitive statewide elections, so they’ve decided to strictness-max. South Carolina is way more conservative than North Carolina and, for precisely that reason, the South Carolina G.O.P. is less willing to inconvenience its citizens. It’s in swingy North Carolina that Republicans want to make it as hard as possible to vote.

The core weirdness of the SAVE America Act is that it not only tries to require that all states adopt voter ID laws but also imposes standards that are stricter than those currently used in any state — it includes two additional elements on top of voter ID.

One requirement is the idea that when you register to vote, you should show up with proof-of-citizenship documents on hand. The bug here is that widely owned photo IDs such as driver’s licenses don’t indicate whether you’re a citizen, so you’d either need to come in with your passport or else with a birth certificate.

This is a little annoying and it’s also unnecessary. The Bipartisan Policy Center argues that it would be more convenient and more reliable to use back-end verification — ID establishes that the person registering is who they say they are, and then the election official verifies citizenship in the available databases. If they flag you as a non-citizen, they’ll reach out, and then you’d have the chance to provide documents to show they messed up.

The bill’s other problem is that these significant changes in how elections are administered would be effective immediately upon enactment, giving states zero days to establish new procedures. This is necessary to maintain the pretense that the purpose of the bill is to prevent Democrats from cheating in the midterms, but it’s obviously not how you’d structure something you actually intended to become law.

So why have Republicans taken this tack with the SAVE America Act? This is one of those situations where I think it’s regrettable that conservative media is so propagandistic and obsessed with lib-owning, because I can’t actually figure out what’s going on.

I think that if congressional Democrats were pushing a federal mandate that was at odds with how progressive states did something, we would see dialogue about it. But I haven’t found a single article where someone from the Wisconsin G.O.P. says, “Here’s why we wrote our voter ID bill this way and here’s why we think the SAVE America Act version is worse.” And there’s no counter-article where someone says, “No, what they’ve done in Wisconsin is bad and we need the SAVE standard.”

I suspect the reason is that a critical mass of Republicans doesn’t actually want the bill to pass — they want frontline Democrats to vote against it so they can say so-and-so is against voter ID. As a result, the bill is maximalist and unreasonable at every margin.

Democrats are out to lunch on voter ID

A federal voter ID bill is a smart thing for Republicans to try to get Democrats to vote against because it’s such an overwhelmingly popular idea.

I think a lot of conservatives who’ve watched Democrats’ stubbornness in the face of public opinion on this have come around to thinking that maybe the conspiracy theorists are right and Democrats are trying to do large-scale fraud after all.

The real truth is stupider than that: Back in the Obama era when low-propensity voters leaned toward Democrats, progressives convinced themselves that mobilizing non-voters could make unpopular progressive causes into electoral winners. This idea never made sense, because it was always the case that sporadic voters and non-voters were less ideological than regular voters, so the way you “mobilize” non-voters is just to be more moderate.

Beyond that, though, the turnout patterns have clearly flipped, and these days it’s Democrats who do better if disengaged people stay home and don’t vote.

But the progressive ecosystem never wanted to admit that its interest in eliminating every possible voting friction was instrumental, so the movement has been slow to update on the fact that administrative barriers to voting now probably favor Democrats.

This is especially true because one of the main complaints Democrats are raising against the SAVE America Act is that providing documentary proof of citizenship is especially burdensome to married women (a G.O.P.-leaning group) who have changed their names (a more G.O.P.-leaning subgroup) and don’t have passports (an even more G.O.P.-leaning subgroup) because their driver’s licenses won’t match their birth certificates and they’ll need extra paperwork.

I’ve tried to really push some Democratic elected officials on this, and they swear up and down that they’re just principled defenders of maximizing the ease with which married, native-born women with traditionalist gender ideals who don’t travel abroad are able to vote and that it would never in a million years occur to them to even wonder about the partisanship of this demographic.

But, clearly, the fact that Democrats are willing to take such an unpopular stand here indicates some kind of deep fear.

To be clear, I think the best available evidence from scholarship is that voter ID laws don’t actually reduce turnout. That’s because the propensity to vote among the kinds of people who don’t have ID is super-low anyway. But a lot of Democrats have gotten very invested in the idea that these laws are racist — Chuck Schumer even called them Jim Crow 2.0 — so it’s hard to climb down and acknowledge that they’re not a very big deal.

I don’t think it’s out of the question that documentation rules for registration would reduce voting. But this change would help Democrats in the short term and its long-term impact is totally ambiguous, so I think there’s nothing to fear here, though I do think Congress might want to consider making it free to get a passport.

Time to bust the filibuster

I wrote my first-ever anti-filibuster article in April of 2005 when Republicans controlled the Senate. Democrats were filibustering some judicial nominees, and Republicans were considering a “nuclear option” to break the filibuster.

I suggested then, as a principled person, that Democrats should refrain from unilateral disarmament on the judges but say that if Republicans wanted to end all filibusters and create a “majority rules” Senate, they’d be happy to do that. At the time, this was a contrarian case that progressives hated, but it would eventually become conventional wisdom on the left.

I continue to think that the filibuster is bad.

What’s more, I continue to think the most realistic way to get the filibuster reformed is to do it in a principled, bipartisan way. The SAVE America Act is a bad bill with questionable provisions that at least a few Republican senators seem to have significant doubts about. But the core concept of requiring voters to use photo ID when they vote is fine.

Meanwhile, the White House and a handful of senators like Lee are wildly overstating the importance of this bill and encouraging Republicans to nuke the filibuster. That’s not going to happen on a party-line G.O.P. vote. But if progressive opponents of the filibuster come to the table and say they’re willing to provide the extra votes needed to scrap it, they could get it done.

The SAVE America Act might pass as a result, its implementation would be a mess, and Democrats could point at that mess and say, “We told you so, vote these morons out.”

Alternatively, with no filibuster in place, the Republicans who know this bill is a mess might need to stop hiding behind the filibuster and actually say, “Hey — we support voter ID but we need to roll back the implementation timeline and bring the rules into alignment with current practice.” If they do that, I think Democrats should vote for the revised bill. But one way or another, the world won’t end, and future Congresses will be able to proceed with more sensible rules.

The fundamental stakes of the filibuster are just much, much larger than the rather small stakes of the SAVE America Act. People should think about how they’re going to feel in 2029 and act accordingly today when the prospects for change are better.
