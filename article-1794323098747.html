Leftists Complained That I Didn’t Account for Partisan Lean So I Accounted for Partisan Lean

Surely NOW they’ll be reasonable

Last week, my article about the relative performance of leftist Democrats versus normie Democrats included a chart that went viral. Here it is — you may have encountered it on Politically Engaged Shut-In Twitter:

As you might expect, this drew the attention of the leftist reality-denial machine, which is currently being driven so hard that it must have steam shooting out of the rivets. Matt Bruenig declared the analysis “not valid” in a tweet he has since deleted. The leftist consensus was that the blindingly-obvious message of the chart — that leftist candidates perform worse than normies — could be tossed out on the grounds that it’s harder to cause a blue shift in a state that’s already blue.

This was the most annoying kind of pushback: The kind that may contain a grain of truth. Because the theory being posited by Bruenig and others is plausible — maybe it is harder to summon a big blue shift in an already-blue state. I found this to be something of a nit-pick, because the assumed effect would have to be gigantic in order to change the chart’s fundamental signal, which also needs to be considered in the context of the sprawling galaxy of evidence that shows that far-left candidates perform badly. To me, this is like climate change: There is endless evidence showing basically the same phenomena, but committed non-believers point to the one miscalibrated weather station in Aruba or the odd snowy day in Atlanta and say “See: Hoax.”

But let’s try to quantify the theory that big shifts are harder to achieve in blue states and then apply it to my chart. We can estimate how much each state shifted between ‘24 and ‘26 by comparing the statewide House vote in those two years. And, this just in: Tuesday was a good night for Democrats! The average shift was 16 points! This is a good data set¹ despite me having to make an educated guess at Alaska,² where Balto appears to have dragged the outstanding vote into a river and heroically frozen to death.

To measure how “blue” or “red” each state is, I used the Cook Report’s Partisan Voting Index. Then, I plotted my two data sets on the same chart and added a line of best fit:

There’s a lot of variance in the data, but big blue shifts do seem to be broadly more common in redder states. The Pearson’s correlation coefficient for this data is -0.55, and I once paid a university $100,000 to tell me that that is a moderate correlation.

So: How does that affect my chart?

The line of best fit suggests that each point of partisan lean correlates with about 1.4 points of Democratic shift. That would argue for giving a candidate in a D+10 state 14 “extra” points and penalizing a candidate in an R+10 state the same amount. For what it’s worth, I find this extremely heavy-handed; it treats a middling correlation like an iron law, and therefore is as likely to shuffle the results as to provide clarity. But I’m trying to give leftists every benefit of the doubt by coming up with a method for correcting for state lean and then treating that method like an immutable law of the universe.

I re-ran the chart with two changes:

  1. I used the most recent data — vote totals are more complete now than when I was writing last Wednesday. This caused minor changes but nothing significant; the five Bernie-endorsed candidates were still the five worst performers by raw shift margin.

  2. I added or subtracted 1.4 points to a candidate’s margin for each point of partisan lean in their state. So: This led to a brutal 15.4 point penalty to Scott Colom on one extreme and an engorged 19.6 point bonus to Ed Markey on the other extreme.

And that produced this chart:

There’s redemption for Markey, though he was always the oddball — he’s only included because I wanted all five Bernie-endorsed Senate candidates on the chart. He’s the only one in this set who didn’t run a competitive race, and he and Ossoff are the only ones who are already Senators. And not for nothin’: Tom “Not Better Than Kamala” Osborn is the populist independent recruited by the same team that brought you the Graham Platner debacle. I don’t consider Osborn a leftist — and apparently Bernie doesn’t, either — but I don’t want to miss an opportunity to mock the notion that a group of 20-something Ivy League-educated activists are the portal into the mind of the common man.

Even with this sizeable adjustment, normie Democrats in competitive races did 5.6 points better than their leftist colleagues on average. And, frankly, the adjustment I made was too severe, which became obvious when I ran the numbers for all races (not just competitive³ ones):

This confirmed my suspicion that my adjustment was too strong, because this is gibberish. The list of worst performers is just a list of candidates who ran in extremely red states, and the best performers are all in very blue ones. To believe this chart, you have to believe that a quarter of all Senate candidates un-performed Kamala ‘24, and that Rhode Island’s John Reed is a political colossus that the bards will sing about for thousands of years. It also makes me think that the competitive race/not competitive race distinction is very important, which makes sense: Surely there’s a difference between a pro-forma rout of some sacrificial lamb and a hard-fought race where each candidate spends the GDP of Peru calling their opponent an incontinent Nazi.

I think that the truth probably lies somewhere between the adjusted chart and the non-adjusted chart. It does seem to be a bit easier to cause a big shift in a red state, but the extra 1.4 points I awarded per point of partisan lean were way too much. And I think that the competitive/not competitive distinction matters. So, if I was to guess at what reality looks like, it would be the midpoint of the unadjusted/adjusted chart for competitive races only, which is this:

That seems about right: Colom broke out, Cooper and Ossoff are strong, Talarico was running against a cartoon villain. Peltola’s Fake Dan Sullivan prank hurt her, and Osborn was a retread whom voters possibly figured out is not related to legendary Nebraska Cornhuskers coach Tom Osborne. And the leftist candidates were weak — consistent with many other findings — with Jackson having the toughest assignment of the four.

Remember that even when I went way too far giving the benefit of the doubt to the leftist argument, normies still did 5.6 points better than Bernie-backed candidates in similar races. In the “moderately adjusted” version, they did more than ten points better.⁴ A precise number, though, isn’t important — we will never determine whether normies outperform leftists by a bit or a tad or three metric smidges. It’s just important that we know that normies outperform leftists by some amount, and that that truth is about as well-established as heliocentrism. The narrative that the American people cry out for left-wing populism is false, and I think that leftists should change their beliefs to match that reality instead of demanding that reality change to match their beliefs.
