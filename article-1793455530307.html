When the Maoists came for Mbappé

And Napoleon?

Jeremiah Johnson
Oct 31
 
READ IN APP
 

Welcome to The Weekly Scroll!

The Weekly Scroll is a once-a-week roundup of the most interesting, strangest, and funniest stories from around the social internet.

This week, we’re looking at the war between European soccer fans and Chinese netizens, what’s driving recent French youth protests, and surprising digital political strategies in Brazil.

China vs. Mbappé

Greetings, readers of The Argument. This edition of The Weekly Scroll is coming to you from France, where I’ve been traveling this week. As you would expect, the baguettes are indeed much better than America’s, and the Vietnamese and North African food is outstanding. (The croissants are, oddly enough, no better than those I can get in New York.)

Most importantly, I’ve been getting served a lot more French content while browsing in Paris. And that means I’ve had a front-row seat for the pitched battle between French soccer fans and Chinese netizens.

Over the past few weeks, we’ve talked about how Twitter’s automatic translation feature is leading to real-time, dynamic international discourse that wouldn’t have been possible in previous eras. In this case, the story begins with a very silly meme trend that puts French soccer superstar Kylian Mbappé’s face on various dictators.

In April, Mbappé was asked about his favorite memes of himself during a backstage interview for the French podcast The Bridge. Turns out he loves the dictator memes! “That makes me laugh the most, it’s the one where there’s a video of a guy, I think it’s a Chinese dictator,” he said, referring to a meme of his face superimposed on a portrait of Mao Zedong.

The backstage footage (which has since been taken down) reached escape velocity on social media on Monday. The popular Chinese sports account Dongqiudi quickly slammed Mbappé: “Mbappé’s remarks have hurt China and the Chinese people. We cannot translate them, but regardless, his comments have already offended the feelings of Chinese fans and the Chinese people, and such remarks are unacceptable,” it said in a statement, according to a Google translation.

It’s important to understand that Chinese social media is intensely nationalistic, to a degree that would shock most Westerners. Social media mobs, with the tacit approval of the Chinese Communist Party, routinely go after non-Chinese companies or celebrities for any perceived slight to China’s honor, no matter how small.

John Cena was famously forced to give a groveling apology after he called Taiwan a country in 2021. Chinese state television refused to air NBA games for more than a year after one team’s general manager posted in support of protesters in Hong Kong. A Chinese celebrity dropped Versace after it was discovered that a Versace shirt implicitly referred to Hong Kong and Macau as countries, leading the brand to issue a formal apology. Marriott, Delta, Gap, Samsung, and dozens of other companies have faced backlash due to similar offenses.

Mbappé’s offense caused him to lose a beverage deal, a story that became the No. 1 trending spot on Chinese social media site Weibo. And the company wasn’t alone: Chinese brands immediately began dropping Mbappé as a spokesman. Mbappé’s accounts on Chinese social media were banned or restricted. Despite apologies from Mbappé and LaLiga, the Spanish league in which he plays, Chinese state television announced it was no longer going to be showing the league’s games in China.

Chinese netizens got so furious about the perceived insult that they started defacing water bottles and posters featuring the superstar’s image.

What’s particularly notable is that most major commentary accounts on Chinese social media are so afraid to highlight criticism of Mao that they won’t even quote Mbappé’s words. That means that many of these users are incredibly angry but also have no idea what Mbappé actually said or did.

The conflict on Twitter highlighted the vast chasm that separates the player’s critics from his defenders. Chinese Twitter’s¹ criticism of Mbappé drew a reaction from French users (and, later, Western users in general) who defended freedom of speech.

In response, several Chinese users tried to turn the tables, with one asking “Since Mbappé can make jokes about our leader, shouldn’t we also be allowed to make jokes about Napoleon?” China-sympathetic accounts also asked how Americans would feel if someone pointed out George Washington was a slave owner.

One of the most interesting aspects of this kind of cross-cultural battle royale is how, despite automatic translation, users from various countries still don’t seem to actually understand one another. The Chinese poster daring to insult Napoleon presumably did not expect French people to respond that not only did they not care but that they would happily join in.²

An analogous situation played out earlier this month when, during an argument about two Bangladeshi workers who were executed after allegedly being intimate inside a Saudi Arabian mosque, a devoutly Muslim account asked, “So if two people were caught having illicit sex in a church people will just look, laugh and pass?” The user did not seem to realize that the obvious answer in many cultures is, “Well, we obviously wouldn’t kill them.”

For all that commentators in the West are accused of ignorance of other cultures’ norms, that ignorance is a two-way street. Liberal values like free speech, freedom of religion, and the rule of law aren’t obvious in every culture. What seems like a clear “gotcha” in one country reads like a bewildering non sequitur in another.

Automatic translation has enabled us to communicate across language barriers, but, while you can translate the text, you can’t translate culture.

A coda on French protests

It’s a bit outside the usual scope of The Weekly Scroll, but I would be remiss if I spent a week in France and didn’t talk at least a bit about the recent wave of protests. The demonstrations and their aftermath have so far involved thousands of arrests spread over several weeks, more than 2,500 businesses vandalized, and business losses totaling roughly €1 billion in one weekend alone.

I can’t claim to have done a scientific survey, but I did talk to taxi drivers, shopkeepers, students, retirees, and many other Parisians over the last week. The large majority seemed to agree on three points:

  • The original student protesters made some legitimate demands.

  • Rioting and looting are not legitimate expressions of that protest.

  • It’s unlikely that these protests will cause real change.

One thing I noticed while talking to students is that, despite the revolutionary imagery and the “Revolution Now” graffiti outside my hotel, the student demands are not actually revolutionary. They want school facilities that aren’t in disrepair, teachers who actually show up, and a college application process that doesn’t feel like a random roll of the dice.

They’re not trying to overthrow the Fifth Republic. They mostly just want the system to work fairly — the way they believe they were promised it would work.

You can see this in recent youth movements and social-media-powered protests around the world. The “Cockroach Janta Party” in India had a distinctly nonradical list of demands that essentially amounted to “make the public examination process fair.” The Gen Z 212 movement in Morocco was far less radical than the youth movements of the Arab Spring — it focused on shifting spending from flashy World Cup venues toward hospitals and education. Serbian students have used “let the institutions do their jobs” as a rallying cry.

Nonrevolutionary protests don’t always stay that way. Youth movements in Nepal and Bangladesh started with a narrow focus but ended up overthrowing their respective governments. I suspect that’s not going to happen in France, because for all its problems, France is still a very wealthy country and a stable democracy. The revolutionary aesthetics are more of a nod to French history. France doesn’t need a revolution — it just needs a government that can make hard choices and deliver basic services more effectively.

But then again, it is France, so you can’t really rule a revolution out.

Lula’s Army

Last weekend, Brazil held the second round of its presidential election and delivered a shocking result: Though he lost by almost 2 percentage points in the first round earlier this month, Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva, the sitting president, stormed back to defeat right-wing challenger Flávio Bolsonaro by more than 3 points.

How did such a big swing take place in just three weeks? Part of the story seems to be a misguided change in tack by Bolsonaro. After narrowly winning the first round, in which he touted his moderate bona fides, the challenger began boasting about radical changes.

But more interesting and perhaps just as impactful was the creation of the online organizing group “Lula’s Army.”

Started by left-wing politician André Janones, Lula’s Army was a WhatsApp channel that eventually grew to nearly 11 million subscribers. Rather than focusing on just broadcasting propaganda, the channel used its military theme to get users to engage in a tight loop of participatory tasks. Janones urged supporters to enlist as “soldiers” and assigned them what he called “missions.” According to one Reddit post, members could be “promoted” up the ranks. The missions were concrete tasks — but usually very easy to complete, lowering the barrier to mass participation.

Some days, missions would involve sharing campaign videos and promoting hashtags. Still others directed supporters to Google Bolsonaro scandals to push up negative associations on Google Trends. Lula himself would tell viewers during a live debate to Google cases involving his opponent. Searches for “caso Ana Clara” rose 5,000% that month, according to Brazil Online. Members would also be asked to do real-world persuasion by sending prewritten messages to friends and family.

Lula’s Army effectively gamified politics. It’s impossible to say exactly what impact it had on vote totals, but it seems obvious that it was significant (especially because Bolsonaro tried to copy the strategy once it was clear how well it worked). There’s an interesting account on the /r/neoliberal subreddit about how it activated older, non-online, nonpolitical citizens. More so than any political operation I’ve seen in America, it made political participation social, fun, and easy for voters who are normally difficult to engage.

I would be very surprised if we don’t see similar efforts in future U.S. elections.
