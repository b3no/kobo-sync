What happens if we build it and nobody dies?

The world after superintelligence

Kelsey Piper
Nov 17
 
READ IN APP
 

My online and offline lives used to be very different. Online I corresponded with people whose charts and diagrams suggested that the Singularity would happen in our lifetimes, and that in five years or so our world would be unrecognizable. If it doesn’t get us killed, I’ve been told more than once that it’ll be like stepping through a time machine — awakening one day in 2200 in a world where almost nothing remains the same.

Offline, the people I talked to largely assumed that things would mostly stay the same. Parents like me worried about which middle and high schools we’d send our kids to and commiserated about college admissions. We figured we would need to work our own jobs until retirement and prepare the next generation to succeed at theirs.

But AI comes up more at the playground these days. I don’t know, people will confess with a nervous giggle, what kind of economy my kids will graduate into. I don’t know what we’re trying to do here in the first place.

If you care about preparing your children to lead good lives, it is disconcerting to realize you have absolutely no idea what it will mean to lead a good life in 15 years — and that you’re just as clueless about it even if the next 15 years go well.

Let’s set aside, for a moment, the fear that companies like OpenAI and Anthropic will put their AIs to work building more powerful AIs, unsupervised, until we build a machine god that has no interest in working for us. Let’s imagine that through whatever combination of policy, luck, and technical breakthroughs, the bad outcomes are avoided: no war with China, no sovereign AI succeeding in coups against the world’s governments, no sudden plague wiping out AI’s human competition. In other words, we achieve superintelligence — a scenario in which general-purpose AI can do virtually every task better than a human being — and we find a way to make it benign.

What do we do with it then?

“What is striking to me is how little reassurance even some of the happiest scenarios offer,” biology researcher and occasional Silicon Valley observer Ruxandra Teslo writes in a recent essay. “One common and ostensibly optimistic vision is known as ‘handover’: humanity cedes control of its future to a godlike AI and we become the comfortably maintained inhabitants of a civilisation whose direction is no longer ours to decide.”

Some people are excited to pursue this very outcome. “If we do build a godlike AI, and determine to our own satisfaction that it’s fully-aligned, I’d rather let it choose its own direction for itself and for humanity than have it be under the control of the US president,” Scott Alexander writes in his defense of handoverism. Others are more wary but regard some kind of handover as inevitable — Teslo quotes one of the researchers at the leading AI labs as saying, “I cannot see any other option for where humanity will go.”

But it does seem, to me, that this would suck.

More importantly, I think we could do so much better. If AI becomes as powerful as researchers believe it will become, we ought to be able to use it to build a world that we are actively eager to live in, rather than one we are unhappily resigned to. The world that futurists are envisioning is one where GDP rapidly rises a hundredfold or even a thousandfold — and yet where there is not all that much that most of us can look forward to.

Most people find the futurist projections where we are uploaded onto and immortalized by computers to be somewhere between disturbing and horrifying; even those of us who can take that one in stride just have to walk a few steps farther to find something we find disturbing and horrifying. To me the thing that is most painful to contemplate is a world in which none of the virtues that I have strived to cultivate matter, a world in which I cannot offer my children any meaningful guidance because none of the challenges or choices they face are comprehensible to me.

A good future would be one in which we still do things that matter. They need not all be things that matter to me, if they matter to the people who do them — I will accept that for some people, what matters will be speedrunning video games. This world would be one friendly to ambition and aspiration. It would be one where we neither exist in completely disconnected bubbles nor need to spend most of our time staying in the good standing of a rigidly ideological community. And it should sound cool: if I told my kids we were moving there, they should be excited.

That’s not a high bar, but I haven’t seen too many post-superintelligence visions that clear it. Part of the problem is that these visions often treat the end of scarcity as if it settles the question of what makes a good life. But a post-scarcity world is not necessarily a utopia.

What scarcity does for us

I want to end material scarcity, obviously. But I don’t think that ending material scarcity will be sufficient to make the world a good place — and I worry that the political, social, and economic freedoms that are an essential part of the good life are rooted in the kind of material conditions we live under now. If people no longer need one another’s labor or goods, I worry we may lose some of the ordinary reasons we have to cooperate with people outside our own communities.

Start with a feature of the modern world that I love (and many other people hate): transacting with strangers. I know many people who think — either because of reactionary or Marxist political commitments — that it’s a tragedy that the modern world requires us to transact with strangers. They lament purely economic exchanges that do not depend on a deep shared history between participants, or a delicate balancing act between giving and receiving favors.

But I love this feature of the modern world; I find it liberating. If I wish to buy a shirt I do not need to be afraid that the tailor, having heard rumors of my discreditable conduct or failure to attend church, will want nothing to do with me. We have this whole sphere, the commercial, in which we are not as subject to the hierarchies and internal struggles common to close-knit personal communities.

Liberalism offers social and political technologies for getting along with each other despite vast differences, the agreement to exist peaceably alongside people whom we would never call friends or neighbors but are willing to call fellow citizens. It’s hard to imagine liberalism taking root in a world where almost no one ever spoke to anyone other than their fellow villagers; it is especially suited to a world where we transact with strangers, where we and the strangers both have something to gain, and where we can extend each other bounded trust despite our differences.

My worry about the post-scarcity world promised by evangelists of superintelligence is that, while few people benefit from being isolated, the pull toward it is powerful. In our world today this is counteracted by the necessity of working with other people. We build commonality when we do things together — watch the same movies, go to the same grocery stores, solve the same problems — and we build friendships through showing up for one another and doing mildly hard things as a favor.

In a completely post-scarcity world we will no longer be able to provide value to one another through our hard work; this world will no longer require the mutually beneficial stranger interactions that help make the case for liberalism. It will of course still have lots of space for another kind of interpersonal interaction: watching influencers, getting into internet discourse. But doing work for someone else makes them better off, while the discourse/influence world is wholly zero-sum.

We’ve observed in The Argument many times that a positive-sum world lays the foundations for liberalism. And without that foundation, I worry that the stakes of the interpersonal arena will grow higher and higher — that we’ll be stuck, effectively, either withdrawing entirely from human contact or spending almost all our time trying to stay on the right side of community dynamics which are now the only source of validation and meaning.

A world without work

One meditation on this I found particularly chilling was written by a close friend, Asterisk editor in chief Clara Collier; she considers accounts from a series of societies, aristocratic or otherwise, where work was not necessary or expected. They all sound horrible: gossipy, status-obsessed societies where, because one cannot demonstrate value by just doing excellent work, one instead demonstrates it by winning endless social status games. The most brilliant and ambitious people of Versailles in the 18th century, in her account, spent all their time obsessing over resolving matters of precedence and who could wear which things.

Some people daydream of living in a Jane Austen novel, but I find the elites who were products of such societies often to be both miserable and contemptible. And a future of LARPing aristocrats would involve having their vices without even needing their virtues.

Surely we can do better than that. The only restriction here is our own imagination: Are our thoughts so impoverished that we cannot construct a future for which we feel eagerness, hunger, desperate hope?

To the detriment of speculative futurism, it has been conducted almost entirely by people who believe that AI might transform our world overnight within the next 10 years. The people who believe that are very weird — disproportionately childless and atheist and high-openness. Transhumanists who want to transcend our physical bodies or live forever in the digital world are overrepresented in their ranks.

Because these futurists are so different from the average person, they struggle to imagine a post-scarcity society that the rest of us would even want to live in. I’m reminded of this every time an AI company CEO remarks about how AI could “sort of baby sit your kids” (as Elon Musk has said) or help raise your newborn (as Sam Altman has quipped), or when someone muses about the “impossible task” of coming up with reasons why your daughter is special (as Marissa Mayer memorably remarked).

It’s easy to describe what we can subtract from the world — no disease, no aging, no scarcity — but hard for those subtractions to cohere into a positive vision that inspires any excitement.

Three paths forward

So, if not handover, what? I can see three potential paths. One vision is exit: You could live in the real-world equivalent of J.R.R. Tolkien’s Shire, some pastoral separatist community that does not notice or take any interest in the broader machinations of the world happening far beyond its borders. Lots of individual humans would want that, probably, and I wish them the best. But I would be sad were that the final state of all humanity. I don’t think that we built modern industrial civilization just so we could ultimately beat our retreat from it into the Garden of Eden again.

I would like for my actions to reverberate beyond my immediate vicinity; I don’t want to be cloistered or irrelevant. I want to go to the moon, or at least to help with a project to go to the moon; if I am to be a gardener, I want to learn a lot about genetic engineering and garden some really cool crops that have never yet been seen on this earth.

A second vision, on the other end of the spectrum from separation, is integration: the union of humans with the machines. You upload yourself, scanning all your brain patterns to produce their precise replica on silicon; then you can copy yourself, grow yourself, modify yourself as you wish, unbounded by the tedious restrictions of biology. If one wants to remain on the forefront of science and technology in an age of lightning-fast superintelligence, it’s hard to imagine doing that without being incomprehensibly augmented by AI. I know plenty of people who, for this reason, would become a computerized being in a heartbeat. But this would mean saying goodbye, in an important way, to friends and loved ones who don’t want to or for whatever reason cannot be augmented. What, after all, could I offer someone who can process information 10,000 times faster than I can?

I wish good luck to the people who can’t wait to do this. But I will grieve, if my children rush to join them, if there is no wisdom I can provide them that is applicable to their journeys — if they never cultivate any virtues I can recognize and I turn out to possess none that mean anything to them. I could embrace this path eventually — which itself makes me a wild outlier in how eager for weird futuristic experiences I am — but I am distraught at the thought of it happening too fast, of trying to keep up with whatever child of mine pursues it most eagerly.

A third possibility is not one shared way of life at all. It is a future in which humans remain in control of our destinies and choose among many ways of living. People will do lots of different things with that power. We are different from one another, and sci-fi possibilities would magnify those differences: you’d have people returning to Shire-like idylls where they can enjoy a pre-superintelligence pace of life and others ascending into a superintelligence themselves, and every possible choice will open up a thousand more high-stakes decisions, decisions that really matter and that only we can make, which will shape the societies of our choosing.

Most of these societies will probably intentionally limit AI: maybe ban it, or permit only some of its products, or set a maximum pace at which the world around them can change. If you don’t restrict AI, after all, your society’s traits five years down the line are totally unpredictable and unimaginable.

This will require, in many ways, becoming like the Amish. The Amish don’t actually ban technology; what they do is use a community-wide process to determine whether a new technology is good or bad for their aims. It is easy to imagine lots of communities might find themselves doing the same thing. In fact, the intense, passionate anger at an indie game team for using an AI tool to debug a tiny section of their game suggests that many already do.

I don’t want my community to shun people for AI use, but I do expect we’ll draw the line somewhere. I can already see those taboos emerging when I ask my friends where they draw the line: no recording casual conversations, no concealing who wrote something, no algorithmic feeds. It will take intentionality, but we can take up what is good for us and leave what isn’t.

As for the fear that ambition and hard work cannot coexist with a world without meaningful work to be done, I find some solace in my children at the same time as I fear for their futures. Their lives are already post-scarcity; they need not labor. And yet they do not strike me as without ambition or without the willingness to work hard. Among adult societies of aristocrats, perhaps, idleness turns to pathological zero-sum status games, but it doesn’t seem to do that in eight-year-olds. More resources just make their games more ambitious; they fight less when wandering off to do something else is an option.

I worry about how we’ll protect them from a world in which everything is meaningless, but maybe the answer is that they, having already solved that, will protect us.
