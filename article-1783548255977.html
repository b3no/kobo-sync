Upzoned, overbid, under construction

Congress, California, Chicago, and a collapsing office conversion made for a busy housing week.

Jul 8

Congress sent the 21st Century ROAD to Housing Act to President Donald Trump’s desk last month, and he has yet to sign it. Barring a formal veto, the bill becomes law automatically on Friday.

Meanwhile, California flipped the switch on its most aggressive zoning law yet, and a Federal Reserve working paper got dragged into the immigration wars. Also, a Midtown apartment conversion nearly collapsed, San Francisco buyers are forking over millions above asking, and a Kansas City nonprofit thinks it has found the answer to affordable housing.

California’s zoning law is live

SB 79 took effect last Wednesday, overriding local zoning statewide to allow denser multifamily housing within a half-mile of major transit stops. The law sets mandatory height and density minimums, while also including affordability and anti-displacement requirements. The Los Angeles Times estimates that the law could result in a million new units across California.

That is an optimistic estimate, but if implementation is successful, major change is the whole point.

A Fed housing paper stumbles into the immigration wars

A draft Federal Reserve working paper estimates that inflows of unauthorized immigrant workers can explain roughly 30 percent of home price growth and 20 percent of rent growth in the average metro area during the immigration surge that took place from early 2021 to early 2024. Trump posted on Truth Social that the paper “suggests Biden illegal immigrant wave drove up home prices 30 percent,” the Washington Post reports.

The paper, by Daniel J. Wilson and Xiaoqing Zhou of the Federal Reserve Bank of Dallas, does not say what Trump said it says.

Instead, the authors estimate that unauthorized immigration increased home prices by about 6.6 percent in the average metro area. Overall home prices rose roughly 22.4 percent during that period, leading the study to conclude unauthorized immigration accounted for about 30 percent of the growth in home prices — not a 30 percent increase in prices. The researchers attribute the effect to increased housing demand in markets where new construction failed to keep pace, rather than immigration status itself. They also found unauthorized immigration boosted local employment without significantly lowering wages or increasing reliance on public safety-net programs.

The paper remains a preliminary working draft — regional Federal Reserve banks sponsor a range of economics research and these kinds of papers do not represent official Fed views.

Chicago’s tenant-law overhaul heads to a fall vote

Mayor Brandon Johnson’s Protecting Renters Ordinance — the first major rewrite of the city’s landlord-tenant code since the 1980s — is working through the Committee on Housing and Real Estate. The package includes a citywide rental registry, a ban on junk fees (with security deposits capped at one month’s rent), a formal Tenant Bill of Rights, and a “just cause” standard that would require landlords to state a valid reason before pursuing eviction or declining to renew a lease.

The ordinance would also require relocation assistance when renters are forced out by demolition, condo conversion, owner move-in, or major rehab through no fault of their own.

Landlord groups are not thrilled. Michael Glasser, president of the Neighborhood Building Owners Alliance, told Block Club Chicago that “over 100 new regulations will raise costs for owners and tenants alike,” while Housing Commissioner Lissette Castañeda countered that roughly 10,000 Chicago tenants lose housing every year due to circumstances out of their control. Alderman Byron Sigcho-Lopez, who chairs the housing committee, said he wants a full council vote this fall.

L.A.’s fees are blocking the housing L.A. says it wants

A new RAND Corporation report found that Los Angeles could build thousands of additional housing units by reducing development impact fees, which the study says often make otherwise allowable apartment projects financially infeasible. While the city has zoning capacity for roughly 870,000 additional units across nearly 95,000 underbuilt parcels, only about 10 percent of eligible multifamily projects are financially feasible. RAND estimates a 25 percent fee reduction could make more than 5,600 additional units possible. Larger reductions could unlock even more housing, particularly mid-sized apartment buildings.

The report notes that lower fees could eventually be offset by increased property- and sales-tax revenue, but emphasizes that financial feasibility does not guarantee projects will ultimately be built, as financing, permitting, and market conditions remain significant hurdles. Still, L.A. is counting on private developers to build both market-rate and affordable housing even as the city charges fees that keep a lot of that housing from penciling out in the first place.

San Francisco moves one step forward, two steps back

San Francisco’s housing market, which was already heating up fast, is spiraling out of control. In the first six months of 2026, 144 homes sold for at least $1 million over asking, according to Compass, with 44 of those sales happening in June alone. During the same period last year, just eight homes met that fate, the San Francisco Chronicle reports. Agents say wealthy tech and finance buyers are racing to close deals before I.P.O. lockup periods expire for employees at Bay Area companies including OpenAI, Anthropic, and SpaceX, the New York Times reports. Across the city, the price per square foot for single-family homes has increased by nearly 14 percent year over year, the San Francisco Standard reports.

In other parts of the Bay Area, though, the housing supply story is complicated. In Lafayette, a city 20 miles east of San Francisco, the long-running battle over the 315-unit Terraces of Lafayette project appears to have reached the finish line after more than a decade of litigation. The developer, O’Brien Land Co., told SFGATE that it hopes to break ground in 2027. At the same time, the Housing Action Coalition has sued the city, arguing its state-required housing plan relies on unrealistic development sites such as parking lots, churches, and downtown parcels that have no active projects instead of making room for the 2,114 homes Lafayette must plan for by 2031.

State data shows most Bay Area cities remain behind on their housing goals, with neighboring Walnut Creek having hit only 4 percent of its 2031 target.

Make sure your office buildings are stable before you convert them

Cities across the country are experimenting with office-to-residential conversions, and New York — which has bet heavily on turning empty office towers into apartments — hit a real bump in the road this week and showed exactly how hard that bet is to execute. At the former Pfizer headquarters on East 42nd Street, which is being converted into roughly 1,600 apartments, structural columns and floors began to collapse Tuesday, prompting evacuations of the building and several neighboring buildings.

The project is the largest office-to-residential conversion in city history, CNN reports, and architects say Midtown towers are uniquely hard to convert because such large areas of floor space sit far from any window, often causing developers to gut and rebuild entire sections to bring in natural light. The building’s developer told the Wall Street Journal that additional weight added during construction on upper floors likely caused two columns to buckle.

Officials say the damage is contained and a full collapse isn’t structurally possible, but the episode is a pretty vivid illustration of why office conversions, for all the political enthusiasm around them, are often more complicated and riskier than building from scratch.

Kansas City is trying to fix the money problem, not the land problem

A planned $100 million fund from the Mid-America Regional Council and Local Initiatives Support Corporation is testing the idea that Kansas City’s housing shortage is a capital problem, rather than a zoning or land-availability one. As The Beacon reports, the region has built 29,000 fewer housing units than a pre-Great Recession pace would predict, home values are up 97 percent since 2010 against 55 percent income growth, and roughly 69,000 low-income renters in the metro are cost-burdened.

Geoff Jolley, executive director of the Greater Kansas City Local Initiatives Support Corporation, described this as a “capital stack” problem: Banks cover about 70 percent of a typical project, and the rest has to come from expensive equity investors, which is enough to push an otherwise affordable project into the red. The fund would pool philanthropic grants, low-cost bank loans, and equity, then use that capital to provide construction loans, preferred equity, and forward purchase agreements. At least 51 percent of the fund’s overall portfolio has to finance units that are affordable to households at or below 80 percent of area median income.

A similar fund in Cleveland has moved about $11 million across four projects — roughly 200 to 225 units total — over 15 months, which is positive growth but far short of Cleveland’s 58,000-unit shortfall for its lowest-income renters, The Beacon reports.

Organizers of Kansas City’s fund are targeting a fall launch once commitments hit $40 million.

Portland has a homeownership program, but it lacks buyers

Portland City Council is reconsidering income eligibility limits on one of its below-market-rate homeownership programs after too few qualified buyers showed interest. Oregon Public Broadcasting reports the program was designed to boost homeownership among lower-income residents, but a shortage of eligible buyers has left the city looking at whether to loosen the income caps. The tension is a familiar one in affordable-homeownership programs, where price restrictions meant to serve lower earners can leave units sitting empty if the eligible income band is too narrow for the local market.

Meanwhile, young adults are living with their parents

The Wall Street Journal reports that nearly half of American adults under 30 are living with at least one parent as costs rise.
