Republicans don’t believe you’re too sick to work

The Trump administration is worried that too few people will lose their insurance

Nicholas Bagley
Oct 19

Guest post
 
READ IN APP
 

Last year, in the distinctly unbeautiful One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA), Congress added the first-ever federal work requirements to Medicaid, the joint federal-state program that provides health insurance for low-income people and covers 1 in 5 Americans.

If you’re an adult and you’re getting insurance from the government, argued congressional Republicans, you should either be working or have a good reason for not working. Medicaid is “not for 29-year-old males sitting on their couches playing video games,” as House Speaker Mike Johnson, R-La., memorably put it.

And so, Medicaid beneficiaries will now have to report their work hours on a regular basis. If they don’t, they will lose their health insurance.

But if the goal of Medicaid is to provide health insurance to people who are legally eligible for Medicaid, the results of this new requirement will be disastrous. We know that because, during the first Trump administration, Arkansas got a waiver to add work requirements to Medicaid. In just six months, nearly 17,000 Arkansans lost coverage. A court later paused the program.

Ninety-five percent of the target population was eligible for Medicaid. That means they were already working or were exempt from the new work requirements because they were pregnant, seriously sick, caring for a dependent, or eligible for other narrow exceptions. They just hadn’t done the paperwork.

As for Johnson’s hope that gamer bros would go get jobs — employment rates didn’t budge. Nationwide work requirements are all but certain to lead to a similar outcome.

You might have thought that studies like the one from Arkansas would have blunted Republican enthusiasm for work requirements. The truth is closer to the opposite. Studies in hand, Republicans could now be confident that work requirements would strip coverage from millions of people, regardless of their eligibility — which would, in turn, reduce federal Medicaid spending.¹ Those savings went toward covering the Big Beautiful Bill’s huge tax cuts.

Now the Trump administration seems to be worried that the new work requirements won’t kick enough people off Medicaid. In June, it issued a rule to make work requirements even more punitive than the Big Beautiful Bill allows.

The rule is not only flagrantly unlawful, it is also a late-breaking change in position that has left states without the guidance they need to implement a massive new change. As a result, an estimated 1.8 million poor people, or about 1 in 10 Medicaid enrollees, will likely be forced off their health insurance for no good reason at all.

Making work requirements work harder

Congressional Republicans understood that many eligible people would lose Medicaid because of work requirements, but they didn’t want cancer patients to lose their coverage because of a paperwork screw-up. (Less charitably, they didn’t want to be blamed for that.) So they included an escape hatch: an exception for people who are “medically frail.” Those patients wouldn’t have to abide by work requirements to keep their insurance.

But “medically frail” isn’t a scientific or medical term. It’s an invented legal category. So who is exempt? And who isn’t?

Although Congress didn’t say much in OBBBA, it did set out some guidelines. The category would “includ[e]” those Medicaid beneficiaries who are blind, are disabled, have a substance use disorder, or have a “serious or complex medical condition.” If you have one of those conditions, you count as medically frail. You can keep your Medicaid, whatever your work status.

Or, at least, that’s the most natural way to read the law. And, in fact, for many months, that’s how the Trump administration appeared to read it.

In early communications, the Department of Health and Human Services (HHS) told states that they would have a lot of latitude to decide who was medically frail. In response, states began to develop lists of qualifying conditions. Nebraska, for example, issued a 295-page list of medical codes. Someone with heart disease, asthma, Type 2 diabetes, or migraines would count as medically frail if their condition was sufficiently acute.

The expansiveness of lists like Nebraska’s apparently raised alarm bells within the administration, which began to worry that too many exemptions would dilute the requirements. On average, Medicaid beneficiaries are sicker than the average American. Three in four nonelderly beneficiaries have a chronic condition; about one-third have a mental illness.

Not all of these beneficiaries would qualify as medically frail, but many would. A team of Harvard researchers estimated that even a narrower-than-Nebraska definition, if applied nationwide, would exempt between one-fifth and one-third of adult beneficiaries from work requirements.

The Trump administration wasn’t standing for it.

On June 1, HHS issued a rule clamping down on the exemption. (A revised version, issued June 29, further tightened the screws.) No longer would a designation of medical frailty mean that you were categorically exempt from work requirements. Beneficiaries would also have to show that their frailty made them unable to work.

To make this concrete: Imagine you use a wheelchair. Or have bipolar disorder. Or that you have autism. Without question, you have a “physical, intellectual, or developmental disability” that “significantly impairs” your ability “to perform 1 or more activities of daily living.” (That language, which is from the Big Beautiful Bill, echoes part of the definition from the Americans with Disabilities Act.) Since you’re disabled, you should be exempt from the new work requirements.

Under the Trump administration’s new rules, however, you wouldn’t automatically be. According to HHS:

Reading the statute to require automatic classification as medically frail or otherwise having special medical needs based solely on diagnosis or condition would risk sweeping in individuals whose conditions do not significantly impair their functional capacity … The phrase … connotes diminished functional capacity that significantly impairs an individual’s ability to meet ordinary demands. In this context, the relevant demand is meeting the community engagement requirement.

To put it in plain English, you are not automatically exempt just because you are sick or disabled. The Trump administration thinks that would allow too many people to avoid work requirements. So now you also need to show that you can’t hold down a job because of that sickness or disability. (That’s what the rule means when it says that your sickness or disability has to “significantly impair” your ability to meet the “community engagement requirement.”)

The rule, in other words, creates a two-part test. First, are you sick or disabled? Second, is that why you can’t work? That second part is the controversial bit.

Creative interpretation

Here’s the problem for the administration. If you read the OBBBA, the exemption from work requirements is categorical. If you fit in one of the listed categories, you’re exempt. Congress could have included a vocational requirement in defining medical frailty. Conspicuously, it didn’t.

But a vocational requirement, the administration says, aligns better with the law’s purpose. Maybe the Trump administration is right about that. Maybe the congressional Republicans who voted for the bill didn’t expect that the “medical frailty” exception would sweep so broadly. But maybe the administration is wrong.

Maybe congressional Republicans didn’t want to face blowback when sick people lost their insurance because of a paperwork mistake. Or maybe they never thought about the question at all.

In the courts, it won’t make a difference. Interpreting laws to align with their perceived purpose has fallen out of fashion, especially among conservative jurists. What matters is what the law says.

“Statutory prohibitions often go beyond the principal evil to cover reasonably comparable evils,” the Supreme Court said in a 1998 opinion authored by the late Justice Antonin Scalia, “and it is ultimately the provisions of our laws rather than the principal concerns of our legislators by which we are governed.”

Not so fast, says the administration. It claims that Congress gave HHS the power to interpret what counts as medically frail, so a vocational requirement is fair game. The basis for that argument is the portion of the OBBBA that exempts people who are “medically frail or otherwise ha[ve] special medical needs (as defined by the Secretary),” (emphasis added).

That “defined by the Secretary” language does give HHS some discretion, but only within the bounds that Congress specified. And here, Congress specified — there’s no wiggle room here — that medically frail people would “includ[e]” the blind and disabled, as well as those with substance use disorders and serious medical conditions. The Secretary can’t write those people out of the statute just because they might be able to hold down a job. That goes beyond his delegated power.

The administration’s argument might have flown in an earlier era. After the Supreme Court’s 1984 Chevron decision, courts were supposed to defer to a federal agency’s interpretation of ambiguous statutory text, especially in complicated regulatory domains (and Medicaid is surely that).

But the Supreme Court — here again, it was conservative jurists leading the way — overruled Chevron in 2024. The whole point was to stop agencies from evading legal constraints through creative interpretation. That’s precisely what HHS has done here.

No country for Medicaid administrators

Deciding whether someone is so sick that they can’t work isn’t as easy as it sounds. We know that because of long, hard experience in one of our other major social welfare programs.

Social Security Disability Insurance (SSDI) provides monthly payments to people who are disabled and who therefore can’t find gainful work. It’s a huge program, covering over 7 million Americans.

To determine eligibility, the Social Security Administration employs north of 1,000 administrative law judges to adjudicate more than 350,000 disability claims every year. In deciding those cases, the judges don’t just collect extensive information about an applicant’s medical history. They also hear from vocational experts about whether jobs are available for someone with the applicant’s condition.

This is a hugely complex undertaking. Did Congress really mean to require state Medicaid offices to create similarly complex machines to determine whether someone is exempt from work requirements? If so, surely it would have said as much. It might have even provided some money to help cover the costs of building up all that administrative capacity. But there’s not a word in the Big Beautiful Bill about any of that. (Notably, Republicans also allege major fraud in the SSDI system, so it’s not clear why they would want to replicate it.)

Instead, the administration appears to be throwing state Medicaid administrators to the wolves.

Before the June rule, the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS) had been telling states that they could rely on medical claims data to figure out who was medically frail. States were even instructed not to hassle beneficiaries if they could figure it out for themselves.

In the June rule, CMS pulled an about-face. Under its most recent guidance, states can designate some medical conditions as so serious that they count as medically frail. But for other conditions (the dividing line is far from clear), the states will have to chase down “additional information” (exactly what is also unclear).

Remember, work requirements are supposed to roll out in January — less than three months from now and, conveniently for congressional candidates, two months after the midterms.

States are scrambling. Because of all these last-minute changes, states will have almost no time to test their systems and will be at the mercy of the consulting companies that they have hired to implement work requirements, a recent Niskanen Center commentary explained. The rushed rollout of a massive programmatic change for a vital safety-net program is likely to go badly.

In June, a group of blue and purple states sued CMS over the new rule. A hearing is scheduled for later this week, and the judge assigned to the case has already flagged that there are “difficult issues” about “the faithfulness to Congressional intent with which the Secretary performed his task.” A decision is expected soon.

Even if the rule is found to be unlawful, as I expect, the chaos will continue. States will wonder when CMS will issue the next round of rules about medical frailty — and whether those rules, too, will be challenged in court. Beneficiaries will be caught in the chaos as state Medicaid officials struggle to implement vague and ever-changing rules.

None of this was necessary. It was a choice.

It’s hard to resist the conclusion that the administration doesn’t care about the smooth functioning of Medicaid because it doesn’t care about the people who get Medicaid. That’s politically foolish — many low-income Americans vote Republican and will be pissed to learn that their leaders care so little for them.

It’s also completely immoral. If the Trump administration wants to change the law governing medical frailty, it should change the law. In the meantime, it has thrown a wrench into a crucial safety-net program just because it can.
