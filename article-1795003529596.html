What does it mean to be “laser-focused on affordability”?

Policy consensus is harder than agreeing on a slogan.

Matthew Yglesias
Nov 18
∙
Paid
 
READ IN APP
 

As a naturally disputatious person, I was a little bit annoyed by how good the vibes were at last week’s “What’s Next” conference from the Majority Democrats. Annoyed in particular because I suspect that many of the Democrats who believe they’ve reached consensus around ideas like Democrats need to be “laser-focused on affordability” don’t actually agree with each other.

Or, rather, that what they have agreed upon is a slogan rather than a plan.

Words are, after all, tricky things and full of different kinds of ambiguities that clever sloganeers exploit. The way that politics works is that participants in political debates talk about highly technical policy areas while simultaneously trying to communicate with normal people who are not versed in technical jargon.

Doing this effectively is a real skill. But a lot of the skill resides precisely in not being clear about exactly what you mean.

For example, back in John Fetterman’s 2022 campaign, when he was a progressive hero, he used to say that he was determined to “make more shit in America” as part of his solution to inflation. Part of what made this turn of phrase so good is that it’s ambiguous between an abundance-flavored agenda of supply-side reforms and a protectionist agenda of tariffs. If a writer turned in a piece to me that was ambiguous in that way, I’d ask them to try to be clearer about what they’re saying. But in politics, it’s good to be unclear. And while obviously Fetterman has fallen out of favor with the left, the consultants behind him also worked on the Zohran Mamdani, Graham Platner, and Abdul El-Sayed races and continue to be quite skilled and influential, not least in popularizing “affordability” as an alternative to “inflation.”

What’s good about this term, though, is precisely that it’s less clear.

In a campaign speech or ad, it’s optimal to find terms that resonate with large groups of people while being vague in their specific implications. But if you’re trying to reach consensus on a policy agenda, you need to actually work through the disagreement, not find turns of phrase that cleverly paper over it.

Multiple approaches to affordability

If we took the term “affordability” literally, I think it would mean something like what economists call “real income.” In America, for example, cars are broadly affordable — it’s more common for a family to own two cars than to own zero — even though cars are expensive. Incomes are high enough that people can afford them. And, indeed, Americans of 2026 can afford more (and better) cars than they could afford in 1986 or 1956. In India, by contrast, incomes are low, so most people can’t afford cars.

This definition has two virtues. One, I think, is that it makes a lot of sense as a question of technical economics. Would you prefer a world where you earn $250,000 and a burrito costs $20 or a world where you earn $65,000 and a burrito costs $9? Rationally, it’s clearly better to be in the former world.

The other is that in terms of progressive politics, “affordability as real income” lends itself to some obvious policy fixes.

These include reversing Donald Trump’s tax cuts for the rich and using the proceeds to reverse his cuts to SNAP and Medicaid, which would make real incomes higher for more (and more sympathetic) people than it would lower them for. But in addition to welfare state expansion, “affordability as real income” also suggests YIMBYism and other supply-side, pro-growth reforms.

On the merits, I think it would be great if Democrats would coalesce around an affordability agenda that meant rising real incomes. But is this what resonates with voters? The fact is, real median household income was higher in 2024 than it was in 2021 and voters still seemed pretty mad.

Real median household income has fallen in 2026 thanks to the war in Iran, so it’s understandable in those terms why voters are furious about affordability. But real median household income soared to an all-time high in 2025, and my recollection is that voters were already pretty cranky pre-Iran.

I think that voters are probably looking for relief in terms of actual prices and that policymakers almost certainly can’t deliver a general fall in the price level. But they could deliver a rate of inflation that returns us to the customary 2 percent annual rate (or even slightly lower); we just keep not doing that. And America could also have lower interest rates, which don’t show up in inflation statistics but are certainly a price that people see and care about.

My advice to a politician who wanted to deliver that outcome would be to support the Fed hiking rates until inflation returns to target and then to ask Congress to pass deficit reduction bills to help bring interest rates down. Then pair that with supply-side reforms to make sure that real incomes keep rising.

On this interpretation, what the United States needs is essentially an International Monetary Fund structural adjustment program. If Democrats win total control of the government in 2028, they could reverse Trump’s tax cuts for the rich but not his spending cuts. Instead, they could add new cuts to Medicare graft and implement all the Government Accountability Office’s recommended program-integrity stuff. They could kill off ineffective federal programs and try to appoint some kind of commission to look into politically sensitive topics such as the explosion of questionable veterans’ disability claims. This is tough stuff. Not, I think, because the voters would hate it, but because the activists and donors and volunteers who brought the next president to the White House would hate it. They want to see progress on their key issues, not an I.M.F. structural adjustment program.

The other approach to nominal prices, the one that elected officials seem to prefer, is to do stuff that won’t work.

For a while, trying to address housing affordability by cracking down on algorithmic rent-setting was in vogue. This did not succeed anywhere that it was implemented, but there are lots of different things that have prices and lots of different organizations trying to use computers and data to set prices, so there’s a lot of politician whack-a-mole happening around different pricing strategies. This is appealing because, in contrast to anything budgetary, it doesn’t involve tradeoffs. A trillion bucks of deficit reduction is a trillion bucks you can’t use for something else. A crackdown on personalized pricing is free. The problem is it won’t make prices lower, and if you’re in office, people will notice that.

Ideas that don’t work have obvious limits as a policy agenda. On the other hand, ideas that don’t pose tradeoffs have an obvious appeal. But the aversion to tradeoffs raises the question of what it means to “focus” on something.

Focus on affordability … as opposed to what?

One of the huge triumphs of the 2026 midterm campaign is, per this Blue Rose Research polling, that Democrats managed to persuade voters that they — just like the electorate — were very focused on the cost of living.

In the context of a midterm campaign, this mostly comes down to good ads and solid message discipline. It’s also a huge shift from two years ago when Democrats were seen as focused on other stuff. If you’re a campaign consultant, I think this gives you a happy story to tell, which is something like, “hire us and we’ll portray you as laser-focused on affordability and it will work.”

But I think the problem is that the 2024 ads were also good. When you govern, though, you get judged on your governing agenda.

I know a lot of Biden-era Democrats were very frustrated that polls kept showing them as very focused on L.G.B.T.Q. issues when their perception was that they spent a large share of their workweek dealing with economics and almost no time “focused on” L.G.B.T.Q. issues. But that’s not the only way to think about the idea of focus. I would say that Biden-era Democrats were treating L.G.B.T.Q. issues as moral absolutes that were beyond compromise. It’s totally fine and appropriate for people working in politics to hold to certain moral principles in an uncompromising way. But it is also fair for voters to notice which issues you feel that way about and to see them as the focus of your politics.

My sense is that the phrase “laser-focused on affordability” is being used to describe two opposite approaches to this. One group of people thinks that by exclusively talking about affordability, they can make it politically viable to remain completely uncompromising on non-economic issues. Another group thinks Democrats should prioritize affordability by adopting softer views on non-economic issues.

During a campaign, these ideas can seem similar. But when governing, it gets tougher. Suppose there’s a mass shooting next April that gets a lot of media coverage. Will House Democrats pass an assault weapons ban and pass it to the Senate because the vast majority of urban/suburban liberals think the widespread availability of these weapons is morally scandalous, or will Hakeem Jeffries say that he personally supports such a ban but he’s “laser-focused on affordability” and isn’t going to waste the House’s time on a message bill with no chance of being signed into law?

My guess is that most of the people who currently agree that Democrats should be “laser-focused on affordability” would be very frustrated with Jeffries sticking to that pledge the second it means giving up on something they feel strongly about.

2027 vs. 2029

Because Democrats like to discuss internal party strategy in public, it’s sometimes hard to know if people are giving their real thoughts about tactics or if they’re just making the tactically wise choice to say things that are publicly palatable. I’ve heard a bunch of newly elected members of Congress say that Democrats were given a chance by the public but not a strong vote of confidence, and that’s true. I’ve also heard a bunch of newly elected members of Congress say that the public will reward or punish them based on whether they can deliver big relief on the cost front.

That flies in the face of everything we know about the operation of American public opinion. Donald Trump is the president, and this is the Donald Trump economy no matter what Hakeem Jeffries says or does.

I don’t think Democrats should deliberately sabotage the economy, because that would be immoral. But in political terms, there is absolutely nothing wrong with passing a bunch of bills that sound good, and then Trump either signs them or he doesn’t, and you complain about the ones he didn’t sign.

But this completely flips if Democrats win in 2028. In 2029, Democrats badly need to implement policies that actually work.

I saw Rob Flaherty say on Twitter recently that “policy is downstream from message,” which is thinking like a candidate rather than thinking like a governing regime. The Biden administration was aware that they had a big cost-of-living problem on their hands, and they had plenty of smart people working there who had ideas that could help in small ways.

But those people were not empowered to override interest group opposition. So the administration was left with basically messaging gimmicks that would be great for a Senate campaign but didn’t help a sitting president.

The next administration needs to not do that. They need to empower the smart people and prioritize cost-effectiveness over green demands or labor union demands or farmer demands. That’s the laser-like focus. And they need to accept that a lot of the substance of a laser-like focus on costs is going to be a little bit tedious and technical and that the opponents of the policy may not always be bad guys out of central casting. That’s a hard messaging problem, and they’re going to need really smart communications professionals to help them make stuff sound exciting and give advice on what to emphasize and what to downplay.

The point, though, is that not in this Congress and not in the 2028 campaign, but in the actual White House in 2029, the laser-like focus on affordability very suddenly and discontinuously stops being an exercise in showing how much you feel normal people’s pain and becomes an exercise about doing policy analysis correctly.

Needing to care a lot about the merits of technical questions is sort of everyone’s worst nightmare in politics, since it makes it very hard to achieve consensus and politicians are natural people-pleasers. But the focus on affordability ultimately has to mean more than just repeating the slogan, and that means caring about the merits.
