A Funny Thing Happened on the Way to Fascism

The challenge of writing comedy in a pre-satirized world

Ron Charles
Oct 30
 
READ IN APP
 

In a thoughtful new book called The Future of Jokes, Robin Ince asks, “When the world is such a joke, how do you parody it?”

That’s the abiding question for satirists in the Age of Trump. How can a profession based on the comic exaggeration of folly and vice compete with the Home Depot of folly and vice that is this White House? One can hardly repost any official statement without warning readers, “Not the Onion!”

The president is nattering on about election interference while a South African crypto-fascist tries to buy himself a senator for Texas. Our special envoy for peace calls war criminal Vladimir Putin “a humanitarian.” Measles revivalist RFK Jr. claims Arabs “don’t produce science” just a year after Omar Yaghi won a Nobel Prize for chemistry. And all of this ignorance, racism, and cruelty is swelling like a boil on Satan’s ass, which Trump no doubt would admire for its muscled shapeliness.

Writing political satire these days must feel like selling water to fish.

Fortunately, that hasn’t discouraged comedians Ginny Hogan and Carlos Greaves. Their new book, Obey in Advance, offers helpful advice on “how to survive (and thrive) under authoritarianism.”

And I love that the word “thrive” is written on the cover in glitter. Just because we’re all headed for the gulag doesn’t mean we can’t have a little whimsy.

You may have seen Greaves’s Instagram posts in which he plays a newscaster covering the midterms. In one, Argoth the Unrelenting has released an attack ad against Democrat Ashley Schaefer that’s so vile even some Republicans are disavowing it. Illinois state senator Screethee the Unblinking, Bat Demon of the Night Realm, says, “I was all for Argoth’s blood magic hexes on transgender youth, but this ad was in poor taste.”

That’s funny, but it’s just half a demon beyond Vivek Ramaswamy’s attack ad on Amy Acton that exploits the rape she endured as a child at the hands of her stepfather.

The vanishing distance between satire and actual horrors is the challenge Greaves and Hogan confront as they lampoon these moral Lilliputians in Obey in Advance.

“We are not fascists, and if you call us fascists, we will arrest you,” declares Stephen Miller in one of many spot-on parodies.

A White House press release announces that Black Americans may only express their outrage about “centuries of cruelty and exploitation” in a 10-by-20-foot parcel of land off Highway 61 in Nebraska between 11 a.m. and 1 p.m. on the first Tuesday of February.

There’s even a wicked essay here in the voice of your favorite bookselling astronaut, who explains, “‘Democracy Dies in Darkness’ was never a stern word of warning; it’s always been my end goal.”

“Trump wouldn’t exist without this whole ecosystem of people who are supportive of him in different ways,” Hogan tells me. “Some of them are overtly supportive, some of them pretend to find him distasteful, but kind of turn a blind eye, and I think that you can satirize all of those different people in different ways, which enabled us to write an entire book.”

But she readily acknowledges the dilemma.

So many times, she says, events overtake works of parody even before she can finish writing them.

It’s enough to make satirists question the purpose of their work.

“The hypocrisy is so blatant that exposing it doesn’t feel all that useful anymore,” she says. “But I actually think that the most important point of satire right now — and the thing that motivates me to do it — is catharsis for everybody who is living through this.”

She and Greaves are looking to the future, too, imagining satire as not merely subversive but archival. They’re “trying to write a comedic book that feels like a time capsule.”

“Of course, there will be actual historical books,” Hogan says, “but so much of what is happening is just so absurd. We almost thought that a humorous summary could, in many ways, be more accurate.”

It’s impossible to predict how our aquatic descendants might regard tales of today’s political madness. Imagine the historical fiction some gilled Hilary Mantel might write about JD Vance’s craven service to Trump.

Maybe, given enough time, context will fall entirely away from our best political satire, leaving ... what?

This week marks the 300th anniversary of Gulliver’s Travels, inspired by the political feuds and intellectual fashions of early 18th-century England. But Jonathan Swift deliberately camouflaged his contemporary targets. In a splendid new edition from Cambridge University, editor David Womersley argues that Swift took pains to make sure his book was read as “a satire against mankind in general, not against a few individual and local malefactors.”

As Womersley explains, though, a depressing concession lies at the heart of that ecumenical aim. If satirists hope to correct human folly, what does it mean that their jokes keep pricking future generations?

It means, I think, that we’ll always need them.◆

Postmarked

If you were lucky enough to get a letter from Edward Gorey, you knew even before you opened it because the cartoonist couldn’t help illustrating the envelope.

Last year, a delightful collection appeared called From Ted to Tom, featuring full-color reproductions of letters and elaborately illustrated envelopes that “Ted” Gorey sent to Tom Fitzharris in the mid-1970s.

The Edward Gorey House in Yarmouth, Mass., has kept up the tradition for years with its annual All-Ages Envelope Art Contest.

This year’s theme is The Utter Zoo.

“This is no peaceable kingdom,” the curators announce, “this isn’t some petting zoo, this ain’t no disco. We’re talking creatures, either real or imagined, who are tired of being teased and they’ve (literally) got a bone to pick.”

Grab an envelope and get doodling.

Underlined

A periodic feature highlighting a passage from something I’m reading.

While it is true that there is nothing particularly endearing about the physical appearance of a spider (no soulful eyes, no attractive pelt, no winning playfulness), the strength of our aversion to them remains mysterious. Is it because they are ubiquitous and at the same time masters of camouflage? Is it because they so often settle down in sunless spaces and dusty corners and come to be associated with things disgusting or unclean? Or that they hide in plain sight, taking us by surprise only when we look carefully at a wall, a ceiling, or a shower drain? Who has not at some time been ambushed by a spider appearing as if out of nowhere, furtively floating down a gossamer thread? Flies buzz to alert us to their presence; bees and wasps emit a humming sound; butterflies flutter into our field of vision with a burst of color. Spiders are almost like ghosts, materializing before our eyes in uncanny ways.

From “Arachnomania: Spiders and the Cultural Work They Do for Us,” by Maria Tatar (Princeton University Press).◆

Picture This

Get out your Ouija board. Halloween marks the 100th anniversary of Houdini’s death.

If the world’s greatest magician has cast a spell on a young person you know, give them a copy of Houdini’s Library, by Barb Rosenstock.

This enchanting biography for readers ages 4 to 8 highlights the miraculous effect of books. The story goes back to Hungary where little Erik Weisz was the son of a rabbi who owned more than 100 volumes.

When the family emigrated to Wisconsin, those books came along too, but poverty eventually forced the rabbi to sell them off.

Erik — now Ehrie — was working in a New York tie-making factory 60 hours a week when he happened to find a book about magic by the legendary Jean Robert-Houdin in a used bookstore.

Inspired, he changed his name to Harry Houdini and dedicated his life to performing magic — and acquiring every book on magic he could find.

Rosenstock gives an exciting sense of Houdini’s exploding career and astounding feats of escape. There he is getting chained up by police! Nailed into a box! Hanging from a bridge! And Mar Delmar’s extraordinary illustrations — created as three-dimensional sets made from hand-painted paper — look like little theatrical performances.

Everywhere Houdini traveled around the world, he bought more and more books, mainly on magic and spiritualism. At the time of his death, the trap he finally couldn’t escape, he’d assembled a library of some 15,000 volumes and had his own personal librarian.

A significant part of his collection now resides at the Library of Congress (details), but other pieces fell into the hands of private collectors — and could, through the invisible hand of capitalism, fall into yours:

Tomorrow, Potter & Potter will conduct an auction of 100 lots of Houdini-related books, photos, and paraphernalia — including his handcuffs. Current bid: $7,000 (details).◆

This Week’s Poem

You may know Aimee Nezhukumatathil as the author of two essay collections about food and nature, Bite by Bite and World of Wonders, but she was a celebrated poet long before those books appeared.

Her new collection, Night Owl, begins, “I do my finest listening in the dark.” Here between twilight and dawn, she reflects on her life with a mixture of wit and tenderness.

The moment I read this poem, back in May, I prayed to the Great Pumpkin — and the publisher — to let me reprint it for Halloween.

When I Was a Vampire

In fourth grade, I wanted to be a man.
I mean, I wanted to be a vampire
for Halloween and I only knew them
as men — like Dracula with much might

and muscle in his neck. I liked the way
my friends looked at me when I first swore
out loud at school — the wild-pop of delight
and horror when I let loose with it.

My mother didn’t live with us that year.
That and always swearing felt like blood
at the corners of my mouth. I checked out
theater books from the library to learn

how to shade a sunken cheek and studied
biographies of Bela Lugosi. My mother lived
nine hundred miles away that year. I waved
a shopping list full of items my determined

father couldn’t find: spirit gum, liquid latex,
and fake blood — gelled like jam, I told him.
My mother was absent. I wanted to be a vampire.
I learned how to powder my brown self white

with poufs of baby powder all the way up
to my slicked hairline. Plastic fangs for a dollar.
I wore my own crisp, white oxford, a smear
of banana ketchup on the collar. I walked

into school straight as a licorice stick. My mother
missed the Halloween parade. When she saw
the pictures weeks later, she asked my father
how on earth could he do this, let me out

of the house like that? My friends won’t believe
me now, but I was so close to being a man.
When I wore my fangs and clicked them open,
I felt a shiver of blood just under my gums.

From “Night Owl: Poems.” Copyright © 2026 by Aimee Nezhukumatathil. Published by Ecco, 2026. Reprinted with permission of the publisher. All rights reserved.◆

Batty Books

The day we arrived in Coimbra, you could bake pastéis de nata on the sidewalk. So forbidding was the heat last July that this Portuguese city, about 125 miles from Lisbon, felt like it was under a curfew.

But no matter how addled by the temperature, I would not be deterred. For years, I’d heard about the Joanina Library, reputed to be one of the most beautiful libraries in the world. Now I just had to step out of the shade and cross the courtyard at Coimbra University, an expanse of gravel that felt like walking across the Seventh Circle of Hell.

Reader, it was worth it.

The Joanina Library, a Baroque masterpiece commissioned in the early 18th century by King John V, was designed to awe. We didn’t enter what’s called the Noble Floor so much as gape at it: three enormous rooms, each separated by towering arches topped with crown-like cornices.

The lacquered bookshelves, soaring up two stories, are made from intricately carved wood. (Even the “marble” columns are made from wood.) A balcony runs around each room, while hidden staircases sneak down the corners, and ladders slip between the shelves.

The elaborately painted ceilings burst with gold. Honestly, I felt like we were walking through a Fabergé egg designed for Nancy Pearl.

And there on each of the embossed reading tables lies a leather blanket ready to be spread out over the surface at the end of the day.

Because of the bat guano.

Yes, every night bats swoop through the Joanina Library munching on tiny bugs that could harm the ancient volumes.

It’s a symbiotic relationship between bibliophiles and winged creatures that’s been going on here for more than two centuries. None of the librarians looked particularly long in the tooth, but blood-red drapes covered the windows. The light was poor.

Standing outside the library, our tour guide, Joana Job, pointed up to the top of the giant doors and said the bats squeeze through a hole in the corner. I couldn’t see it, but my echolocation is weak.

Apparently, when it rains, bats sometimes congregate at the door like impatient parishioners.

“But these are Coimbrian bats,” Joana noted. “They are very small bats.”

I asked her if she and her friends celebrate Halloween. “We do because of globalization,” she said. “But we really don’t. Instead, we have another holiday that translates roughly into ‘Give me cake.’”

That’s a holiday I could sink my teeth into.◆
