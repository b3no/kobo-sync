Thousands of years of indoor air pollution

The too-late invention of somewhat safe ways to cook and heat with wood

Matthew Yglesias
Aug 20
∙

READ IN APP
 

I’ve been thinking lately about the question of historical realism in dramatic depictions of the past.

And also about chimneys.

Their absence isn’t always obvious in either fictional (such as “The Odyssey”) or fantastical (such as “House of the Dragon”) depictions of ancient life, but chimneys are a more recent invention than you might think.

Scientists today believe that humans have been making fires for at least 400,000 years. The chimney, by contrast, seems to have been invented in England sometime in the 12th century and only slowly gained popularity over the course of several centuries.

Very early humans were presumably making their fires outside. But to survive winter in even temperate climates, you need a heat source inside your shelter. And in the extreme cold of the Ice Age that afflicted the world 20,000 years ago, people most certainly needed shelter.

Archeologists believe that Ice Age humans spent much of their time in natural rock shelters enhanced by deliberate attempts at insulation and that they spent summer nights in dome structures. All of these would have been heated by fire, of course, but that just meant open hearths in the middle of the dome or cave — essentially a campfire, but inside.

And that was the basic state of home heat for many thousands of years.

People understood that you needed some kind of hole in the ceiling or everyone would suffocate and die. But the upshot is still that people were breathing absolutely filthy air all winter long — something you rarely see depicted on screen because it would be ugly and depressing.

And even though this is long gone as a mainstream concern in developed countries, indoor air pollution from open fires continues to be a major problem facing the global poor.

Cooking as a health hazard

Literally millions of people die every year due to respiratory and pulmonary problems caused by inhaling smoke indoors.

These numbers are thankfully falling, mostly due to economic development in Asia. But it’s nonetheless a huge problem.

You can see clearly that indoor air pollution deaths track the availability of electric, gas, or propane cooking facilities rather than reliance on coal, charcoal, wood, crop waste, or dung. Asia still has by far the most deaths but has also seen the biggest decline in recent years.

Recently there’s been a bit of an effort to foment panic about the indoor-air-quality impact of gas stoves versus electric in rich countries. But it’s obvious if you’ve ever seen a wood fire burning that the gap in particulate pollution — the stuff you see in visible smoke — between solid fuels and gas is enormous.

When you look at these stats, though, the good news is that the countries where people still don’t have access to cleaner fuel sources are mostly quite warm.

It’s horrifying to think of the millions of people dying prematurely every year in Africa and South Asia due to all the smoke they’re inhaling just from cooking. In 12th-century Europe you would have had the same cooking needs plus even more wood burned for overnight winter heat.

A chimney doesn’t provide perfect ventilation, as anyone who’s ever sat around a modern hearth can attest. But it’s an enormous step up from letting smoke accumulate around the ceiling and then leak out of a poorly insulated structure.

Wealth and waste

One aspect of this that I find fascinating is that even when chimneys did start to appear, they didn’t exactly take off like wildfire.

That’s partly because while a chimney makes your fire less likely to kill you, it also makes the hearth less effective at its core competency of warming the room. The whole point of the chimney, after all, is to funnel stuff away from you quickly and expel it outside the home. But that stuff is hot! Letting it diffuse through the air in the room means that a higher share of the stored energy in the firewood ends up heating your home. A standard fireplace set into the wall and ventilated by a chimney offers a good amount of radiant heat from the flames and the coals. But the convection heat — hot air and gases — is mostly just going up the chimney.

This convection heat is actually a majority of the total energy of the fire, so from the standpoint of a thrifty person trying to keep warm in conditions of fuel scarcity, the chimney seems like a bad deal.

The chimney upside that persuaded people, slowly but surely over a multi-century span of time, is that chimney use allows for a multi-floor building. The open hearth doesn’t really work in that setting, while a chimney can pipe smoke from a ground floor up out of the building through additional floors.

By the 16th century, English people were using coal rather than wood to heat their homes. And coal was really the killer app for chimneys. Coal made energy abundant enough that wasting heat was less of a problem. But coal requires better airflow than wood to burn, and in this respect chimneys are a big improvement on an open hearth. The hot air rushing up the chimney leaves behind a void that pulls in other air, ensuring a better-oxygenated fire.

In America, meanwhile, we had ample firewood, so in the 18th century Benjamin Franklin and David Rittenhouse started iterating on metal-stove designs that would do a better job than traditional fireplaces of channeling smoke out of the house while radiating heat into it.

In Germany, Scandinavia, and Eastern Europe, by contrast, innovation went in the direction of the masonry stove. There are a variety of national masonry-stove traditions, but the commonalities are twofold: the gases go through a convoluted path before reaching the flue to extract more heat before warm air leaves the house, and the structure is surrounded with a large thermal mass. The idea is that instead of the fire warming the air in the room, it warms the masonry structure itself and then the masonry sheds heat slowly throughout the night.

I went down a YouTube rabbit hole of people extolling the superior virtues of this design compared with the traditional American wood stove. And it is, indeed, much more efficient at translating wood energy into home heat.

The downside is that the mechanism itself is larger, more complicated, and harder to build. It’s also harder to keep clean. I think you should see the less efficient American metal-stove design not as an inferior technology but as a correct response to the Firewood Abundance enjoyed by Americans relative to Europeans — a transatlantic gap in energy efficiency that continues to this day.

Innovating late in the game

Another dimension of fire efficiency is how fully a given piece of wood combusts into gases rather than turning into ash or particulates. One of the reasons why a natural gas or propane stove is so much less polluting than a wood fire is that the combustion is much more complete. Wood is just not as good at this, but, even so, stove design makes a huge difference.

For example, I got a Solo Stove outdoor fire pit earlier this summer. They market these devices as creating “smokeless” fires, which sounds like total bullshit. And of course the fires you build in them are not genuinely smokeless.

But honestly, it comes a lot closer than I thought.

This is because, relative to a traditional campfire built on the ground, this stove creates a steadier flow of air by elevating the wood and having little holes in the bottom of the fire pit.

But the really clever bit is the hollow gap inside the walls of the fire pit and the second ring of holes at the top. This means that not only does the wood itself burn, but the smoke and soot that result from the combustion get hit with a second wave of hot air — creating a secondary burn and ensuring that relatively little smoke and particulate matter ultimately rises out of the pit.

They, of course, sternly warn you not to use your fire pit inside and I, of course, have not. But obviously if you took this technology back in time several hundred years to the period when everyone was heating their homes with chimney-less fires, it would be a huge improvement.

In a sense, then, this is an example of a product category that is the opposite of “ahead of its time.” The first smokeless pits came on the market around 2011 rather than 1,000 years ago when they would have been more broadly useful.

By the same token, the best way to use biomass fuel for cooking is a “rocket stove,” which is based around a relatively narrow tube in the shape of an L or a J. The long, narrow, vertical tube (in effect a chimney) concentrates the heat from the fire and creates a powerful draft. The short horizontal tube pulls in a very concentrated blast of fresh air. This ensures highly complete combustion of the fuel and maximizes the amount of heat energy that goes into the pot or pan on top of the stove. It’s both more economical and cleaner than conventional biomass cooking.

This, again, would have been useful essentially throughout all of human history, but it was invented in Oregon in the 1980s by a team looking to address indoor air pollution in poor countries.

It’s a great design, but by the time of its invention, even a cleaner biomass stove was still really dirty compared with electricity or propane.

But for the vast majority of human history, everybody everywhere was relying on biomass cooking and would have benefited from these innovations. Permaculture weirdos have even perfected the rocket mass heater, which combines principles from the rocket stove and the masonry stove to create the ultimate high-efficiency wood-burning stove — that’s cool, but you can just heat your home with gas or electricity, which are better.

Serious problems with minimal attention

I suppose I should link this back to a timely political controversy, perhaps argue that the history of the chimney goes to show that Abdul El-Sayed should reflect on why he’s doing worse than every other frontline Dem Senate nominee and find some ways to move to the center.

But that doesn’t really make much sense.

In some European cities, especially in Denmark, they rely heavily on “district heating” where instead of having pipelines ship gas to individual home furnaces, the heat itself is piped to homes. If we had that kind of infrastructure, we could capture waste heat from data centers and pipe it into the district heating system to improve the overall efficiency of our energy infrastructure.

Unfortunately, not only is constructing this kind of infrastructure in the United States unlikely, but in practice people probably want to keep data centers far away from houses, which would make repurposing the waste heat less appealing.

I guess the winning linkage is that while the closure of the Strait of Hormuz is annoying Americans via higher gasoline prices, a much more substantively serious problem is that it has induced shortages of liquefied petroleum gas in middle-income countries.

That means lots of people have switched back to biomass cooking at least part time with predictably dire consequences for their respiratory health.

This returns us to where we started, which is that indoor-air-quality issues are well known and well understood in the sense that experts do not dispute their existence, but they’re also highly neglected. The modern attention economy creates incentives to pay attention to things other people are already fighting about and to ignore long-simmering problems — even when they’re directly adjacent to big newsworthy events.

But if you actually care about improving things, it’s important to find time to talk about stuff that isn’t the same thing everyone is already screaming about.
