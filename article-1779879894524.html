Time to freak out about the national debt

The longstanding bill is coming due.

Matthew Yglesias
May 27
∙
Paid
 
READ IN APP
 

Last week, interest rates on long-term U.S. government debt reached their highest levels since before the Great Recession of 2008.

How exactly inflation, interest rates, and the budget deficit relate is unfortunately one of those technical economics questions that experts disagree on. Trying to puzzle through the relationships here seems unlikely to persuade anyone or sell newsletter subscriptions.

Specifics aside, however, everyone does agree that they are in some way intertwined. And so far this year, they’re all moving in the wrong direction more or less in tandem.

The increase in government borrowing costs mechanically pushes up spending, which makes the budget deficit worse. A rising budget deficit generates inflationary pressure. And inflation drives up interest rates since investors demand more compensation.

The proximate cause of last week’s bond market action, in which rates rose sharply, was almost certainly the war in Iran delivering an inflationary impulse to the economy rather than anything that happened on the fiscal side. But it clearly does not help that rich countries all around the world not only borrowed extensively during the pandemic, but have done very little to consolidate their balance sheets since then. Britain is probably the clearest example.

Amidst the meltdown of Keir Starmer’s premiership, the rise of Reform on the right and the Greens on the left, and everything else that’s happening in British politics, nobody’s calling for a new program of fiscal austerity. And you see something similar in France and the United States and many other places: There’s a new populist right that is very focused on immigration and relies on the votes of older, less-educated people, and there’s also an insurgent left calling to expand state spending commitments. Nobody wants to spend political capital making the numbers add up!

But the problem is growing severe, to the point that bond market reactions to policy announcements have become a major factor in British policymaking.

We aren’t quite there yet in the United States, but we’re pretty close, and it’s high time that policymakers start taking this seriously.

And a key point is that while ideological progressives hate the idea of fiscal discipline, Democrats have traditionally benefited from debates over the deficit because those debates tend to highlight their most popular ideas and Republicans’ most unpopular priorities.

Interest rates are becoming a problem

Here in the United States, interest rates are currently 0.55 percentage points higher than the Congressional Budget Office projected in their most recent forecasts. That’s a relatively small number, but it amounts to $2 trillion in additional spending over the next 10 years if rates stay elevated. That’s a lot of extra money in exchange for literally no extra government services.

The joke used to be that the federal budget was an army with an insurance company attached. But since Covid, interest payments have become larger than Medicaid or the Pentagon and are projected to soar ahead of Medicare thanks to elevated interest rates.

But this mechanical projection might even be too optimistic. The extra $2 trillion in spending implies higher budget deficits even without policy change, which could push interest rates even higher.

Higher interest rates for the government also typically translate into higher interest rates for the private sector. I am of the view that the public’s level of negativity about current economic conditions is somewhat irrational and hard to explain based on objective indicators.

But it’s still worth looking for actual problems and trying to solve them.

Compared to pre-Covid conditions, the interest rate you’d pay on a mortgage or an auto loan has gone up a non-trivial amount. That not only burdens potential homebuyers, it depresses new construction. It also tends to deter people with older, cheaper mortgages from moving, even when moving would have other upsides, and that creates deadweight loss for the economy.

The scale of these interest rate problems is not large enough to fully explain the bad vibes, but these are genuine problems and they’ll obviously get worse if we keep cutting taxes in the face of an aging population.

We’re not even picking the low-hanging fruit. Back in January, the Trump administration floated a plan to curb excessive payments to private insurance companies in the Medicare Advantage program — a rare example of a cut to domestic spending that progressives would largely applaud — but after a couple of months of listening to lobbyists, they decided not to do it.

Things like the $1.8 billion slush fund to make payouts to insurrectionists are not the fundamental driver of the deficit, of course, but they don’t help. Nor does starting and then apparently losing a war with Iran that will require extra money to restock our ammunition. We’re also losing hundreds of billions of dollars in tax revenue thanks to Trump’s incredibly misguided move to defund the tax police.

To actually balance the budget would require some painful policy choices. But to at least move the budget in the correct direction could be achieved relatively easily through the application of common sense if you had a political consensus that accepted the premise that moving in the correct direction would be desirable.

Partisan versus ideological interests

As someone who was on the “don’t worry about it” side of the Obama-era deficit wars, I am genuinely losing my mind about how the politics of this flipped around.

Back in 2010 or 2011, basically all the mainstream, conventional, moderate Democratic Party economic-policy wonks were arguing that with inflation and interest rates low, the deficit should be higher. Arrayed against them were elected officials and political operatives insisting that voters want low deficits. Centrist types were especially insistent on that point, but it’s important to recall that Bernie Sanders’s star-making 2010 talking filibuster was driven by his objections to a stimulative deal the White House reached with Mitch McConnell that Sanders felt tilted too far toward G.O.P. tax priorities.

Because the macroeconomic situation changed fundamentally in 2021–22, those of us who care about things like inflation, interest rates, and unemployment have changed our tune. But at this point it’s become hardened conventional wisdom on the Democratic side that deficit politics is a loser.

I think that this is wrong, and the fact that most Democratic Party politicians think it’s wrong reflects a stealth ideological makeover of the party. What is definitely true is that if you accept the premise that the economic-policy goal of the Democratic Party is to transform the United States into a Nordic-scale welfare state, acknowledging deficit concerns makes that harder. The basic issue is that raising taxes is hard, and even when you have a popular tax-policy idea (like taxing a small number of very rich people), there’s a lot of pressure to use the ensuing revenue on middle-class tax cuts. Putting deficit reduction “ahead in line” of welfare state expansion makes that Nordic dream even more difficult.

But is that the right premise?

Both Bill Clinton and Barack Obama governed on the more restrained premise that the economic goal of the Democratic Party is to generate broadly shared economic prosperity. They both enacted policy measures to lift up the poor, they both increased taxes on the wealthiest, and they both centered fiscal discipline in their politics. In the context of these relatively restrained policy goals, a fiscal-policy debate played into the hands of Democrats rather than Republicans.

Conservatives like to argue that the United States has a “spending problem” rather than a “revenue problem.” You can characterize it that way if you want, but the “spending problem” turns out to be a “paying people the Social Security and Medicare benefits they were promised” problem, and defending the existing social safety net is an extremely popular economic-policy objective.

Many on the left blur the lines between the popularity of economic populism like “tax the rich rather than slashing Social Security benefits” (extremely popular) and economic populism like “enact a $6 trillion program of safety net expansion” (most of the individual items are popular but in the aggregate it scares people) or “dramatically reconfigure every aspect of the health care system” (alarming to voters).

If you can drag the conversation back to the deficit, then you get to do the good kind of economic populism where you say, “Yes of course we should tackle wasteful spending, but we also need to make good on our commitments and that means asking for a larger financial contribution from the people who’ve benefited from an incredible generation-long winning streak for the owners of financial capital.”

Of course, plenty of rich people will never support a political party that wants them to pay more, no matter what you say. And that’s fine. A center-left political party is never going to be the natural home for the majority of rich businessmen.

But a center-left political party that’s talking about taxing the rich in the context of a sober-minded and balanced approach to the budget deficit — the way Clinton-era and Obama-era Democrats were — doesn’t need to gratuitously insult successful entrepreneurs. That kind of party can make voters see the risks of turning the economy over to a Republican Party that is heavily focused on the most crass and short-term interests of the people who need the least from the government.
