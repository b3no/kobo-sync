The Story of 7-Year-Old Me, My Nemesis, and a Very Bad Teacher

My latest true story about discovering the worst parts of myself.

Despite writing many personal essays and true stories, this is the story I’ve found the most challenging to write. In fact, I’ve written this story at least three times since my childhood, and scrapped my draft every time. The events are just so weird, the kind of narrative that conjures up statements like, “Wow, truth really is stranger than fiction!” or more realistically, “She obviously made this up.” But I think, thirty years after the events of this story happened, I’ve finally cracked it. I hope.

My public elementary school seemed to revolve around one particular main character—a second grade teacher in her mid-sixties, who went simply by the name “Doc.” She used this name because she was the only teacher to have a doctorate in education. Looking back now as an adult, I realized this must have been irritating as hell for the other teachers, one step less annoying than a Harvard-educated teacher showing up and declaring that everyone must call him Crimson.

Over her many years at the school, Doc had become the figurehead of our school. She was, seemingly, exempt from the required curriculum. She taught niche topics unrelated to a typical second grade syllabus. She took her students on field trips that the other second grade teachers didn’t. She put on a self-written play and puppet show every year, and the other second grade teachers didn’t. She was revered as a sort of quirky maverick who didn’t play by the rules. To have your child in Doc’s class was a gift. But despite all her fun excursions and activities, she was not known for being an “easy” teacher. It was an open secret that first grade teachers did not place their remedial students with Doc. And given that I was a smart-ass seven-year-old, perhaps the only person at school who believed themselves to be more special, smarter, and more of a main character than Doc, I wanted to be in her class. Because she was so unusual, and because I was still reeling from social isolation after I told my first grade class that I was an alien in a skin suit (and they believed me, which is kind of on them) I was under the impression that Doc would see what was special about me—see past the bad parts that everyone else apparently saw, realize how kind and smart and special I was, and bring out the best in me.

The summer after first grade, when I was about to turn seven, I received a letter informing me that she would be my teacher. I cheered as the fictitious Crimson may have upon discovering he was admitted to his alma mater.

On the first day of school, I was determined not only to impress Doc, but to be her favorite. I showed up wearing a makeshift English boarding school uniform that I pieced together from a GAP Kids navy pleated skirt, some dorky sweater vest and a white poplin blouse. Knee socks and a hair bow may or may not have been involved. My heart raced as I stood in line behind the other kids, all of us about to enter the hallowed halls of Doc’s second grade classroom. Finally, it was my turn. I looked up at her, towering over me. I remember her being impossibly tall, but it’s possible my memory is skewed from having been a child and she was just average-sized. She had short, permed brown hair and heavy powdered foundation, high drawn-on brows, and red lips that befitted a Broadway actress.

Finally, I said what I had been practicing all morning: “Twelve times twelve is one hundred and forty-four.”

For some reason I expected Doc to smile, cheer, or do anything to assure me that I was special. Instead, she said, “Oh, great, you’re one of those.”

I quickly learned that “one of those” was a very all-encompassing term, because Doc found most of her students exasperating. Her reputation as a “tough” teacher was, if anything, undersold. She did things in our class that today might put a teacher on the local news. For example, we had two students in our class who came from other countries. They could speak English, but struggled a bit more with grammar and spelling. When one of them made a particularly egregious spelling mistake, Doc asked him to stand on his desk and bend over so she could spank him in front of the class.

It was just a few swats, and I don’t think it actually hurt, but I’m sure it humiliated him. And nothing happened to Doc. It was 1997, and corporal punishment in American schools had been illegal for a while. I don’t know if the boy told his mom. Maybe he did, but she thought this was normal because of a cultural difference. I don’t know if the other kids told their moms either. Knowing me and my big mouth, I’m sure I told my mom, but I’m not sure she believed me because it just so happened that I had a special interest in spanking at the time (sparked by reading old-fashioned books, not personal experience). That summer, I had filled an entire marble notebook with nearly identical stories about “little girls being very bad” and getting spanked, which had my full name on it. I lost this notebook on the camp bus, never to be recovered. I still like to think about who might have found it with the intention of returning it to me, opened it up, read even one page and decided such a deranged child was better off without it.

Doc did many other things that were far more “normal” than the isolated spanking incident. While we were completing an assignment in class, she would loom over each child’s shoulder, stab our paper with her fingernail, and loudly bellow, “WRONG!” When I was in the middle of an art project and accidentally spilled glue on my hands, she accused me of doing it on purpose and dragged me to the class sink to berate me for my carelessness. Even today, my mom tells me that I used to really enjoy school before I entered Doc’s class, reveling in learning things that might make me seem special or smart, but after a year with Doc, my enthusiasm for school never came back.

I was correct in my assumption that Doc would have a favorite student, but it wasn’t me. Doc’s favorite student was a girl named Melissa, the tiniest person I have ever seen, both physically and figuratively. She looked like a skinny, frail Cabbage Patch doll. Her handwriting was so small that it was nearly illegible. She barely spoke, and when she did speak, it sounded like a whistle that could only be heard by kittens. She was notorious for crying easily, and she was still inconsolable over her “long distance boyfriend” who moved to New Jersey after they met in kindergarten. She was fragile, cute and teensy in every possible way, and I hated her for it. Whether it was true or not, I saw myself as a bit of a lumbering, grotesque, masculine ogre, knocking things over without trying, destroying buildings by accident, constantly in the way and cute to nobody. I wanted to be well-behaved, tidy, and neat, but I worried that nobody saw me this way. During a random lesson on Greek mythology (absolutely not required by the school) Doc loudly proclaimed in front of the class that I was like Eris, the “goddess of quarrels.” My parents’ friends also joked about how I would “make a good lawyer” one day, which felt like a very polite way of saying I was acting like a little cunt.

You may wonder why I saw myself as such a lumbering, malevolent disturbance, and I guess I don’t really know. I have never felt adequately small, feminine or pleasant. At some point in childhood, I developed an obsession with the fact that I was a nine-pound newborn, and felt embarrassed that I must have looked monstrous and grotesque compared to the other more delicate, feminine babies. I was once banned from a shoe store at an embarrassingly late age (perhaps nine?) for throwing a tantrum when I was unable to fit my foot into my ideal size metallic pink Mary Jane—a moment of which my mother frequently reminded me whenever I was being difficult, perhaps because she knew how embarrassing it was. But I would not blame my parents for this—yes, they were strict at times (although not in comparison to your average ‘90s parent!) but they were good parents who clearly loved me, even if they did horrendous things like making me clean up my room sometimes. If anything, they did their best to discourage my obsession with being as tiny and girlish as possible. But regardless of why I felt this way about myself, I just…did. It doesn’t really matter why. I did not feel like a little girl. I did not feel delicate and cute. And I especially did not feel like a good girl.

I was surprised that Doc—someone so bold, strong and outspoken—would favor someone like Melissa, the kind of kid who would never have survived to age three in the Victorian era, who might catch a bout of consumption from the wind blowing against her cheek the wrong way. Perhaps that was what Doc liked about Melissa. It should have come as no surprise to me when Doc awarded Melissa the starring role in our under-sea theme puppet show as the Mermaid Princess. Meanwhile, I was given the role of some random fish.

I cried to my parents that night about this blatant favoritism. Melissa was shy, hated being onstage, and nobody could hear anything she said. Why would Doc give her this role, if not only because she was the favorite? She probably didn’t even want it!

But little did I know, things were about to change for me. As it turned out, Doc had a rule that the parents of every child in her class invite her over to their house for dinner. Because she was older, I thought this might be some kind of archaic tradition from the seventies that she had simply refused to drop, but as it turned out, nobody ever did this, except for the practice of “boarding round,” where each family temporarily housed the teacher (However, this was mostly done in rural communities in the 1800s.) My dad’s theory: Doc wanted free food and alcohol.

It was fall, so my mother worked tirelessly on an autumnal feast from the Martha Stewart Living magazine, where my father subscribed her under the pseudonym Itza Goodthing. She finished the dinner with a dessert so intricate and so unnecessary for the occasion that it may have warranted a mental health welfare check: pumpkin spice cake made in pumpkin-shaped molds, covered with a muted pastel orange marzipan and sculpted into pumpkins with intertwining green vines and leaves. I’m still not sure what she was going through at the time, but probably something.

Doc arrived, had dinner with my parents, and apparently drank more than a full bottle of wine. I had a slight idea of what it meant to be drunk, and I realized Doc was being particularly warm and friendly that night. So I did what any child with their eyes on the prize would do—I planned to isolate her and change her mind about the puppet show.

I asked her to come to my room to see my dolls. She followed me, stumbling up the stairs after me. I felt so powerful, and for once, not in that King Kong-Godzilla-monster kind of way. I sat her down next to my American Girl Doll, Samantha Parkington, who was deep into a tea party at a whicker table with Felicity Merriman (just a couple of liberals getting brunch like everything is normal) and explained to her that she should make me the Mermaid Princess before we started rehearsing.

“You said in the description of the character that the Mermaid Princess has a voice like twinkling bells,” I reasoned with her, as she slumped onto the floor. “Melissa’s voice is too quiet for anyone to hear. I know you don’t think I’m dainty and tiny like Melissa but I can do that voice if you let me try.” At this point, I began talking in a breathy, high-pitched voice, which sealed the deal. Doc agreed to make me the Mermaid Princess, and shockingly, was true to her word. At school that Monday, she announced without any explanation that the casting would change, and I was our new Mermaid Princess.

Melissa noticed that I had usurped her. I had been jealous of her for a long time, but finally, she was jealous of me. For some reason, I decided to become her friend. It’s hard to explain, but there is something very exhilarating about gaining the approval of someone you hate. Making friends was hard for me, but I didn’t struggle to make friends with Melissa. Maybe, on some level, she knew I wanted to become her and wear her body as a skin suit (a futile endeavor; it wouldn’t have fit.) I began copying her as religiously as possible, resisting the urge to make my handwriting normal-sized as I tried my hardest to write the roundest, smallest letters I could. I tried to speak more quietly, be more timid, be more feminine. I went over to her house and discovered her treasure trove of horse toys, and immediately developed a special interest in horses.

My parents still maintain that my obsession with horses was entirely manufactured and I didn’t really like them. My dad recalls that I would see a photo of a horse in a book, weakly point to it and say, “Awww,” in a way that read “phoning in the horse obsession.” I will give my parents credit for playing along with what they believed was a fake hobby and buying me countless horse toys over that Christmas. That said, I really did like horses. It’s entirely possible that I only liked them because I wanted to be Melissa, but the passion for horses was genuine. I memorized all the different breeds of horse, wrote stories about horses, and sometimes, regrettably, pretended to be a horse.

Just as I thought I was riding high—success in puppet show casting, friends with my nemesis, Doc’s ascending favorite, all the Breyer horse figurines a girl could want—something horrible happened: I got laryngitis from some kind of virus, which peaked on the day of the puppet show. Doc was furious, and told me that my voice (on the rare occasion anyone could hear it) sounded like an old man who chain-smoked. I wasn’t sure what she expected me to have done differently, but on some level she believed I had done this on purpose. In front of the class, she screamed at me for “ruining the entire show.”

Although Doc said she wished she hadn’t changed the casting, it was too late to change it back, so I had to perform as the Mermaid Princess despite sounding like RFK Jr.

After our curtain call, Doc took the stage and announced that this show was very special, because one amazing student had powered through a terrible illness to be there. I smiled, realizing that perhaps, even if Doc was a bit rough around the edges, she meant well. Maybe she even loved me. At that point, she announced that Melissa was the special, sick student because she had braved her case of very contagious pink eye to be there at the puppet show with everyone. Then, she awarded Melissa with a new toy—a brand new, pink My Little Pony figurine—as the audience (who was no doubt about to come down with their own unique cases of pink eye) clapped.

I had never hated Melissa more. She still thought we were friends, and of course, she had done nothing to me (aside from exposing me to pink eye) so there’s no reason she wouldn’t have thought everything was fine between us. If anything, she had more reason to be angry than I did, because I had stolen her role, and she seemed to genuinely like me. We were still friends, at least in name. I sat next to her on the bus. We played horses together at recess. But every minute I spent with her, all I could think about was how much I hated her and how badly I wanted to be her.

One day, as everyone was getting ready to leave, I quietly pilfered the pony from Melissa’s backpack. I had never stolen anything before, and despite everyone around me viewing me as a troublemaker, I didn’t actually break the rules that often. I had an intense sense of guilt that usually prevented me from doing things that were obviously against the rules. For example, when I was very young—perhaps four—I was playing in my front yard when I spied a crying little boy on the sidewalk past my front gate. I had said hello to him to cheer him up, but was destroyed over the guilt of breaking the “don’t speak to strangers” rule. I eventually confessed this crime to my parents when I was eight, after living with the crushing guilt for four years. Most of the “rules” I broke, either at home or at school, were due to forgetfulness or bad time management, not wilfull disobedience.

True to character, I felt awful about stealing the pony almost immediately after it happened. I wanted to return it, but I was already on the bus home and I didn’t want Melissa to know I was the person who stole it. I didn’t even want the pony anymore. I couldn’t explain, even to myself, why I had done this. I wanted to be Melissa so I could be assured that I was a good girl after all. And everything I did to get closer to her just reinforced my belief that I was bad.

The next day at school, Melissa was distraught over the loss of the pony. She even blamed herself for “losing” it. I couldn’t live with myself anymore. That day at lunch, I claimed to have “found” the pony in the backpack of the class bully, Matt—someone I felt was a believable suspect, who wouldn’t really be bothered this crime being added to his repertoire. Melissa hugged me and thanked me for “saving” the pony, never once stopping to wonder why I was poking around in Matt’s backpack, or how I recovered it so quickly.

But I still yearned to bring chaos and peril upon Melissa, in a way that made me hate myself more, which in turn fueled my hatred for Melissa. I decided to tell Matt that Melissa was “spreading rumors that he stole her pony.” Matt didn’t care, because this probably gave him some degree of bully cred, but his sister found the accusation deeply insulting. So while my guilt prevented me from keeping the stolen pony, I was able to keep my drive for chaos at bay by giving Melissa a new, completely manufactured, enemy—Matt’s sister. I still don’t know what came of this newfound animosity. It’s possible that everyone just forgot about it because it was stupid.

I didn’t know this at the time, but we were the last class of Doc’s career. She retired after the year ended, although a lot of parents suspected that she had been kindly asked to leave. A few other antics contributed to this decision, which were mostly unrelated to Melissa.

First, she had introduced us to three important concepts which parents did not think were appropriate for seven-year-olds: the concept of cancer (her ex-husband died from it; we heard a lot about her ex-husband), the F-word (for the purpose of not saying it, but most of us didn’t even know what it was) and the Holocaust. Her Holocaust lesson was probably the most egregious given that the school was at least 50% Jewish and the parents were hoping to educate their children on the Holocaust privately when they were at a more appropriate age. Doc had presented “Jews versus Nazis” as a sort of fun hide-and-seek game that we played at recess before parents heard about it.

But perhaps the incident that was most directly implicated in Doc’s “retirement” was what she did with a boy named Luke. Luke’s parents were experiencing a tumultuous divorce, and he had developed a bizarre tic which would now be referred to as a traumatic dissociative disorder. Because he struggled with his parents’ screaming at each other, any loud noises would send him into a trance where he would writhe around on the floor and sometimes act like a pre-verbal baby, but when he snapped out of it he wouldn’t remember what he had done. Doc had uncovered this condition by mistake when she dropped a large metal sheet as she was setting up the stage for our class play (which, side note, was self-written and involved many old-fashioned racist tropes, including an Italian kid playing a “smelly Italian boy.”) Instead of offering Luke comfort, Doc had found this fugue state amusing, and as soon as he came out of the trance, she dropped the sheet again and laughed as he crawled on the floor. For the next few weeks, she would purposefully create loud noises at random times to see if she could trigger Luke. Luke’s mother found out about this and had him transferred to another class. Miraculously, Doc stayed employed until the end of the year.

For a long time now, I’ve wondered if perhaps Doc was in the early stages of dementia. Maybe she used to be a very different type of teacher—unique, creative perhaps, but not abusive or sadistic. Or maybe she was always this way, but the norms of the era when she started teaching were different, and she was able to get away with this kind of behavior more easily. As you can see, even in the nineties, she still basically got away with it. Given how outrageous all of this was (and for the sake of relevance, I’ve omitted some stories) I’ve struggled to write about this experience because it’s hard to do so without just listing the litany of increasingly unbelievable things she did to the children.

But I think what sticks with me the most about this experience was how—perhaps unintentionally, perhaps intentionally—Doc brought out the worst in me. The pure id. The part of me that simultaneously viewed myself as the best person in the world and the worst person in the world. The little girl who viewed herself as too big, too manly, too unruly, and too rough, who wanted nothing more than to be dainty, small and good, but who couldn’t stop being bad.
