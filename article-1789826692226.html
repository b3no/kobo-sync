What stake does Airbnb have in the supply game?

A Q&A with Daniel Hornung

Halina Bennet
Sep 19
∙
Paid
 
READ IN APP
 

This week, Airbnb announced its new Housing Accelerator, committing an initial $250 million in funding that it claims can unlock $5 billion in capital over 10 years, with a goal of constructing 750,000 homes that Airbnb says have cleared most regulatory hurdles but lack the financing necessary to make it across the finish line.

The accelerator also includes a regulatory reform component, in which Airbnb will work with organizations fighting for supply-side regulatory reforms — land use, zoning, permitting, impact fees, and building codes — in communities where legislative work is already in motion. It will also award a $1 million innovation prize each year for five years for a nonprofit or start-up developing a particularly innovative technology to make it faster, cheaper, and easier to build housing. Last, they’ve launched a City Index, an annual ranking of cities worldwide based on their housing policies.

The natural question is why Airbnb, a for-profit company overwhelmingly focused on short-term rentals, cares about any of this. To learn more about their thinking, I spoke with Daniel Hornung, director of the Housing Accelerator and previously a deputy director on President Joe Biden’s National Economic Council, where his portfolio included housing issues.

Our conversation has been edited for concision and clarity.

Slow Boring: Walk me through the mechanics of the accelerator. What is the “last dollar” capital doing that other financing in these deals couldn’t?

Daniel Hornung of Airbnb: We’re looking for projects that have their regulatory approvals done, have their entitlements done, have most of the capital lined up, and need some additional capital to get over the finish line. In many instances, what we’re finding is that it’s not just that they need some additional capital, but they need some degree of concessionary capital to get over the finish line — projects where some of the financing was lined up before the market conditions changed, before interest rates went up or construction costs went up, or rent dynamics changed in certain markets, and are ready to be built but for a small additional increment. Some projects need an additional influx of capital to meet affordability requirements around rent and income restrictions.

If you’re a developer for that kind of project, you have to go out and get the last increment from usually an investor trying to make a 15 to 20 percent return. When we can offer a return profile that is much more generous — well under half of that in certain circumstances — we think we can get a bunch of deals through the finish line.

How do you screen the projects stuck on the one-yard line from the ones that were never viable?

We’re at the start of that process.

We announced our first on Monday. We’ve been inundated with interesting project proposals that we’re going to have to go through over the next few weeks and are hoping to move quickly to announce several more over the next several months.

How are you thinking about that evaluation process?

Part of it is the baseline criteria: Is the financing lined up? Are the regulatory approvals lined up? Are we meeting certain loan-to-cost requirements?

But part of what we’re going to try to do is invest in projects that could be a proof of concept for innovative ways to get more housing built that aren’t already a major part of the practice.

Examples we’re excited about: neighborhood revitalization, places where the private market might not have comps and might not have the ability to price things effectively. We can come in and take a bit more of a risk than traditional investors might. The same goes for innovative construction methods or things like public-private partnerships outside the major public subsidy sources, where a mayor or governor is doing something particularly new to get more financing — how can we pair with that to get more state and local action?

We are thinking about what are the projects where our dollars aren’t just like getting a project built, but either sending a signal to the market, to the public sector, or to technologists about ways that you could do this in a more innovative, cost-effective way.

Airbnb isn’t a real estate developer. Why make this kind of investment at all?

Airbnb is at its core a housing company, not a housing investor, but it touches housing in such real ways, and it’s part of the conversation on housing in communities throughout the country. We felt strongly that we wanted to be part of the solution on housing supply in a new way. Airbnb has long been a tool that hosts use to generate income and, in certain circumstances, help pay their rent or their mortgage, but given the importance of housing supply and the salience of housing as a major economic issue, it felt important for us to contribute to this issue in new ways.

That’s the genesis of investing in housing projects and trying to be a bigger player in advocacy related to housing, and the last two components — innovation and data — leverage our unique capacities as a technology company that’s so close to housing to make a difference outside of just investing and advocacy.

When the Housing Accelerator was announced, Airbnb C.E.O. Brian Chesky noted that short-term rental bans haven’t lowered prices where they’ve been tried. But some studies find rent effects in the margin, especially in tight, high-tourism markets. What’s your read?

My read of the literature is that, one, full-time listings are an incredibly small part of the overall housing stock. Two, where there have been bans, we don’t see significant effects at all. We see things like a lot fewer Airbnb listings, but you end up not seeing significant changes in vacancies. You don’t see larger significant changes in rents or home prices. And even if you look at the studies that show some impact — because there are studies that do show that — we’re talking about minuscule impacts compared to the impact that could come from building more housing.

Fundamentally, we are talking about a massive challenge, particularly in the markets that you were talking about, where we’re short a substantial number of homes. The most important thing we can do is build more housing.

Late in the Biden administration, there was a proposal for a national cap on rent increases — a ceiling for large landlords to keep certain tax benefits. Were you supportive of that at the time? It seems at odds with your approach today.

One of the big things we did in the Biden administration, two or three years before that, was put out a Housing Supply Action Plan that was a whole-of-government effort to both use the administrative tools available to us and a set of legislative proposals to increase supply as the major challenge here, alongside things on the demand side, like increasing housing choice vouchers, which would be so central to providing housing stability for the lowest-income families.

The proposal you’re mentioning was about taking away tax benefits from large institutional housing providers that were raising rents above a certain threshold. I think that you could have a debate about the degree to which, in certain emergency circumstances, those kinds of policies should be part of the solution. I think the central thing we should be doing is building more housing, getting the market in better equilibrium, and at the lowest income distribution, increasing the assistance that’s available to truly low-income families in need — that even if the market were in equilibrium, needed additional support.

On the policy side, these land-use fights are intensely local and personal. How do you think private, corporate money and advocacy can actually move them?

I think you’re right. Where we can be helpful in moving the needle politically, either with our resources or with our voice, on measures across the board of regulatory reform issues — not just short-term rental regulation, but regulation that matters for building housing — we want to be helpful.

As we start supporting efforts in this area, we’re going to look particularly at statewide legislation and statewide ballot measures — not exclusively, but as a way to start. It’s hard at the intensely local level for a company like ours to come in and be immediately helpful without doing more credibility building and partnership building over time. So we started this part of the accelerator by supporting groups on the ground that are already doing the work — an acknowledgment that there are folks doing great work pushing for our rules to change, and we wanted to start with those state and local leaders as opposed to starting with something big ourselves on this front.

A year from now, five years from now, what would make the accelerator a success? A disappointment?

We want a real tempo of investments, such that we’re investing in communities throughout the country on a regular basis over the next year or two and can look back and say we’ve deployed the capital we had to build a lot of housing, in a way that can be an example for further projects and for the kind of experimentation we know is important here, that the private market alone won’t do. We want to be investing in good projects so that we get our capital back and can reinvest more.

We want to be making tangible progress in improving the overall conditions that relate to whether housing can get built — the rules, the innovation landscape, the data landscape — because it’s not enough, even if we were able to put an infinite amount of capital, just to build more units and do more deals. There are parts of our overall housing ecosystem that don’t work in a way that allows supply to respond to demand, and we want to play a role in helping to fix that.

Anything else?

There’s no requirement or even encouragement that any of these units be listed on Airbnb. In fact, our first investment, because of the way that deed restriction works for the city of Austin, there are no Airbnbs allowed, period.
