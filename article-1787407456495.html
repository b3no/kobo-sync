Welcome to The Weekly Scroll!

The Weekly Scroll is a once-a-week roundup of the funniest, most interesting, and strangest stories from around the social internet.

The internet is not just a strange, hyperselected web of people hiding from reality; in many ways, the internet is reality. What happens online shapes real life just as real life shapes what happens online.

Every week, I sift through social media so you don’t have to, collecting the most notable trends, memes, and online dramas in one place. Think of it as a guided tour through the more insane parts of the web, with commentary about what it all means.

If you enjoy internet nonsense but don’t have the time to doomscroll for hours every day, you’re in the right place.

This week, we’re talking about conspiracies in the Lindsay Clancy murder trial, surprising piracy trends, Reddit’s disappearance from ChatGPT results, and whether Bama Rush is dead.

Why do young women think Lindsay Clancy is innocent?

There’s no real mystery about who killed Lindsay Clancy’s children.

In January 2023, while struggling with severe postpartum mental illness, Clancy strangled all three of her children while her husband was out running errands. She then attempted to kill herself by jumping out of a second-story window, which left her paralyzed from the waist down. Crucially, Clancy herself does not dispute this story. She has confessed.

Clancy, who is currently standing trial for murder, is instead disputing whether she was responsible. Her defense rests on an insanity plea — that she was suffering from postpartum psychosis and had repeatedly asked for help in the weeks before killing her children — and the trial is to resolve whether her mental state was so altered that she can’t be held criminally responsible.

But, bizarrely, some corners of social media are investigating a different angle to the case — the possibility that Clancy has been framed and didn’t kill her children at all.

Share

As the trial has unfolded, conspiracy theories have flourished among women on TikTok suggesting that Clancy is innocent, that perhaps she was set up and her husband or other shadowy forces are to blame. “Can someone who’s actually watching the case explain to me why you think Lindsay Clancy is guilty?” asks one TikTok with more than 120,000 likes. “He killed the kids … he threw her out the window,” says another. Some accounts accuse the husband of suspicious behavior because he has “dead eyes” or isn’t emotional enough during the trial, or perhaps just because he’s a Gemini.

Again, I want to emphasize that literally everyone agrees that Clancy killed her children, including Clancy, her entire family, and her legal defense team.

Part of what’s going on is that this has become less about the facts of the case and more a debate over whether women are given enough support after birth. Lots of women have struggled with postpartum mental health challenges. Lots of mothers have felt overwhelmed or abandoned by systems that didn’t take care of them when they needed it. And if you believe that Clancy’s actions were the result of postpartum psychosis, it’s not difficult to empathize with a young mother who broke under the strain.

That’s part of why hundreds of pink-clad women rallied outside the courthouse to support her this week, and it’s why a GoFundMe to support her parents has raised more than 1 million dollars.

People struggle with believing that someone is both sympathetic and wrong. Tragic figures can be guilty ones, too, but on the internet, empathy becomes entangled with absolution. So the person you empathize with must be factually innocent.

This instinct is prevalent both on and offline, but given how difficult it can be to communicate nuance online, social media discourse, in particular, privileges morally clean stories. Social media is a fantastic vector for conspiracy theories, and true crime podcasts have convinced a generation of women that there’s always a stunning twist in criminal cases, a solution to be found if only you can put the evidence together in the right way. That’s why so many of the pro-Lindsay TikToks are obsessed with compiling evidence for a case where none of the facts are in dispute.

I often think of conspiracy theories as originating from a moral commitment, not from factual uncertainty. Conspiracies can be a form of value expression. Serial was about whether the criminal justice system was unfair to brown men. The O.J. Simpson case was a proxy for police racism.

Many women see Clancy’s struggles as their own, begin with the conviction that she’s a tragic figure and a victim in this story, and therefore they can’t accept a world where she killed her children. And whatever else you may say about the case, they’re right in believing this is a tragedy.

Piracy trends

Online piracy was around before the internet even truly existed — users were swapping files back on bulletin board systems as far back as the 1980s.¹ Since then, piracy has gone through several cycles of boom and bust.

In the 2000s, music piracy ran rampant. It was so prevalent that some estimates showed 95% of music downloads were illegal, and at one point BitTorrent made up one-third of all internet traffic!

To its credit, the music industry eventually adapted and made it easier to legally pay for music. Piracy analysis firm MUSO found that music piracy entered a steep decline in the late 2010s, likely due to the ubiquity of simple options like Spotify and Apple Music.

Does that mean piracy is dead? A new report from MUSO suggests it’s not that simple. 2025 saw large, sustained drops in every major category of piracy with one major exception: publishing.²

Publishing is the only sector MUSO tracks where piracy grew in 2024, and in 2025, it was the only category to not see a double-digit decline. Music piracy is comparatively dead, falling a shocking 40% year over year from an already low baseline.

What’s driving the persistence of publishing piracy? Manga. According to MUSO, nine of the top 10 publishing piracy sites are manga-specific platforms, and manga accounts for 73% of all publishing piracy. It’s a sign of the medium’s persistent popularity, but perhaps also of problems with the manga industry’s digital distribution strategy.

To a first approximation, platforms like Spotify and Apple Music have all the music. You don’t need to worry about where you’ll find a particular song or artist; it’s right there for one simple monthly fee. That’s part of why music piracy is so rare these days.

By contrast, there are literally hundreds of manga-reader apps, and more than a dozen major digital manga platforms just for the English-speaking North American market. Each of these platforms has exclusives — Fullmetal Alchemist on Square Enix Manga UP!, Attack on Titan on K MANGA by Kodansha, One Piece on Shonen Jump, etc. If you want to follow them all legally, you need to juggle a large number of subscriptions and constantly switch between apps.

It’s no accident that two of the largest categories of piracy (manga publishing and television) share the feature of content splintered across dozens of services. Piracy is driven by convenience just as often as it’s driven by cost, so the least convenient industries experience the most piracy.

Reddit is disappearing from ChatGPT

Here’s an odd piece of news: ChatGPT has all but stopped citing Reddit in the last few weeks.

ChatGPT Reddit citations fall off a cliff
Percent of ChatGPT citations sourcing Reddit posts

Reddit used to make up around 4% of all citations in ChatGPT results, but that number has fallen to around 0.5%.

It’s hard to say for certain why this is happening now, but one clue might be a relatively quiet announcement Reddit made at the end of June — that it would soon require users to log in to use “old.reddit.com.” This version of Reddit allows users to read a text-rich version of the site that’s similar to how it looked a decade ago. Because it’s a simpler layout, it’s also easier for machines to read.

A Reddit admin said that the logged-out Old Reddit experience was a “significant source of abusive scraping and automated traffic on the platform.” The move seems aimed at AI bots, and Reddit’s placement in AI citations may have been a casualty of trying to protect Reddit’s data from being scraped.

Last week, we talked briefly about how the end of Google Search traffic has been a disaster for smaller, independent businesses. It seems like the same unreliability may apply to traffic coming from AI citations — the lesson here may be that if you don’t own the platform, you can never truly depend on traffic from external sources.

Is Bama Rush dead?

It’s August, which means college students are heading back to school. And that means it’s time for another year of #RushTok.

If you’re unfamiliar with sorority rush, it’s the process where incoming college freshman women meet different sororities in a series of preplanned events, while both the sororities and their potential new members evaluate whether they’re a good fit for each other. The process culminates in “bid day,” where the women learn which sororities have extended them invitations to join. And #RushTok is the annual spectacle where these young women document their rush experience, while the sororities themselves post choreographed dances and videos to look as impressive as possible for those new recruits.

#RushTok first popped up in 2020, but it truly took off in 2023 when the documentary Bama Rush debuted and commentators like Anne Helen Petersen began documenting the young women going through the process.

But this year, something feels different. Petersen noted the vibe shift on her blog, Culture Study:

“In 2023 and ‘24, once I fine-tuned my TikTok algorithm to focus on BamaRush, the OOTDs [outfits of the day] would come streaming in. They were largely from out-of-state women, many of them dripping in the accoutrements they thought would guarantee acceptance, but there were still dozens of them. This year, I had to search.”

Petersen theorized that, as the phenomenon grew beyond the most prestigious University of Alabama “Old Row” sororities to other schools and chapters, the sheen wore off. Sororities and fraternities are about exclusivity. The whole spectacle of bid day is about watching the matching process reproduce a caste system with the coolest/hottest/funniest people getting matched to the coolest/hottest/funniest chapters.

Once a marker of exclusivity, #RushTok became something that anyone at any chapter was a part of. Potential new members who promoted themselves on social media were seen as passé, too gaudy to join the elite social club. As a result, it’s on its way out.

Petersen noted that many women now seem to use RushTok as a content strategy, not as a way of getting a bid, even dropping out of Rush once their social media star has risen.

What started as Bama Rush is no longer contained to Alabama, and it no longer really captures the experience of rushing a Southern sorority. Instead, it’s a strange simulacrum of “Bama Rush.” And so it’s completely understandable when Petersen proclaims that “Bama RushTok is over.”
