The Hotness Landlords Are Here To Collect The Rent

How dare Audrey Hobert be famous without looking like an influencer.

A while ago, I wrote about how the term “the male gaze” is frequently used incorrectly to describe anything that men find attractive, including individual heterosexual women attempting to attract a partner. The correct usage of the term is that media (including media not intended to be sexually arousing) is created with the assumption that heterosexual men and their desires are the default, and women (sex objects) must be beautiful to justify their existence, no matter the context.

This might sound like a wacky woke feminist conspiracy theory, until you see the way people (including, I should point out, women themselves, because they are not immune!) have been talking about the pop star, Audrey Hobert, who has committed the egregious sin of failing to be pretty enough (in their opinion, anyway) to justify being seen.

I don’t spend a lot of time thinking about pop musicians, or really any celebrities at all, but a few weeks ago I was introduced to Audrey Hobert via a clip of a music video for her song, “Bowling Alley,” on Twitter. The main reason I saw it at all was because someone was complaining about it sucking, about her being cringe, and most importantly, about her being too unattractive to have the audacity to appear on our feeds (like, cool it buddy, I saw a literal open anus on my feed the other day.) And, thanks to a clip of Hobert’s Tiny Desk performance, she has been the trending topic now for several days, specifically for being so unspeakably ugly that people cannot believe she is being “forced” on them, via the videos they keep clicking and engaging with.

In case you’re wondering, this is the woman who we are supposed to believe is our modern-day Quasimodo:

If you’re looking at her and thinking, “Uh…isn’t she just a normal, if not pretty cute, young woman?” you would be correct! But from the looks of Twitter—far beyond the usual suspects of groypers and open woman-haters, who are actually pretty absent from discussions of pop singers—you’d think she was the subject of a documentary about the bravery of living with an ass for a face:

The (fairly ridiculous) looks-based critiques about Audrey Hobert have been happening for ages now, but this particular round of discourse was kicked off by this tweet by an “anti-bullying advocate” which says bluntly what many others are saying less directly: female creatives, no matter what their art form, must look like Instagram models to earn the right to be seen.

She later doubled down and asserted that Audrey Hobert is actually being catapulted to stardom because of “ugly privilege,” because an attractive woman would never be allowed to make such terrible music. (I only agree with this in the sense that many people are discovering her music via viral tweets insulting her appearance…but in that case, the haters only have themselves to blame.)

Despite the fact that I spent all my teen years writing and performing my own music (which, I’m aware, was no doubt very cringe) I am about as far from a music snob as you can get. If you enjoy music, it’s good music. I do not see the point of labeling any type of music a “guilty pleasure.” I once listened to The Rocky Road to Dublin fifty times in a row because I was having fun. And I like Audrey Hobert’s music! I’m sure a lot of you are thinking, “Okay, yeah, you would, she makes music for basic white horse girls who aren’t remotely sardonic or nihilistic, and how dare they,” but it actually doesn’t matter if you consider her audience worthy of enjoying something. For better or worse, a good amount of people genuinely enjoy her music and relate to it. (She also co-wrote some of Gracie Abrams’ biggest hits, so clearly “ugly privilege” cannot explain her success.)

I don’t particularly care if some people aren’t fans of Audrey Hobert’s music. Variety is the spice of life, and while I may be a loser, I am not yet at the echelon of loser required to yell at people over their taste in music. The problem I have is that, while people claim it’s “just about the music sucking,” the vast majority of Audrey Hobert related ire I’ve seen since I first became aware of her existence is about her appearance, and it’s often paired with a strange degree of vitriol that wouldn’t even make sense in the context of a truly hideous person, let alone someone who just doesn’t look like an influencer.

In 2006, journalist Erin McKean wrote that “prettiness is not the rent you pay to take up a space marked ‘female.’” Most women would agree with this statement, and yet, a great deal of people who are incredulous about Audrey Hobert’s success despite being unaccountably hideous, are women, and not the Pearl Davis “based antifeminist” types. They have, of course, internalized the idea of the male gaze, but for whatever reason aren’t recognizing it (is it because she’s otherwise “privileged?”)

There are two sub-complaints to the “Audrey Hobert is ugly” drama. One is that “out of nowhere,” Audrey Hobert has been forced on us, and presumably there is some kind of shadowy force behind this (has anyone asked THE JEWS what they’re up to??) Of course, “forced on us,” really means that their X feeds have become the Hobert Hour, and this is usually because they engaged with a video about Audrey Hobert. Many such cases.

Second, people are mad that she’s a “nepo baby.” Big asterisk here- I am reluctant to call anyone a nepo baby if they are more famous than their parents, whose names you don’t know. Audrey Hobert’s dad is a sitcom writer, who is likely responsible for her former job writing for a sitcom, but could not possibly be responsible—at least not solely—for her current degree of success. But even if he were, Audrey Hobert’s music is quite literally indistinguishable (as in, she wrote it) from that of Gracie Abrams, a more notorious nepo baby whose dad is more famous than she is, and I do not see fifty million nasty comments about Gracie Abrams being an “unseasoned pilgrim not meant to be a star” every day. Of course, plenty of people have been up Gracie Abrams’ ass for nepotism too, but the degree of nastiness doesn’t even come close to what people say about Audrey Hobert.

You can spin it however you want, but people are mad that a woman is successful and famous but, in their minds, unable to pay the “prettiness rent.”

I couldn’t help but be reminded of the type of criticism leveled at Lena Dunham in the 2010s which she now admits caused her intense mental anguish. Again, people claimed they “just didn’t like her” or thought she had no talent (you’ll have a hard time convincing me that Girls was a bad show or that Lena Dunham is a bad writer, but go ahead and try!) Lena Dunham also faced outsized accusations of being a nepo baby, despite the fact that the only “nepotism” from which she benefited was that her parents (whose names and works you probably didn’t know until after she got famous) helped to fund her low-budget indie film Tiny Furniture. The film caught Judd Apatow’s eyes, which kickstarted her career, but the story has been inaccurately retold as her parents introducing her to Judd Apatow.

Now, Lena Dunham is a more controversial presence than Audrey Hobert, but the criticism feels strikingly similar: how dare this woman, who doesn’t fit my ideals of beauty but who would be completely unremarkable if sitting across from me on a subway, dare to exist in front of me without being Hot Enough? Clearly, someone else is propping her up, because she doesn’t deserve this! It’s a different version of “she probably slept to the top,” except perversely, the accusation is that nobody could have wanted to sleep with her, so clearly something else was afoot.

Some people, who otherwise do not consider themselves misogynistic, have tried to rationalize the Audrey Hobert lookism by saying that she’s making the “wrong kind of music” for her face, and that if she made alternative or indie music, her looks would pose no problem. I feel like I shouldn’t even have to address this point, but as many of you have gleaned from some of my personal essays, I’m a glutton for punishment: it’s beyond ridiculous to say that a creator should only produce content that “matches their face,” especially given that music is typically, uh, listened to, and only when viewing a music video or live performance would a musician’s face come into it at all.

But Audrey Hobert does not need reminding that she’s “ugly.” She has written and spoken extensively about her face, almost as if she’s famous for having some kind of horrific deformity. In a sense, it has become part of her brand—you can tell from how she presents herself that she is leaning into looking unglamorous, and not aspiring to resemble Madison Beer. Her song “Wet Hair” includes a monologue all about being rated a “3” by her peers in middle school, and about feeling ugly and unwanted. A great deal of her songs are explicitly about being ugly—in her song “Phoebe,” she refers to herself as having a “fucked up face,” and ultimately accepting herself as she is. In my favorite Audrey Hobert song, “Thirst Trap,” she sings about taking hundreds of sexy selfies but looking bad in all of them. If you had only listened to this content and never actually seen her, you might be tempted to imagine her as a literal blobfish with legs and would be shocked to discover a naturally blonde, tall, slim white woman.

I refuse to enable the delusion that if Audrey Hobert worked in your office, you’d think she was anything other than pleasant-looking. Women who look like Audrey Hobert have their weddings announced in the papers every day! And yet, this heavy focus on her “fucked up face” seems validated by the denizens of Twitter, who can’t seem to understand why she’s allowed to be famous without rising to their definition of hotness.

But this all reminds me of something else, and I implore you to bear with me: British TV and movies.

I’ve written before that British media is partially superior to American media because of their reliance on average-looking actors. This isn’t because I’m opposed to watching beautiful people onscreen, but rather because, per my earlier article, if I’m watching a show about a tenured female police officer in Northern England with a thick Yorkshire accent and a grandchild, it would be a bit jarring if she looked like Emily Ratajkowski.

Thankfully, Happy Valley starred Sarah Lancashire:

I would not particularly enjoy a show about a supermodel who was unaccountably played by Sarah Lancashire, but last I checked, there isn’t a terribly high risk of any of us encountering such a show.

See also, Olivia Colman playing the “office cute” love interest in Peep Show (and in case you’re wondering, yes, she also played the mother of the “boy with an arse for a face.”)

Neither of these women are ugly either—when a young Olivia Colman was presented as the object of desire in Peep Show, it was completely believable. Men date and marry women like Olivia Colman every day, and the point was never that she was the most beautiful woman on Earth but that she was the cute coworker. She was perfect for that role, and it would have been weird if Sophie the loan manager had been played by a young Angelina Jolie. The same is, of course, true for the unremarkable-looking male actors on Peep Show, but unremarkable-looking men don’t seem to be under the microscope quite so much in the first place.

Looking like a regular person—even a cute regular person—can be a real asset in an industry full of people who have been prescribed identical influencer faces and Kardashian bodies. That doesn’t mean anyone will be able to “coast” on looking average, only that, “How could she be famous when she isn’t even hot?!” is not a reasonable question, unless the fame in question is related to a beauty pageant or Sports Illustrated cover.

Audrey Hobert is not benefiting from “ugly privilege,” but is, basically, acting as the young Olivia Colman of pop music. Olivia Colman, like Audrey Hobert, is good at what she does, but the fact that she looks like a normal person—not hideous, not grotesque, just normal—makes it refreshing to see her as opposed to another petrified middle-aged actress who is unable to move her eyebrows. Audrey Hobert is creating music that many women can relate to, and while I know every woman on Twitter identifies as a Mean Hot Girl, I refuse to believe women don’t look at Audrey Hobert and find her relatable too. Who amongst us hasn’t tried to take a good selfie but eventually shrugged off the quest because we looked unrecognizably awful in all of them? (Just me and Audrey Hobert? Okay.) Audrey Hobert’s song “Sue Me” is marked by a chorus that consists of solely of the line, “Sue me, I want to be wanted,” and cringe as some people may find this, there is a reason so many people like it—many women want to be wanted, and don’t see themselves as particularly desirable. When most pop music is about already being desirable and hot, perhaps it’s nice to listen to a pop star who actually knows what it’s like not to feel wanted? Yes, I’m aware plenty of flawless-looking pop stars have sung about their own insecurities, and I’m sure they have them, but these songs are a lot more believable and heartfelt when you actually trust that the person singing them has been rated a “3” by a middle school classmate. (Even if, as I am sure, plenty of men would be into her today.)

You do not have to like Audrey Hobert or her music, any more than you have to enjoy watching Girls or reading Famesick. But ask yourself for a moment if these songs and TV shows truly suck so badly that they make you angry, or if you’re just mad that a woman isn’t paying her prettiness rent.
