As one housing reform works in Texas, another in Colorado hits a budgetary hitch

What happens to the push for more housing when the budget dries up?

Halina Bennet
Aug 26
∙
Paid
 
READ IN APP
 

A bill Texas passed last year is already proving its effectiveness at changing outcomes on the ground, according to the Texas Tribune. House Bill 24 took the wind out of the sails of a Jim Crow-era state law that had previously allowed neighboring property owners who collected enough signatures opposing a project to require a city council supermajority to approve it. Under that rule, the threat of petition was often enough to deter developers before a project was even proposed.

“It became an implicit threat,” Mercatus Center researcher Salim Furth told the Tribune.

HB 24, the new law, requires just a simple majority to override a petition, and the Tribune found evidence that it has already removed friction from development.

One such case is the South Austin Christian Church, a shrinking congregation with a property of just shy of an acre in Austin. The church wants to sell its land to a developer for a 64-unit project that is 100 percent income restricted, with more than half of the units reserved for residents making 50 percent or less of area median income. Twenty-nine neighboring property owners filed a petition opposing the project, which under the old law would have killed the development or forced a supermajority. Now, under the new law, its legal effect was negligible, and the council approved it unanimously, despite the neighborhood protest.

The Tribune does offer the caveat that Austin’s council has had a pro-housing supermajority for years, so there is some disagreement about whether the new law truly had a deciding impact on this particular outcome or just set up guardrails against a potentially less friendly council in the future.

Regardless, Texas lawmakers are embracing the push: The Tribune reports that Governor Greg Abbott wants state lawmakers to go further on housing-friendly legislation in the upcoming session.

Reform … not working

Denver’s “Single Family Plus” accessory dwelling unit program, on the other hand, is a pro-housing experiment that’s facing more friction where the rubber hits the road, Westword reports. The program was launched in 2019 in west Denver to give financial and technical support to homeowners who want to build A.D.U.s provided that they will rent them long term at lower-income rent rates. Colorado in 2024 legalized A.D.U.s in every residential zone, but Westword reports that Denver’s $200 million budget deficit is now squeezing the subsidy program that makes the housing feasible for homeowners.

In the Single Family Plus program’s five years in operation, its $1.64 million budget has helped build 26 A.D.U.s that rent for an average of $1,200 per month. The program originally aimed to create 28 units by the end of 2026 but expanded to 44 after A.D.U. legalization. The city is proposing to cut the remaining committed funds from $890,000 to $640,000, which would effectively halt new applications by the first quarter of next year. Westword reports that the amendment of the current contract will be reviewed by the Denver Department of Housing Stability, which operates the program, before it goes to the city council in the fall.

Separately, at the same time the state was passing its A.D.U. legalization, it also committed $8 million statewide to help A.D.U.-supportive jurisdictions. Denver is not considered A.D.U.-supportive, Westword reports, so the program’s lending partners are blocked from accessing that funding, losing an additional $18,000 to $25,000 per project.

Reform needed?

A ProPublica investigation found that Oregon has spent an unprecedented sum of money on building its affordable housing stock over the last five years, but because of a carve-out in state law, the public is kept from seeing where those dollars go. The state has funneled $1.4 billion to developers for subsidized housing, and over those five years, the cost of building each unit has doubled, with dozens of future projects lined up to receive almost an additional billion in state funding.

Because of the total absence of transparency, ProPublica writes, it’s impossible for economists, journalists, or researchers to analyze the investment meant to help ameliorate the crippling housing crisis that has resulted in one of the highest rates of homelessness in the country.

The investigative newsroom could not find anyone in Oregon who would give a compelling reason for the secrecy and cited examples in comparable, competitive markets like California and Washington, where financial information about housing projects has been repeatedly released publicly, and where making that information public has not hindered development.

Local policy and development

Omaha officials on Monday announced the first four housing projects funded through the city’s $40 million Urban Core Housing Fund, which was launched last year in an effort to create nearly 1,900 subsidized and workforce housing units. The projects are slated to build or renovate 466 homes, including the renovation of a vacant 225-unit apartment complex. So far, the fund has committed nearly $5.94 million in low-interest loans, with 290 units subject to long-term affordability requirements and more than $34 million remaining for future projects.

The California Senate passed Assembly Bill 2128 on Tuesday, a piece of legislation meant to shield low-income residents who receive federal housing assistance from new Department of Housing and Urban Development work requirements and time limits on their housing benefits. The bill prevents California housing authorities from enforcing these federal restrictions. Supporters argue that the new rules have the potential to destabilize housing for vulnerable residents and result in increased homelessness, the People’s Vanguard of Davis reports. Opponents of the legislation say it undermines federal efforts to encourage employment and self-sufficiency. The bill now moves back to the California Assembly for a vote on the Senate amendments before being sent to Governor Gavin Newsom.

In New York City, Mayor Zohran Mamdani announced on Tuesday that the city’s Housing Court will launch a process to fast-track the most urgent cases, including those involving “vacate orders, immediately hazardous building-wide conditions or elevator outages, and Article 7A proceedings in which the city or tenants seek the appointment of third-party building management because of landlord neglect.” The plan aims to assign eligible cases a judge the same day they are filed and cut down on delays as part of the mayor’s broader Block by Block housing agenda, which also includes building goals and increased funding for tenant legal representation.

Madison, Wis., churches are increasingly redeveloping aging or underused buildings into housing as congregations shrink and housing needs grow. Projects underway aim to add hundreds of units of housing while preserving services such as food pantries, behavioral health programs, and community gathering spaces, the Cap Times reports.

Casper, the second-largest city in Wyoming at about 60,000 people, is attempting to address its own housing shortage with a planned project to provide 24 permanently affordable rental units in the form of poured-concrete domes. Wells Fargo has committed $1 million for the project, which will also train local students in construction, HousingWire reports. Developers hope the approach will offer a model for other rural communities facing affordability challenges — as kit homes from Sears once did in the same Western city.
