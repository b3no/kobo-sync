Is that Hachette knocking?

A small announcement with big implications for Canadian publishing

Interesting story in Publishers Weekly on September 29. Hachette Livre, one of the Big Five multinational publishers, announced a shuffle of its English-language executive suite. David Shelley will be running things at Hachette UK and Hachette Book Group (US). He reports to the head office in Paris and will work on “new markets, global synergies, and M&A, among other areas.”

I forced myself to read on and learned that Shelley, who split his time between London and New York, will be moving back to London. He has appointed Ben Sevier as CEO of Hachette Book Group (US). He promoted Richard Kitson to president and chief operating officer for global English language at Hachette.

I was just about to doze off when I noticed this: “Topping the structural changes is the formation of a new HBG publishing program in Canada that will be overseen by Sally Kim, one of Shelley’s earliest hires as president and publisher of Little, Brown,” a Hachette imprint. The announcement said that Kim’s mandate is to “discover and publish exciting Canadian writers.” Shelley was quoted as saying "I am also excited about our move into the Canadian market led by Sally.”

At the moment, only three of the Big Five—Bertelsmann’s Penguin Random House, NewsCorp’s HarperCollins, and KKR’s Simon & Schuster—are operating in Canada, with Lagardère’s Hachette and Holtzbrinck’s Macmillan merely distributing their US and international books here rather than originating new books from Canada. The announcement didn’t mention how extensive Hachette’s Canadian publishing program might be, or what imprint(s) it might involve, or who will be doing the acquiring on the company’s behalf. But news that a fourth member of the Big Five is making a move into our market is indeed exciting. Not least because, as far as I can tell, Canadian law would not permit it.

Canada’s Revised Foreign Investment Policy in Book Publishing and Distribution (1992), administered under the Investment Canada Act, says explicitly: “Foreign investment in new business enterprises will be limited to Canadian-controlled joint ventures.”

So Hachette cannot simply set up Hachette Canada Publishing in Toronto or Montreal, hire Canadian editors, acquire Canadian books, and operate a conventional Canadian publishing house controlled by Hachette.

The policy also generally prohibits a foreign company from acquiring a Canadian-controlled publisher, except in extraordinary circumstances involving financial distress and a full opportunity for Canadians to buy it.

Maybe Sally Kim is planning to run Hachette Canada from New York? The rules only apply to foreign-controlled businesses “physically located in Canada.” Is that the workaround? It would be hard to square with Hachette’s stated intention to form “a new HBG publishing program in Canada,” but you never know.

Maybe Hachette is going to argue that because it already has a sales and distribution branch plant in Canada, it is exempt from the prohibitions on foreign ownership of a Canadian publishing firm. Investment Canada has a loophole permitting existing businesses to expand or reorganize without being considered new businesses. I don’t think it would work—the book policy understands the distinction between distribution and publishing and would not consider the latter to be a mere extension of the former. Two years ago, when Vivendi had temporarily become majority owner of Hachette, Heritage Canada reviewed the transaction and explicitly defined the relevant Canadian assets as distribution and marketing businesses, not book publishers.

Perhaps Hachette has done its homework and plans to follow the Simon & Schuster playbook? HarperCollins and Penguin Random House had active publishing businesses in Canada before the book policy came into effect decades ago, so they were grandfathered. Simon & Schuster wasn’t. Its first step into Canada was the acquisition of a distribution business, Distican, in 2002. S&S agreed, as a condition of the transaction, that it would restrict itself to sales, marketing, and distribution—it would not operate a publishing program.

But S&S wasn’t happy being a distributor. That’s dull work compared to publishing. So it spent years expensively lobbying the federal government to have its prohibition against publishing removed. It succeeded in 2013 by artful means.

S&S was furiously lobbying to win the right to publish books in Canada amid a federal review of foreign investment policy. To improve its odds of success, it laid out a ridiculous sum of money to outbid Penguin Random House Canada for Prime Minister Stephen Harper’s earnest little hockey history book, A Great Game. The deal was negotiated by S&S’s Canadian office but contracted through New York because S&S Canada couldn’t yet publish a book in Canada.

S&S’s acquisition of Harper’s book was announced February 7, 2013. On May 29, 2013, Heritage Canada, quite aware that S&S had acquired the Canadian prime minister’s hockey book and was prepared to publish it out of New York, cleared S&S Canada to begin publishing here.

Heritage Canada’s explanation of its decision was rather weak. The book policy test for approving a company like S&S to enter the market is whether there are net benefits to the “Canadian-controlled publishing sector.” Not net benefits to Canada, or its cultural bureaucrats, but net benefits to the existing Canadian-owned publishing industry. None were identified, unless you count small amounts of money S&S promised for internships at a couple of universities. S&S actually used the weakness of the Canadian-controlled publishing sector against it, arguing that Canadian authors needed more big-time publishers on the scene because the little guys were cheap.

So if I were hired by Hachette to help it set up in Canada, I’d advise it to pay top dollar for the next Mark Carney book, line up a couple of top lobbyists and a blue-chip law firm, promise all kinds of benefits to Canada from Hachette’s presence in Canada, regardless of whether or not they’ll materialize, and expect to be in the door within a few years.

If Shelley, Kim, et al balk at the time and cost, I’d advise them just to operate their Canadian publishing program out of the US. HarperCollins Canada and PRH Canada are increasingly directing workflows and decision-making through their Manhattan offices. You don’t actually have to be in Canada to publish a lot of Canadians, and you already have market access through your existing sales and distribution operation.

It goes without saying that Hachette hasn’t asked me about this and I really haven’t a clue what’s going on here. I just riffing off a couple of paragraphs in Publishers Weekly. We need a reporter to start making calls. I’m not in that business anymore.

I did snoop around a bit to see if there was any evidence that Hachette has been lobbying in Canada—couldn’t find anything. I didn’t see any Canadian federal notifications or filings related to Hachette. That matters because Heritage Canada issued an explicit interpretation in 2023 saying that a foreign investor intending to establish a new Canadian cultural business has to file a notification even if it already owns a business in Canada. Nor could I find any new corporate registrations involving Hachette, and the company’s own website still describes it as HBG Canada, “a marketing and publicity company.” It’s possible that the company is talking directly to the government without lobbying the government, or that it has filed its notification very recently and it’s not yet been posted online, but my guess is that Canadian expansion is more idea than project at the moment. For the time being, I feel reasonably confident in saying that Hachette has not yet been authorized to establish its proposed publishing business in Canada.

I’m sure Hachette will figure something out. They’re smart people with a formidable commercial operation—not as large as Penguin Random House, or HarperCollins, for that matter, but with better books than the latter. Little, Brown stands with the very best literary publishers in the world. The John Murray imprint publishes extraordinary history. The Canadian regulatory world will not daunt this crew.

From my perspective as a Canadian independent publisher, there’s no crying need for another well-capitalized multinational bidding for the relatively small number of Canadian books with literary merit and/or commercial potential. I wish there were a way we could simply swap Hachette for S&S Canada or HarperCollins Canada—better balance with one German, one French, and one American multinational here. But given the federal government’s performance in the S&S affair, I’d bet we’ll soon be hosting them all.

Recent decades have seen an explosion of government-approved casinos, sports betting, and online gaming opportunities. The politicians and officials behind them meticulously track the revenue they receive, but turn a blind eye to its often devastating human costs.

Through gripping personal stories and a comprehensive investigation, journalist Rob Csernyik shows how public officials evade accountability for lives shattered by gambling addiction―for instance, rarely recording gambling as a risk factor in suicide. From the rise of video lottery terminals to the explosion of smartphone betting, A Losing Hand shows how modern gambling has become more accessible, more immersive, and more dangerous than ever. The result is a crisis hiding in plain sight, one that policymakers have ignored for decades.

A powerful work of investigative journalism, this book forces a reckoning with the true cost of gambling in Canada.
