What New York City taught me about Dutch values

The Dutch apparently had no idea they had values.

Matthew Yglesias
Jun 29
∙
Paid
 
READ IN APP
 

Years ago, I was in the Netherlands with some other progressive journalists and think tankers, talking to various people on the Dutch center-left about the problems of the world.

At one point the group was meeting with one of the junior PvdA ministers in the fourth Balkenende cabinet, and he said that immigration is a more challenging issue for European social democrats than for Americans. His explanation was that America has a streak of explicit propositional nationalism to it — Superman can talk about “truth, justice, and the American Way,” but there’s no Dutch Way.

I was genuinely taken aback by this and said to him of course there’s a Dutch Way.

He didn’t know what I was talking about, so I told him that I was taught in school very specifically that New York City’s traditions of tolerance and diversity are Dutch values that were passed down to us by the settlers of New Amsterdam. We’re not, like, running around speaking Dutch in New York and certainly not promoting an ethnic state of Dutch-ancestry people. But we celebrate our understanding of Dutch culture and Dutch values, and if we can tell wave after wave of immigrants to New York that they are welcome in the city for Dutch reasons but also must learn to practice open-mindedness and tolerance for Dutch reasons, then surely they can do it in the Netherlands!¹

This guy probably thought I was putting him on. Dutch values in New York? So I started talking like crazy conspiracy guy about how the Mets wear orange and blue because the Dodgers wore blue and the Giants wore orange, and orange and blue are the colors of the flag of New York City because those are the colors of the Netherlands. The Knicks, too, wear orange and blue. And of course, Knicks stands for Knickerbockers, which is some kind of long-obsolete ethnic term for people of Dutch ancestry.

The only American president not to be a native English speaker was New York’s own Martin Van Buren, a native Dutch speaker. Brooklyn is named for Breukelen in Utrecht, and Harlem is named for Haarlem in North Holland. New York defines itself as somewhat distinctive from the rest of the United States of America and chooses to do so specifically with reference to a certain notion of Dutch-ness.

I don’t think he bought it.

This was an interesting experience for me, though, because it was a powerful reminder of the extent to which these national or sub-national identities are simultaneously very real and also totally made up.

Imagined communities

There are such things as historical facts. But the idea of history is linked to the idea of storytelling. Good history should be accurate (or at least avoid inaccuracy), but it also involves narrative choices that are beyond questions of fact.

I read John Keay’s “India: A History” recently and it starts with the Indus Valley civilization, whose main population centers are in what’s now Pakistan.

Part of why he does this is that trying to tell the history of the specific geography of contemporary India wouldn’t make any sense. The lines that were drawn on a map in 1947 corresponded to real divisions within the Indian subcontinent that had existed in some form or another for hundreds of years, but the precise geography of contemporary “India” is completely ahistorical and reflects a lot of midcentury happenstance.

The story of India has to include the story of the British Raj and the Mughal Dynasty and the Maurya Empire and a dozen other things, none of which correspond exactly to the borders of today’s India.

Tracing it all the way back to Moenjodaro is, I think, the kind of thing where you could have gone either way as an author. There’s little known about the Indus Valley people because their writing hasn’t been deciphered, and it seems like the Sanskrit language and the foundations of Hinduism come from outside the subcontinent. I might have told the story in a different way that starts with the Indo-Aryan migration.

But what Keay is choosing to do is to tell the story in more the way that corresponds to how contemporary Indians construct their national identity.

And that’s fair enough; people have to make choices about these things.

Here in the United States, we clearly trace “our” political and cultural history to the British colonists. Even in the subculture that does land acknowledgments, the structure is that “we” (the descendants of British colonists) are acknowledging that “they” (the descendants of the Native Americans who lived there before the colonists arrived) were here first.

In most cases, of course, the speaker will not be actually descended from either group. But the founders of the country are our (metaphorical) fathers who brought forth on this continent a new nation that we belong to, while the indigenous peoples who were here first are other nations.

Indians, by contrast, do not see the Vedic conquerors (or the Mughals or the British) as the founding fathers of the nation — their nation goes all the way back to the first identifiable civilization in the area.

This is all a long lead-in to say it’s notable that New York City clearly considers Peter Stuyvesant, the final director-general of New Netherland, to be among its Founding Fathers.

There’s a Peter Stuyvesant Little League that plays pretty close to the Stuyvesant Town mega-development. A little ways away from all that, you can find Stuyvesant Street — the only street in Manhattan that actually runs from east to west — and over in Brooklyn the Stuy in Bed-Stuy is Stuyvesant.

You absolutely could say that New York as a political community derives from England or that it derives from the Lenape, but generations of New Yorkers have chosen to define themselves as the heirs to New Netherland. And if Stuyvesant is ever canceled and has his name removed from all this stuff, it will be because of entanglements with the slave trade, not because New Yorkers decide to side with the English people who beat him rather than the Dutch guy who lost.

The legacy of Dutch values

In terms of values, the key thing is that the Netherlands was a haven of religious toleration at a time when most of Europe was governed by a principle of state religions.

  • The Thirty Years’ War ended with the concept that each German prince could pick whichever religion he wanted and impose that on his little state.

  • Henry IV won the French Wars of Religion and became king, but found he had to convert to the Catholic faith to consolidate power.

  • The English Civil War, which had a strain of liberal parliamentarism versus absolute monarchy, ended with Oliver Cromwell establishing a theocratic regime.

But the 17th century Netherlands was a land of toleration. The country’s origins relate to Protestants rebelling against their Habsburg overlords, but, having secured their independence, the Dutch did not establish a Cromwell-style Calvinist autocracy.

The Pilgrims who founded New England fled religious persecution in England and went to the Netherlands before migrating to America. Sephardic Jews expelled from Spain moved to the Netherlands, and later they were joined by Ashkenazi refugees from the Thirty Years’ War. The oldest continuously functioning Jewish congregation in the United States is Congregation Shearith Israel, which was founded by Sephardic Jews who were living in Brazil. They came to Brazil during a brief period of Dutch rule, but after the Portuguese reconquered it they had to go somewhere, so they went to New Amsterdam.

Interestingly, Stuyvesant — the director general of the whole New Netherland at the time — was actually against this and tried to prohibit them from settling. But they appealed to the Dutch East India Company and he was overruled.

Anyone familiar with the standard patriotic narrative of the United States has probably heard that the country as a whole was founded by various religious dissenters and that tolerance and pluralism are hallmarks of the country that are enshrined in the Bill of Rights. If you visit the Jefferson Memorial or go to Monticello in Charlottesville, you’ll hear a lot about Thomas Jefferson’s role in all this.

But the specific narrative I was taught in New York is that several of the original 13 colonies were founded in a spirit of religious particularism (Massachusetts for Puritans, Maryland for Catholics, Pennsylvania for Quakers) and New York — uniquely — was founded on the Dutch principle of religious tolerance.

You can just believe things

I always remember this interaction because the Dutch guy’s incredulity was genuine, but it also genuinely shocked me at the time.

I had never really thought in an analytical way about the claim that New Yorkers are tolerant as a legacy of Dutch values — I took it for granted. But the Dutch had no idea what I was talking about; they were jealous of the concept of an American Way.

And there’s something to that.

If New York City can kinda sorta make up a distinct narrative about itself based on facts about the Netherlands, so can the Netherlands itself. The New Amsterdam values of my youth, at the end of the day, are really just the same broad values that liberals tend to ascribe to the United States as a whole. The difference is maybe just that the United States in the 1980s was a broadly more politically conservative place and the Walter Mondale/Michael Dukakis voters of New York City felt a little alienated from it. So they told themselves they were being Dutch.

This is a little bit fake but it’s obviously based on strands of truth. And I think there’s something constructive about it. The Dutch guy was right, after all, that it’s easier to promote assimilation if you give people something to assimilate to. And probably every nation can come up with some broadly factual way to associate itself with praiseworthy values and then claim those values as its own and define being a good member of the community partly in terms of expressing those values.

You can’t just go buck wild and make stuff up that has no grounding in facts or it won’t be credible (and you’ll be associating your national identity with the non-praiseworthy value of lying). But it’s also not necessarily a question of accuracy-maxxing — you’re trying to tell a story.

America as a whole has some pretty good stories that we can tell about ourselves, and I think we ought to tell them. Positive European stories would necessarily be somewhat different, but they’re still out there, waiting to be told.
