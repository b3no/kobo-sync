Coalescence or ... no-alescence

Candidates in Texas are being left out to dry by members of their own parties — even as their counterparts in Michigan are gaining support from unlikely places.

Sep 2

The Texas race for U.S. Senate, which is perhaps the most dramatic contest of the cycle, has gotten even more contentious. The two candidates have gone sharply negative against one another during the last week, as both nominees also face some trouble lining up support from members of their own party.

State Representative James Talarico’s first negative ad ran on August 29. The 30-second spot targets Texas Attorney General Ken Paxton on allegations of corruption, marking a shift in Talarico’s tone from earlier campaign messaging, the Texas Tribune reports. Though the representative has attacked his opponent during rallies, speeches, and in an online ad, this is the first time that he has used a paid television spot to outright dunk on the Republican. Until now, Talarico’s campaign has spent nearly $29 million on mostly positive ads designed to introduce the Democrat to a perhaps-skeptical red-state population.

This new ad is very different in tone.

“Ken Paxton is the most corrupt politician in Texas,” Talarico says. “We cannot afford Ken Paxton’s corruption.”

A poll taken in July found that voters across the aisle overwhelmingly view Paxton as corrupt.

Two days and $15 million later, Paxton allies launched their own first major set of ads in the general, one of which highlights the candidate’s record as attorney general while the other attacks Talarico as “weak on China,” Politico reports.

As the candidates come at each other, they each face an uphill battle toward achieving party coalescence due to opposition from their primary opponents.

U.S. Senator John Cornyn, who lost to Paxton in the primary, told CNN that he won’t campaign with the Republican nominee. Meanwhile, the incumbent is fervently fundraising on behalf of other Republican candidates running across the country, the Texas Tribune reports.

“Paxton’s a poor campaigner,” Cornyn, who historically has succeeded in putting up massive fundraising hauls, told the Tribune. “He’s lazy and doesn’t raise money. And he basically is not offering a very compelling case for his election. He’s making this harder than it needs to be. But so be it — we are where we are.”

Many of Cornyn’s most reliable donors have thus far sat out of Paxton’s race against Talarico, and Cornyn suggested to the Tribune that President Donald Trump could pick up the slack with his $400 million super PAC. Semafor reports that the G.O.P. is depending on the president to step in since the party does not have the budget to spend massively in Texas — a state they should not have had to touch at all — at a time when Republicans are trying to win races in more traditionally competitive states.

The resentment is palpable on both sides. Representative Jasmine Crockett, whom Talarico beat in the Democratic primary, suggested she does not plan to campaign with him, consistently claiming that Black voters aren’t enthusiastic about Talarico, who is white. Crockett, who is Black, has voiced her frustration about the primary result, calling the contest she lost to Talarico a “racist race.”

She told CNN that she would be campaigning down the ballot, “making sure that people that maybe are getting overshadowed because it seems like it’s only about one big race, I think amplifying them, making sure that I can raise their profiles and introducing them to people, that’ll give something more to voters,” The Hill reports.

Complicating the Texas race, there may be fewer polling locations in the state come the general. Tarrant County, the state’s third-largest county by population and home to Fort Worth, will have 92 fewer places to cast ballots than in the 2022 midterms after county commissioners voted 3 to 2 on Tuesday night to approve a slimmed down list of polling sites for November, according to the Fort Worth Report.

The closures come amid a Trump administration push to cut down on voting mechanisms. A whistle-blower from the U.S. Postal Service is alleging that the agency has already been secretly carrying out Trump’s order on mail-in voting, despite a court order blocking the plans, the New York Times reports. The report, published Tuesday, said that the U.S.P.S. had plans to complete a new digital system by September 1 to oversee ballots and reject the ones deemed invalid by the administration’s illegal guidelines.

Coalescence in Michigan

While the actual content of the Democratic Senate primary in Michigan may appear more contentious than the Crockett/Talarico race, party leaders are rallying behind Michigan’s Democratic nominee for Senate, Dr. Abdul El-Sayed, despite lingering controversy over his relationship with leftist streamer Hasan Piker during the primary.

Representative James Clyburn, who previously backed Haley Stevens, El-Sayed’s main opponent in the Democratic primary, has publicly voiced his support for El-Sayed, The Hill reports. El-Sayed recently expressed regret for comments he made following an attack on a Detroit-area synagogue earlier this year.

“I support his election,” Clyburn told NBC’s “Meet the Press.” “He has apologized. He says he will not campaign with Piker anymore. I’d say that it’s worth checking him at his word. All of us deserve second chances.”

Haley Stevens and Mallory McMorrow, the two more moderate candidates El-Sayed defeated in the primary, have likewise endorsed him. But while Clyburn has accepted El-Sayed’s change of heart, Michigan Attorney General Dana Nessel continues to criticize the candidate’s views while urging Jewish voters to back him.

At the same time as Democrats get it together for El-Sayed, the G.O.P. is coalescing behind Mike Rogers, Michigan State University’s Institute for Public Policy and Social Research reports. The Republican nominee for Senate is polling ahead of Representative John James, the party’s nominee for governor, in the institute’s latest survey.

Alaska Democrats zero in on leading candidates while Republicans remain fractured

On the topic of coalescence, former State Senator Tom Begich withdrew from Alaska’s race for governor after coming in second to fellow Democrat and current State Representative Jonathan Kreiss-Tomkins. Begich said he was stepping aside to give the leading Democrat the greatest chance at winning against Republicans Bernadette Wilson and Dave Bronson, who came in third and fourth, respectively, the Anchorage Daily News reports.

Stepping into fourth place amid Begich’s departure is former Alaska Attorney General Treg Taylor. That means three Republicans will be competing, all of whom seem keen to stay in the race rather than coalesce behind a single candidate, the Juneau Independent reports.

Following a similar trajectory, Democrat David Leslie, who came in fourth in the race for U.S. Senate, withdrew just before the Monday night deadline, elevating Republican Gerald Heikes to the November ballot. Former Representative Mary Peltola is the sole Democrat facing off against incumbent Senator Dan S. Sullivan, Dan J. Sullivan, and now Heikes.

Other race updates

Senator Ed Markey held on in Tuesday’s Massachusetts Senate primary, beating a challenge by Representative Seth Moulton. Markey won nearly two-thirds of the vote and has all but secured another six-year term in the Senate.

Last week, Trump-endorsed Senate candidate Darline Graham won the South Carolina primary runoff to replace her late brother, Lindsey Graham, by about four points. She will face Democrat Annie Andrews, a pediatrician focused on public health and affordability, in November.

Senator Susan Collins has agreed to participate in four debates against Democrat Troy Jackson before November, the Portland Press Herald reports. Her willingness to appear signals that her team sees Jackson as a formidable opponent, despite his late entry into Maine’s Senate race after former candidate Graham Platner’s departure.

The Washington Post reported on a trend of ex-Republicans who are running as Democrats in 2026 races. The Post found at least three such candidates for the House. “Part of the hope is that these party-switching candidates can reach Republicans who, like they did, have soured on Trump.”

The New York Times published an investigation into how anonymous donors are shaping the midterms. “All across America, the midterm elections are drowning in dark money.”

Spending from corporate PACs in the 2026 cycle so far has surpassed all spending during the 2024 presidential cycle. To this point, corporate donors have spent a cumulative $646 million this cycle, compared with $461 million across the 2024 cycle.
