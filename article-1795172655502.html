Democratic candidates found God. But did their voters?

Electing pastors is easy; closing the “God gap” is hard

Ryan Burge
Nov 20
∙

∙
Guest post

In the 48 hours after the midterm election results came in, 16 reporters interviewed me. Whether they were from large mainstream outlets or niche Christian publications, they all wanted to talk about the same thing: how the 2026 elections could signal the rise of a “new religious left” in the American political landscape.

If you just look at the winning candidates, you might think they were onto something. Adam Hamilton, a man who in 1990 started a United Methodist Church in suburban Kansas City (which is now one of the largest churches in his entire denomination), became the first Democrat in almost a century to win a U.S. Senate seat in Kansas. The incoming U.S. senator from Texas, James Talarico, is a seminarian in the Presbyterian Church (USA) who took a break from the campaign trail to discuss how his Christian faith shaped his political views on Ezra Klein’s podcast.

Two ministers from other mainline Protestant denominations, Sarah Trone Garriott and Lindsay James, won House races for the Democrats. Iowa Gov.-elect Rob Sand is fond of quoting the Bible, and soon-to-be U.S. senator from Mississippi Scott Colom cites his faith when detailing his issue positions.

All this led to The New York Times running a post-election piece about a “new Christian left,” and The Week declaring that “mixing religion and politics proved decisive in the Democrats’ midterm victories.” These postmortems followed pre-election coverage crediting Talarico with “reviving a long-neglected tradition of left-leaning American religiosity” and reporting that “party strategists and officials have grown excited about the number of candidates for whom religion is a major part of their biography and identity.”

Share

It’s enough to make anyone wonder: Has the Democratic Party found God? And has this newfound piety pulled religious voters back under the party’s tent?

As a guy who spends nearly every single day of his life digging into the data about American religion and politics — and as someone who spent 17 years as a pastor myself — I have to answer both questions with a resounding “no.” If the lesson Democrats take from 2026 is that they should set their sights on winning over religious voters, they’ll be drawing exactly the wrong conclusion.

There’s a simple reason for that: As U.S. religious disaffiliation has grown, any semblance of a religious left has been disappearing rapidly over the last several decades. While religious appeals may occasionally put certain candidates over the top with marginal voters in close races (as they likely did for Hamilton) even those benefits are fast diminishing as the electorate grows more secular.

In other words, an explicitly Christian appeal to religious voters might be one tool in a successful Democrat’s toolkit — but it’s a hammer that risks finding vanishingly few nails, if recent trends in religious affiliation and partisanship continue on anything resembling their current paths.

The rise of the “God gap”

The biggest story in American religion since the early 1990s has been the rapid rise in the percentage of Americans who claim no religious affiliation on surveys. Just 35 years ago, the share of adults who reported that they weren’t aligned with any religious tradition was only 5% of the population. In the most recent polling, that has increased nearly six-fold. Now, about 28% of all adults are nonreligious.

But what many people don’t know is that the “rise of the nones” has been happening asymmetrically across the Democratic and Republican coalitions. In fact, the vast majority of that disaffiliation has occurred among left-leaning voters.

One-third of Democratic voters now claim no religious affiliation
Share of presidential voters with no religious affiliation, by vote choice

Back in 1960, just 1% of Democrats and Republicans said that they had no religious affiliation. But as early as the mid-1970s, a “God gap” had already started to emerge between these two voting blocs. By the late-1980s, a Democrat was about twice as likely not to claim religious affiliation compared with their Republican counterpart — though neither side boasted a huge share of nonreligious voters.

However, in the last 20 years or so, the size of the “God gap” has exploded. In the 2008 election between then-Sen. Barack Obama and the late Sen. John McCain, 22% of Obama’s voters were nonreligious compared with just 13% of McCain’s. By the time Kamala Harris faced off against Donald Trump in 2024, the nonreligious made up fully one-third of all Democratic voters — a 12-point increase from Obama’s first coalition.

Meanwhile, the share of Trump voters who were “nones” was exactly the same as it was for McCain four presidential elections earlier.

It’s hard to claim that we’re seeing the rise of a religious left when the data shows a rise of the nonreligious left. And the “God gap” is only poised to grow larger as older and more religious voters age out of the electorate: Around one-third of Gen Z is nonreligious, compared with less than 20% of Baby Boomers.

Furthermore, the rise of the “nones” in the Democratic coalition has been matched by a dramatic fall in the share of Christians voting for the party — and white Christians in particular. Nonwhite Christians have kept the overall decline from being even steeper — in 2024 they made up roughly a quarter of Democratic voters, up from less than 10% in 1960 — but there just aren’t enough of them to replace the white Christians who left the party.

White Christians went from 4 in 5 Democratic voters to under one-third

Back when John F. Kennedy won the White House in 1960, over 9 in 10 of the ballots cast for him came from Christians — and nearly 85% were from white Christians specifically. But the white Christian share of the Democratic voting bloc began to erode quickly: In the 1972 contest, just 64% of George McGovern’s votes came from white Christians, and it was almost all downhill from there.

The last time a Democratic presidential candidate received a majority of their votes from white Christians was Al Gore in 2000. Twenty years later, when Joe Biden was on the ballot, the white Christian share was down to only 33%. Harris did no better when she ran in 2024.

Decline of the mainline

It’s because of this poor showing with white Christians specifically that white religious candidates such as Talarico, Hamilton, Trone Garriott, and James are the ones who have been recently credited with reviving religious strains of Democratic politics. Avowedly Christian Black candidates such as Sen. Raphael Warnock of Georgia, a prominent minister himself, and Sen.-elect Colom have received less attention for their faith in part because an overwhelming share of Black Christians have long voted for Democrats.

But it’s truly noteworthy that Hamilton, Trone Garriott, and James won races in disproportionately white Kansas and Iowa. Those states used to be the places where large concentrations of mainline Protestants lived and worshipped.

Mainline Protestants — an umbrella term that includes denominations like United Methodists, Episcopalians, Lutherans, and Presbyterians — used to dominate American religious life. Historians have argued that over half of all Americans were members of mainline churches in the late 1950s.

Traditionally, these denominations have not been nearly as conservative as their evangelical cousins. Churches like these ordain women and also have no prohibition against same-sex marriage.

However, the most notable trend in the mainline over the last 75 years has been an absolute numerical collapse. I witnessed it firsthand: The mainline congregation I led (the First Baptist Church in Mount Vernon, Illinois) closed its doors in 2024 after a long decline in membership.

The Seven Sisters of mainline Protestantism have lost 54% of their members since 1987
Combined membership: United Methodist, ELCA, PC(USA), Episcopal, American Baptist, UCC, and Disciples of Christ

The traditions that make up the vast majority of the mainline are often called the Seven Sisters. Back in 1987, around the time when Hamilton was working his way through a United Methodist seminary and planting his church in Kansas City, the total membership of these seven Protestant denominations numbered over 24 million. But while Hamilton’s church membership soared, the broader mainline moved in the opposite direction.

By the late-2000s, the mainline had already dropped below 20 million members; those losses have only accelerated since. In the most recent membership data available, the number of people on the rolls of the Seven Sisters is down to just over 11 million — a decline of 54% in less than 40 years.

Some denominations have been hit harder than others: The United Methodist Church by itself had about 11 million members in 1968 but is now down to less than 4 million. Talarico’s denomination, the Presbyterian Church (USA), has dropped from 3 million members in the mid-1980s to just over 1 million in 2025, according to data recently released.

The kicker? The average mainline Protestant is in their 60s, meaning these drops are only likely to grow steeper.

In other words, the very foundation of the religious left is crumbling by the year.

Coalition of the “nones”

The Democratic coalition has already changed to reflect this reality. Just look at the religious composition of younger voters in the 2008 Obama coalition compared with those who supported Harris in 2024.

Most young Obama voters were Christian. Most young Harris voters had no religion.
Religious composition of Democratic presidential voters under age 40

In 2008, a majority of younger voters who supported Obama were Christians — and they were actually evenly split between white and nonwhite voters. While the nonreligious were a big part of the Obama coalition (39% of those under 40), they were not close to a majority.

If these Obama-era numbers had held steady, there might be a case to make that a religious left could be a central pillar of how Democrats could win going forward. But the religiosity of younger Democratic voters declined precipitously in the years that followed.

When Harris was on the ballot only 16 years later, just 16% of her under-40 voters were white Christians. While 53% of Obama’s young voters were Christians when he beat McCain, less than 30% of younger voters for Harris were Catholics or Protestants.

The biggest shift, of course, is in the nonreligious share. Around 4 in 10 young Obama voters were nones. Among Harris voters, that was creeping close to two-thirds of young voters. This is why any discussion of a rising religious left needs to be qualified. Unless an unprecedented religious revival sweeps through the United States, the majority of Democratic voters in the future will be nonreligious — in fact, that reality may arrive during one of the next two presidential election cycles.

While the decline in American religiosity has stagnated for the last few years, it will soon hit a demographic cliff. The oldest Baby Boomers are now entering their early 80s. This is a group that is not only numerically large, but also fairly religious — much more so than the members of Gen Z and Gen Alpha who will replace them in the electorate. So when a generation of folks that is 20% unaffiliated is replaced with a cohort of young adults that is 33% nonreligious, secularism will only accelerate.

None of this is to say that the Democrats should totally ignore people of faith, of course. Most voters are Christian, but American Jews, Buddhists, Hindus, and Muslims typically vote Democratic. (Those groups combine for just 5% to 6% of all American adults.) And in states like Kansas or even Texas, there are still significant numbers of white Christians to be courted in swing races.

The Argument director of political data Lakshya Jain has made the case that Democrats can’t simply wait for secularization to hand them a durable majority, and from a pure statistical perspective, he’s right. Roughly 60% of Americans still say religion is at least somewhat important in their lives — it’s hard to win a national election if you’re losing these voters by 30 points. But his argument counsels something pretty narrow: The difference is between making an effort not to alienate religious voters and trying to build a winning coalition around them.

The first approach is mathematically necessary for the Democrats to remain viable in the near term, but any real effort to organize a religious core is likely to fail. The remaining Christian voters are increasingly older, whiter, and more evangelical than the mainline Protestants who once formed the backbone of the religious left.

Democrats don’t need to win these voters; They simply need to give them fewer reasons to vote against Democratic candidates, and the way to do that probably isn’t quoting the Gospels.

Appeals to shared Christian values will increasingly become hard to make as the “God gap” continues to widen. References to stories like the Good Samaritan may land well for older voters who grew up attending Sunday School and sitting through church services. Younger progressives, however, are increasingly being raised in secular environments where those kinds of references will be meaningless.

In my view, the best way forward for the Democratic Party can be found in what the sociologist Robert Bellah called America’s Civil religion. He argued that the United States has its own set of sacred documents and rituals — like the Declaration of Independence and certain presidential addresses like Washington’s inaugural — that give the country a spiritual depth and bind its citizens together. Civil religion allows politicians to speak about God in a generic way and advocate for things like “the common good” without explicitly linking those appeals to teachings from any religious text.

If Democrats can do this effectively — and, in 2026 at least, I think they did — they can tap into an American spiritual tradition that will likely last much longer than the Christian denominations from which they once drew the vast majority of their voters.
