Bay Area officials want more housing to keep up with the A.I. boom. One developer sees a different opportunity.

As the A.I. industry pushes up costs in San Francisco, one developer has opted to lean in and build office space for the city’s wealthy new tenants.

Halina Bennet
Sep 14

San Francisco Mayor Daniel Lurie declared a “rent emergency” on Thursday. The declaration is little more than symbolic but puts real policy proposals — albeit not the kind of supply-side policy proposals that would address the underlying issue — on the table. Proposals include eviction delays, a cap on catch-up rent increases of 10 percent a year for rent-controlled units, and $3 million toward legal aid for about 400 households actively facing eviction. The mayor also highlighted the Ellis Act, a piece of legislation he sponsors, which would provide displaced tenants with an additional $3,000 were it enacted.

Supervisors Jackie Fielder, Danny Sauter, and Matt Dorsey each have legislation of their own in the mix.

In the past year, San Francisco had the highest median rent increase in the country at more than 25 percent, with an average one-bedroom unit now running at more than $4,000 a month, the New York Times reports. According to the San Francisco Standard, asking rents citywide jumped 14 percent in the period from March to July alone. Landlords have begun more than 2,400 evictions this year so far, putting the city on track for the highest single-year total in a decade, the Times reports.

The housing meltdown and subsequent emergency are the result of the city’s accelerating corporate and high-income growth fueled by artificial intelligence. As the industry, and its players’ purses, continue to grow, everyone else — restaurant workers, hotel staff, teachers, first responders — is priced out into oblivion.

While Lurie’s current proposals come out of a fairly left-wing playbook, back in December he signed the Family Zoning plan, a supply-oriented legislative package that allows for taller, denser housing.

Despite his and city planners’ desire for residential development to ameliorate A.I. displacement, some developers are capitalizing on the moment in a different way. Two years ago, Lincoln Properties began making plans for a high-rise near Union Square in San Francisco. The developer was uncertain whether the building would primarily contain office or residential space and put forth two plans: one tower that would include both housing units and office space and another that would be solely dedicated to more than a million square feet of office space.

Today, as the city is urging developers to build housing and office attendance is still lagging, the residential route seems like the obvious choice. But the developer last week made its intentions clear: “The all-office is our focus and what we intend to build,” Lincoln Executive Vice President Brendan Wong told the San Francisco Chronicle. “We are in active discussion with potential tenants that want the quality and attributes that the all-office tower provides.”

Why the focus on office space? A bet that A.I. companies will continue to grow and that Anthropic or OpenAI might like an expensive new office building.

The city isn’t happy.

“The proposal that includes residential units was our preference as it contributes to downtown revitalization while also producing much needed housing,” Planning Department Spokesman Patrick Hannon told the Chronicle.

Airbnb commits $250 million to development

Airbnb launched a $250 million housing accelerator on Monday, which will first target developments that are permitted but stuck due to lack of financing. Airbnb-commissioned research estimates that nationally, about 750,000 housing units are sitting in this kind of last-dollar limbo, and the company says its investment is designed to unlock $5 billion in capital over the next decade.

The inaugural deal is $6.4 million toward 201 affordable units in a public-private redevelopment project of more than 500 units in Austin, Texas.

The accelerator will also provide funding for local pro-housing advocacy groups that push for zoning and permitting reform, for an “Airbnb City Index” that will rank cities on housing production and affordability, and for a $5 million prize for construction-technology innovation.

Certainly, this is both a philanthropic move and a business move, as well as a reputational one. Airbnb has a clear interest in encouraging municipalities to loosen housing rules and in being seen as part of the solution.

“We’ve been embroiled in lots of debates with cities about whether or not Airbnb is a contributor to housing becoming unaffordable,” Airbnb Chief Executive Brian Chesky told the Wall Street Journal.

As a company caught in the crosshairs of the affordability debate, Chesky said that Airbnb has a responsibility to come up with some solutions.

“I think it can benefit Airbnb, but I think this is primarily not about Airbnb,” he told the Journal.

Market conditions

Mortgage rates crossed 7 percent again, landing at 7.12 percent at the end of last week. The increase was driven by the 10-year Treasury yield’s climb toward 5 percent as the war in Iran continues to escalate.

Pending home sales and purchase applications are entering a stretch of even more undesirable year-over-year comparisons since rates were falling and homebuying activity was largely recovering at this time last year, when rates were under 7 percent.

Local housing markets tell their own stories:

  • San Antonio renters are experiencing a favorable market, as rents have fallen between 2 and 3.5 percent compared with previous years. A post-Covid development boom resulted in high vacancy rates, giving renters leverage — at least for now. According to the San Antonio Report, the market is likely to revert in the coming years.

  • Austin, on the other hand, is a good market for homebuyers as prices continue to fall. But these conditions are unlikely to hold, the Austin American-Statesman reports, because a shrinking supply and high mortgage rates are converging to eventually — after two years of favorable conditions for buyers — do away with buyers’ leverage.

  • California’s condo market — like others nationwide — is shrinking as an ownership entry point, the Los Angeles Times reports, worsening the affordability crisis overall. Condo construction has collapsed across the country as liability issues and costs have mounted, causing developers to move away from a form of housing that once offered many buyers an entry point into homeownership.

  • A report by the National Multifamily Housing Council and the New York University Schack Institute of Real Estate published last week found that most states will need more than a century to close the gap between housing construction and the more than 22.4 million rental households that the report estimates are rent-burdened. North Carolina stands out as a relative bright spot, potentially resolving its shortage in as little as 17 years because of lower construction costs and local subsidies. The report recommends tax incentives and deregulation, among other methods, to increase development and offers some ways to preserve existing affordable housing.

Local zoning fights and housing investments

  • The Savannah City Council on Thursday rejected a zoning measure designed to increase multifamily construction in districts that are not zoned for residences, Savannah Morning News reports. Supporters said the change was necessary to address the city’s housing shortage and affordability crisis, while opponents said that they needed additional time to evaluate the measure’s impact.

  • Ohio’s Residential Economic Development District (REDD) program on Friday awarded $14 million in grants to 10 counties across the state for workforce housing development. This is the program’s second round of awards, bringing total funding allocated since the first round in March to $25 million. To qualify, housing projects must include at least 100 residential units and be located near a major economic development project expected to create at least 700 new permanent jobs or attract at least $700 million in private investment. Grant money can be used for housing-related infrastructure, site preparation, development capital, and other needs.

  • The Salt Lake Tribune published an article this week about the generational burden of worsening housing affordability. Local experts believe that the combination of high prices, mortgage rates, and down payments will keep an entire generation from achieving homeownership if Utah cities don’t make smaller lots available and if builders don’t use them.
