The Straight Women Who Actually Find Men Attractive

Phoebe Maltz Bovy's Book, "The Last Straight Woman" identifies something I've always wondered: maybe that "universal" female experience of finding men bothersome and gross isn't so universal.

I just finished reading The Last Straight Woman by my friend, Phoebe Maltz Bovy, and I have to admit, the first feeling I had upon reading a couple chapters was intense fury that I had not written this book myself. Everything she said, I couldn’t help but nod and say, “Yes, YES!! This is what I’ve been SAYING!!!” (Except she’s been saying it longer and much better than I could have, and, so on.) She also made some points that never even occurred to me, but as soon as I read them, I was like Oh my God, I’m such an idiot that I never thought of this. It was like watching the most compelling horror movie ever (the horror being, I suppose, feminist manifestos insisting straight women could simply choose not to be straight the same way one could choose to stop buying one particular type of apple—yes, she cites this) where every plot twist feels like something you should have seen coming, but foolishly didn’t. And to make it worse? She’s hilarious.

When I first heard that Phoebe was writing a book about straight women, years ago, I had a brief moment of non-judgmental but confused curiosity where I was like, “Okay, what about them? The fact that they’re straight?” Thankfully, I did not have the standard Bluesky reaction of “STRAIGHT women? Existing and writing about themselves? a MONTH before Pride? How distasteful!” But anyway, “the fact that they’re straight” is actually…pretty compelling, at least the way she writes about it. Phoebe is not writing about how all women should be straight, nor is she writing about how straight people are oppressed. Just that most women are straight—and that a key part of being straight is (and I kid you not, hold onto your seats) actually being attracted to men.

Some of you unfamiliar with Phoebe’s beat might still be like, “Okay, and?” but there’s actually so much interesting content here that I found myself dog-earing nearly every page of her book.

In particular, the part that got me the most—and what most of this article will cover—was the way straight womanhood was presented during what Phoebe aptly calls the era of “Ban Men Feminism.” In particular, she references supermodel/activist Emily Ratajkowski’s Ban Men feminist book, My Body, and the entire Ban Men (but be hot while doing it, but NOT for men, in fact, it’s IRONIC, even if nobody can tell the difference) ethos. Emily Ratajkowski frames all men and all male attention in general as predatory and annoying, with this implicit assumption that all women could relate to such a plight. And she’s hardly the only one. Many straight feminist writers have theorized that we would all be much happier in women-only dance parties, indefinitely, deriving zero pleasure or arousal from men ourselves. While I think I can safely go on the record to say that most women do not look like Emily Ratajkowski, Phoebe points out that plenty of non-Ratajkowski-appearing women, like Lena Dunham, considered her book a great example of what “every” woman experiences—basically, yes, we’re “straight” but also, we’d be very happy to see all men disappear tomorrow so we could just sit around and drink wine together in peace.

Not to go all Tumblr on you, but I did not realize that female heterosexuality was apparently aromantic asexuality. To quote Taylor Lorenz, “People learn things on their phones every day!”

I actually had something in drafts about this a while ago about the fact that I had a very hard time relating to this concept—and actually, Phoebe’s book is helping me to make sense of it, because when I wrote it, I couldn’t quite understand why I couldn’t relate. Obviously, I am no Emily Ratajkowski, but even as a woman who was (and perhaps still is—ask my husband!) reasonably conventionally attractive, I never related to the idea that men were all ugly, maybe passable, lecherous ogres and I was forced to interact with them so I could pay my bills and hopefully not be killed. And, per the reviews of My Body, clearly plenty of women who are not conventionally hot agree with her anyway.

It all reminded me of a Substack post that essentially presented the same premise, but in a slightly different way, attempting to show men what dating is like for women:

Imagine that the human species is quite different. In your world, there are no human women, but there are still basically two human sexes. There are human males like yourself, and then silverback gorillas. Now, all of the silverback gorillas really want to fuck you. They are all much stronger than you and could easily kill or permanently injure you if they wanted to. And you barely find any of them attractive. Their impulse control varies, but probably at least a few of them have sexually harassed you at some point. You know that many of them are nice and willing to protect you and give you resources in various situations, expecting little in return, but again, you aren’t that into many of them, if any.

This person, notably, is a man (I am 99.9% sure) so it stands to reason that he would see women’s experiences this way. He does not want to fuck the myriad of gorillas on the bus, so why would we? And although I disagree with this premise, a lot of women would probably agree with it! I certainly agree with some of it. Yes, most women experience sexual harassment at some point, and yes, I am not attracted to most men (not only do I have physical preferences, but most men are out of my age range, for a start!) But when I was young and single in college, surrounded by young and single men at the peak of their physical fitness, did I feel this way? Not at all! And according to Phoebe’s book, there must be at least one woman (the last straight woman, Phoebe herself, Amy Klobuchar, and maybe a couple more hiding somewhere) who would agree.

It’s not that I never got male attention. My first “boyfriend” was in elementary school (although I’m not sure he was aware of our relationship) I dated several boys in middle school, and I dated throughout high school and early college, ultimately meeting my husband at nineteen during my second year of college. And yes, plenty of my male attention was also unwanted. I do not claim to find all men attractive on the basis that they are men, any more than a straight man wants to have sex with every woman he’s ever seen (as Phoebe points out but in different words, many straight men claim this to be true, but they are filtering “women” to mean “single, childless women in my desired age range—potentially not close to my own age at all—who are thin and at least a 7/10” which…isn’t all women.)

But yes, I got unwanted male attention. Although I worked in tech for over a decade, I did not experience “constant” unwanted sexual attention per the stereotype, but I did have one boss tell me I had a “big ass for a white girl” (I am ashamed to admit I was both creeped out and slightly flattered if only because I was working on my gains at the time—that said, I was creeped out enough to feel uncomfortable around him from that point onward.) I used to belong to a 24 Hour Fitness and I definitely had to ward off a few men who repeatedly asked to help me adjust my form to flirt—an approach I might not have minded, actually, except I was already partnered. Most of this felt complimentary and not particularly aggressive. One guy was a little annoying, but I was mostly irritated that he was well into his fifties if not older, and I was foolishly worried that this meant he thought we were the same age.

I also lived in San Francisco during this time, so sometimes I fielded catcalls and approaches from men who, if we’re being honest, would have sexually harassed a Saint Bernard if it was wearing Lululemon.

And I’ll go a step further: sometimes the attention was unwanted and illegal. I have been sexually assaulted before too. Typically, when I talk about the unrealistic nature of telling straight women to just stop dating men, there’s someone in the flanks ready to be like, “Sounds pretty #privileged of you not to realize how dangerous men can be!” with one radfem on Twitter lecturing me that “I just hadn’t been raped, yet.” (Is that a threat? lol.) But anyway, yes, you’ll just have to believe me that I have experienced sexual assault and harassment! So why don’t I feel like the famed Emily, who apparently speaks for every woman, who is just so fed up with men and their disgusting urges, to the point that she can’t even?

I guess because I did not view this degree of attention as too annoying and predatory to turn me off men. Certainly not enough to warrant banning them from my life, as Emily claimed to do, placing them as the last priority in her hierarchy of needs. In fact, I have been known from time to time to enjoy male attention, to enjoy having sex with a man, and to find men attractive—to the point that if someone asked me if I’d take a pill that would turn me into a lesbian, I’d say no. (For one, I’d have to change my whole aesthetic—I have quite literally never been approached by a lesbian because I think I give off insane straight vibes. And I just bought new straight clothes.)

I quite like having lesbian friends, and I greatly treasure my lesbian readers who, for some reason, enjoy reading about all my straight stuff (true #allies, really.) I do not enjoy being straight in a “straight pride” or “straight day of visibility” way. I do not think straight people are oppressed. It’s just a neutral. I am straight, and liking men is part of it. I can’t imagine what it would be like not to like men.

Although I did create a straight pride sofa concept for Ikea. So far, no callbacks.

Anyway, I remember being in a group therapy session for women where the other three women were commiserating about “constant” male attention, and feeling like they were being visually undressed whenever they interacted with a man, gawked at by every man on the street, and never “taken seriously” on account of their beauty. I will try to say this as objectively as possible, but these women were not significantly more attractive than I was, and we were all within the same five-year age range. It is not flattering to say, but I did not hear their complaints and think, “Oh, poor them.” Instead, I wondered if I was secretly hideous. At first, I said nothing, but eventually I spoke up and said, “Gosh, I feel kind of ugly, I can’t relate to this at all.” One of the women sighed, rolled her eyes, and said, “Trust me, you’re lucky. You’re not missing anything.” The therapist doubled down and congratulated me on somehow dodging the burden of male attention. Perhaps ironically, this therapist sexually harassed me at a later date by asking if he could take a “sexy pic” of me for an exposure therapy exercise. But either way, I didn’t go off men. I did, however, stop seeing this particular therapist.

At the time, I assumed these women genuinely just received more male attention than I did, and I couldn’t figure out exactly why. But now, I’m coming to a new conclusion. We probably received relatively similar amounts of male attention (not to be an ass, but I was the only one in a relationship) but they were fed up with it, and I wasn’t. I found it annoying sometimes, and a couple times I found it scary, but mostly I found it flattering, and it didn’t bother me enough to complain about it in therapy. Perhaps I was starting out with lower self esteem than they were, but also, maybe I just found men in general less bothersome.

Perhaps there are many Emily Ratajkowskis in the world, even ones far less attractive than she is, who truly do not see men as anything more than a nuisance (although I should warn you that some of this may be performative—Emily has a boyfriend, so obviously didn’t really find all male attention so annoying!) But if this is the majority female position, it’s not one to which I can relate, and I’m convinced many other women feel this way but don’t want to say anything because A.) ironically, saying “I actually like men” might trigger too much male attention, B.) women who claim to hate all male attention will call you a pick-me and say that you’re just desperate for validation and C.) people will call you ugly.

In fact, for a while and perhaps even now, expressing this viewpoint was seen as misogynistic. I remember seeing a post on Reddit in the 2010s where a woman described herself as plain-looking and said she always felt inadequate when attractive women bemoaned the constant stream of male attention, because she didn’t get much at all. Although she was being vulnerable and not hurling any insults herself, she was downvoted into oblivion and her comments section was full of women calling her “lucky” and “privileged,” and warning her that she should never be so foolish and arrogant as to wish for the curse of beauty. I’m always shocked by how many models apparently hang out on Reddit, let me tell you.

Phoebe also covered R.O Kwon’s incredibly popular and “relatable” 2019 Paris Review essay, On Being a Woman in America While Trying to Avoid Being Assaulted, detailing the seemingly infinite internal monologue of “is there a man hiding in a nearby bush to rape me” that was apparently typical for every woman in America. I remember reading this when it came out, in fact, and wondering, Why is everyone relating to this? Even as a woman with an anxiety disorder, who had been sexually assaulted, I did not think men were perched atop every building like horny gargoyles waiting for their chance to assault me. If anything, I was more worried about getting mugged or stepping on a used needle, and given that I was living in San Francisco, these things were fairly reasonable concerns (the irony is not lost on me that many of the people who applauded this essay might have branded my mugging fear “Karenish.”) The times I had been assaulted, it wasn’t by strangers, and very few of the sexual assault stories I heard from other women involved dark alleyways and masked criminals. And yet, the essay was a hit! Women everywhere applauded it for so eerily speaking their thoughts. All I could think was, if this is how every woman feels, I’m not as neurotic as I thought.

I can only assume that I’ve perceived male attention the way I have—sometimes unwanted, sometimes annoying, and very rarely, scary…but not something I’d banish from my life or performatively claim to want to banish—because I actually enjoy men myself. Again, not all men, some are quite bad, but a life without men would be seriously lacking. I still wonder if the straight women who claim not to enjoy men at all are serious or just letting off steam and I’m taking it too literally. Perhaps my problem is that I’m simply unable to understand hyperbole. But if that’s true of me, it’s true of a few other women out there who, despite being feminists who want to have jobs and equal rights, are unaccountably straight.
