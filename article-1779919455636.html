The 21st Century ROAD to Housing Act finally passed the House

Stripped of its pesky build-to-rent provision, the bill now heads back to the Senate.

Halina Bennet
May 27
∙
Paid

A note before we begin: We are hosting a happy hour next week on Thursday, June 4, in Washington, D.C. for paid subscribers! Details are at the bottom of this newsletter.

This past Wednesday, May 20, (while I was on vacation, of course) the House passed its amended version of the 21st Century ROAD to Housing Act by an overwhelming 396-to-13 vote, with all dissent coming from Republicans — a rare, genuine bipartisan accomplishment.

The House package includes 56 provisions. These touch housing supply, manufactured housing, mortgage financing, rural and veteran housing, and community banking. The new version of the bill would authorize incentives to build new homes, streamline infill housing approvals, create a program to convert abandoned buildings into housing, and establish grants to modernize existing homes. In a statement, House Financial Services Committee Chairman French Hill (R-AR) called it proof that “Washington still works,” while Ranking Member Maxine Waters (D-CA) urged the Senate to “meet this moment.” A Bipartisan Policy Center poll released earlier this month found that 89 percent of voters supported legislation to make housing more affordable, which is about as close to a mandate as Congress ever gets.

The Senate version of the bill that the House amended would have forced sales of purpose-built single-family rental homes, also known as build-to-rent housing, within seven years. The National Association of Home Builders and the Urban Institute had warned that the build-to-rent provision in the Senate package could reduce rental supply by 40,000 to 72,000 units annually.

The House version instead caps corporate ownership of single-family homes at 350 properties, allowing institutional landlords to keep building new rentals but not to accumulate ever-larger existing portfolios. The White House has signaled support for the House version.

Representative Josh Harder (D-CA), co-chair of the bipartisan Build America Caucus and one of the most active legislators on this bill, told me this week about how the negotiations landed. Harder co-led the April letter signed by 76 House members calling on leadership to strip the build-to-rent provision before any final vote.

“We ended up in a very good place there,” he said.

Harder was candid about what didn’t make it through.

A provision called Build Now, which would have changed incentives for Community Development Block Grants, was dropped at the White House’s request, though Harder noted it remains alive in the Clarity Act, a separate Senate bill that is mostly about cryptocurrency.

“Ultimately we’re left with again the strongest pro-housing bill that we’ve seen in decades,” Harder said. “I think this is very likely to be the only major bipartisan policy to win this Congress, besides reauthorizing bills ... This is the only new major bipartisan policy initiative of this entire Congress, period,” he continued.

The bill now heads back to the Senate, which passed its own version in March by an 89-to-10 vote.

“There’s still work to be done,” Senate Banking Committee Chairman Tim Scott (R-SC) and Ranking Member Elizabeth Warren (D-MA) wrote before the House vote, though both said they remain committed to getting something to the president’s desk. Memorial Day recess ends next week.

Meanwhile, appropriators are moving in the opposite direction

On the same day the House passed the 21st Century ROAD to Housing Act, the House Appropriations Subcommittee on Transportation, Housing and Urban Development circulated a fiscal year 2027 spending bill that would cut Department of Housing and Urban Development funding by nearly $6 billion, reducing HUD’s budget from $77.3 billion this year to $71.4 billion next year — a roughly 7.6 percent decrease.

The cuts are specific. Continuum of Care funding, which provides permanent supportive housing for people experiencing homelessness, would be cut by $232 million (over 5.7 percent, according to National Low Income Housing Coalition analysis). The legislation would eliminate emergency rental-assistance vouchers, and it would also bar public housing agencies from issuing most new federal rent vouchers to additional families, with narrow exceptions for veterans, foster youth, and family unification cases.

But the bill’s future is less cut-and-dried than its numbers. Steve Womack (R-AR), chairman of the appropriations subcommittee, has been openly skeptical of the White House’s most extreme proposals, including the full elimination of Community Development Block Grants. Congress has repeatedly ignored Trump-era proposed HUD cuts in previous budget cycles — final HUD funding for this fiscal year came in $7.2 billion above the prior year — and this initial pitch is unlikely to be the final word.

The full Appropriations Committee markup is scheduled for next week.

Mortgage rates keep climbing; some markets see price cuts

The macro backdrop for housing markets was, once again, dominated by the war in Iran’s effect on interest rates. The average 30-year fixed mortgage rate climbed to 6.51 percent for the week ending May 21 — up from 6.36 percent the prior week and the highest level since August 2025, according to Freddie Mac. Before the war’s outbreak earlier this year, rates had briefly dipped below 6 percent for the first time since 2022. Mortgage applications fell 10 percent in April compared to March levels, and existing home sales rose just 0.2 percent between March and April.

The Wall Street Journal this week reported with more detail how the Iran war has put housing’s expected “spring thaw back on ice.”

The regional markets underneath the national numbers, as always, are a more nuanced story. Realtor.com identified the five metros with the highest share of listings seeing price reductions — all in Sun Belt or Mountain West markets that experienced explosive pandemic-era growth. In those places, Realtor.com’s Senior Economist Jake Krimmel explained that a combination of new supply and softening demand is leading to the cuts.

April housing starts, released by the Census Bureau on May 21, showed the split between single-family and multifamily new construction continuing to widen. Total starts fell 2.8 percent month-over-month to 1.465 million annualized — but that headline masked a 22.7 percent month-over-month surge in multifamily starts, which were up 11.5 percent year-over-year, offset by a 9 percent drop in single-family starts.

Total building permits rose 5.8 percent to 1.442 million. As long as mortgage rates remain elevated, single-family starts are likely to soften further.

And as high mortgage rates continue, homeownership feels increasingly out of reach. According to Newsweek, a growing number of priced-out millennials and Gen Z-ers are turning to cryptocurrency as an alternative wealth-building vehicle.

Pre-approved plans are catching on

The New York Times’ “Your Money Adviser” column from Friday zeroed in on pre-approved building plans — an increasingly mainstream supply-side tool in which a local government approves a set of standard residential designs in advance, and any developer who wants to build to those specs gets a streamlined permitting process, saving time and some money.

A Pew Charitable Trusts report released earlier this month found that about 40 U.S. jurisdictions now have some version of these programs, and that early this year Washington state, Tennessee, New York, Pennsylvania, and Hawaii all introduced legislation encouraging the use of pre-approved building plans. The Niskanen Center has estimated that pre-approved plans can save roughly $10,000 per infill project compared with custom designs. The savings from reduced permitting time can be larger: Each month of delays due to permitting is estimated to raise construction costs by 1–3 percent, which, in a market like Seattle, can translate to $30,000 per unit, according to Pew. South Bend, Ind., which in 2022 launched one of the first of these programs in the country, reported more than 200 new units using pre-approved plans over three years — roughly 7 percent of all units permitted.

Pre-approved plans are also included as a provision in the 21st Century ROAD to Housing Act, which would encourage their adoption nationwide.

New York City, block by block

New York Mayor Zohran Mamdani had a busy week in housing news, with three overlapping storylines landing on May 26.

The biggest was Mamdani’s release of Block by Block: The Housing Plan for a New Era, a comprehensive blueprint that covers everything from new construction to tenant protections to public housing and homeownership in New York City. The plan’s headline target is 400,000 affordable housing units over 10 years, which will be divided into 200,000 new rent-stabilized homes and 200,000 preserved or stabilized existing ones.

Additional key pieces include a $5.6 billion five-year capital commitment to the city’s housing authority — the largest pledge in decades, according to the plan — paired with a guarantee of continued public ownership and operation. The plan also doubles the size of the Open Door affordable homeownership program, launches a new initiative called “Our Home” to convert rental buildings into co-ops for working-class New Yorkers, and proposes a tenant-based equity pilot designed to allow long-term renters to build wealth connected to their buildings’ appreciation without taking on the risks of outright ownership. The Bronx gets a dedicated multi-agency initiative focused on proactively addressing housing quality, public health, and economic inequality.

The plan is set to officially launch this fall.

The second story, reported by the Wall Street Journal ahead of Tuesday’s announcement, is that the mayor’s proposed rent freeze will come with a carve-out for some distressed landlords. The broader plan also includes easier access to financing for property improvements, tax exemptions, a streamlined pathway to clear housing-code violations, and a city loan program to help landlords cover tenants’ overdue rent and avoid evictions. Eligible owners of certain city-financed, rent-stabilized affordable buildings will be allowed to impose a one-time rent increase on vacant units — determined case-by-case, potentially amounting to hundreds of dollars per month — even if the Rent Guidelines Board enacts a broader freeze at its June vote.

“The reality is, they will all remain affordable,” Housing Preservation and Development Commissioner Dina Levy told the Journal.

The Rent Guidelines Board’s preliminary vote in May set a range of 0–2 percent for one-year lease renewals; tenant advocates are organizing ahead of the final vote, the New York Times reports.

The third piece is that Block by Block also builds on the SPEED reforms Mamdani released May 13 by institutionalizing their targets inside a formal multi-year plan. The SPEED package intends to cut development timelines by up to two years for projects requiring a zoning change; Block by Block commits to holding those gains and extending them to preservation and public housing work as well.

State and local news

California: The San Francisco Chronicle reported this week on the decades-long effort to convert 2,422 acres of the former Concord Naval Weapons Station into a planned community east of Oakland. The property, one of the largest infill development projects in Bay Area history, is now in negotiations with its third master developer, Brookfield Properties, after deals with previous developers collapsed in 2020 and 2023. Brookfield’s plan envisions 12,272 homes, six million square feet of commercial space, and more than 800 acres of green space developed over roughly 30 years, built entirely with union labor, with the first phase slated to break ground in 2030.

Illinois: The state’s spring session ends May 31, and Governor JB Pritzker’s BUILD plan, which includes accessory-dwelling-unit legalization, parking reform, single-stair rules, and middle-housing provisions, has been stalled by a counterproposal from the Illinois Municipal League, which prefers incentives over pre-emption. With just days left in the session, Illinois Senate Democrats unveiled an eight-bill package incorporating altered elements of the BUILD plan alongside progressive initiatives, Capitol News Illinois reports. There’s hope that a consensus will be reached before June.

Maryland: On Tuesday, Governor Wes Moore signed two housing bills. The Maryland Transit and Housing Opportunity Act leverages at least 300 acres of station-adjacent land to enable production of more than 7,000 new housing units and unlock nearly $1.4 billion in state and local tax revenue in a move that targets zoning reforms around high-density rail stations. The Maryland Housing Certainty Act establishes that project approvals are governed by laws in effect at the time an application is submitted, and prohibits local jurisdictions from collecting development impact fees until after construction is complete.

Nationwide supply push

A wave of supply-side housing reforms hit recent legislative sessions, with states across the country experimenting with different avenues for motivating growth. Georgia’s SB 447, signed by Governor Brian Kemp this month, tightens deadlines for permitting decisions. Wyoming’s Fast Track Permits Act creates a 30-day deadline, with permits automatically approved if the deadline is missed. In Kansas, SB 418 allows duplexes, townhomes, and A.D.U.s by right anywhere they are zoned. In Virginia, Governor Abigail Spanberger signed bills designed to legalize A.D.U.s statewide, reduce parking requirements near transit, and allow religious organizations to build affordable housing on their land.
