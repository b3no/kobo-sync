Pub Week: Fucked Up People Are Fucked Up

idealizing the mentally ill is a way to erase them

Freddie deBoer
Oct 26
 
READ IN APP
 

My new book, All In Your Head: Illness as Identity, Trauma as Fashion, and the Desire to be Disordered officially publishes tomorrow. You have to forgive me for being so scattered and unresponsive lately; there’s the book, and trying to will it into the public consciousness, and my wife’s been sick, and there’s our toddler… Well, it’s been a long wait, this book. Will it be my last to be published? I really don’t know! Hard to say. If it is to be my last, then it’s a good one to go out on. I’m proud of it, think it deserves to be read and engaged with, and I hope the powers that be will allow it to reach its audience. Speaking of, if you’re interested you can read this piece in the New York Post, this piece on me in Unherd, or this review from Publisher’s Weekly. There will be more, though I don’t know how much more. Either way, I of course would love it if you would pick up a copy of the book in whatever format you choose, from whatever bookseller, or ask your local library to order it. What you might enjoy the most is walking down to your local bookstore, taking in the ambience for awhile, and picking a copy up. Then read it!

And now perhaps it’s time for me to say, in simple terms, why I wrote it.

I have been saying, for some time, that the unavoidable reflex in contemporary American politics is avoidance. When I say that, I’m referring to a phenomenon that I’ve found increasingly inescapable: critics responding to my arguments not by saying “you’re wrong, and here’s why,” but instead by saying “that doesn’t matter.” It’s a constant, now, a meme; on BlueSky, assertions that something bad doesn’t matter are used like commas. And there’s a lineage. When I started criticizing illiberalism and censorship on college campuses, around 2010 or so, this was the standard response from ordinary liberals - not to say that those campus hijinks were good, not to deny that the students involved were censorious, deluded, and otherwise misguided, but to insist that it just didn’t matter what happened on campus. Who cares what a bunch of college students think!, they would say. I would point out that I’m a creature of academia, that the college campus is my spiritual if not my literal home, and I care about intellectual diversity and academic freedom on campus no matter how trivial that world might have seemed to be. And anyway, campus politics would soon prove to be very consequential no matter your perspective; the social justice politics those campus activists espoused colonized American progressivism with stunning rapidity and effectiveness in the decade that followed.

I bring this up because that’s always been the default response to my disability writing, among a certain kind of person who’s dramatically overrepresented in media - not to argue that I’m wrong, but to insist that what I’m arguing about isn’t important. Sure, the TikTok tics were fake, they say, but who cares? Sure, autism has become trendy, a class signifier and personality surrogate, and rates are rising at impossible speed, but why does it matter? Sure, unscrupulous people request wheelchairs at the airport just to beat the lines, sure, test takers secure bogus accommodations to juice their scores on the SAT and MCAT and LSAT, sure, people pay online “doctors” and fill out a form in three minutes to get an ADHD diagnosis and the amphetamines they seek, sure, but why get mad about that? Sure, our media celebrates the 1% of the mentally ill who can live beautiful lives of romance and creativity without medication, while pushing the actually disabled further and further into the margins, but what are the social consequences of that, really? Again and again and again and again - OK, yes, maybe, but so what, who cares, can’t we talk about something more pleasant? I wrote the book to answer those questions, and for 300 pages I lay out specific, material, and serious consequences of this stuff. I wrote the book to answer the question, why do you care about this stuff? This is why I care.

These answers are, as I said, practical, real, socially consequential. They’re important. But I will freely and proudly admit: the vulgarity is enough. For this anger I carry around with me, for the duty I feel to object, the vulgarity is enough.

Because that is, indeed, what I think - that it’s vulgar for people who are basically fine to playact as deeply sick. It’s vulgar to put the demand for attention ahead of medical need. It’s vulgar to turn suffering into style. It’s vulgar to reduce debilitating disorders to yet more fodder for personal branding. It’s vulgar for self-diagnosed educated professionals to drape themselves with the language of “neurodiversity” while exhausted parents struggle to keep severely autistic children alive. It’s vulgar for the media to constantly hunt for the rare inspiring schizophrenic outlier, trotting them out for the cameras in the name of willpower and good vibes, when so many others rot and die because their brains prevent them from doing any better. It’s vulgar for social media to light up with “mental illness doesn’t do that” whenever the mentally ill act like the mentally ill, gatekeeping disorders which have been documented precisely because they’re so devastating. These things are vulgar, they’re obscene.

Core to my message is this: fucked up people are fucked up.

I have been around a lot of fucked up people in my life, genuinely fucked up people, debilitated people, nonfunctional people. A lot of that, it’s true, has been thanks to my exposure to people in psychiatric contexts, in hospitals, in group therapy, in support groups. But plenty of them, I’ve met other places; plenty of my experience simply comes from being a human being in the world. Wherever I’ve met them, I have on too many occasions in my life met a sick person and said to myself, oh, they’re not gonna make it. No chance. They’re gonna kill themselves, or they’re gonna overdose, or they’re gonna wander into traffic, or they’re just going to end up in prison or sleeping on a storm grate. There is so much devastation, out there, if you find yourself in the position where you’re forced to look. There is so much loss. There are so many people who never really had a chance. And, yes, I think it’s deeply, deeply disordered (to pick a word) that so much of our disability discourse is about the least afflicted, the least in need, the least fucked up. I think in fact that this dynamic is often disgusting. In so many cases - your Ivy League “neurodiversity activists,” your bored white collar workers who think that ADHD is a superpower and that they’re an oppressed class, your too-online adolescent picking and choosing from the DSM like it’s the menu at a chic tapas restaurant - the optics could not possibly be worse, the irony more bitter. I am very willing, indeed proud, to say that while the practical consequences of these bad developments are paramount and come first, I would oppose this ugly behavior with great passion simply because it is so ugly.

We’re living in a period of time where our culture is largely written by a modern aristocracy, the hyper-educated, culturally-savvy, perpetually-anxious strivers, the front-of-class kids, the tryhards. Though achieving that status is no guarantee of any particular material outcome, and though there are some who enjoy it who never achieve affluence, that class is as a whole among the most obviously and materially privileged in the history of our species. They enjoy an unprecedented bounty of monetary and technological and cultural and social wealth, and they collectively define what it means to be enviable in our modern culture, though plenty of us rush to insist that there’s anything to envy. These are the modern American magi. And yet a core, clear lesson of the 21st century is that, for them, the affordances of contemporary urbanite flourishing are not enough. Being smarter than everyone else is not enough. Being more stylish than everyone else is not enough. Being more cultured than everyone else is not enough. Complaining about everything that they don’t have even as they have more than almost everyone is not enough. They need to be morally superior as well; they need to be good. And the product of this deep, grinding, anxious need to be good is what we might call 21st century politics.

That they have started using debilitating, enervating, disabling disorders to further burnish their credentials as beautiful victims is bad enough. That they’re so self-obsessed that they find it necessary to borrow society’s regard for the sick and use it for their own ends is bad enough. But what’s unforgivable is that, in doing so, they have so thoroughly sidelined the actually sick - the gentrification of disability. The book is also that, a catalog of the ways that the people who most need help are slowly, inexorably marginalized in the conversations that are ostensibly about them. When it comes to our moral betters, this exclusion is explicit. Because it’s those people who insist that the various sins that are so often found among the severely mentally ill are disqualifying, that they, we, do not deserve sympathy. I’ve used Kanye West as a convenient shorthand for this tendency before, because he is so quintessentially bipolar and because they hate him so much. But of course the most salient examples are those who will never receive any public attention at all. They’re the ones who need to be forced into the collective attention of our culturati, to make their devastation visible, and this book is my probably-doomed effort to insist that the unpalatable crazies not be erased, even in their ugliness.

And about them, I would say… do you know how many of them have domestic violence arrests? How many have old social media posts with racial slurs in them? Do you know how many have DUIs? How many of them speak and act in ways that horrify the pinched, proud morals of the American cultural upper-class? Do you know how many of them are genuinely, persistently unpleasant people, how guilty they are of being abusive towards others, how unusually likely they are to leer at women, to complain about the Jews? They can do a lot worse, too - most of them are harmless, in the physical sense, but some of them aren’t, and even the ones who aren’t are given to behavior that’s antisocial, sometimes criminal, even when not committing some sort of identity crime. But they do the identity crimes, too. That’s why our moral aristocracy rejects them. And what I’m here to tell you, what my book is here to tell you, is that in rejecting people who do unpalatable things, who commit microaggressions and gender violence and racial offenses, they abandon the disabled writ large. They do this despite the endless pride with which they announce their hatred of stigma and “ableism,” their love for the disabled, their haughty, unthinking embrace of some theoretical category of those who are crazy but woke - a construct, a construct that looks just like them.

There’s no such thing as a love for The Mentally Ill that excludes people who have been accused of stalking, of racism, of those unmentionable modern sins. It would be convenient, so tidy and neat, if there was. But there isn’t. Is that uncomfortable? Yes. That’s why mental illness is a problem from hell. And that’s why you shouldn’t pretend. It’s that hypocrisy that I can’t stand, and that’s why I wrote this book. And I hope that you’ll buy it and read it. Even if you don’t share my anger, maybe you can come to understand it, can come to see that I have earned it, not just that I feel it, but why.
