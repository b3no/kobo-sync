The SAVE America Act (and related orders), explained

Trump says he won’t sign a popular housing bill until Congress passes his voting overhaul.

Jun 26

President Donald Trump’s effort to enact the SAVE America Act, his proposal to overhaul federal election rules, has run into a series of setbacks over the past week. Frustrated by the bill’s lack of progress in the Senate, he’s now threatening to withhold his signature from the bipartisan 21st Century ROAD to Housing Act — the largest housing bill in decades — unless Congress passes his voting legislation. Here’s a breakdown of the bill that would rewrite federal election rules, the administration’s related executive actions, and where those efforts stand.

What is the SAVE America Act?

The Safeguard American Voter Eligibility Act (SAVE Act) — or, in its current, broader form, the SAVE America Act — is a bill moving through Congress that contains two distinct proposals. It would require anyone registering to vote in a federal election to show documentary proof of citizenship such as a passport, a birth certificate, a REAL ID that specifically marks citizenship, or one of a handful of other accepted documents. That would be a change from current law, which instead simply requires attesting to citizenship under penalty of perjury. The bill would also require photo ID to be presented at polling stations and for mail voters to provide either a copy of a photo ID with their ballot or give the last four digits of their Social Security number plus a signed affidavit. This legislation would additionally create a private right of action that would permit individuals, not just government agencies, to sue election officials who register someone without the required documentation.

The bill’s backers, including its sponsors — Representative Chip Roy and Senator Mike Lee — call it a basic integrity measure. Noncitizen voting in federal elections is already a felony, and election administrators who have studied the issue determined that audits have found almost no actual cases of it — a 2025–26 citizenship review of Utah’s entire voter file turned up one confirmed noncitizen registration and zero noncitizen votes, the Salt Lake Tribune reports.

So this bill, if passed, would likely do little to solve a problem that is already exceedingly rare. Rather, it would more consequentially tighten voter access: An estimated 21 million eligible citizens lack ready access to a passport or birth certificate, according to data from the Brennan Center for Justice, and the requirement would apply to people updating an existing registration as well as new registrants.

Passport ownership skews heavily by state. Fewer than a third of citizens hold one in West Virginia and Mississippi, while more than two-thirds do in New York and California, Center for American Progress data shows. Much of the debate around this bill assumes making it harder to vote would advantage Republicans, but passport-owners are surely a liberal-leaning group.

Where the bill came from

The SAVE Act first appeared in 2024, as Trump and his allies pushed claims about noncitizen voting that election researchers have not been able to substantiate. The bill died at the end of that Congress. Republicans reintroduced it in January 2025, and the House passed it in April on a 220-to-208 vote, with four Democrats joining every Republican. The Senate took no action.

A broader version of the bill was introduced in early 2026 — this time it was called the SAVE America Act — and passed the House on a 218-to-213 vote. It has been sitting there since because getting a bill to a final Senate vote generally takes 60 votes to clear a filibuster, and Republicans are far from the seven Democratic votes that would be required.

The bill hasn’t budged, and Trump has had enough.

How a housing bill got caught in the middle

On Tuesday, Congress passed the 21st Century ROAD to Housing Act, a massive, bipartisan housing package. In a shocking feat of cross-party collaboration, it passed the Senate 85 to 5 and the House 358 to 32. Vibes were high in Congress and in YIMBYland, and a signing ceremony was set for Wednesday in the Capitol. Trump canceled it hours beforehand, posting on social media that he was holding off until Congress sent him the SAVE America Act, which he called an urgent priority while downplaying the housing bill’s importance by comparison. He’d signaled this before: In March, he wrote that the voting bill came ahead of any other legislative priority.

Senate Majority Leader John Thune has declined to eliminate the filibuster to push the SAVE America Act through, and House Speaker Mike Johnson has raised the possibility of attaching election provisions to a future budget reconciliation bill, which would require only a simple majority. Reconciliation rules limit what can be included, and it’s unclear election policy would qualify, The Hill reports.

Meanwhile the housing bill sits unsigned. Under the Constitution, it becomes law automatically after 10 days, excluding Sundays, if Trump takes no action while Congress is in session, unless Congress adjourns first, which would expose it to a pocket veto. Johnson said on Thursday that he would send the bill to the president. Whether Trump will sign it is anyone’s guess.

The separate fight on voting this week

While the SAVE America Act is stuck in the Senate, the administration has tried to reach some of the same goals, including tighter mail-ballot rules and new federal voter lists, through executive action instead. In March, Trump signed an order directing the Postal Service and the Department of Homeland Security to compile lists of citizens or eligible voters and directing the U.S.P.S. to withhold mail ballots from anyone not on those lists.

That order, like much of this agenda, has run into the same obstacle in the courts. On Thursday, U.S. District Judge Indira Talwani in Boston blocked U.S.P.S. and D.H.S. from carrying out those directives in roughly two dozen mostly Democratic-led states and Washington, D.C., that had sued.

Talwani’s opinion concluded that election administration belongs to states and Congress under the Constitution, not the president, and that the federal Help America Vote Act does not give the president any such authority.

“Notably, nowhere in HAVA does Congress prescribe who should be included on state voter lists,” she wrote. “Further, neither in HAVA nor any other federal statute does Congress authorize the federal government to create their own voting database. Instead, Congress, consistent with the Constitution, has left that authority to the states alone.”

It was the third such setback for the administration’s voting agenda this week, after another judge on Wednesday made permanent an earlier block on citizenship-documentation requirements from a 2025 executive order and a third judge ruled against a related federal voter-data system on Monday. The White House has indicated it will appeal, NPR reports.

While the legislative SAVE America Act and the executive orders are different tools, they are aimed at overlapping goals. For now, both efforts are stalled — one by a Senate without the votes, the other by the courts.

With midterm primaries underway and the general election several months out, the practical window for any of this to change how the 2026 elections are run is narrowing.
