Republicans are scrambling to beat the odds

Still, Democrats aren’t betting on a wave.

Halina Bennet
Oct 21
∙
Paid
 
READ IN APP
 

Less than two weeks out from Election Day, Democrats nationwide are in a good, not great, position. Polling and vibes are in the party’s favor — they’ve been shifting leftward for months — but that tilt isn’t overwhelming, and everyone is haunted by fears of a systematic polling error. The momentum, which appears to be quite real, could, of course, come up short on Election Day. Still, most roads point left.

The New York Times/Siena poll published Wednesday showed Democrats leading Republicans by 11 percentage points — 52 percent to 41 percent — among likely voters when they are asked about specific House candidates, a gap that widened slightly from the Times’ previous survey in September. This latest poll, conducted among 3,494 voters from October 12 to 19, found that among registered voters, the divide is 51 percent to 39 percent, respectively, when respondents are asked about House candidates. President Donald Trump’s approval rating stands at 36 percent among likely voters, a three-point drop from the September poll. Fifty-nine percent of likely voters disapprove of the job he’s doing. The New York Times reports that this is the lowest approval rating for any president before a midterm election since Harry Truman. Just ahead of Trump is George W. Bush in 2006.

Democrats in the October poll lead on trust across every issue surveyed and published, from the cost of living (55 percent to 40 percent among likely voters) and immigration (50 percent to 46 percent among likely voters) to foreign wars (51 percent to 41 percent among likely voters) and artificial intelligence and data centers (48 percent to 37 percent among likely voters).

Despite their party’s apparent lead, Democrats remain wary. As the Washington Post puts it, “Democrats are up in the polls. They’re sick to their stomachs.”

“All these races that are showing we’ve got a two- or three-point lead or it’s a tossup, we could end up losing all of them by two points,” former President Barack Obama said at a fundraiser in Virginia, the Post reports. “It’s within the margin of error.”

Joe Bolkcom, a former Democratic state senator in Iowa, told the Post that the party has some PTSD around being too optimistic. In the Hawkeye State, a poll in 2024 showed Kamala Harris winning there days before the election. She lost it to Trump by 13 percentage points.

The New York Times confronted the problem of precision in polls, explaining that though some have inflated Democrats’ chances of winning many times, the level of success among Democratic candidates in polls of red states during a Trump term is unprecedented.

“There is no reason to assume that the polls will systematically overstate the Democrats’ standing as they did in the 2016 and 2020 presidential elections,” the Times reports. “In fact, the polls haven’t really done so in midterm or off-year elections during the Trump era, including the 2018 or 2022 midterms. Just last November, the polls actually underestimated Democrats in New Jersey and Virginia — and by a lot.”

So what are the Democrats’ chances of winning the House? The Senate? Both? I’m no oracle, and neither are the betting markets or the polls (and some of those polls, most recently Wedgewood, are altogether fake), so I can’t possibly know. What I do know is where some of the key races stand.

The Senate

Michigan: A Marist poll taken October 15 through 18 had Abdul El-Sayed, the Democratic nominee for Senate, at 50 percent and Republican Mike Rogers at 48 percent support among likely voters. El-Sayed is up 52 percent to 45 percent among registered voters.

That lead, while a lead indeed, is small; it’s far smaller than it should be for a Democrat running in a true purple state when Republicans’ and the G.O.P. president’s favorability are so low, even within their own party. This race, like nearly every pivotal race for Democrats, will come down to turnout.

“In a close race, even a sliver of doubt about Abdul could lead to defeat,” an anonymous Black leader told CNN. “And this race feels far closer than it should.”

Alaska: Republicans are worried about their party’s incumbent Senator Dan Sullivan, who faces a formidable opponent in former Representative Mary Peltola. Making matters worse for them, another candidate named Dan Sullivan continues to hang on in the race, creating an unsolvable problem: He could siphon votes from their guy.

The state uses a ranked-choice voting system, which Politico reports is worrying Republicans because the non-incumbent Sullivan could take first-choice votes from the senator. Some voters who mean to support the incumbent might even accidentally skip ranking him.

Recent polls have Peltola winning after ranked-choice tabulation. As Sullivan continues to slip in the polls, some Republicans are quick to point to the system as the reason why he might come up short.

“It’s what the race is going to come down to: If he loses, it’ll for sure be because of that. And if he wins, it’ll be in spite of that,” a Republican operative in Alaska told Politico.

Meanwhile, Trump has waded into the race in a last-minute attempt to sway voters. He has made big promises, including broadband investment and pardons for two Alaska auto mechanics who violated clean air regulations, Politico reports.

Maine: The final debate between incumbent Republican Senator Susan Collins and her opponent, Democrat Troy Jackson, became heated. Jackson told Collins to “shut up” after she asserted that a protester, a Bowdoin College student with a fake press credential who interrupted the debate by yelling about his opposition to data centers, “was obviously one of Troy’s supporters,” the Portland Press Herald reports.

“Oh, shut up. I don’t even know that kid,” he replied.

The comment overshadowed the rest of the debate. The Maine G.O.P. called Jackson’s remark unacceptable, and Collins said on Fox News that she was struck by his comment and that “the fact that it seemed like such an automatic, quick reply was very telling.”

Jackson doubled down, telling CNN during the “State of the Union” that Collins had accused him of planting the protester.

Kansas: Incumbent Senator Roger Marshall, the Republican nominee, has no one on payroll, but he does have Trump stumping for him. The senator’s opponent, Adam Hamilton, told CNN he was glad the president showed up and it was a sign that “the Republican Party felt they needed to send the most powerful man in the country to try to save Roger Marshall, and I think it’s only going to hurt him.”

Hamilton raised $10.6 million in the third quarter — outraising Marshall and nearly tripling his second-quarter haul of $3.66 million after Democrats became bullish on his race during the campaign’s final months.

Ramaswamy’s bad bet

This week’s biggest controversy was in the Ohio governor’s race. A pro-Vivek Ramaswamy ad was released on Monday and featured his Democratic opponent Amy Acton’s estranged mother attacking Acton, saying, “It’s not easy for a mother to say this, but Amy was a serious problem. She is a liar; she lies about her past.”

Acton was removed from her birth mother’s home as a child after her mother’s husband was charged with raping her when she was between 10 and 12 years old.

The ad immediately drew bipartisan backlash and set off an uproar. Ramaswamy’s primary opponent, Casey Putsch, said on Tuesday in a post on X that he is “crossing party lines to vote for Amy and end Vivek’s political future in America.” Republican James Fishback called on Ramaswamy to drop out of the race, calling the ad “disgraceful” and saying that his actions are hurting other Republicans, echoing other G.O.P. calls for Ramaswamy to distance his campaign from the ad.

Acton’s campaign released an ad on Tuesday that features Acton’s brother discussing the physical and sexual abuse that she and, to a lesser extent, he faced and calling Ramaswamy “not fit to lead.”

Against warnings from members of the G.O.P. that the ad was in poor taste and would hurt their candidate’s chances, Ramaswamy’s campaign on Tuesday took credit for producing it and doubled down by spending $4 million to launch a statewide ad campaign that began Tuesday night, Axios reports.

That might have been a bad bet.

The race was already tight. A Marist poll shows Acton ahead by seven percentage points, and Decision Desk HQ moved the race from a tossup to leaning Democratic. Time magazine reports that Ramaswamy is facing a much tougher fight than Republicans had expected. After Ramaswamy’s ad ran, Acton’s Kalshi odds of winning spiked.

Will money and Trump hurt or save the G.O.P?

The National Republican Senatorial Committee — the campaign arm of the Senate G.O.P. — took out $20 million in loans in September, while the Republican National Committee, which entered September with $126 million cash on hand, put in $50 million for House and Senate candidates. The G.O.P. has a significant cash advantage, but Democrats also got a boost this week when former Mayor Michael Bloomberg gave them $80 million.

The New York Times reports that much of the G.O.P.’s financial advantage is in Senate races, not House ones, which the party believes it is less likely to win. Cumulatively, the G.O.P. has spent nearly a billion dollars on Senate ads and nearly a third of that on ads supporting House candidates. Those ads, the Times found, have included fewer and fewer mentions of Trump as Election Day draws closer. Meanwhile, Democrats are including him in their spots more and more, attempting to tie his increasingly underwater approval ratings to their Republican opponents.

Still, Trump is showing up at rallies across the country — mostly in places where he won handily in 2024, like Dalton, Ga., where he campaigned for Senate candidate Mike Collins and gubernatorial nominee Rick Jackson. Vice President JD Vance appeared at a CBS town hall in Michigan on Saturday, where he faced a relatively unfriendly crowd, the Washington Post reports. The husband of an Army master sergeant who was killed in an Iranian drone strike in Kuwait in March asked him about the failures that led to her death, and a Catholic nun pressed him on his treatment of immigrants.
