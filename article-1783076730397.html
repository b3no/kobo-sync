Reformist Democrats need more team spirit

Plus how the Tea Party was misunderstood, N.Y.C. media, and Andy Beshear’s presidential campaign

Almost all of the questions today were about Democrats’ ongoing tea party moment in primaries, which got me thinking about the actual Tea Party of the Obama years.

At the time, the Tea Party talked an enormous amount about government spending and budget deficits, and mainstream Republicans largely treated it as a movement about fiscal conservatism. A desire to harness Tea Party energy was part of why Mitt Romney picked Paul Ryan as his running mate and challenged Obama on a platform of steep spending cuts rather than Moderate Massachusetts Mitt pragmatism. Around this time, I worked with Vanessa Williamson’s husband and I knew Theda Skocpol from college, so I was aware of the book the two of them wrote in 2013 called “The Tea Party and the Remaking of Republican Conservatism.” It was a sociological deep dive into Tea Party activism and featured the striking and contrarian claim that actually the number one issue motivating the movement was immigration. Very few people in 2013 agreed with that, but after watching Donald Trump’s 2016 primary win, I think everyone familiar with this literature reached the conclusion that Williamson and Skocpol were right all along.

I raise this because, while we’ve had a ton of takes about what is driving the restive left on the Democratic side, I don’t think we’ve had anything equivalent to Williamson and Skocpol in the sense of a genuinely good-faith, curiosity-motivated look at what’s going on. This is not to say I have my own secret theory. I also don’t want to overread the analogy. Just because the Tea Party seemed to be about one thing and turned out to be about another doesn’t mean the left insurgency is like that. It may just be that it’s actually a bad analogy. But I do want to draw the distinction between disinterested scholarly inquiry and factional or partisan combat. A lot of influential figures in right-wing politics really wanted it to be the case that the Tea Party was about widespread demand for entitlement cuts, and they successfully imposed that interpretation on the world for a period of years until it all blew up in their face.

lindamc: I’m a Common Sense Dem at the end of my tether. I split my time between DC, with an incoming socialist mayor, and Ann Arbor MI, where socialists are seeking to unseat the local government that has (as noted in a recent mailbag) finally started building here. Everyone knows about the Senate race 😩. Many/most youngs I know are self-described socialists with little/no understanding of what that means in historic terms, and the overwhelming majority of my fellow olds are Heather Cox Richardsonians. Virtually every social gathering I attend features someone making a glib, ignorant complaint about data centers. A “swing dancing in the park” event I attended a few days ago started out with pronouns and cultural appropriation and never made it to demonstrating actual dance steps. Things seem to somehow get worse and worse.

How do you cope with this? Despite fighting hard against it, I feel something like actual despair a lot of the time.

I’m feeling a little bit untethered myself this summer.

But one thing that I try to remind myself of is that a lot of what I am experiencing ambiently is not so much a real political trend as the output of the extraordinary New York-centricity of the national media.

Imagine if the whole national press were concentrated in San Francisco. You’d have stories talking about how Dan Lurie is the most popular mayor in America. But they’d also be talking about how his successes were really prefigured by Brooke Jenkins. And about how part of why both Lurie and Jenkins are succeeding is that they belong to a broader reform faction in San Francisco politics that’s winning Board of Supervisors races and contesting internal Democratic Party offices and poised to make a run at the school board. You’d see Matt Mahan down the road in San Jose. You’d talk about how his gubernatorial bid fizzled, but you’d also know Tom Steyer’s leftist gubernatorial bid fizzled, too.

And if the press were a little less New York-centric, we’d hear more about Chicago, too: The left won power there, and it’s been an embarrassing failure they don’t talk about anymore.

An electoral backlash to Mayor Brandon Johnson is clearly coming, and the only real question is whether it will be a retreat to status quo mainstream Democratic governance or if we’ll see a more dramatic form of pushback. A City That Works, which began as a Chicago-focused Substack very much aligned with Slow Boring values, has now launched a PAC that is going to try to get not just a good mayor but some good City Council members elected to bring some of the San Francisco magic to the Windy City.

From a D.C. perspective, I am sad that Kenyan McDuffie lost.

But it’s possible that the only way to move forward from where D.C. was under Muriel Bowser’s third term was to take this detour. Voters in the city wanted change, and McDuffie read as continuity. Something I tried to say to people (mostly unsuccessfully) is that the direction of change matters. But now that there’s been a regime change in the city, the opening exists to articulate a smart reformist critique of the status quo and to find some outsider figures to run for office on a smart program.

Even in New York, where a lot of attention has been paid to Darializa Avila Chevalier’s continued advocacy for prison abolition, it’s clear that Zohran Mamdani understands perfectly well that a successful mayor needs a safe city.

As someone who was engaged in a lot of very nasty disputes about policing in the summer of 2020, I am personally extremely annoyed that the left’s surrender on this has taken the form of acting peevish about anyone raising their old tweets. I would love for someone to write on the internet, “You know what, I disagree with Matt Yglesias about a lot of things, but not only was he correct about police defunding, he was gutsy to say so at a time when it was controversial.” To the best of my knowledge, nobody at all has ever done that even as virtually the whole left has moved toward my view. So I’m aggravated about the situation and I find the discourse — especially in N.Y.C.-centric media — to be fairly despair-inducing.

But while the situation is somewhat grave, that’s mostly just because politics is important and the stakes always feel high. The actual trends across the country are fairly mixed and there are reasons for both optimism and pessimism.

Where I do give the left credit is on two related points where I think there are valuable lessons to be learned.

One is that they have real team spirit. Bernie Sanders has been tireless and fairly selfless in touring the country to elevate the profile of Bernie-aligned candidates and help them raise money. Then his more successful acolytes like Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez pay it forward and do the same. And even the different members of this movement say they are members of a movement rather than insisting that each one is a beautiful unique snowflake.

The other point is that they are not terrified of losing.

It’s easy to imagine a world where Graham Platner’s campaign ended up looking like an embarrassing failure rather than a striking success. And Steyer’s campaign really was an embarrassing failure. But having failed in an embarrassing way with Steyer, they just moved on to some wins in New York and then talked a lot about those wins. I know people who were involved in directing money to Baldacci and then immediately felt like they were in hot water for being involved in something that failed. But the fact is that he came very close to winning his primary and he was the right candidate. It’s too bad that more wasn’t done to support him (and that he’s not more mediagenic), but you need to try things. And you need to have some grace for people who try things and it doesn’t work out.

lwdlyndale: The Times finally decided to write something about Andy Beshear and his bid for the White House: Do you think there’s any chance we could see some real #Andymentum in the 2028 cycle, or do you think, like most of the chattering classes, that he’s obviously doomed?

On the subject of trying things, I think the problem with Beshear-type politicians is that they are excessively risk-averse.

There’s a level where, yes, Beshear seems like a strong candidate on paper, especially for primary voters who say electability is their top priority. But to beat Republicans, he needs the nomination first. So the easy strategy is to just emphasize his winning credentials in a non-specific way and say he’s not a far-left socialist but also not say anything that could alienate any mainstream Democrats whose support he needs. The problem with this is that it’s boring and indistinct and vague and ultimately leaves him with no firm footing when inevitable misfortunes come his way.

An alternative strategy would be to:

  1. Pick some positions that are popular with the median voter in Kentucky but unpopular with most Democratic Party elected officials.

  2. Articulate those positions clearly and say they make you a different kind of Democrat and that’s why you can win nationally.

  3. Make the case that the path to progressive goals is to be a 50-state party that can expand Medicaid, crush MAGA, and enact big durable change in the country.

This will probably not work! The odds are good that step one just kills you. But it might work. If he tried it, I would say nice things about him. And so would other people. And some others would say mean things about him. And there would be a proper argument about it. The problem is that if you’re Beshear, the default strategy might be to run a lame campaign that stirs no controversy and fails for obvious reasons but sets you up for a cabinet job. What I want to see is him try a higher variance strategy where he really explains what people in Kentucky think and care about and where he challenges presidential primary voters to care about winning in places like that.

Jesse Ewiak: It’s the day after Election Day in 2026.

  • Graham Platner has won by 6 to 7 points.

  • Abdul El-Sayed has won by 3 to 4 points.

How cooked is the moderate wing in the upcoming 2028 primaries and should they just put their power behind somebody like Ossoff instead of attempting to slam Shapiro or someone similar through the primaries?

I think this is the wrong way to think about it. What is “the moderate wing” at this point, really? To me the worst way to think about 2028 is to start with the idea that there’s A.O.C. and everyone else is “moderate,” so Democrats need to try our hardest to elect a moderate (i.e. not A.O.C.) and therefore decide if we should get behind Shapiro or Newsom or Ossoff or who. What I want to do is start with the values of the Common Sense Democrat Manifesto and then back whichever candidate best embodies those values. I am open to that person being Shapiro or Ossoff or someone else or even A.O.C.

I want to be a policy demander and see some elements of the field try to move toward my views.

Damian: I listened to Matt’s recent podcast ranking Biden’s mistakes and wanting to discredit Bidenism. This made me wonder what Biden got right (an admittedly short list) that Matt’s would want to see replicated in Democratic politics.

Biden got a lot of things right in the sense that I agree with his position on abortion rights and I think it’s correct to enforce the Clean Air Act rather than try to bring back coal. I thought both the enhanced Affordable Care Act subsidies and the clean-energy-production tax credits in the Inflation Reduction Act were good ideas.

But I think the spirit of the question is more what did Biden bring to the table distinctively that Democrats writ large should learn from. And here I think it’s not that much. Criticisms of Biden’s handling of the Israel/Gaza conflict are so over the top that I count as a defender of the administration — I just do not think it’s true that they were complicit in the perpetration of a genocide. And unlike Trump, they were able to avoid getting dragged into a war with Iran. But if you think about the administration’s policy goals as of October 8, 2023, they did not succeed.

Elsewhere in foreign policy, though, I think Biden was more successful. His Ukraine policy basically worked. The various export controls related to A.I. were good and involved a fair amount of deft diplomatic work. To me it’s a shame that the conventional wisdom is to shrink all of foreign policy down to Israel and then condemn the Biden team for failing to bring the world’s most famously intractable conflict to a satisfactory resolution. Biden was, by background and inclination, a guy who was most knowledgeable about foreign affairs and most interested in foreign affairs, and he did his best work in that area. Withdrawing from Afghanistan, for example, though politically costly, was the correct call. Even if it was not executed perfectly, it was executed much better than the previous 20 years of war.

On domestic issues, it’s not that it was all bad. It’s that it wasn’t really Biden’s focus, so he was mostly just going along with the consensus. This led to an egregious failure around immigration, because the consensus was kind of incoherent, and insofar as the economic ideas differed from Bill Clinton or Barack Obama they were worse.

City of Trees: Why isn’t rent seeking as captivating to people who think that things are rigged against society? To me, it seems like a very straightforward case of government and businesses colluding with each other to enrich the rent seekers at the expense of the citizens. But it doesn’t seem to motivate as much as more complex conspiracy theories, accusations of concentrated corporate power, or the like. I have some guesses of my own, but what’s your take?

“Rent-seeking” itself is, obviously, a kind of technical term that is not likely to capture people’s imagination.

But I agree that it seems like there should potentially be a politics in saying “nefarious forces are using political power to rig the economy and make you worse off” and then instead of naming fake stuff you name the real stuff.

A partial answer is that by far the most economically consequential form of rent-seeking is in the real estate market and there actually is a pretty robust YIMBY movement in the United States that is winning some elections and passing some laws. So I think the question is does what’s worked for YIMBY politics generalize? Here it’s tough because the economic gains from tackling any one non-housing set of rent-seekers is pretty limited. If you take a very right-coded group of rent-seeking interests, like car dealership owners, it’s pretty easy to go viral on Bluesky with a call to crack down on them. But doing actual political organizing against an entrenched lobby is harder and the actual upside of reforming car-dealership-franchise laws is modest.

Now if you go bigger and say we’re taking on the car dealers and the dentists and the American Medical Association and also the Jones Act and promoting port automation and all the rest, then you have substantial political upside. But to do that, you need to be willing to take on interest groups that are not as heavily right-coded, including labor unions. And to do that you need to challenge the big-picture conceptual framework that is deeply entrenched on the left, which holds that since 1980 or so, the United States has been ruled by something called “neoliberalism” that is characterized by helter-skelter deregulation. In a lot of ways I actually think that’s a bigger barrier than anything more properly structural, like union power. A funny thing about the D.S.A. primary wins is that in almost every case, they are beating solidly progressive Democratic incumbents over the objections of public sector unions. Mamdani doesn’t owe anything at all to New York City labor.

But ideology matters. The people voting for D.S.A. candidates are overwhelmingly not working-class themselves, but they have a very sincere romantic relationship with the idea of working-class politics, which involves an idea of labor union politics that instinctively rejects the idea that some maritime union whose members probably aren’t even progressive at all is posing bigger problems for average people’s economic well-being than anything Jeff Bezos has ever done.

srynerson: Would you support a constitutional amendment to increase House terms to four years, with 50% of the House being up for reelection every two years, so there are still House elections every two years?

I’m not sure there’s a good word to describe what I want to say here, but there’s a category of reform proposals that strike me as probably a good idea (and thus, in some sense, I support them), but I would really not recommend dedicating any time, money, or mental energy to the cause.

The United States House of Representatives suffers from a very large and worsening problem of partisan gerrymandering. Not only does this cause a lot of direct bad consequences, but it is creating a tendency to turn state elections just into proxy wars over gerrymandering and that’s going to have really bad downstream consequences for the quality of state government. There is also a very straightforward solution to the problem of partisan gerrymandering, namely proportional representation. This would be a heavy lift to get done, but it’s not impossible and it would have big benefits. There are plausible arguments for other reforms and I’m not against them, but I think it’s a bad idea to get distracted by them.
