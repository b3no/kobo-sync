The Western water crisis has a simple solution

Stop giving the majority of the water away to farmers for almost nothing!

Matthew Yglesias
Aug 25
∙
Paid
 
READ IN APP
 

Any time I read about water scarcity issues in the United States, I get kind of annoyed.

For starters, the United States is a land of relatively abundant water. Our freshwater resources per capita are above average globally, and while we’re no Canada or Brazil, we easily outpace the world’s other major states in terms of water availability.

We do have a problem, though: While on the whole the United States is quite wet, a significant region of the country is quite arid.

The good news is that a sizable majority of the population lives in the well-watered Eastern United States. Indeed, if you look at a map of the United States at night, the divide is striking. You can trace I-35 from Fort Worth up through Oklahoma City and Wichita, then northeast to Kansas City, Des Moines, and Minneapolis. Way more people live east of that line than west of it, and this historical settlement pattern basically comes down to water availability.

But the Western United States is quite beautiful, and the advent of air conditioning means that places like Las Vegas and Phoenix are now livable, which has created more and more issues around water scarcity in the dry part of the country. A lot of the water used in this dry region comes from the Colorado River and is managed carefully as a critical natural resource. Unfortunately, there just isn’t enough water in the Colorado River to meet demand, and this drives most of the conflicts around water use.

Last week the Trump administration sharply cut the water available to Arizona, California, and Nevada. This is, as far as I can tell, the right thing to do. But it’s going to make those conflicts even more intense.

Part of my annoyance with this discourse is that the relatively small minority of the population that lives in the relevant part of the country has managed to inflict its various water-related neuroses on America as a whole. You often get the sense from policy debates that the United States in general suffers from critical water shortages and that we all need to spend a lot of time worrying about this.

Even worse, the whole Western water conflict has an incredibly simple solution: End federal law’s current stipulation that a huge share of the available water must be sold at very low prices exclusively for the purpose of agriculture.

The issue here is well known, well understood, and not particularly controversial. It’s just that nobody seems to want to actually fix it.

Water, water everywhere

The American Prospect is not normally where I turn for advocacy in support of free markets and strong property rights. And yet Ryan Cooper, in a casual aside in an article advocating that we completely drain the Lake Powell reservoir to reduce wasteful evaporation, points out the actual problem here is that “cattle feed farmers paying $30 an acre-foot (or about 0.009 cents per gallon) while cities pay between $500 and $2,000 per acre-foot is preposterous.”

As Cooper says, “give something extremely valuable away for almost free, and people will waste it.”

Back in 2022, Shawn Regan wrote a big National Review article about Lake Powell running dry, and he mentioned somewhat casually that agricultural water prices in the West are low — sometimes only a few pennies per thousand gallons — “owing in part to federally subsidized water projects and limitations on transferring water to municipal uses that are valued more highly.”

Way back in 1986, when the problem of acute Western water scarcity was largely hypothetical, Reason magazine ran a piece lambasting the region’s then very right-wing politicians for their failure to appreciate prices and markets. Vincent Carroll argued that “before the job of conservation is everywhere turned over to the regulators, Western states — which, after all, give conservatives some of their most lopsided political victories — might try pricing water at its true cost and uprooting obstacles to its free exchange.”

All of this is correct. There’s nothing new or interesting to say about it.

Water is both rivalrous and excludable. If you don’t want people to waste it, you should create economic incentives to conserve it. Either it should be expensive for people to buy water, or else they should be able to sell the water they save to someone else. Obviously the option you pick makes a lot of difference to the interest group politics involved. But conceptually “you can use a ton of water for your farm if you want but it will cost you dearly” and “here’s a bunch of free water for your farm but also you can sell it to someone else if you want to” are the same solution.

The point, either way, is to shift the scarce water to high-value uses and to shift low-value water-intensive activity to other parts of the country where water is plentiful. But even though I can cite numerous articles from progressive magazines and right-wing think tanks all advocating the correct solution, it keeps not getting implemented.

It’s the alfalfa, stupid

I came to this originally as a lifelong Northeasterner and housing guy.

Over here, “but the water!” as an objection to new housing is almost purely pretextual. It is true that if a given area lacks a municipal water and sewer system, that limits how much housing it’s feasible to allow before wells run dry or get contaminated by sewage. But that just goes to show that if there’s meaningful housing demand, you should build a water and sewer system (in New England, some places refuse to build out sewers in order to block housing).

That’s not the situation in the West, though. The amount of water available for Western suburbanites is genuinely constrained by scarcity.

But that’s not because household water use is especially high. Here’s a great graphic reproduced from a Nature article by Brian Richter et al. that shows that total use of Colorado River Basin water for all household and industrial uses is less — a lot less — than the use of water for growing alfalfa.

“The whole crisis is because of alfalfa” sounds ridiculous, in part because alfalfa is a funny word. But also because it’s so random. Wheat? That’s in bread and pasta. Everyone loves wheat. Cotton? That’s my shirt, I get why we need cotton.

But alfalfa?

Alfalfa is grown as animal feed. But the particular absurdity of the situation is that it’s grown precisely because it’s a smart choice when water is cheap. The great thing about alfalfa is that it’s a perennial plant and you can harvest it by machine, so the cost structure of alfalfa cultivation is dominated by water. Note, though, that the high alfalfa share here isn’t because alfalfa is a particularly thirsty crop. It’s not like the almond situation where the water-intensity is through the roof. There’s just been a big pivot to alfalfa because demand for alfalfa — from Western dairies but also from places like Saudi Arabia and China — is high.

So I want to be clear that I’m not scapegoating the alfalfa farmers. The issue is that Western water rights are governed by a mix of “prior appropriation” (places that claimed water early get to keep using it) and “beneficial use” (if you’re using the water, you get to keep it, but if you’re not, someone else can have it) and this incentivizes agricultural rights-owners to use water as if it were plentiful even though it isn’t.

A rational allocation would be better

I remember the first time I went to Arizona and was a little shocked to see cotton farming. I asked a guy, “Isn’t this the desert?” and he pointed out that in a lot of ways the desert is the ideal place to farm. After all, plants need sunshine. Rain and cloud cover are bad for crop growth. If you think about the history of agriculture, the cradles of civilization were along riverbanks in Iraq and Egypt — i.e., the desert — because the optimal place to farm is a place that has water but no rain. Indeed, to this day Egypt has some of the most productive agriculture on Earth on a per-acre basis.

So on some level, I get it. Farming in the most arid part of the country isn’t a totally crazy idea.

But in the modern world, this is just not a very high-value industry. The entire American alfalfa crop is worth about $8 billion in revenue. Even in a bad year, the casinos of Las Vegas earn more than twice that. Arizona has attracted tens of billions of dollars’ worth of investment in semiconductor manufacturing. New housing in California is, obviously, incredibly valuable.

It makes no sense that because agricultural users got to the water first, it has to be used for agriculture forever.

In some sense, the most reasonable thing would be to just charge everyone the same amount for water. That would be a financial windfall for the residential users throughout the region and would cause a natural shrinkage of the agricultural footprint, as much of the current farming activity would become uneconomical.

The real point here, though, isn’t that the farmers are living high on the hog and need to be taken down a peg. You could easily let them keep their windfall water rights but turn those rights into property that they’re able to sell. Then existing cities or industrial users or real estate developers looking to build new housing or whoever else could just buy out the alfalfa farmers. The land currently under cultivation could shift to less water-intensive uses. Even a golf course uses significantly less water per acre than an alfalfa farm. Much of the land would, of course, turn into housing, but some could become non-irrigated parks with native vegetation.

Farming activity would shift to the parts of the country that have less sunshine but more water — which historically of course is where most farming has happened, with situations like the Nile being exceptional.

Toward water abundance

There’s even more that can be done beyond rational allocation of property rights.

The United States is a water-rich country with some arid regions. But there are plenty of countries that are entirely arid. In the United Arab Emirates and Israel, around 80 percent of their freshwater comes from desalination plants. In Singapore, it’s 25 percent.

In America, there’s no reason to contemplate desalination on that scale. But it could make a lot of sense in Southern California, where many people live and where many more people will live in the future if recent statewide YIMBY legislation is efficacious. Southern California is very nice and, as evidenced by the extremely high cost of land, tons of people would like to live there. If that became possible, housing-wise, I’m sure they’d be happy to pay a modest price premium for desalinated water rather than the current astronomical premium for scarce land.

But the last time someone tried to build a desalination plant there, it got blocked by the California Coastal Commission.

The larger story is that desalinating water takes electricity, and part of the case for energy abundance is that it lets you overcome water scarcity.

For now, though, I still think this kind of focus on larger solutions is probably unnecessary or at least premature. The overwhelmingly dominant issue in the arid West is that water is moderately scarce — not U.A.E. scarce, but scarce enough to cause problems — yet a huge share of the available water is set aside for agriculture on terms that encourage agricultural users to deploy it as if it’s not scarce.

This is a silly way to allocate resources, and everything is downstream of that.

I get that the current approach is deeply rooted in the traditions of the American West, but this is just another example of complicated customary property rights being worse than simple ones. If you don’t want to take away the farmers’ windfalls, then let them formalize their windfalls by selling the rights. The problem will then be solved for an indefinite span of time, giving us ample opportunity to work on desalination and other issues.
