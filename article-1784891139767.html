I wrote about Troy Jackson for the New York Times.

He is not totally my cup of tea as a politician and he has some weak spots, but he was a consistent electoral overperformer in his State Senate district, and while that diminished somewhat as he became an orthodox progressive on abortion and L.G.B.T. issues, he still ran very strong in 2022. Past performance is no guarantee of future political success, but it’s a decent indicator. And I’m happy progressives found someone like that to be happy with. We’re like a million light-years from this being the case, but if the default strategy for selecting House candidates (and downballot statewide offices) was “find a state legislator who overperforms” and the default strategy for selecting Senate and gubernatorial candidates was “find a House member or downballot statewide officeholder who overperforms,” we’d be in a much better place.

One reason this would be good is it would encourage ambitious backbench politicians in safe seats to actually care about electoral performance. I don’t totally know why Rashida Tlaib’s electoral performance (about neutral in 2020, bad in 2022, actually a small overperformance in 2024) is so much better than Ilhan Omar’s (downright terrible in all four of her races, with her best one being far worse than Tlaib’s worst), but I wish this was something Tlaib got credit for and Omar faced pressure to work on because the gap is genuinely enormous.

Matt Bruzunski: Who do you think is the best speaker in Dem politics right now and do you agree with my assessments here: Jon Ossoff gives excellent prepared speeches, but I’ve found him a little dull in informal interviews, though not awkward in the way Kamala Harris sometimes was, despite also being a strong stump speaker. AOC is almost the reverse she’s great in interviews and off the cuff settings, but less impressive in formal speeches. Among the likely 2028 contenders, Josh Shapiro might have the best overall combo? I also think aside from the occasional strange phrase like “the warmth of collectivism,” I think Zohran Mamdani may be the most naturally talented at both speeches and interviews. Buttigieg is obviously articulate, but he can also come across as stiff and more broadly, how does a rising Dem develop a distinctive speaking style without sounding like an Obama imitation?

My main thought on this is that as Democrats have increasingly become the educated people’s party, they’ve come to really overrate the literary quality of speeches relative to their content. Conversely, I don’t think it’s the worst thing in the world that guys like Shapiro and Ossoff seem like they want to sound like Obama — Obama was great at speeches.

But — and I think this is the important part — Obama’s best speeches actually said something distinctive.

This is from Obama’s 2004 convention speech:

Don’t get me wrong. The people I meet in small towns and big cities, in diners and office parks, they don’t expect government to solve all their problems. They know they have to work hard to get ahead and they want to. Go into the collar counties around Chicago, and people will tell you they don’t want their tax money wasted by a welfare agency or the Pentagon. Go into any inner city neighborhood, and folks will tell you that government alone can’t teach kids to learn. They know that parents have to parent, that children can’t achieve unless we raise their expectations and turn off the television sets and eradicate the slander that says a black youth with a book is acting white. No, people don’t expect government to solve all their problems. But they sense, deep in their bones, that with just a change in priorities, we can make sure that every child in America has a decent shot at life, and that the doors of opportunity remain open to all. They know we can do better. And they want that choice.

I don’t think anyone in contemporary Democratic Party politics would say that today.

Not because they’re “bad at giving speeches,” but because they wouldn’t stand by the content. Obama’s facility as a politician is that he said and did things that were very exciting at the time to progressives but then he also said things that acknowledged substantive sympathy for widely held doubts that people have about progressives.

Notably, he’s not attacking some far-left fringe here. He’s saying that he hears the concerns that middle-class people have with mainstream American progressive politics, that it is too wasteful with taxpayers’ money and too indulgent of irresponsible personal behavior. The most famous paragraph of the speech brilliantly critiques left-wing identity politics while making it go down more smoothly by framing it as a critique of cynical right-wingers. He closes, as a person who opposed the Iraq War but is aware that it was popular back in the winter of 2002–03, by speaking respectfully of the people who were on the other side of the argument.

Yet even as we speak, there are those who are preparing to divide us, the spin masters and negative ad peddlers who embrace the politics of anything goes. Well, I say to them tonight, there’s not a liberal America and a conservative America — there’s the United States of America. There’s not a black America and white America and Latino America and Asian America; there’s the United States of America. The pundits like to slice-and-dice our country into Red States and Blue States; Red States for Republicans, Blue States for Democrats. But I’ve got news for them, too. We worship an awesome God in the Blue States, and we don’t like federal agents poking around our libraries in the Red States. We coach Little League in the Blue States and have gay friends in the Red States. There are patriots who opposed the war in Iraq and patriots who supported it. We are one people, all of us pledging allegiance to the stars and stripes, all of us defending the United States of America.

Ossoff has gotten a lot of deserved praise for his anti-Trump set pieces, but neither he nor anyone else is operating on anything close to this level in terms of the actual content of what he’s saying.

One interesting thing about Obama is that he published “Dreams from My Father” way back in 1995. It’s a book that is primarily of interest today because its author went on to become president of the United States. But it’s a pretty good book on its own terms that got decent reviews at the time. Which is just to say he had the intellectual capacity and presence of mind to put provocative ideas down on the page.

Compared to Obama’s speeches, Trump’s are a mess in terms of their formal rhetoric. But like Obama, Trump was doing noteworthy takes before he was in politics. And when he entered the electoral arena, he did so by giving speeches that contained distinctive ideas. His mode of expression tends to be very crude and inartful. But you listened to Trump in 2016 and it was clear how he differed from the other G.O.P. candidates in the field. He was conveying his point clearly. I don’t think there’s really anyone visible on the landscape right now who performs this mission well — reflecting a kind of larger listlessness in the party.

David Muccigrosso: Matt — When you coined the “Pundit’s Fallacy”, did you have anyone in particular in mind? coughcough Andrew Sullivan cough.

Genuinely, no. The whole point of this coinage is that it’s genuinely very widespread.

For those who don’t know, the “pundit’s fallacy” is insisting that the road to electoral success is to line up with the writer’s specific constellation of views. One thing I’ve been thinking about with regard to this is the heavy emphasis in certain circles recently on ideas like the “annoyance economy” and the preoccupation with Ticketmaster fees. I am open to being corrected, but I’ve never seen any real effort to present evidence that these are widespread public concerns or that voters are looking for politicians to focus on them. They feel to me like the slightly idiosyncratic concerns of youngish political staffers. Conversely, none of my friends are ever whining about the price of gasoline. It just happens to be the case that this is clearly something a large fraction of the population cares about a lot.

But you get all kinds of examples like this.

Objectively, Trump totally sidelining the abortion issue has been smart politics. But if Democrats win in the 2026 and 2028 cycles, the most predictable thing in the world is that the anti-abortion movement will try to make a comeback by arguing that sidelining them was a big mistake and millions of pro-life voters stayed home.

I do try to guard against this myself by paying attention to survey research. The Broockman-Kalla optimized agenda for a moderate Democrat to win the 2028 primary and beat JD Vance that I wrote about earlier this week is composed of ideas that I think are fine on the merits. I wouldn’t encourage politicians to adopt them if I thought they were really harmful and reckless. But if you were trying to appeal to me personally, the agenda would be more progressive on immigration and more conservative on energy issues. I’m also not as enthusiastic as the mass public is about making “no cuts to Social Security,” like, the number one progressive economic policy — I’m more interested in helping poor people. But you need to follow the evidence.

Mediocre White Man: Where should LeBron play next season?

I think the move that would have made the most sense would have been for the $15 million that the Spurs spent on signing Tobias Harris to go to LeBron instead. That would put the Spurs immediately in the mix to contend for a championship. And while they obviously don’t need LeBron to win, to the extent that their roster has any real weaknesses, they appear to specifically be in the “veteran leadership” / “knows how to win the big games” space. The nature of Wembanyama’s game is that there’s plenty of room for someone else to handle the ball, but you also wouldn’t be relying on LeBron to play 33 minutes per game across the regular season if he’d rather downshift and save it for the playoffs.

Even with the Harris deal signed, LeBron still could go to the Spurs, but that would have to be for a minimum deal. Reporting seems to indicate that he’s open to that, but I assume the guy still likes money. More to the point, the Spurs are saying they’re not interested and want to remain heads down in long-term development mode.

An idea nobody is talking about, of course, is that he could play for the Knicks! A franchise that, as the world saw last year, has the highest ceiling in the world in terms of popularity. The Knicks are not as iconic as the Lakers due to the much lower level of actual basketball success in New York. But a Knicks team that actually wins games and contends for a championship is a bigger deal than anything else in the world of basketball. Why shouldn’t the most famous player in the world be a part of that? This does not per se fill a need on the Knicks side, but you could send Josh Hart to the bench and field a fearsome Brunson-Bridges-Anunoby-James-Towns starting five.

Marc Robbins: Great Man of Alt-History time.

Benjamin Franklin decides to join with his son and become a Loyalist, rejecting the cause of independence. Robert E. Lee decides to accept promotion to Major General and command of the Union Army, offered by Lincoln.

How would things have been different?

I don’t really know anything about Franklin’s role in the American Revolution, so I can’t speculate here.

As for Lee, my understanding is that he really was offered field command back in April of 1861 and also that he was genuinely very skeptical of secession. That said, I don’t think he was ever skeptical about the idea that he would follow Virginia if Virginia seceded. So I don’t really think this was a close “what if?”

But if you do posit that for some reason Lee stays loyal, history becomes quite different. Not only was Lee a very capable tactical general, but his aggressive command style was better suited to the Union’s actual strategic situation. One can make the case that Lee is badly overrated as a general and that his choices didn’t really make sense given the Confederates’ fundamental deficit in manpower. But put him in command of Union forces and this disposition would have been correct. The view would have been that the best way to minimize the long-term loss of human life is simply for the Union to strike as hard and fast as possible, crush the rebellion as soon as possible, and just not worry too much about downsides or potential losses.

I think it’s reasonable to posit that in this world, rather than Lee saving the Confederacy’s bacon during the Peninsula Campaign in June 1862, he might have won the war for the Union that summer. This would have been before the Emancipation Proclamation and, broadly speaking, before the war experience gave the Radical Republicans the preponderance of power inside the party.

Oliver: Is the Falklands dispute as one sided as British sources make it seem? I would be interested in an American perspective.

I think it’s very one-sided. The British position is that Britain has continuously administered the islands since the 1830s, that the population has a right to self-determination, and that the residents prefer British rule. The Argentine position is that both Britain and Spain claimed the islands in the 18th century, that Spain never recognized British possession of the islands, and that Argentina inherits the Spanish claim to the islands.

As far as historical arguments about sovereignty go, this is not the craziest thing I’ve ever heard, but it’s also not particularly compelling. And against it you have to weigh the fact that there was no problem with British sovereignty until circa 1982. The population was not complaining. Argentina’s non-possession of the islands was not a problem for Argentina. A military junta in Buenos Aires provoked a war for no real reason other than to try to bolster its domestic standing. And it’s unfortunate that the various post-dictatorship progressive tendencies in Argentine politics have gone along with this cynical gambit rather than straightforwardly seeing it as an example of how the old regime was bad.

JA: Most of the center-left and socialist left seem to agree that Democrats are held back by too-progressive cultural views, and once they ditch those, they can exploit their advantage on economic issues. The idea is that expanded government spending, financed either by taxation or inflation, would be popular.

Are Democrats’ positions on economics broadly more popular than republicans’? If so, is it because people would be willing to bear the higher costs of expanded social services, or do they just underestimate the cost of providing those services?

I think the difference on economics isn’t that every single Democratic Party idea is popular. It’s that Democrats acknowledge conceptually that public opinion matters and that it’s important to pay attention to what is and is not politically viable.

People still disagree about what is and isn’t popular. I tend to think, for example, that folks in the populist camp over-read the data on the potency of anti-corporate messaging and discount the fact that lots of people deem successful businesspeople more admirable than academics and professional activists. And I think progressives tend to look at the very real popularity of defending the existing social safety net and get somewhat overly optimistic about the popularity of further expansions. But we’re still having a genuine empirical look at things. Nobody is getting “canceled” because they disagree with the structure of a specific child-care proposal. And unlike on issues related to identity or climate change, there is always a real spirit of pragmatism and a general willingness to try to see what’s viable and get that done.

So it’s not exactly that I think left-wing economic ideas are systematically more popular than left-wing cultural ones — abortion rights is extremely popular, after all — so much as I think that the entire Democratic Party ecosystem has a much more pragmatic and workable approach to economic issues.

BlueLineBlueState: I have read a number of pieces trying to explain why the closure of the Strait of Hormuz hasn’t had the catastrophic impact on oil prices that was predicted. The most convincing argument I have seen is that China has dramatically reduced its oil purchases reducing global demand to the point where isn’t a true shortage.

I am wondering both what you think explains the only moderate increase in oil prices, and what happens if the strait remains closed for the next few months.

This China explanation seems to be what the most knowledgeable people are pointing to, and I don’t have any grounds on which to doubt it.

But I think it just raises other questions. The baseline expectation for why oil prices should have gone higher is that the Hormuz disruption was larger, quantitatively, than the disruptions associated with the invasion of Ukraine, and that’s true even when you account for the Saudi bypass pipeline. So if China was able to counteract this time by cutting purchases and relying on its reserves, why didn’t it do that in 2022?

AM (no link): Would Mamdani be pro or anti Batman? And which should he be for his politics?

In this storyline, the Joker travels to Lebanon with a stolen nuclear weapon that he sells to Hezbollah. Batman and Jason Todd (the then-Robin) foil the plan to nuke Tel Aviv, but the Joker kills Robin. He then makes a deal with Ayatollah Khomeini (really) to become Iran’s ambassador to the United Nations. With diplomatic immunity thus secured, Batman can’t confront him and seek vengeance. Fortunately, Superman was covertly in attendance — in part to prevent Batman from doing anything that could provoke a major diplomatic incident — and is able to use his super-inhaling abilities (?) to prevent the gas from killing everyone. Batman then chases the Joker onto a helicopter, but one of the Joker’s henchmen accidentally kills the pilot, and the chopper crashes. Not to say that Mamdani is aligned with Iran per se, but at least the iteration of Batman that I grew up with seemed much more Zionist-aligned than Mamdani would be comfortable with.

On the other hand, he seems to have a good working relationship with Police Commissioner Jessica Tisch, so presumably he could work with the caped crusader even if they don’t see eye to eye on Israel.
