What Would Sex Be Like In A Matriarchy?

Matriarchies in history and today can give us some hints as to how female power would change dating and sex.

Every now and then, my husband and I finish all the binge-watching shows on our list and are left asking each other, “Well, what’s on Netflix?” after the kids go to bed. One of these nights, we decided to watch Ladies First, a comedy starring Sacha Baron Cohen playing a middle-aged, chauvinistic ad executive who bonks his head and wakes up in a world where women are in charge.

In this world, men are expected to be beautiful, svelte, hairless and young, and they aren’t taken seriously on the merit of their work. They are forced to compete for the sexual attention of elderly female executives (none of whom are terribly concerned with their own looks) in order to get promotions.

This premise felt, somehow, very 2005 and very 2020, but very not 2026. It had all the 2005 notes of, “Just imagine if women openly farted, haha” while retaining a great deal of 2020 “the confidence of a mediocre white man” vibes. Perhaps predictably, the movie currently stands at 26% on Rotten Tomatoes.

I have several logistical gripes with this movie, including but not limited to the fact that Sacha Baron Cohen’s character lives a life of luxury more befitting Elon Musk himself than a creative director at an ad agency in London, who would typically make £83,000 to £102,000 a year. But my biggest gripe, perhaps, was more of a question: is this really how women would behave if they were in charge?

No matter what the antifeminists say, we do still live in a patriarchy (a far more egalitarian one than in the past, surely, but definitely not a matriarchy.) Men still hold most positions of power in our government, leading American CEOs are overwhelmingly male, and the sex objects used to sell products are almost always women, while the default consumer of these products is male (this is the actual definition of “the male gaze,” not to be confused with “trying to look attractive for men,” which is just part of being attracted to men.)

So, in keeping with my series of investigative research into bizarre questions, such as “What happens to male gorillas who can’t get pussy?” or “What happened to picky eaters in olden times?” I will attempt to answer the question: What would attraction, sex and dating really look like if women were in charge?

One reason it’s hard to fully conceive of a matriarchy is that patriarchy is downstream from women being the ones who gestate, birth infants, and lactate, while also being smaller and weaker than the average man. Therefore, real-life matriarchies have not truly been gender-flipped, given that women were still the ones getting pregnant, and the ones who would be less physically capable of defending themselves. “Matriarchies,” as they have actually existed in real life, have never been total gender-reversals.

And besides, if women could call all the shots tomorrow, as they did in Ladies First, what percentage of them would prefer to hand off all childcare responsibilities to a stay-at-home dad? I would place bets on the fact that most women, if given the option, would prefer an egalitarian or almost-egalitarian model of childrearing as opposed to working long hours while their husbands handle all the baby stuff. Perhaps I am wrong, but this is a generalization I feel comfortable making. It’s also simply not practical, given the aforementioned issue of lactation.

But what I’m most interested in uncovering is how sex and dating would work in a matriarchal society. Surely, some things would still be reliant on the biological differences between men and women. But if you look at how the most deeply patriarchal societies treat sex and dating—where women have little choice in the matter, where a man can (in some cases) have multiple wives, where a woman has no option to divorce an abusive husband—it makes you wonder what that dynamic would look like with the genders “reversed.” But again, even if women had all the power in the world, would they actually be interested in setting up a Female Taliban?

Alas, a few matriarchal societies have existed, and we can look at how dating and sex works within them to get an idea of how Ladies First would actually play out—or not.

Perhaps you’ve heard right-wing men online say that if a man does anything domestic—from taking their child to the park to getting married at all—he is “longhoused.” This term comes from the indigenous American Iroquois confederacy and the matrilineal, multigenerational houses they built, which were shaped like this:

The Iroquois confederacy (or Haudenosaunee) was matrilineal, meaning that families lived within extended maternal clans. Some components of this society were male-driven: the chieftains were all male, but powerful matriarchs could nominate or remove them. So not exactly a world where men were subservient to women, but certainly one where a maternal lineage mattered more than paternal, which could be considered a matriarchy in some sense.

So what about dating and marriage? Because the clans were matrilineal, girls stayed with their own maternal line even after marrying, and a husband would leave his natal family to join his wife’s—essentially the opposite of how multigenerational living works in most patriarchal societies. “Dating,” if you could really call it that, was traditional in some ways, as it might have been within a patriarchy, but female-led. At least from the limited sources we have today, we know that most marriages were semi-arranged by female elders, monogamous, and often involved women slightly older than their husbands. There wasn’t much of a taboo around divorce, and in the case of divorce, the wife would retain custody of the children while the husband would return to his natal family. (When Christian missionaries encountered this society, they lectured them about their degree of divorce and sexual “immorality.”) The lack of taboo around divorce worked both ways. A divorced man would be no more of a pariah than his ex-wife, and both could remarry.

Even within most matriarchal societies, marriage was still between one man and one woman. There have been rare polyandrous societies (where a woman took multiple husbands) but they were not matriarchies. For example, certain rural communities in Tibet practice fraternal polyandry, where a woman marries multiple brothers. (Most Tibetans are monogamous.) However, in the case of fraternal polyandry, the woman often has little to no say in this decision, and the marriages are arranged by elders within the family—hardly a dommy mommy luxuriating over a harem of himbos, as one might imagine. Indeed, fraternal polyandry has less to do with female power (I will die on the hill that most women would not choose multiple husbands even if given the option) and more to do with the fact that consolidating a marriage this way prevented inheritance and property-related strife. Multiple husbands within one family also provided a surplus of protection and labor.

But perhaps the best-studied and most interesting modern-day matriarchy exists among the Mosuo people of southwest China, referred to as “China’s Kingdom of Women.” Western women have become so fascinated by the Mosuo that their province has become a hotspot for feminist tourism—and thus far, they have not mandated any KN95s. The Mosuo are best known for what they call “walking marriages,” where “married” couples simply meet for nocturnal booty calls and continue to live separately (with their maternal families, of course). Both partners can date other people or get divorced, without much hullaballoo. Fathers, who live separately from their children, are treated almost as uncle figures, where a child might be equally close, if not closer, to their maternal uncles who live in the same home. Childrearing is communal and matrilineal. These walking marriages aren’t even really marriages in any Western sense—there is no transfer or joining of property, no expectation of monogamy, not even the expectation that children will know who their fathers are. They are not arranged, and instead chosen purely on the basis of personal preference and attraction.

And speaking of attraction, Mosuo women can afford to have different priorities when seeking a mate, compared with women in patriarchal societies. Since childrearing will be a communal affair and marriage doesn’t confer a transfer of resources, a man’s financial or economic position is low on the list of priorities. With a man’s resources and socioeconomic status demoted to a non-issue, mates for walking marriages are chosen primarily for conversational chemistry and physical attractiveness. Unsurprisingly, in more egalitarian societies, women begin weighting men’s physical attractiveness and conversational chemistry higher than they might otherwise have—something modern-day Western men find anxiety-provoking, it seems. However, there is no evidence that these societies unfairly weight men’s attractiveness more heavily than men weigh women’s, as we saw in Ladies First, where we were subjected to a scene of Sacha Baron Cohen wearing a bra for his testicles.

The Mosuo society is probably the closest to a full “reversal” of patriarchy, where no patriarchal social conventions continue to exist in any real way, as opposed to other matrilineal societies, which still practice monogamous marriage and arranged marriage, even if the grandmothers are doing the arranging and inheritance occurs on the maternal line.

So, how close is any of this to Ladies First?

Ladies First assumes that if women were in charge, they would simply behave like predatory, chauvinistic men. The sex lives they desired, and their priorities for a mate, would basically mirror that of extremely shallow, extremely powerful gay men. Before I began researching matriarchal societies, I had a feeling this dynamic was unique to a particularly goofy movie and not replicated in any real-life society, past or present. But hey, good to double check my hypothesis with eight cumulative hours of research.

In some ways, matriarchal societies do replicate the world of Ladies First by virtue of men having less power and fewer rights than women (most men reading this would probably not want to live amongst the Mosuo, while fathering children but having no actual custody of them.) But when it comes to dating and sex, there seem to be a few consistent themes across matriarchal societies, compared with our own.

One is that there is less emphasis placed on a man’s resources and financial standing. This makes sense in a society where inheritance is passed through the maternal line anyway, and where women are the main owners of property. If a woman’s own financial situation is taken care of, this frees her up to focus on other attributes when looking for a partner. This indicates that a woman prioritizing a man’s wealth over things like his personality or looks is not necessarily an innate component of female sexuality, but rather a survival tactic that makes more sense in a patriarchal society. (It arguably doesn’t make as much sense in relatively modern and egalitarian societies, but I would argue that a man’s income is becoming less important in relation to his looks and personality in modern-day America for this reason.) This also validates something I’ve thought for a while, which is that a man’s income can accentuate existing attraction, but women are not “attracted to money” if there’s nothing else going on.

Second—and this one actually surprised me—there seems to be less of an emphasis on lifelong monogamy within matriarchal societies. I’ve written about this before within the context of modern Western society, but for much of recent history, especially within American pop culture, the stereotype was that men wanted to sow their wild oats while women wanted marriage and babies. In fact, most of the jokes about the “longhouse” seem to equate convention, marriage, monogamy and childrearing with disproportionate female power. The implication of the longhouse joke is that men are naturally wild, feral creatures who are forcibly domesticated by women enforcing marriage and monogamy. But it seems that in societies without patriarchy, monogamy and marriage are actually less important, including (at least from what we can gather from limited sources) the Iroquois in the infamous longhouses themselves.

So, while I wouldn’t go so far as to say “marriage is patriarchal” (and I know other people might say this—again, I wouldn’t, because I think humans are naturally monogamous to some extent) it’s fair to say that matriarchal societies tend to have a less dogmatic view of marriage, even in cases where monogamous marriage is still the default. Divorce is easier to get and less taboo across nearly every matriarchal society. While patriarchy may be downstream of innate biological differences between men and women, you could make the argument that enforcing strict monogamy, with a taboo around divorce, would be more beneficial for men than for women.

Moreover, in a society where resources are inherited through the paternal line, keeping women’s sexuality in check is necessary to ensure paternity of the children. If paternity matters less because resources are actually passed down through the maternal line, sexuality (especially of women) will be less policed, because there is never any question as to the maternity of any given child. So while there is probably something innately human about wanting marriage or monogamy, these conventions are more strongly enforced within patriarchies. Are men the real bridezillas after all?

All this to say: it really do be like that sometimes. And now you can watch Ladies First without asking all the same questions I asked. You may, however, find yourself asking something else: “How much longer is left in this movie?”
