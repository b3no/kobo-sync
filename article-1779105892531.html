This OCD Relapse Brought to You by Hantavirus

Not me falling face first into another OCD rabbit hole--I mean, rat hole

Cartoons Hate Her
May 18
∙
Paid
 
READ IN APP
 

Sometimes, I write about my OCD—all the early signs of it, how I suspect everyone on Reddit has it, how it manifests as health anxiety, and most of all, how nobody around me can stand it. And today—how it was doing really well until, thanks to the notorious hantavirus cruise, my Twitter algorithm turned into what I am calling the HantaZone Situation Room.

Generally, if I’m not writing about my OCD at any given point, things are going pretty well for me. For the past couple months, this has been the case. I have a good therapist who specializes in the most proven technique for OCD—ERP, otherwise known as exposure-response prevention, where you deliberately expose yourself to things that make you anxious and then avoid doing maladaptive things to reduce the anxiety.

But the biggest challenge of ERP is actually doing the exposures—including when things are going well for you. This is doubly true if your OCD subtype is something unrelated to a tangible phobia, where you could theoretically go months or longer without actually encountering a trigger, and when your trigger cannot be self-imposed. (Since my OCD revolves around rare fatal diseases, I have a hard time triggering myself on purpose by thinking about diseases—it has the same effect as trying to tickle myself.)

Therefore, after a period of peace with my OCD, I usually become complacent. I no longer worry as much about whether or not I’m engaging in reassurance-seeking or other maladaptive behaviors, because on some level, I believe I don’t have OCD anymore. But of course, at some point, a trigger will show up and pull me back into the insidiously sticky vat of molasses that is my OCD, and having forgotten the importance of the whole “response prevention” part of ERP, the obsession will consume me effortlessly.

That’s what happened this past week, courtesy of hantavirus and (of course) Twitter.

I want to give myself credit where it’s due: given my great work with ERP for the past few months, it took multiple failed hantavirus “triggers” for my OCD to even be activated.

First, I saw the news of the hantavirus cruise ship—nothing. I wrote it off as the same threat Ebola posed to me in 2014, although it’s notable that my OCD was untreated in 2014 and I actually thought I had contracted Ebola from a WeWork toilet upon which a very disgusting employee had apparently projectile vomited and only lightly wiped clean. This fear eventually faded when I entered my “pulmonary embolism era,” but that’s another story.

Initially unfazed by hantavirus news, I patted myself on the back, remembering how I was foolishly convinced at one point that mpox would kill over 100,000 children, and would infiltrate our soil and crops. I chuckled at how, during that outbreak, I had suggested investing in a hydroponic garden, my only evidence a legitimate-looking infographic that turned out to have been created not by health authorities as I assumed, but by a singular deranged person on Twitter with good graphic design skills. Wow, what a rube I was! I saw someone tweet that they were preemptively diagnosing themselves with “long hanta” and I laughed, not experiencing even the slightest bit of anxiety.

But interacting with that tweet was a bad idea, because suddenly my entire algorithm shifted to the HantaZone Situation Room. At first, I was like Oh God, Yaneer Bar-Yam again? as I slowly recalled the menacingly smug avatars of all the academics, virologists and general kooks that had plagued me during covid and convinced me that locking down for eighteen months and robbing my parents of their opportunity to meet my child until after he turned one was the only way to be a responsible parent. At least I already had Eric Feigl-Ding blocked. And as these people started to reappear on my feed (I had seriously thought they had all just vanished for Bluesky years ago) my first thought was, “Hell no. I’m not going there again.”

But, well, I went there. First, it was curiosity. This is always how an OCD relapse starts—you think that because you haven’t been feargooning in Claude and ChatGPT rabbit holes, one little curious Google search is harmless. Lots of people Google this stuff, right? It makes sense to want to understand current events, right? So I started Googling. More tweets appeared. I asked Claude. I started reading about the 2018 Andes virus outbreak and whether or not that outcome was reassuring or scary. I started investigating whether any healthcare workers caught it in 2018 (no) and the fact that people caught it from what appeared to be casual, brief contact (scary.) I tried to calculate the window of infectiousness. I started asking about what happened to all the other people who have been catching this virus in South America for decades if not longer. I started researching the timeline of the cruise infections and how they stacked up to the average (not maximum) incubation period.

(Fun fact: diseases with long incubation periods are catnip for OCD because OCD thrives not on tragedy, but uncertainty. See: common OCD obsessions with rabies, prion disease and HIV. The fear is less about getting the disease, and more about being antagonized with uncertainty for a long period of time.)

Then, I began fact-checking the wackiest people on Twitter as a form of self-reassurance. My chosen technique was searching their profile for “monkeypox” to see what they had tweeted about that world-ending pandemic.

Amusing as this compulsion was, it was still a compulsion. I began scanning every scary tweet about hantavirus for signs that the person tweeting it was unreliable, doing a private little “this you?” session. I did the same thing with people who said reassuring things, checking to see if they had political or financial motivations to lie to me. And of course, I haven’t been able to trust anything on the news or put out by the CDC for over six years, so I didn’t even bother with them. (Is it, perhaps, ridiculous that I am assigning more value to the words of a professor with an animal avatar on Twitter than the CDC? I don’t know, I’m mentally ill. You tell me.) At one point, deep into an argument with Claude about the Andes virus’ R0, Claude sassily asked me, “What would it take for me to trust someone?” which felt like a sign that I had taken it too far. By then, my Twitter feed had dissolved into random 200 follower accounts claiming to be afflicted with hantavirus themselves, and a bunch of people insisting that Israel was orchestrating another “plandemic” by creating all forms of hantavirus because “hanta” is Hebrew for “scam.” One must wonder why they would leave such an obvious calling card, obfuscated only to people who don’t have access to Google. In case you were wondering, the etymology comes from the Hantaan River in South Korea where the virus was first identified.

Eventually, I got around to a bunch of right-wingers declaring that they “would not comply” with whatever “scamdemic” was being shoved down their throats. Simultaneously, they believed hantavirus was being maliciously “released” by the government/Jews/transgender Erika Kirk, but also that it was a “scam” with which nobody should cooperate. Not that any of them were correct, but judging by their train of logic, they believed they were about to be hit with a bioweapon with a 40% fatality rate, and the best course of action would be to don a tricorn hat and lick everyone in the town square. Perhaps I could learn something from the radical acceptance practiced by MAGACock1776.

I realized things were completely off the deep end when I was avoiding going downstairs to watch a movie with my husband because I was too busy translating local news on contact tracing from the Calabria region in Italy. Eetsa-me, mental illness!

I was worried about something a bit more specific than “hantavirus.” I was mostly worried about the fact that our family is set to spend the summer abroad, and that I would be tasked with deciding whether to cancel our trip (one which involves multiple other extended family members and friends’ tickets, and all of them are relying on us being there). On the one hand, it felt easy for me to say that my children’s lives are more important than any vacation, which is obviously true, but that line of thinking is exactly what got me into a self-imposed covid lockdown for eighteen months and missing a close friend’s wedding in checks notes 2022, because it was an “unmasked indoor gathering.” After 2020, any risk—any at all—felt irresponsible, until at one point my brother staged an intervention. I did not want to go back to that place, especially because my brother is pretty busy with his job now, and is unlikely to shake me out of my delusion again.

And let’s say there was a significant hantavirus outbreak. Not getting on the plane would be an easy choice, and my husband would agree with me. But my main hantavirus fear (which hasn’t fully been ameliorated yet) was that hantavirus would be just widespread enough for the choice to get on the plane to be a close call, and that in a best case scenario, I would spend all summer worrying that both of my children would suddenly die. The worst case scenario is that they would actually die, along with my husband, and only I would survive, and the entire thing would be my fault for agreeing to get on the plane. As I worried about this, every moment of cuteness and happiness with my children—every goofy, crooked smile, every mispronounced word, every strawberry-scented cheek kiss—was tainted with the bitterness of impending doom.

Crucially: I do not feel better about this yet. It’s hard for me to even write this without getting sucked into the vortex all over again, but I’m trying. My therapist reminded me that I’m married to a cautious man who is just as invested in our children’s survival as I am, and if I can’t trust the CDC or the health authorities, I should at least be able to trust him. (#trad #based therapist.) Her advice: outsource all HantaZone decisions and research to my husband and trust him to make the right decisions. I briefly wondered if my therapist was actually my husband in Mrs. Doubtfire drag, but I agreed with “her” plan anyway.

At that point, things improved. Not that the situation with hantavirus has necessarily improved, but my urge to check said situation every five seconds had improved. My husband promised he would notify me every day with anything I needed to know, and I felt less urgent about Googling it. This was not a perfect ERP exercise, given that I was not deliberately exposing myself to a trigger. But I was at least reducing maldaptive behavior.

I’m lucky given that I’m married to someone I trust who enjoys being in control, and seemingly has a bottomless capacity for tasks and decisions that he’s willing to bring under his authority—every renovation, every plumber or electrician we’ve ever hired, every big decision, even what I order in restaurants. Perhaps unsurprisingly, my husband has OCD too, but a different subtype, so he understands what I am experiencing but faces no risk of falling into the same traps. He also has the inverse of my fear of “something going wrong and it being all my fault.” I fear ownership, he craves it. Nothing terrifies my husband more than someone else being in charge, and nothing terrifies me more than something being my fault. People joke about how insane we both are, but I don’t think I could function with anyone less insane, or anyone insane in a different way.

As for my relapse, I want to say it’s going well and it has been resolved but I am only about 40% better. I have to remind myself that this is just going to keep happening for the rest of my life, provided I am not already riddled with hanta. I will have good months and bad months, and a Google search or AI prompt triggered by one small “curiosity” will plague me into torment for an indeterminate amount of time. So be it. But at least I can just hand my mess off to my husband and hope for the best.

And at least I don’t have to worry about any other disease outbreaks, right? Certainly nothing highly lethal that people on Twitter will insist is airborne and being pointlessly obfuscated by health authorities…right?
