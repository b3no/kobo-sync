Why AI can solve hard math problems but can't count

AI keeps whiffing on this simple question

Kobe Yank-Jacobs
Jun 3
∙
Paid
 
READ IN APP
 

There are no P’s in the word “Google,” but someone still needs to tell Gemini this — Google’s own AI Overview keeps suggesting there are P’s when asked.

Back in 2024, asking a model to count the R’s in “strawberry” became the internet’s canonical example of weird AI failure modes. Most models of that generation counted letters wrong about half of the time.

Since then, LLMs have made rapid leaps in everything from scientific reasoning to agentic coding, while major businesses have begun to rely on them in critical areas like finance. And yet, somehow, they still suffer on tasks like counting the R’s in “strawberry,” the P’s in Google, or the N’s in the days of the week, which I asked ChatGPT to do today:

How is it that models can now solve historic math problems but still fail to count letters in a word?

Share

The typical explanation here has to do with “tokenization.” LLMs don’t read English characters; they break words into subwords, like “st -raw - berry.” Each segment of the word is a token, which is used because it’s the most efficient unit of language to compute (compared to the vast library of whole words). Under this theory, the counting issue has to do with how familiar an LLM is with a given token from its training data.

That explanation never quite made sense to me. “Strawberry” is a very common word. Even if it were tokenized several different ways within the training data (“st -raw - berry” and “straw - be -rry”), its tokens would still be quite familiar.

Indeed, a 2024 study found weak evidence for tokenization as the central issue. The overall frequency of a word or token had no impact on the error rate. What the study did find is that errors were correlated with longer words, as well as with words in which letters were repeated more often.

“Strawberry” was harder than “straw.” LLMs really were having a counting problem, not just a familiarity one.

To get to the bottom of this, it’s helpful to think about how you or I might handle a similar issue: We would use our memory.

Suppose I asked you how many chairs there are in your favorite coffee shop. You might call up a mental picture of your favorite coffee shop and count the number of chairs in your head. This combines a few types of human memory — “episodic” memory is how you call up the mental image of the shop; “working” memory helps you hold something fixed in the mind for a short period of time to manipulate it; and “procedural” memory is the know-how involved in counting.

Human beings have many different kinds of memory — and very often, we use them as an ensemble to reconstruct knowledge, not just to query it. LLMs have essentially two kinds of memory. Neither one is perfectly suited to the letter-counting task.

One kind of memory is encoded in the models’ weights (the large set of numbers that compose the model, shaped by the training data). Here, the model doesn’t store something like the population of New York City directly, but because that fact is so common in the training data, it will get the answer right reliably enough to consider it memory. That kind of memory is implicit in the model.

The other kind of memory is its context window. If you talk to an LLM for an hour straight, it is looking at and recalculating every prior message in the chat on every turn, but then, when you open a new chat, it won’t remember that conversation.¹ The stored messages of a given chat are the model’s “context.” This is comparable to our short-term, working memory.

Neither of these makes the model call a word to mind to count the letters, like we do.

A hint that memory is the answer to this problem is that you can nudge a model, using the tools it has at its disposal, to mimic the human counting procedure — and when it does, it can get the answer right.

While I was querying ChatGPT about the N’s in the days of the week, I was also having another conversation about ChatGPT’s failure with Claude (to prepare for this article, not to gossip). In the middle of that conversation — where I was discussing the ways LLMs fail and humans succeed — I picked a random moment to ask Claude to count the N’s in the days of the week. Claude got the answer right and told me why, referring back to our conversation.

“I verified it in code rather than counting from memory — which is exactly the tell,” it explained back to me. “To answer reliably I had to spell each day out and tally, the way a human re-examines, rather than retrieving ‘the number of N’s in the week’ as a stored fact.”

Now, it’s important not to take models’ descriptions of their own behavior at face value. When it comes to using tools, most model interfaces display an icon to confirm what’s actually happening in the background. In this case, Claude did, in fact, use a tool to figure out the answer — and as Claude notes, coding was an important tell.

Increasingly, models are being wrapped inside architectures and being given tools that help them seem to overcome some of their limitations. In most cases, this works.

For instance, while storing a lot of context is still a limit, top models are now given access to compressed versions of other chats, which makes them seem like they remember more. Similarly, when it comes to math, they are given scratchpads to hold external information for long tasks, which seems to increase the length of their memory.

All of these are shortcuts around the memory problem, but they are not the same as the model itself successfully doing the task. They just push the problem to another skill, which is the model knowing when to call a tool (use an external capability). With the letter-counting issue, we are seeing this gap — the seam between what models can actually do and when they can make a timely tool call to compensate.

Claude was likely influenced to reach for a tool in this case because we were having a conversation about LLM memory.

At that point, I turned back to ChatGPT to see if I could get it to make progress. I merely said, “Try again,” and when it got the answer right, I asked how:

“I stopped treating the list as familiar words and counted letter-by-letter,” it reported to me.

I was actually more impressed by this than Claude’s one-shot success. ChatGPT at least seemed to mimic the human method, but it didn’t even call an outside tool. It seemed to do this within its own reasoning process.

Buoyed by ChatGPT’s seeming progress, I thought I would ask it to do the strawberry problem but walk it through the steps: I asked it to spell strawberry and planned to ask it to count the letters in my next message.

Unfortunately, ChatGPT got overconfident. It spelled “s t r a w b e r r y” perfectly, and then, without my asking, it volunteered how many R’s were in the word it had just spelled.

Apparently, there are still two.²
