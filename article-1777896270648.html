Gentle Parenting Fails to Answer: "What If That Doesn't Work?"

If your child won't say please and thank you to adults, what exactly is the "natural consequence?"

I’ve really been loving Elizabeth Grace Matthew’s work on gentle parenting (or should I say, less gentle parenting) as a former permissive parent who, for a while, lived in fear of my toddler being mad at me. But one problem with criticizing gentle parenting is that, much like The Patriot Act, gentle parenting has given itself a name that makes any dissenter look like a monster. What, you don’t want to be gentle with your child? Some people call gentle parenting “respectful parenting” or “peaceful parenting,” so even worse: any parent who criticizes the ethos appears like a disrespectful, warlike parent.

Gentle parenting is incredibly hard to define, with some people using the big tent definition of “not hitting your kids.” (And yes, let me go on the record and say hitting your kids is bad.) But instead of throwing up my hands in confusion, I will define gentle parenting, based on what most of the respected gentle parenting sources say. Gentle parenting is best defined by three things: reducing or eliminating the hierarchy between parent and child, validating children’s emotions and “big feelings,” and most importantly, not providing rewards or punishments (or any “imposed consequences”) for any kind of behavior. The idea is that your child will behave better than children raised with rules, rewards and punishments, simply because they want to and because they feel safe and loved.

Taken to its extreme, I saw a mom on Reddit accuse parents of emotional abuse for providing stickers or any other incentive for potty training, instead insisting that children need to use the toilet out of genuine, intrinsically-motivated desire. Her child was four and still not potty-trained, and she had no plans to start pushing the issue.

This incentive-phobia is primarily why I am a gentle parenting dissident. Sometimes, you really do need an incentive (hopefully positive, but sometimes negative) to get kids to do things. And yes, kids have to do things. If you are a stay-at-home mom of one toddler who has hired help for all your chores, you can probably let your child scamper off and dilly-dally when you try to put her raincoat on, and happily say, “Okay, Brylee, we aren’t going to the park today!” and that will serve as a fine natural consequence, and maybe she didn’t want to go anyway. But if you have more than one child or literally anywhere important to go, and your child is relatively spirited and strong-willed, gentle parenting will sometimes fail.

And the gentle parenting experts will simply not have a solution for when it does.

Gentle parenting is good—as a first line of defense. My second child (now 2.5) has a much easier temperament than my older child (now 5.5.) She’s very independent, and often she’s excited to do things for the sake of doing them without any incentive (she is now fully potty-trained, and I’ve dispensed nary a single unicorn sticker.) I can see why, if someone’s first child is like her, they will believe gentle parenting is a foolproof strategy, because she basically never needs any positive or negative consequences to do things. Had she been my first child, I would have been tempted to write a book on my parenting genius, and I probably would have wondered why it was so hard for all these other idiots.

I can “gentle parent” my daughter because it works, and why would I put myself through more stress than I need to? When she’s being silly about putting her shoes on when we need to go somewhere, I ask her, “Can you find your Elmo foot and put it in the shoe? What about your Cookie Monster foot?” and she grabs her shoes and lets me put them on, giggling at the droll concept of an Elmo Foot. She enjoys doing things I ask her to do, and even rushes to grab a rag when she spills water because she finds it fun to clean up.

But…what if this strategy doesn’t work? What if your child is not like her? Well, with my son (especially at her age) it didn’t. He’s spirited and stubborn, often for seemingly no reason. Of course, this goes along with him being the child who gave us The Elder, so his rough moments are totally worth the positive. But anyway, remember when Kim Cattrall said she didn’t want to be in any situation, even for an hour, where she wasn’t enjoying herself? She was inspired by my son, but in his case, instead of an hour it’s two seconds. Just an example: he had a couple small toys on the floor and we asked him to please clean them up before we watched any TV. This task would have taken less than a minute. He was so aghast at the idea of cleaning up his toys that he refused, even though he knew he was going to lose TV, and then screamed about how unfair it was that he wasn’t getting any TV, threatening to “grind our souls into chum,” channeling his inspiration of the moment, The Flying Dutchman from Spongebob. My husband and I kept telling him how easy it would be to just clean up the toys, but in response he put his arms into his T-shirt, and through tears, said, “How can I clean up the toys when I don’t even have arms?!”

Now, some gentle parenting experts might say that “no TV until you clean up your toys” is a natural consequence, and I was still doing gentle parenting because I wasn’t imposing any incentive (I maintain that I was—the TV privilege only existed in the first place because of me, and it was also my choice to revoke it). But there are other times when “natural consequences” fail, or just don’t exist, and we have to impose a consequence that is very clearly not natural.

For example, our son got into the habit of shushing us when he didn’t like what we were saying. I thought it was kind of funny, especially because he shushed us in a particularly sassy way, but I said, “Buddy, that’s rude, you shouldn’t shush people—especially not grown-ups.” But then he began shushing his grandparents, random adults who said things he didn’t like, and even once, a French man at the bakery in Paris (I almost died of embarrassment, although I’ve learned “la petite mort” is apparently not the correct French term for this). I realized that calmly discouraging the shushing did nothing. The “natural consequence” of shushing was that people would be annoyed at him, but since his targets were always adults, everyone knew better than to lash out at a child. Instead of getting visibly annoyed, they’d politely ignore the shushing or uncomfortably laugh. As a result, there simply was no natural consequence of shushing. My husband and I had to create a rule to eliminate the shushing, so we did. One shush (or any other wildly disrespectful behavior, like calling someone “stupid”) meant he lost the ability to watch his favorite TV show, but he could still watch other TV during his allotted TV time. If he did it again, he would lose all TV for the day. Gentle parenting advocates would tell you that the shushing was “testing boundaries” and “developmentally normal” and we should have simply ignored it and allowed him to express himself. Only problem was, he was acting like a huge jerk to people who weren’t us.

Imposing consequences—even ones like “no TV if you keep shushing adults”—is not gentle parenting. This is “behavioralist” parenting, popularized by books such as 123 Magic. And while it might not always be the first thing I grab in my parenting toolbox, it ultimately works if the incentive is strong enough (For every kid that’s different—time-outs do nothing to deter my son’s naughty behavior, so we don’t do them. But losing TV privileges for a day works.) Gentle parenting influencers will warn you that behavioralist parenting creates “people pleasers,” but almost all millennials were raised this way, and people don’t seem very pleased with them.

In fact, when I first became a parent, I was particularly reluctant to impose consequences not just because of Internet wackos, but because my son’s preschool teacher (a woman in her sixties with a PhD and decades of experience in early childhood education) once passive-aggressively sent him home with Punished by Rewards, a book that warns against any form of rewards or imposed consequences, including grades. She did this to us specifically because during our parent-teacher conference, we mentioned that we were experimenting with time-outs and earning rewards via sticker charts, and she wanted to warn us of the serious mistake we were making. I felt humiliated, called out, and generally terrible. Every time I got the gut instinct that refusing to de-incentivize objectively naughty behavior wasn’t a good idea, I reminded myself that someone with a PhD, who was working with young children before I was born, aggressively endorsed that strategy.

Before I realized that it was okay to impose a consequence if gentle parenting fails, I frequently turned to mom groups and gentle parenting forums for advice. I also watched lots of Big Little Feelings and Transforming Toddlerhood. And what I realized from consuming all this content was that if your gentle parenting methods hit a wall (and with a child like my son, they will) there is no recourse. There is nothing else you can do. In fact, these people are so convinced that they’ve discovered the One True Way that if gentle parenting fails, they will just accuse you of doing it wrong, or insist that your child is autistic. (To that I ask, “What then? Do autistic children get to shush adults at bakeries?”)

Here’s an example from when my son was a toddler. He would throw pretty huge tantrums if he couldn’t find a desired toy, and the desired toy would never be something easy to find—it was always “that little piece of plastic that used to be connected to my stomp rocket tube” or something like that. And as I said earlier, redirecting him didn’t work. He would even go to sleep for the day, wake up, and ask about the desired item again. I turned to a gentle parenting forum, and they suggested avoiding this predicament in the first place by "toy rotating.” I reminded them that even if I only had a few toys out, he would obsess over some random household object that he stashed somewhere, or ask about things he hasn’t seen in months. Then they suggested redirection and distraction. Didn’t work. When I explained that he would frequently throw tantrums that lasted all day, or return to the obsession after a nap, they just decided he was “probably autistic,” without any attempt to explain what the parent of an autistic child in this situation should do.

Gentle parenting also does not provide any solution for what to do about a child who is behaving in a rude or disrespectful manner, especially to adults. See: the shushing. But this problem basically extends to all etiquette and manners. Popular gentle parenting accounts have warned us against the dangers of teaching children to say sorry “if they don’t really mean it” (news flash: even most adults don’t really mean it) and even against encouraging kids to say “thank you” for presents. Most importantly, according to the core gentle parenting ethos, children shouldn’t have any concern for the feelings of adults, and if you tell them that they are behaving in a rude or disrespectful way, you are “making your emotions their responsibility.” Presumably, it’s normal for a two-year-old to behave this way, but it’s simply not normal for a five-year-old to refuse to say please and thank you. And given that the only natural consequence of being an asshole is that nobody will want to hang out with you (something certain introverted kids—especially my son—don’t mind at all!) you need to have some kind of last resort so you can impose a consequence for age-inappropriate rudeness. Gentle parenting refuses to explain what that would be, and instead ensures us that it’s “developmentally normal” to be rude. Okay, but until when? Surely, kids need to be saying thank you—even if they don’t really mean it—in middle school.

I learned this the hard way when my mother-in-law chided me for my son’s manners. She had just met the young son of her building’s doorman, who had extended his hand to shake and said, “Nice to meet you.” She reminded me that when my son met the doorman, he had shouted, “Smell my butt.” Somehow, she was able to relay this to me with a straight face, although I had a hard time keeping it together.

One reason that gentle parenting’s failures are so severe when it comes to etiquette is that we no longer live in a society where manners are enforced in any real way. Strange adults in public cannot remind your children to say please and thank you. And if your kid offends an adult, even an adult in your family, the adult will likely be nice about it because they don’t want to step on your toes. Therefore, only parents can teach manners, and gentle parenting tells parents that it’s “making our emotions our children’s responsibility” to teach manners. It’s no wonder then, that a lot of kids just never learn manners unless there is some kind of incentive. The natural consequence of being rude is that adults get angry at you, but most adults will simply tolerate bad manners from a child who isn’t theirs, so that doesn’t really work!

And what about misbehavior that needs to be corrected in the moment, often immediately? For example: refusing to get dressed for school, refusing to leave the house to go to an appointment, etc? Gentle parenting does have one last resort for these time-sensitive things, which is to physically move a child if necessary, while warning them that you will “move their bodies now” (for some reason you’re supposed to say “your body” instead of “you.”) But what if a parent can’t pick up their child, due to their child’s size or their own disability? My son is very tall for his age, and by age four, if I tried to “move his body” he would thrash so much that I wouldn’t get very far without posing a real risk of dropping him. Moreover, by then I had a baby to care for as well, who was often strapped to my chest. What exactly was the “gentle parenting” solution to getting him to leave the park when he didn’t want to leave? Well, I was given the following guidelines:

  • Warn him with “you have a short time left to play.” (OK.)

  • When it’s time to leave, calmly let him know we need to leave (Hahahaha)

  • If he throws a tantrum (and of course he does) get down on his level and say, “I hear you. You really wanted to keep playing! We’ll come back tomorrow.” (Hahahahahahahahaa)

  • DO NOT tell him that you are going home and encourage him to join you. DO NOT walk away. This will send the message that you will ABANDON him. (Note that this was the only way my mother was able to get me to leave parks.)

  • If all else fails, “move his body.” No suggestions for what to do if you cannot physically transport a writhing forty-pound child on your own.

This puts parents of non-unicorn children in a bind because there truly is no good last resort. If doing anything “behavioral” is verboten, you will eventually find yourself in the same boat as my son’s preschool teacher: Once, when I was volunteering with the class, I saw her frantically chasing a child through the park shouting, “Stop your body! I’m not going to chase you! Stop your body! Please stop your body!” This school was opposed to any kind of “imposed consequences,” even a one-minute time out, so the smarter and more spirited kids usually figured out pretty quickly that they could do whatever the fuck they wanted. Also, I didn’t want to say this at the time, but as she was “enforcing the boundary” that she wouldn’t chase the child, she was quite literally chasing the child because there was no alternative! What was she going to do—let her run into traffic? The problem was, the kid knew nothing would happen if she ran away. She wasn’t even going to get a time out. The natural consequence was getting hit by a car, but many three-year-olds still believe they’re immortal.

Gentle parenting is fine. Good, even. I understand for many people my age, it’s “breaking a cycle.” But anyone who feels the need to break toxic cycles from their childhood probably experienced something much more traumatic than losing their TV privileges for a night. Sometimes, you have to just do it the ‘90s way. Perhaps this will create a generation of “people pleasers,” but at least it won’t create a generation of people who believe they should never say sorry or thank you if they “don’t really mean it.”
