On the occasion of my friend and ECW founder Jack David’s eightieth birthday, his colleagues put together a booklet of recollections and tributes. Another friend, Dan Wells, founder of Biblioasis, is one of the contributors and he’s granted me permission to share his piece. It’s a wonderful little window into this ridiculous business. I’m sure you’ll enjoy it. Dan’s experience of Jack is almost identical to mine. And be sure to check out the many great books at ECW and Biblioasis—they’re two of Canada’s finest. Here’s Dan:

The first time I met Jack David was … sometime in the early days of Biblioasis. I can’t remember exactly when: 2006, or 2007, or 2008. Biblioasis was only a couple of years old and through a program for new presses provided by the Association of Canadian Publishers Jack drove down to provide a one-day mentorship. He pulled himself out of this giant blue car—for a guy who’s lived in an auto town most of his life I’m terrible at identifying vehicles, but in my mind’s eye it’s morphed into an old Parisienne—and spent a couple of hours up in the office on the mezzanine above our first bookshop asking us questions.

It’s not what I expected to happen: I’d assumed that, as mentees, we’d be the ones asking questions. Why had we started a publishing company? What were we hoping to accomplish? Why did we make the decisions we did about where to print? How were we approaching publicity and marketing? How did that work out for us? What did we have in the pipeline? How many copies did we think we could sell of that? Really? That many? How were we going to pay for things? No, it certainly wasn’t going to be the translations from the Serbo-Croatian. Jack seemed to chuckle at almost all of my answers and with every little laugh and follow-up question I became more and more certain that I didn’t know what the hell I was doing. The light in the office, high-up as it was in the rafters and lit by a single bare bulb, was less than ideal. I remember that Jack’s transition lenses never seemed to adjust to the half-light, so I couldn’t really make out his eyes, though I did catch the odd flash of mirth.

Eventually we broke for lunch and went at his request down to the Tunnel Bar-B-Q. He’d done his undergrad at the University of Windsor and had been a TBQ regular. How were we going to pay for things? “You could do sports books,” Jack told us. I’d mentioned that I was thinking of republishing Russell Smith’s pseudonymous pornographic novel Diana: A Life in the Second Person. “Yes,” Jack said, “that would work, too. You could publish porn. That might pay for the poetry and translations.” If we published porn, I asked him, wouldn’t that affect the kind of literary books we wanted to publish? Wouldn’t it be harder to attract the kind of writers we actually wanted to work with? “Yes,” he told us, “it would. There would be a lot of authors who wouldn’t want to be on a list with such smut. Though with such smut you’d at least still have a list. And a publishing house.” Local history? There was that trademark Jack David shrug, though I didn’t know it at the time: shoulders up, elbows pinched into his belly, palms open to the sky. And in this better restaurant light more than a little mirth behind those glasses. “I dunno. Might work.”

And then there was a story. About the origins of ECW, their movement into fiction at the beginning of the 80s, a first cross-country tour involving John Metcalf, Leon Rooke, and Hugh Hood, and the dawning awareness that he’d confined himself to a life of absolute penury. And then a shift born of some sort of alchemical mix of curiosity and desperation into popular culture in the mid-1990s, and two books, a biography of K. D. Lang which sold better than anything that ECW had published to date and a sports book titled Wrestle Crap: The Worst Moments in Professional Wrestling. “That latter one,” he told us, “sold over 750,000 copies.” And it led to Wrestle Crap II and various other spin-offs. I’ve never been much of a poker player, so I have no doubt he saw the look of horror which must have crossed my face.

If you hang around long enough, publishing has a way of beating the preciousness out of you. Twenty years on, we have neither had our Wrestle Crap nor our 750,000 copy bestseller, though we did experiment with sports and regional history and cookbooks and music to pay our bills for a while. Though a strange thing began to happen: our mercenary motivations metamorphosed very quickly into something very much like care and opened up other publishing vistas to us in ways that we couldn’t have foreseen. You try something and—shoulders raised, elbows pinned into your sides, palms open to the sky—shit happens. And, sometimes, it’s even good.

I didn’t know how to read Jack that day, and it wasn’t all because of those transition lenses of his. I was likely too damn earnest, too panicked, too afraid to be an obvious fool when it would have been obvious to Jack even without his transitions that this was exactly what I was. How could it have been otherwise? I’d started a literary press in a rust-belt town with no money and no experience; and to top it off, our logo was a windmill. I was surprised when he called me a few months later to check in, and then a few months after that, and then again after that, until it was almost routine, each time asking me question after question before he’d tell me a story structured like a parable. There was this guy he started talking to, there was this manuscript this agent had sent, there was this thing he pulled out of the slush pile. It might have been a book about the Georgia Peach or some memoir of a fella who had been a studio musician for the Beatles or a book by a woman who designed golf courses. He’d tell me about what he’d learned and what excited him about it, and what kind of rigamarole he might have had to go through to acquire rights. “And,” he’d conclude each time, “it’s a really good book.”

How many copies would it sell? What was something worth? He’d mimic crumpling up a piece of paper into a ball and throw it up in the air. Or there’d be that elbow-pinning shrug and smirk.

What has become apparent over the years and now decades, as Jack continued to call me and then I called him, most often urgently, in a moment of crisis, is that he is man of immense generosity and care and curiosity, motivated by a love of books, yes, but even more by a love of both people and publishing. That look on his face that I couldn’t make sense of had been amusement and curiosity. It hasn’t changed a bit over the years.

Recent decades have seen an explosion of government-approved casinos, sports betting, and online gaming opportunities. The politicians and officials behind them meticulously track the revenue they receive, but turn a blind eye to its often devastating human costs.

Through gripping personal stories and a comprehensive investigation, journalist Rob Csernyik shows how public officials evade accountability for lives shattered by gambling addiction―for instance, rarely recording gambling as a risk factor in suicide. They refuse to confront the disturbing question of how many Canadians are dying from activities actively promoted by their governments.

From the rise of video lottery terminals to the explosion of smartphone betting, A Losing Hand shows how modern gambling has become more accessible, more immersive, and more dangerous than ever. The result is a crisis hiding in plain sight, one that policymakers have ignored for decades.

A powerful work of investigative journalism, this book forces a reckoning with the true cost of gambling in Canada.
