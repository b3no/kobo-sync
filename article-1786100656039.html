Why I Gave Up On My Dream of Big Parties

I liked the idea of hosting big parties, but maybe I just wanted to dress up and chat.

There are many jobs that people think they want to do, because they want to be the type of person who does that job, despite not having any interest in what that job actually entails. A good example is “owning your own business.” I have met countless people over the years who dreamed of owning their own business, but didn’t actually have a business idea. Naturally, I was curious as to why they liked the idea of owning a business if they were not inspired to, well, start a business. (I notice the same thing with people who want to write a book, but don’t have an idea for a book, and on top of that, hate writing and never do it.) Often, the explanation of the would-be-business-owner boiled down to the fact that they liked the idea of being a CEO, or “being their own boss,” but they weren’t totally sure (or interested in) what it involved. They didn’t actually want to deal with overheard costs, payroll, business strategy, marketing, or quite frankly any of the work that goes into owning a business.

For most of my life, I had the same attitude about throwing big parties.

As I’ve written before, I’ve had a long history of throwing parties—parties that I was hoping would be big and fantastic, but never were. I threw a few good ones which still fell short of my expectations, and a lot of bad ones. (You can hear about this in more detail on the really funny Search Engine episode about my predicament.) My biggest obstacle was getting good turnout (most people just never RSVP’ed at all and seemingly everyone I knew was “out of town” every weekend) but attendance aside, the parties were not as fancy or fun as I had hoped.

But I’m realizing that perhaps it’s not just that I couldn’t throw a big party…but that I didn’t really want to. I wanted to be the type of person who threw big parties. And this is why, at least for now, I’m abandoning this goal—and replacing it with something else.

As I mentioned on the podcast episode, my dream party resembled the formal yet delightfully chaotic parties my parents threw when I was a little kid. They threw one Christmas party a year, and occasionally an outdoor spring party. These parties were fancy, extremely well-attended, and yet, also child-friendly, so you could expect to see adults of all ages in cocktail attire, alongside impish children in their baby finery chasing each other around the table, often staying up till midnight and passing out on some bench or sofa. Sometimes, an adult would make an ass of themselves too, such as my second-grade teacher who showed up, got shitfaced, and did a big j’accuse that someone stole $40 from her wallet. These parties meant so much to me that I looked forward to them all year, even into high school when I should have found them dorky. And to this day, I still have a recurring dream that I’m getting ready for my parents’ Christmas party in my childhood bedroom but none of my dresses fit and they’re actually all my mother’s dresses. Probably not worth reading into that one.

Fun as they were, there are a bunch of practical reasons that I can’t throw these kinds of parties. The two biggest reasons are as follows: First, my house is smaller than my parents’ house and my husband is far less tolerant of mess and chaos than my parents were. Second, ignoring the RSVP ratio issue, I do not have nearly as many potential guests to invite in the first place. Even now, when I am pretty happy with my number of friends, I still don’t have a huge swath of potential attendees, let alone attendees who all know each other and would be excited to get together. My parents had a wide variety of acquaintances and connections from their jobs and their college alumni network, many of whom knew each other. Even doing my best socially, I simply don’t have these communities. My husband and I both work 100% remotely, so even coworker friends are off the table.

The main reason I got into throwing big fancy parties—or attempting to—was in part because I was not being invited to the kinds of parties I wanted to attend. I loved the formality and excitement of nightlife, but the club was missing the intimacy and conversation of a house party. Meanwhile, house parties were missing the pomp and ceremony I craved. I went to house parties sometimes in San Francisco in my twenties—often totally random ones, where we knew someone who knew someone—but they were very casual (a cocktail dress would have been wildly out of place) with only some warm beer and maybe a bag of Cheetos which nobody but me would touch. (Is it rude to eat an entire bag of Cheetos if nobody else at the party is eating it?)

Casual parties were fine, of course, but they weren’t my fantasy. Neither was a night out at a nightclub. I had an obsessive drive to combine these two experiences. The obvious solution to my predicament: throw big, opulent cocktail parties. Be the change you want to see in the world, I told myself. But it was hard to make this a reality. I wanted to throw swanky martini parties, but these events simply did not exist in my particular “broke twenty-something startup worker” community. I was not rich. Yes, my husband and I grew up well off, but our families weren’t financially supporting us, so we were living off a combined $20K income and spending it all on rent, which leaves very little room for martini glasses and centerpieces. Also, the setting was never right. I did not live in a cool Manhattan high rise or even a funky Brooklyn loft. in college, I lived in a tiny college dorm room, and then later, a decrepit San Francisco apartment infested with black mold and a neighbor who was constantly shrieking at nobody in particular (still not sure why, I stopped monitoring that situation years ago).

Sometimes, I would hobble aspects of a fancy party together, leaving out some crucial component for the sake of my budget. For example, I once served roast duck but couldn’t pony up the money for decent napkins, so everyone had to use paper towels. Another time, I tried to make “jello shots” but didn’t have any shot glasses, so I displayed a Tupperware of lime jello made with tequila, which had a few non-chopped strings of cilantro petrified in there. Not sure what the hell I was thinking with that one, but to my credit that was more of a pregame than a party, and I was twenty-one.

My parties suffered for many reasons, but mostly the same reasons that I could not replicate my parents’ parties: my place was too small, we didn’t have enough money to actually decorate or cater, and I didn’t have enough friends. Because I frequently invited people I didn’t know very well, it was also very difficult to get them to adhere to the whole cocktail dress code (if dressing up isn’t your thing, why would you do that for a near-stranger?) so I was stuck with parties that were neither well-attended or fancy—made all the worse by the fact that I would wear something extremely showy, which made other people feel weird, especially if they didn’t know me that well. I was the change I wanted to see in the world, but…nobody else was clamoring for this change, quite frankly.

There was only one exception, which may have been a random strike of luck—I hosted a prom-themed birthday party where people were encouraged to either wear prom-adjacent attire, or clothes that were trendy when they were in high school. Almost everyone I invited came, people had fun, and basically everyone participated in the theme (it helped that we had a casual opt-out which was still on-theme.) We even had a voting ceremony for “senior superlatives” and the option to crown two attendees Prom King and Queen (and despite being a massive narcissist, I had a rule that I could not win Prom Queen at my own birthday party.) So clearly, it worked out once, but there was a much larger denominator of parties which fell short.

But as the Search Engine episode made clear, a big part of why I’ve never had great invite lists in the first place was that I am simply not a very likable person, which limits the amount of people I know well enough to invite, and further limits the amount of people who even show up. This was an issue that was at its worst in my childhood and teen years and has slowly gotten better over time, although I am certainly under no delusion that everyone likes me now either.

Some humorous evidence of this social weirdness was relayed on the podcast by a close friend of mine from San Francisco who described me performing a litany of musical impressions, from Lana Del Rey to Iggy Azalea, underneath my husband’s decorative gimmick Ass Man license plate, in a not-very-nice apartment, all while my husband ordered everyone to listen to my impressions, then commanded me to do more impressions. Obviously, this is Michael Scott tier—no, wait, worse, it’s David Brent tier behavior. But even when I had abandoned this schtick, I had to recognize that without a core community (a church, an organization, a college alumni network, close work friends) I was just not going to have enough people who knew each other and were excited enough to see each other that they were willing to leave their houses, socialize with strangers and dress up. After all, the promise of a party full of people you don’t know very well might not even be appealing to most people without a dress code (personally, meeting a bunch of new people and dressing up is exactly my kind of thing, but I eventually realized this is unappealing to most people). Even when my social life is going well, I have a bunch of individual friendships, maybe a few that connect but no group. And as I’ve written about before, I probably never really wanted a group either.

Even if my social skills were perfect (and they’ve come a long way, but are obviously not perfect) I don’t think I’d be able to find thirty people to invite in the first place, let alone get them to my house, have them dress up, and throw a great cocktail party that they all enjoy. It’s just not going to happen, for all the reasons outlined here and more. So while I was very curious to hear my friends and family muse to PJ Vogt of Search Engine about why I can’t get good party turnout, by the time the investigation was over, I wasn’t sure I even wanted to throw big parties anymore.

I don’t even think this is just a “me” issue anymore. Every day, I see a new story about nobody RSVPing to a party, including parties which couldn’t possibly be related to an annoying adult (Seriously, would you not RSVP to a literal child’s birthday because you don’t like the parents? So yeah, I am annoying, and that was a big part of why I couldn’t get good party turnout, but clearly the issue is more widespread and fixing my social skills isn’t going to resolve the turnout issue entirely. It seems odd to me that people would simply decline a party invitation with zero conflicts (I’ve never done it!) but this apparently happens all the time!

It’s not a sour grapes thing (don’t put it in the papers that it’s sour grapes!) It’s not that I don’t want to attend big parties or that I don’t enjoy them. It’s that I’m not sure I’m cut out to throw them. Perhaps this is just me being socially inept (many such cases) but up until that episode (and up until reading the commentary about it) I thought that a host’s job was basically to make food and drinks and welcome people to the party. I’m aware this might seem obvious to other people, but it did not occur to me that as a party host, it was my responsibility to strategize about which attendees might get along and then introduce people who didn’t know each other—something especially difficult to do if all your friends are disparate strangers to each other, like mine. I had enough trouble making people like me, now I had to make sure they liked each other? EMOTIONAL LABOR MUCH?? Strategizing around different introductions and groups is not my strong suit, and it’s not a social skill I’ve honed. In any case, I had the revelation that I had been throwing parties, and then experiencing them as if I were a guest.

And that’s probably what I really wanted all along. I wanted to be a guest at big parties, meeting lots of new people but ultimately having zero responsibility for everyone’s enjoyment of the night. I might enjoy cooking, dressing up, decorating, or conjuring up a theme, but I really don’t enjoy the work of making introductions, checking on people to make sure they’re having fun, building a community from which I can invite people, or any other unsexy party etiquette that specifically falls to the host. I am the party version of the guy who wants to run a business but actually just wants to drive a nice car and wear a suit.

So how can I replicate the parts I wanted, without trudging onward in a completely doomed quest to throw big fancy parties?

Obviously, I’ve said this enough: I wanted the opportunity to dress up in an otherwise casual and drab world. So I had to figure out how I might incorporate this into my life in other ways.

First of all, I had to examine what I liked about dressing up. Did I like wearing fancy clothes specifically, or did I just like wearing something fun? Because these are not the same things. Of course, I do still enjoy a nice cocktail dress, but since working on elevating my everyday wardrobe, I can find an occasion to wear something “fun” just to have lunch with a friend or even take my kids out. And no, by “fun” I don’t mean a bodycon bubblegum pink two-piece Barbie suit with a big cutout right at my ass crack, I mean…something like the below outfits.

From left to right, the events: children’s birthday party, movie/dinner double date, toddler class with a close mom friend, and shopping with an old long-distance friend who was in town.

Finding ways to have fun with outfits that can be worn outside a nightclub or cocktail party has been a major way for me scratch the fashion itch without creating entire (not very fun) events so that I would have an excuse to wear a party dress like a four-year-old desperate to wear her Elsa costume. And as I’ve said before, while I love dresses which are too sexy for weddings and too formal for most events, they’re just not practical and I shouldn’t buy them. Now that I can get excited about the right sweater and the right boots, quite literally any outing is an “excuse to wear something fun.”

If I wanted to wear something sexy, then again, problem solved: more date nights! My husband and I hired an occasional date night babysitter so we weren’t solely relying on our in-laws for date night sitting. After all, the only person for whom I’m dressing sexy is my husband, and even if I do enjoy being “shown off” that’s still about him, and I don’t need an entire audience of partygoers to accomplish this. Granted, I had a much bigger appetite for sexy dressing when I was younger, so this is no longer a big motivation. But when the mood strikes, date night is a better occasion than a backyard party with fellow parents, where apparently it’s “not normal” to wear a leash and harness with a ball gag?

And then: the socialization. I genuinely love socializing with people. It took me a while to realize that there was a difference between doing standup comedy for people and socializing with them, so I would venture that I didn’t really “love socializing” at the time of my most disastrous parties. But since learning how to interact with other people, I’ve actually begun to enjoy the normal two-way conversations that come more easily to other people, and making new friends has become something I really enjoy.

But one need not throw a huge party to do this. In fact, the biggest parties I threw never really facilitated good conversations because I was always focused on something else—messes being made, making sure my kids were okay, making sure my husband wasn’t over-extending himself and making everyone too many custom cocktails...and perhaps doing the occasional cheeky Creed impression. Again, what I really wanted to be was a guest. So I replaced “throwing big parties” with other forms of socialization which are more intimate and require less hosting skill and fewer attendees. We have a couple close friends who also have kids, and sometimes we’ll invite one of those families over just to watch a movie and have a glass of wine while the kids play nearby. For Christmas, we invited our close friends and their daughter over for Christmas movies for the kids, dinner and homemade cookies. Sometimes, we will invite two couples and make it a “dinner party,” which enables me to cook something fun and wear something cute (and the term “dinner party” implies everyone dresses up a little) but I don’t have to experience the stress of being a hostess of a large party, let alone worrying about how 70% of invitees didn’t reply at all.

I would be remiss not to point out another reason that I threw parties: “getting attention.” It doesn’t reflect well on me, but evidently, I’ve always enjoyed performing and making people laugh. Especially earlier in my adult years, I leveraged parties as a way to imprison unsuspecting audience members and force them to find me funny. Well, I now have Substack subscribers for that. Sorry, you’ve all fallen victim to my nefarious plot. At least you’re sparing real-life people the indignity of it all.
