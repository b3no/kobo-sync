Everything I Learned About Sex I Learned From Austin Powers

I saw Austin Powers when I was eight. I didn't realize it was parody.

As a little girl in the nineties, my parents were extremely strict about the age-appropriateness of what I watched on television, an artifact of being the eldest (my husband has a brother seven years older than he is, and he was apparently watching Pulp Fiction at six.) I watched Sesame Street in preschool. I watched Rugrats in elementary school. And for reasons I don’t yet understand, my parents took me to see Austin Powers: International Man of Mystery when I was eight.

For those of you somehow unfamiliar with Austin Powers, the movie is a James Bond parody, initially set in the swingin’ sixties, starring “international man of mystery” Austin Powers, a British spy who is irresistible to the ladies. But he’s played by Mike Myers, who is making just about every effort to look as ridiculous (and satirically British—complete with horrendous teeth) as possible. The titular character eventually gets cryogenically frozen and wakes up in the late nineties, where nobody is interested in his randy retro bachelor antics, especially not Vanessa Kensington (played by Liz Hurley) his gorgeous spy partner and the daughter of his paramour from the sixties (problematic age gap!)

Austin Powers is a good movie, but there was just one problem: due to my otherwise sheltered experience with media, I did not know what James Bond was, nor did I understand that any of this was parody. And as a result, Austin Powers shaped the way I viewed heterosexuality for many years to come.

First, the big joke of Austin Powers is that Austin Powers isn’t actually that attractive, so his appeal to women is meant to be funny. He’s dorky, he’s slapstick, he wears a ridiculous pirate-type shirt and crushed velvet suits, he has crooked and yellow teeth, and he’s constantly doing some variation on soyface.

But I did not get this joke. I thought that this was what “catnip for women” really looked like. And I couldn’t help but wonder what was wrong with me that I did not find him attractive. Granted, I was eight, so I didn’t find any grown men attractive, but I at least understood the appeal of The Backstreet Boys or Leonardo DiCaprio (remember, he was once young and lithe.) But this guy? Maybe I’m just a lesbian, I conceded, because the women of the film were quite a bit more attractive.

Now, let’s talk about the women. First of all, I wanted to be Vanessa Kensington. Liz Hurley was the first brunette sex symbol I had ever seen, and naturally I assumed this meant I looked exactly like her. And she dressed like this. Anyone familiar with my most unhinged fashion eras (especially a certain controversial homemade jumpsuit) will immediately understand what this did to me:

The groovy, sexy, over-the-top fashion unlocked something in my brain, doubly for two scenes in particular: the opening sequence, when Austin streaks through colorful 1960s London to the shock and excitement of random people on the street, and a scene where he infiltrates a go-go dance club and unmasks a go-go dancer as a man in drag to the tune of Incense and Peppermints (they don’t let you do that anymore, because of woke!)

I loved that everyone was wearing something showy, flashy and fun. I wanted to participate, and I shortly would when my mom got me a bright yellow vinyl skirt and vest set the following year. Then, for my tenth birthday (a big bash, because I did not have a bat mitzvah or sweet sixteen) my mom sewed me a black mockneck sleeveless cocktail dress emblazoned with various metallic sparkly stars, which I paired with knee high white patent leather boots. Of course, the movie outfits which inspired my aesthetic were literal costumes, not for a tasteful period piece but for a clownish parody, but this fact was lost on me.

But despite the fun outfits Vanessa wears toward the end of the film, it’s important for me not to gloss over the way she is introduced (and the way she appears for most of the movie.) She’s not groovy. She’s refined, classy, unbearably posh…perhaps even a bit of a prude. She wears plain gray suits which do nothing to conceal her beauty. (CBK alert!) She initially finds Austin Powers tiresome and boorish, focused only on their work. She eventually goes Bond Girl mode after Austin Powers unlocks her wild side, but that happens towards the end of the movie.

I wanted to be both sides of Vanessa. I wanted to be demure, serious, classy, even a bit priggish, until some wild, zany external force pushed me into my true, untamed nature (yes, I was having these thoughts at eight, but they weren’t sexual or really focused on “men” at all—much more abstract and focused on being adequately feminine and dainty.) I loved that Vanessa was, in all ways throughout the movie, the pursued. Her constantly rebuffing of Austin Powers is even more significant given that every woman in the sixties wanted him, even her mother. Only with great persuasion does Vanessa eventually come around. She is anything but desperate.

Deep in this obsession, I asked my aunt if I—a school-aged child with a speech impediment caused by a palette expander—looked anything like Liz Hurley (my mom had kindly fed this delusion) and my aunt bluntly said, “Hm, I don’t see it.” Well, fair!

Becoming Vanessa Kensington appealed to me because I was so used to being the girl who liked everyone else more than they liked her (or at least that’s how I experienced things.) I wanted to be myself—bright colors, patterns, singing, theatrics—but only when everyone else (read: the beloved-by-all Austin Powers) made me do it. I wanted to be the person shyly shaking her head while everyone shouted, “Speech! Speech! Speech!”

Such a fantasy had far-reaching tendrils, as it basically shaped how I viewed sex as an adult woman. More specifically, I am turned off by any scenario where I feel like I am the aggressor, the wanter, the pursuer.

Heterosexual desire (and how that desire, or expression thereof, is gendered) is central to the movie. Yes, Austin Powers is considered a sex symbol by the women around him, but the only woman he really wants for something beyond sex (Vanessa) initially finds him irritating. And yet, while Austin truly desires Vanessa, he shallowly lusts after many other women too—satirical variations on Bond Girls (Pussy Galore et al) such as the creatively named Alotta Fagina:

The entire Austin Powers franchise is basically a long series of Austin becoming waylaid by gorgeous woman with ulterior movies, because as good of a spy as he is, women are his weakness. I may be making an ass of myself by attempting to analyze a movie with such an intentionally silly premise, but as an eight-year-old, I didn’t know it was a joke. I saw that women universally desired a fairly dorky-looking guy, that guy could basically have any woman he wanted and he would sleep with basically anyone, and most importantly, the only woman he couldn’t have was the one he liked the most. One must wonder if he would even have liked Vanessa more than everyone else if she wasn’t so much of a challenge.

Even as a child, I remember having a moment where I pictured a female Austin Powers—a slightly doughy, crooked-toothed, middle-aged woman with frizzy brown hair, over whom men fawned and swooned, who eventually unlocked the wild and groovy side of a handsome and uptight male British spy. Perhaps I missed my shot to make this movie—2020 was obviously the time for this, and it probably would have been produced by Amy Schumer and starred Melissa McCarthy. But I remember recoiling at the concept as I thought it up. Something about it just felt wrong. Even more wrong than all these gorgeous women falling head over heels for Austin Powers.

But in the spirit of not understanding the joke of Austin Powers, I trudged onto my preteen and teen years with a low-level, subconscious understanding of how sexuality works—men do the desiring, women are objects of desire. The most essential part of being a woman is being able to make men, even ones who weren’t that attractive, quite literally hypnotized by your beauty (see: the infamous Fembots who quite literally sedate a horny Austin Powers with their boob-laughing-gas.) As silly as it sounds now, this was the standard to which I held myself. I didn’t consciously ask myself, “What would a Fembot do?” but I’d be lying if I said I didn’t sometimes admonish myself for behaving in an inadequately Vanessa Kensingtonish way. I did not feel pretty unless I was garnering obvious gawking, but I also didn’t want anyone to think that’s what I wanted. A quandary indeed, especially for an eleven-year-old whose male counterparts are still sword fighting with pool noodles.

Of course, the “men get to be ugly and sexy, even as a joke, and women don’t” or the “women are the desired party” theme is not unique to Austin Powers, and if I hadn’t absorbed it there, it would have happened elsewhere. I believe some women who grew up around the same time as I did witnessed this sort of content that presented women as one-dimensional objects of desire and rebelled against it by finding it all a bit gross and predatory, shirking the push-up bras and bodycon dresses to establish that they were more than that. But fuck, I just wanted to be that.

Last but not least—my parents informed me that Dr. Evil was meant to be a parody of Lorne Michaels. However, I did not know who that was, and I thought “she” was named “Lauren Michaels” and I couldn’t understand why anyone would portray a woman like that. Justice for the maligned and misgendered Lauren Michaels.
