Welcome to The Weekly Scroll!

The Weekly Scroll is a once-a-week roundup of the most interesting, strangest, and funniest stories from around the social internet.

This week, we’re talking about the rise of AI influencer slop, the hot trend taking over Harry Potter fandoms, and YouTube’s fight against clippers.

AI influencer slop is growing

Comedians don’t take kindly to joke theft.

But comedian George Brett Olson went viral this week with a post about an unusual form of joke theft: A fully AI-generated OnlyFans model was stealing his jokes. Even weirder than that, the “model” was also stealing his outfits:

The thieving account, operating under the name “Tiffany Sloane,” was copying content from real comedians and putting their words in a Pixar Mom-shaped model as a growth hack strategy — a way to drive traffic to an OnlyFans featuring the same AI-generated model.

Now, on the one hand, Instagram appears to have nuked the account, and all its posts have been wiped. But on the other hand, the growth hack worked: Sloane accumulated more than 350,000 followers. And if Olson’s video hadn’t gone viral, “Tiffany” would still be pulling the same stunt and getting away with it.

There are a lot of growth hacks out there designed to drive people from regular social media to the more salacious corners of the internet. Many livestreamers active in the Hot Tub Meta — a content strategy where female influencers stream while lounging in hot tubs wearing bikinis — also have OnlyFans accounts they’re promoting. And a few years back, other adult models realized that podcast clips were hot, so they started filming fake podcast segments of shows that don’t exist to use as clips for their socials.

But the addition of AI (and the blatant theft) makes this feel even grosser than those strategies.

Sadly, I think this is a sign of things to come. Earlier this year, Wired ran a story about fully AI-generated hot MAGA girls who were racking up millions of followers — and yes, driving people to OnlyFans or similar sites. What’s depressing is that these AI influencers aren’t even very good, but the trick still works: The Hot Girl MAGA accounts were not even a little bit believable — one of the characters had selfies with both Donald Trump and Vladimir Putin! And even with stolen jokes, the AI comedian above has no sense of timing or delivery whatsoever.

But there’s an audience out there that will still lap it up. All you need is a generically pretty face and a waist-to-hip ratio that would make an anime character blush, and you’ll be drowning in engagement.

Share

The internet’s gooners aren’t the only ones entranced by low-quality AI slop. Even though YouTube has made efforts to crack down on the lowest-effort AI videos, as I’m writing these words one of the most popular videos this week is this AI-generated short of a baby. It’s been up for eight days and has more than 135 million views. Which, I have to say, is incredibly grim.

Facebook went through a period of Shrimp Jesus slop they still haven’t fully recovered from, with credulous boomers commenting “Amen” beneath bizarre AI-generated pictures. There are now startups bragging that they can automate the entire influencing process with fake personas.

It’s a frightening possibility to think that our tech overlords could be shoveling this kind of low-quality content down our throats because it helps their bottom lines. But it’s both more frightening and more plausible to me to think that maybe, just maybe, this is what people want. They like the slop, they’re eager for more, and whoever’s serving it to them is just fulfilling the authentic demand for unlimited AI-generated garbage.

Colombian livestreamer Westcol learned the hard way exactly how the robot apocalypse will happen: First they knock us out. Then they do Fortnite emotes over our lifeless bodies.

YouTube goes after clippers

Longtime Weekly Scroll readers will know that one of my obsessions for the past year has been how the clipping economy influences our perceptions of online fame. Creators used to make clips of their content as a way to drive people to that content. But today, popular livestreamers, podcasters, and YouTubers exist in a weird paradigm where the entire purpose of long-form content is to create short, viral clips. The clips are no longer promo. They’re the entire game.

The chart above shows how much larger the audience for clips is than the average audience size for three notable and controversial streamers. It also understates the difference by a wide margin because it’s looking only at clips specifically uploaded by the streamer themselves. That matters because the clipping economy isn’t built around the creators, but around an army of freelance clippers who take content and cut it into bite-size clips they think will go viral.

These clippers are often paid by the view, and they’re very good at what they do. How many of you have ever actually tuned in to a livestream from Hasan Piker or Nick Fuentes? Probably very few. But how many of you have seen some third-party account go viral with a clip of something one of these streamers said?

YouTube, it seems, has had enough. Last week it quietly announced that it was updating its Shorts algorithm to emphasize original content. It specifically warned clippers:

Channels identified as primarily aggregating or re-uploading other creators’ videos without significant changes will likely see less distribution within the Shorts feed. If you use clips from another creator, make sure you’re adding real value through your own original commentary, unique edits, or storytelling.

Other sites have already done the same: Instagram announced in April that its algorithm would punish “aggregator” accounts, and Twitter recently demonetized those accounts as well. I have to think this is a positive step — and one that will hopefully stop the same five to six mega-influencers from dominating our feeds.

Making Malfoid

She’s got the same shock-white hair. She’s got the same nasty demeanor. And she’s definitely got the same penchant for saying “Potter” as if the word itself disgusts her. The hottest new fan fiction character is a gender-bent version of Draco Malfoy that the Harry Potter fandom is calling “Malfoid.”

The concept of a female Malfoy isn’t new, because it’s 2026 and every conceivable angle of Harry Potter fan fiction has already been explored dozens of times. But the current hysteria kicked off when Twitter user @redteneri posted the new character on September 22, garnering more than 170,000 likes.

In the weeks that followed, users were posting their own art of Harry and Malfoid falling for one another, cosplaying as Malfoid, and even generating AI videos of Harry and Malfoid making out. One fanfic writer is literally rewriting the entire Harry Potter series with Malfoid subbed in.

What explains the trend’s rapid spread? First, it just seems correct that Malfoy is more interesting if the character is an aristocratic snob who hates Harry … but with an undercurrent of romantic tension. It’s also just fun to draw anime-style Potter characters, I guess?

Fanfic and shipping communities are typically dominated by women, but this iteration seems to be primarily driven by men. Some folks say that men don’t care about romance, but hey! They do! And apparently all that was needed to unlock men’s romantic side was … a gender-bent Draco Malfoy?
