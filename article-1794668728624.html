Welcome to The Weekly Scroll!

The Weekly Scroll is a once-a-week roundup of the most interesting, strangest, and funniest stories from around the social internet.

This week, we’re talking about the Jensen Huang scandal, the prevalence of foreign influence campaigns on social media, whether or not young men are normal now, and how platforms keep losing the battle to declare their design choices as speech.

Jensen Huang’s very bad week

Over the past two weeks, Jensen Huang, the longtime CEO and co-founder of chipmaking giant Nvidia, has gone from arguably the most powerful man in AI to ousted from his company in a sordid sex-and-espionage scandal. Huang had been doing some, ahem, parallel processing with Alice Huang,¹ who U.S. officials say is a Chinese spy.²

Even before the potential espionage revelation, the affair mattered for reasons beyond personal impropriety. Alice Huang was an executive at AI infrastructure company Megaspeed, which was under investigation for allegedly smuggling advanced Nvidia chips into China in defiance of U.S. export controls. The New York Times has reported that the Commerce Department investigation had to be transferred to the Justice Department after it was compromised by, you guessed it, Nvidia.

Share

It paints a troubling picture: Jensen Huang has a relationship with a Chinese spy, then his company coincidentally happens to sell advanced chips to several companies she’s linked to, and those chips end up being smuggled into China in violation of federal law. On its own, this might be enough to topple Huang from his perch at Nvidia, but adding fuel to the fire, the Times reported Wednesday that Huang had a second affair with one of his direct reports.

And as a wise man once said:

Sure enough, Reuters is now reporting a third affair (and I’d be surprised if that’s where it stops). Huang has since resigned.

There are too many angles to this story to cover in one article, and rather than try, I’ll link you to a few good resources: a complete timeline of events, a discussion of what this means for China hawks and AI safety, and an analysis of how the episode exposes the weaknesses of Silicon Valley’s “cult of the founder.”

But this being The Weekly Scroll, there is one angle in particular that I think is worth covering: When the story first broke, I noticed a number of odd accounts with unusually strong pro-Huang takes. According to Tyler Johnston of the Midas Project, these were part of a network of hundreds of sock-puppet accounts all using the same talking points to defend Huang’s reputation.

The accounts were linked to an Israeli influence company that has also supported the Qatari government and the Sudanese Rapid Support Forces, a paramilitary group credibly accused of genocide. While we don’t yet know for sure, based on the available reporting, it seems likely that this group was used by a geopolitical actor to promote the idea of easing chip restrictions to China.

The main foreign influence story here is obviously China’s attempt to secure high-powered Nvidia chips. But the secondary story around social media influence campaigns deserves our attention as well.

For several years, I’ve stressed that we do not take the threat of foreign interference in online discourse seriously enough. If we’re lucky, those influence campaigns are out in the open, like Iran’s Lego propaganda or China’s “wolf warrior” posturing. If we’re unlucky, we only figure out after the fact that Russia has been paying American conservative influencers to spread anti-Ukraine talking points.

Social media is uniquely vulnerable to propaganda efforts because it’s so decentralized, reactive, and immediate. If you think every major power on the planet (and most of the intermediate powers as well) isn’t actively running influence campaigns, you’re in denial. We know that Israel does. We know that Saudi Arabia does. We know that Russia does, and Iran, and China, and India. And it’s not just America that gets targeted: Taiwan, the Philippines, Moldova, Romania, Pakistan, Germany, and dozens of other countries have been targets.

And these are just the campaigns that got caught. Remember: If you find two ants in your kitchen, the best estimate of the total number of ants in your kitchen is not two. We are almost certainly swimming in foreign influence campaigns, and the Jensen Huang story is one more example of how careful you need to be when reading anything relating to geopolitics online.

Let’s check in on men

In the wake of the blue tsunami last week, many liberal commentators are asking: Are men back?

Slate sure seems to think so, declaring “Men Are Ready to Be Normal Again.” Young men backed Democrats over Republicans by 22 percentage points, a major shift from 2024 when 56% of young men backed Trump for president. Republican gun influencer and YouTuber Brandon Herrera, who called liberal women “fat and wrong,” lost a congressional race in a heavily gerrymandered Texas congressional district to Katy Padilla Stout, a woman whose career centered on educating refugees, foster children, and those with special needs.

Perhaps we’re experiencing “chud fatigue,” an exhaustion with the Barstool-Joe Rogan-Logan Paul manosphere and its particularly grating form of brash, loud masculinity. Russell Brand is in jail! Hell, even JD Vance has returned to speaking like a public intellectual instead of a memelord, perhaps because he can see which way the wind is blowing.

Young men! Not so bad!

On the other hand: Stephen Miller, whose idea of a killer political strategy is promoting “White Boy November,” was just made White House chief of staff. The future stewards of Miller’s political movement don’t look any more well-adjusted: College Republicans are in bed with Nick Fuentes, and they’re posting fun images that show the Statue of Liberty in flames while calling non-Christian Republicans “demon worshippers.” Plus, we were just treated to a deranged parade of Malfoid and Anakin memes, so it’s not clear just how normal young men are right now.

How are we to make sense of all this? Are young men back to being a liberal voting bloc? Are they still devoted to being insane edgelords and groypers? It’s probably both at once.

Young men, at large, seem to be less impressed than ever with performative hyper-masculinity as filtered through podcasts, influencers, and conservative politicians. But there’s still a rump of disaffected young men who are too far down the rabbit hole to turn back. They don’t really know how to do anything else but go full edgelord at all times, and they’ve burned enough bridges with “respectable” politics that they’re doubling down. Thankfully, that strategy seems less popular than ever with the majority of young men.

In seemingly unrelated men’s news: Some therapists are now offering therapy sessions where you can talk it out while playing Fortnite, Rocket League, or Mario Kart. And yes, sure, gamers can be women as well, but I think we all know who this is aimed at.³

What’s the deal with algorithms?

Google, Meta, and TikTok are suing the state of California to block a state law that would ban personalized feeds, autoplay, and other features classified as “addictive” for anyone under 16 years old. The law would also require companies to verify ages for any user they provide “addictive” features to.

To paper over an enormous amount of nuance, the case essentially relies on one question: Are customized feeds a form of speech that platforms engage in, an editorial judgment similar to the choices that streaming platforms or TV networks make? Or are feeds products like any other, which can be regulated according to the harms they may create?

Of note: In March, a Los Angeles jury found Meta and YouTube negligent because they contributed to a young woman’s addiction to social media, while a New Mexico jury found Meta liable for endangering children. In August, Meta agreed to pay $18 billion to settle claims the company harms children. And in September, TikTok settled with Alabama for at least $100 million over similar claims.

Reading between the lines of these lawsuits, the central claim is almost always that the feed is a defective product and can be judged on that basis. The social media companies are repeatedly either losing at trial or settling for large amounts before they can get there. I think the future of the “our design features are a simple matter of free speech” argument is dim, based not on my own inclinations but on the reality of what’s happening in courts around the country.

It’s not just America: The EU last week accused TikTok of violating the Digital Services Act, saying that it pulls users into “rabbit holes” that threaten their well-being. And while the EU already has an incredibly strict regulatory regime for technology companies, it’s looking into banning “dark patterns” like urgent-sounding “Act now! Limited time offer!” prompts, loot boxes, and personalized ads.

Reddit is immensely popular because it’s one of the last places on the internet to get genuine, unfiltered human opinions on various products and services without having to use an AI chatbot. It’s so good that it has become common knowledge that adding “Reddit” to Google searches makes them better.
