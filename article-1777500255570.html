A strong month in a fragile market

March data shows momentum, but a build-to-rent crackdown and tighter capital markets threaten to stall future supply.

Halina Bennet
Apr 29
∙
Paid

The most significant federal housing legislation in decades has not yet been signed into law, but it is already strangling the housing market, according to a survey of 14 build-to-rent (B.T.R.) firms conducted by housing policy lobby groups Inclusive Abundance and Up for Growth. That survey concluded that $3.4 billion in B.T.R. investment — about 10,000 units of housing — has already been frozen in anticipation of the bill, according to the Wall Street Journal. While the survey covers only a small slice of the roughly 1,700 firms in the industry, it points to a broader pullback in capital.

The legislation that triggered this pullback, the 21st Century ROAD to Housing Act, passed the Senate in March as a major supply-side package. But a late-stage provision — Section 901, now under consideration in the House — is already colliding with that goal. The measure curtails B.T.R. development to address concerns about private equity firms outbidding homebuyers, but in practice, as Representative Josh Harder told me last week, it sweeps in the entire B.T.R. category, which is currently building 72,000 new rental units per year.

To understand how the $3.4 billion hit happened so quickly (before the bill has even been reconciled between chambers), I spoke with Mike Kingsella, chief executive officer of Up for Growth, one of the groups that conducted the survey.

A mandatory seven-year sell-off requirement in Section 901 creates what Kingsella called “balloon risk” for lenders; he compared this moment to valuation swings during the 2008 financial crisis.

“Lenders have no idea what market conditions overall will be,” he said. “Lenders from Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac to HUD and its 221(d)(4) construction-to-perm lending program, as well as investment committees from state public-employee retirement systems to life insurance companies to pension funds, are essentially saying we’re not going to consider funding any project that smells of build-to-rent.”

Critically, the immediate market effects did not require the bill to have already passed.

The genie, as Kingsella put it, is already out of the bottle.

The Senate voted 89 to 10, and lenders are panicking accordingly, with the pending legislation already freezing the market.

Kingsella’s position is emphatically not that the bill should fail.

“This is a really good bill, and this is a historic bill,” he told me. “It’s a 36-provision bill with 35 amazing provisions that can significantly move the needle when it comes to unlocking more housing production and preservation.”

Kingsella said that the provision, as written, is “internally inconsistent with the stated objectives of the broader legislative package” because it would incentivize owners of multifamily-zoned B.T.R. communities to convert their property into individually platted single-family lots under exclusionary zoning — the precise outcome that other provisions within the bill seek to discourage.

“This would be an unforced error of historic proportions if this bill simply passes as is,” he said.

But Kingsella is optimistic that the bill, if adjustments are made to Section 901, is still a winner. A bipartisan group of 76 House members seems to think so, too: They published a letter last week warning of the provision’s negative implications if it passes.

Starts surge, but Iran looms

Housing starts increased 10.8 percent in March to an annual pace of 1.5 million homes, the highest since December 2024, according to figures released by the Census Bureau. New single-family home starts rose 9.7 percent from February to a 1.03 million annualized rate, while multifamily groundbreakings also advanced, with starts increasing across the country, the majority of them in the Northeast, Bloomberg reports.

The encouraging number, however, comes with a caveat that the New York Times addressed this week: The Iran war is doing real damage to the housing market’s trajectory. Building permits, a leading indicator of future construction, fell in March to an annualized pace of 1.37 million, the lowest level since August, with single-family permits falling 3.8 percent. Permits, more than starts, are a forward-looking signal, and the imbalance between strong starts and falling permits suggests that momentum is hindered by the Iran war.

State and city roundup: Legislatures, governors, and the grind

New Jersey. On Monday, Governor Mikie Sherrill signed Executive Order Number 17, directing state agencies to collaborate to make housing more available and affordable. The move will create a Housing Governing Council led by the state’s chief operating officer, with the Department of Community Affairs, the Housing and Mortgage Finance Agency, the Economic Development Authority, and N.J. Transit also at the table.

A new report by the National Low Income Housing Coalition found the state has only 34 affordable homes for every 100 extremely low-income renter households, and three-quarters of extremely low-income renters spend more than half their income on rent. More than a third of residents spend more than 30 percent of their income on housing. The median home price in New Jersey is over $500,000, while average rents top $3,000 in the state’s most expensive areas, according to the executive order.

Governor Sherrill’s order is still early-stage. She has tasked the newly created Housing Governing Council with inventorying vacant or underused state-owned properties where housing could be built, and has forbidden state agencies from selling state-owned property without her approval. The order does not itself change a zoning rule or fund units, as actual deliverables are expected to come in September in the form of a full housing plan. Critics on the right called the order “gobbledygook,” and some housing advocates and lawmakers have noted that New Jersey’s core problem is that supply is being constrained by local zoning, which the executive order doesn’t address. Still, Sherrill’s framing — “We can’t make New Jersey more affordable without making housing more affordable” — is a good place to start.

Pennsylvania. Two bipartisan housing-reform bills passed out of the Pennsylvania House’s housing committee in recent weeks. One would allow homeowners to build accessory dwelling units in residential areas; the other, called the “Golden Girls bill,” would limit municipal power to cap how many unrelated people can share a single home. The latter is particularly relevant in Philadelphia, where zoning currently bars more than three unrelated adults from living together.

Both bills still face a long path to becoming law.

Maine. A Maine Monitor report published last week examined where Maine stands four years after its legislature required municipalities to update zoning rules to motivate housing construction. Results are mixed. A new bill, LD 2173, recently signed into law by Governor Janet Mills, aims to resolve lingering disputes over land use restrictions. It is the second major adjustment lawmakers have made to the original reform.

As the Monitor puts it, “The four-year debate illustrated the power of Maine’s local control ethos and how it has shaped housing policy at the State House.”

Whether the reforms have actually produced housing is unresolved.

New York City. On April 24, N.Y.C. Council Speaker Julie Menin proposed construction code reforms that she said could create up to 35,000 new housing units on roughly 3,000 small, underutilized lots across the five boroughs without requiring any zoning changes. The proposal targets lots between 15 and 27 feet wide that current building codes effectively render undevelopable, in a move that would allow buildings up to eight stories to be constructed on these sites if the codes are updated.

While Menin’s approach streamlines growth in theory, many small-lot projects would likely still require zoning changes or special approvals and might not qualify for the simple as-of-right path she is describing.

Salt Lake City. A new poll commissioned by a housing advocacy group finds widespread concern among Salt Lake City voters over a lack of accessible housing options and firm support for ideas in a new proposal at City Hall to boost smaller-home construction across residential neighborhoods. The poll lands as the city council considers a significant zoning package that could expand where A.D.U.s, duplexes, triplexes, and fourplexes are permitted — changes that would affect more than 40,000 lots citywide, the Salt Lake Tribune reports.

Telluride. The Colorado Sun published a detailed report this month on what happens when a mountain town’s well-intentioned affordable housing policy creates its own crisis. After a winter with little snow and an early resort shutdown, Telluride’s affordable rental program, which ties rents to income, is seeing nearly 50 vacancies in its rent-restricted housing stock this month, as rising rents tied to household earnings have pushed longtime residents out of subsidized units.

Local advocates and officials say the system, designed to ensure fairness and compliance, may instead be destabilizing the workforce it aims to support, with growing concerns about displacement, long commutes, and the erosion of community fabric.

The Sun reports that San Miguel County’s 2025 housing needs assessment determined the region needed 1,114 new units by 2034. Councilmember Dan Enright told the Sun, “How can we hold that as true while simultaneously, for at least six months now, having nearly 50 vacancies in our rental housing program? Those two facts seem irreconcilable and, to me, that just screams ‘crisis.’”

The town is simultaneously moving forward on a new construction partnership with a private developer for 28 rental units, eight deed-restricted condos, and three free-market townhomes.

San Antonio. One of the fastest-growing cities in the country is one of its poorest major metros — a combination that captures the core tension of the Sun Belt. The New York Times reports that the city’s relatively low home prices have attracted newcomers, but decades of low wages, limited access to higher education, and barriers to homeownership have kept poverty in the metro high.

Roughly 17 percent of residents remain below the poverty line, and the local economy is still heavily tilted toward low-wage service work, the Times reports. The result is in-migration and rising demand without the income growth needed to convert affordability into wealth-building. At the same time, longstanding factors like redlining and underinvestment continue to shape who benefits from growth, underscoring that growth in the housing supply alone can’t close persistent inequality if it is not accompanied by parallel gains in wages and access to ownership.
