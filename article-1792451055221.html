Housing is on the ballot

Rates don’t look like they’re coming down any time soon. Can ballot measures across the country make housing more affordable for renters and homebuyers?

Halina Bennet
Oct 19
∙
Paid

Election Day is just over two weeks away, and voters across the country will have the chance to rewrite housing rules on minimum lot size, environmental review, and fees. Meanwhile, mortgage rates are at their highest in nearly three years, deepening the housing and affordability crises nationwide.

As of Thursday, Freddie Mac’s survey put the average 30-year fixed-rate mortgage at 7.4 percent. A year ago, rates averaged 6.27 percent — more than a full point lower. Fannie Mae responded to persistently high rates by cutting its 2027 mortgage volume forecast 18 percent, from $2.27 trillion to $1.86 trillion, and increasing its projected average rate on a 30-year fixed-rate mortgage for 2026 from 6.7 percent to 7.5 percent. The corporation expects mortgage interest rates to stay high next year, making it more difficult still for people to buy homes and reducing the number of homeowners who refinance. The New York Times reports that the market is chilling as unaffordability — and psychological barriers among people who don’t feel empowered to buy a home — grow.

The higher rates, of course, don’t affect everyone equally. Wall Street Journal reporting shows that America’s housing market now runs on luxury properties purchased at record prices with cash as the country’s richest get even richer and everybody else feels the squeeze. In ski country, the Steamboat Pilot reports that sales are slowing as rates rise, while the top of the market is doing better than ever. If you need a mortgage, forget about it. If you don’t, the world is your oyster.

Localities are experimenting with ways to ease housing pressures by unlocking supply that has previously been unavailable due to regulation. Some of these experiments have proven to be no match for market forces. In Portland, Ore., according to Oregon Public Broadcasting, a program that was proposed by Mayor Keith Wilson and Governor Tina Kotek and adopted by the city council last year to waive certain developer fees is not spurring new construction projects as hoped amid financial barriers driven by the market.

The waiver is “aimed in the right direction,” Eric Hagstette, a Portland real estate broker, told O.P.B. “But in a market like this you need the sum of all parts to come together. And that’s not happening.”

A developer who cannot borrow cheaply, like a driver stuck in traffic, is not very moved by a fee waiver, any more than the driver is moved by a cheaper toll. Discounts like those Portland is offering can’t move the needle if the developer can’t afford everything else.

Still, other states are looking at opportunities to make it easier for developers to build.

On the ballot

Voters in at least five states will answer housing questions directly as they consider the largest number of statewide housing ballot measures in a single year in two decades:

California has three propositions.

Proposition 1 would authorize $11.25 billion in general obligation bonds, allowing the state to borrow that money to fund affordable housing and address homelessness. Of that total, $7.2 billion would provide financing to developers for projects that reserve units for low-income residents. Another $2.35 billion would include $1.25 billion for veterans’ home loans and $1.1 billion to help low- and moderate-income households purchase homes. The final $1.7 billion would cover infrastructure for new developments, homes for farmworkers and university students, tribal construction and renovation projects, and experimental low-cost housing initiatives. Supporters cited by CalMatters say that the money would help house the state’s lowest-income residents and help thousands of homes get built, including projects stalled due to lack of funding, while critics argue that borrowing will cost taxpayers an estimated $500 to $600 million annually for about 25 years and could take funds away from other state priorities. Ultimately, Proposition 1 is a tradeoff between investing in affordable housing now and taking on long-term state debt.

Governor Gavin Newsom, builders, veterans’ groups, city and county associations, and the carpenters’ unions support the proposition, and the campaign supporting the measure has raised more than $12 million. Opposition to the measure comes in part from Republican State Senator Tony Strickland, who said that the veterans will only receive a fraction of the money and that California is borrowing against revenue that might not last.

Even some supporters share the latter concern. Vivian Wan, who runs the affordable housing provider Abode, told CalMatters that Proposition 1 could unlock four of her company’s stalled projects but that the state needs a permanent funding source to solve the crisis.

Proposition 45, also up to voters on November 3, tackles rules rather than funding. It would set deadlines for environmental review under the California Environmental Quality Act and for subsequent lawsuits, covering housing as well as water, energy, schools, and transportation. Agencies would get 365 business days to finish a full environmental impact report, and courts would have 270 days to resolve any challenges. Supporters cited by CalMatters, such as the state Republican Party and the chamber of commerce, argue the proposition will cut down on delays that often lead to higher costs for infrastructure projects. Opponents, including environmental groups and the state Democratic Party, say that the proposition strips away safeguards meant to protect communities, people, and the environment. Perhaps surprisingly, California YIMBY has not taken a side because it says the measure’s biggest effects fall on non-housing projects.

Both sides have raised tens of millions of dollars to support their positions.

Proposition 37 would authorize the California Housing Finance Agency to borrow up to $25 billion for revenue bonds to finance loans for prospective homeowners looking to purchase newly constructed houses and condos. Loans could provide up to 17 percent of the purchase price and would be available to anyone earning up to twice the area median income. Supporters say the assistance would allow prospective homeowners to purchase houses they otherwise couldn’t afford, without putting the burden on taxpayers, since private lenders are behind the loans. Opponents argue that this help on the demand side won’t solve the broader affordability problem, which requires supply-side action.

Elsewhere, Massachusetts offers Question 7, which would cap minimum lot sizes at 5,000 square feet for single-family homes on residential lots with at least 50 feet of frontage, public water, and sewer, excluding Boston. Many Massachusetts towns require at least an acre per lot, and some require two. Both Governor Maura Healey and her Republican challenger, Mike Minogue, support the measure. The state municipal association and Republican Party oppose it, though neither has created a campaign against it.

Hawaii’s Question 2 offers a new way to finance infrastructure for new housing by letting counties borrow money and repay that debt with the additional property-tax revenue generated by the developing area. If approved, counties and municipalities could issue growth bonds for specified public works and improvements like new sewer lines, water pipes, and drainage systems, taking some of the cost burden off of developers.

Rhode Island voters are weighing in on their own Question 2, which would authorize $120 million in bonds for affordable housing development. Funds could support new construction, infrastructure improvements, and the renovation and preservation of existing buildings, with at least $25 million dedicated to expanding opportunities for homeownership. These funds would add to a $120 million housing bond passed two years ago that was, at the time, the largest in state history, the Rhode Island Current reports. The debate over the measure centers on whether another major state investment would do enough to help address the housing shortage to justify the long-term debt.

Florida’s Amendment 3 proposes increasing property-tax exemptions for homeowners who live in their homes. The amendment would raise the homestead exemption for non-school property taxes to $150,000 in 2027 and $250,000 in 2028, with the amount indexed to inflation thereafter. Supporters say the amendment would help homeowners save money and make living in Florida more accessible, while opponents worry that reducing property tax revenue could make it harder for local governments to fund public services. A University of North Florida poll from July found support dropping from 61 percent to 45 percent once respondents were made aware of budget consequences: State economists project a nearly $12 billion annual loss for local governments.
