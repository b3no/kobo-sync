"Repressed Trauma" Is the New Horoscope

Did you think it was "normal" to sometimes have a stomach ache? No, you must have repressed traumatic memories.

On my first day of college, my psychology professor gave us all a written personality test. During our next class, he handed out our results and asked us, by show of hands, how many of us felt our results were accurate. Almost everyone in the class raised their hands, saying their results were some version of “eerily accurate.” Then, he revealed that the tests had actually been completely fake, and everyone had been given the same results. That’s right—everyone, from frat boys to sardonic hipsters to shy geeks—received the same personality results, and all of them felt these results were accurate.

How did all of these college kids fall for the same prank? Well, the personality test results were extremely vague. I don’t remember specifics, but I recall figuring that about 80% of it was true of me, enough to be “surprisingly good,” and then I disregarded the 20% that wasn’t quite right. But more importantly, the specific pieces that I do remember were all descriptions of victimhood—statements like “I do so much for other people and get less appreciation than I deserve” or “I don’t advocate for myself enough because I don’t want to disappoint people.” I realized that these narratives were things that most people believed about themselves, even though there’s no way the entire world is full of people-pleasers (If this were true, people would be, well, a bit more pleased.)

This is the same basic concept behind horoscopes—and by the way, I am not judging people who enjoy astrology for fun. But if you get your horoscope done, you will probably find that most of it seems basically accurate, regardless of what it says. Horoscopes or psychic predictions are rarely specific enough to be clearly false—and if you want to believe in them, you will. This is known as the Barnum effect, and it has kept many psychics gainfully employed.

I see the same sentiment repeated across social media and “TherapyTok,” attributing extremely common ailments—symptoms of “having a body and being alive”—to unrecognized trauma, or even traumatic events one doesn’t remember at all. (It’s giving Satanic Panic “recovered memories.”) This is not just about helping people with trauma process what happened to them and heal. It’s about convincing people that they experienced trauma even if they have no real reason to think they did, based on vague symptoms that could apply to nearly anyone.

Let me ask you: do you sometimes feel lightheaded when you get up too quickly? Do you occasionally have a stomachache? Do you sometimes have trouble sleeping? Does your neck sometimes hurt? Now, you might be saying, “Sure, but that doesn’t mean I had a traumatic childhood—I don’t have any memories of anything seriously bad happening!” But not too fast. Did you know that “not remembering much of your childhood” is, in fact, a symptom of repressed trauma too?

It’s no wonder that you can convince almost anyone they had a traumatic childhood, given that not remembering anything traumatic is now a “sign” that trauma happened. My psychology professor’s prank lives on, except now it’s on social media and people are taking it seriously.

Just to get this out of the way, there is a mind-body connection, and trauma (or other psychological issues) can manifest in physical ways. As someone with health OCD who has been repeatedly been accused of “faking it for attention,” I have experienced real physical symptoms and feelings that were most likely conjured up by mental illness, but still felt real in the moment. For example, there was a three-month phase where I experienced unsettling neurological symptoms, which were bad enough that on a few mornings, I felt like I was at risk of becoming bedbound (I wasn’t). I had dizzy spells, tingling, and fatigue. I considered POTS, inner ear dysfunction, and eventually landed on it being a brain tumor or MS. The more symptoms I read about, the more I developed. At one point, I read that people with brain tumors sometimes have visual hallucinations. Almost immediately afterward, I began to see flashes of blue light in one eye. Ultimately, the symptoms went away when I stopped Googling them for a week, and they haven’t returned in over a year. The fact that they were based on nothing doesn’t mean I wasn’t having them, or that I was making them up—just that they were psychosomatic, or began with something mild (I still suspect it began with an actual inner ear issue) but then continued as a psychosomatic illness. Luckily, I knew better than to look these symptoms up on TikTok, or the “illness” could have persisted long beyond a few months.

And who among us hasn’t felt like they were going to crap their pants upon hearing scary news? Who hasn’t been unable to sleep before a big event? Who hasn’t lost their appetite (or stress-ate) because of some emotional trigger? Of course our minds and bodies are connected!

All that being said, the tent for “physical symptoms which indicate amnesia of unspeakable trauma” has gotten inordinately capacious, and this is exactly why it’s so easy for snake oil salesmen on TikTok to shock people with their “eerily accurate” reads of their physical states. I saw a popular tweet last week that cited not remembering your childhood, feeling lightheaded when you stand up too quickly, stomachaches, and insomnia as signs that your brain is “concealing” a traumatic childhood. And of course, the entire comments section was, “Woaahh, how did you know? This algorithmic targeting is getting too good!” Ah, you’re right. Twitter’s algorithm is so good that it managed to find the only people in the world who occasionally get stomachaches.

First, let’s focus on the childhood amnesia bit, because this seems especially sticky given that almost nobody can remember their childhood. A psychologist on TikTok who goes by the name Dr. Inna, debunked the claim that you are less likely to remember your childhood if you have childhood trauma, and instead asserted that being able to remember your childhood clearly is more unusual than experiencing childhood amnesia.

This is anecdotal, of course, but I have almost perfect memory of my childhood going back to age three, while my husband remembers almost none of his. Just going by the facts, I experienced more events before age eighteen that one might bring up in therapy than he did. And yet, I can remember exactly what my puppet looked like in a second-grade puppet show, all the lyrics from a cassette tape of The Simpsons songs that I received when I was ten, and that I owned this castle toy in 1994 (which I haven’t even seen in over thirty years—my memory was jogged when I randomly stumbled upon it on eBay). Meanwhile, my husband can’t remember any of his birthdays, even ones that occurred in high school!

My husband is more typical in this way, and I am more unusual. This has nothing to do with my husband experiencing trauma or feeling “unsafe” as a child. His childhood was idyllic, if a little hands-off and Gen X-coded. There is a reason I’ve made a living writing in the memoir format—I genuinely just have an insanely good memory. This isn’t always good. There are plenty of things I’d like to forget, and can’t, such as the time I entered a charity fashion show in college and told to dress “fierce,” showed up in a Bebe bandage dress with six-inch heels, only to discover I was A.) the only white participant and B.) everyone else was wearing goofy Sesame Street costumes and breakdancing and then they voted for the winner via “applause” and nobody clapped for me, and when it was my turn for judgment the host said, “Anyone? Anyone?”

Okay, maybe I do have unreleased trauma, but at the very least, you can’t accuse me of forgetting it!

Of course, if you have memories of growing up in an abusive home, even if those memories are hazy, that’s one thing—but if your only evidence is that you didn’t remember anything, that isn’t really evidence at all. People who have experienced severe physical abuse generally don’t suddenly recall it thirty years later after watching TikTok. Most people have very spotty memory of their childhoods because that’s just normal. The most common outcome of any childhood is “very blurry memories,” so people who experienced traumatic childhoods will have blurry memories…because almost everyone does. Such a “symptom” should not be used to convince people with normal, if imperfect childhoods that they are repressing traumatic memories.

Just an example of the Barnum Effect at work: I was on YouTube looking for Bulwark podcast episodes (specifically the ones with Will Sommer) and I was served this ad for a “trauma test,” presenting the viewer as an attractive woman with “Was I abused?” written across her svelte back. Surely, anyone who takes this test is taking it hoping that “yes, you were abused,” was the answer! Why would anyone else take it? Just to double check and make sure they weren’t abused?

Moreover, all of the questions were perfectly engineered to get the “you were abused” result, such as this one, which asked me if I’ve ever struggled with a fear of rejection. Show me one person who has never struggled with this!

It also includes a few pseudo-Rorschach tests where there are clearly two possibilities: a scary one and a non-scary one. In each instance, it’s clear that 99% of people would see the scary thing first.

The test also includes unsettling audio of a child screaming “Stop!!” which then prompts the user to identify what was being said—“stop” or something less upsetting. (People suffering from CPTSD have complained about the triggering nature of this question.) And of course, even for people this app “intends” to help—those with childhood PTSD—it’s a total scam. CPTSD is real and serious, and apps like these don’t do much for it.

Breeze is shockingly light on the “physical ailments” questions, but don’t worry—other scammers have them covered. What percentage of people in America do you think occasionally struggle with insomnia? About 30% (including my husband, by the way, but I maintain it’s irrelevant to whatever demonic preschool rituals he may or may not have forgotten in 1989.) Meanwhile, 80% of the population will at some point struggle with back pain. While 10-15% of the population has IBS, it’s estimated that 75% of IBS cases go undiagnosed. Not to mention all the various subclinical versions of these conditions, which never make it into these studies (I had various digestive issues throughout my twenties—in hindsight, this was from living off a diet of beer and sour candy—but I never saw a doctor for them.) You would be hard-pressed to find any person alive who remembers their entire childhood, never struggles to stay asleep, and has never suffered from any vague physical ailment. That’s why it’s so easy for unlicensed “therapists” and other social media healers to convince people to buy whatever they’re selling by using this horoscope-adjacent method of diagnosed hidden trauma to “heal their inner child.”

There is also the fact that believing you have a particular illness, or experiencing suggestions that you have an illness via social media, can make that illness feel more real, and this effect seems especially strong for teenage girls and young women, known for spending lots of time on TikTok. Need I remind you of the droves of teenage girls who genuinely developed verbal tics similar to Tourette’s, after being exposed to content by an influencer with Tourette’s? (Their tic was identical to the influencer’s—the word “beans.”) Throughout Europe in the Middle Ages, people (especially teenage girls) became afflicted with a mysterious and often fatal “dancing illness,” which is now believed to be a “mass psychogenic illness” with no physiological culprit.

There is real evidence that being exposed to information about a disease or symptom increases the likelihood that someone will experience it. In a 2007 study, a sample of men were placed on a low dose of the hair loss drug Finasteride, but only some randoly-selected men were informed of sexual side effects. The men who were told about erectile dysfunction were significantly more likely to experience it than the men who did not know it was a potential side effect (46% vs. 15%.) This is called the “nocebo effect” and it has been replicated many times. Hearing information about a particular symptom can be a trigger for that symptom to appear. (Reason number 4839248 for people with health OCD to stay off medical websites.)

If people believe that the cause of all their ailments (which may or may not be psychosomatic) is amnesia of childhood trauma, a lot of bad things can happen. First of all, per the example of my own psychosomatic illness, if the symptoms are psychosomatic, assigning significant emotional value to them will probably make them worse. Second, the person might be tempted to blame their families for trauma that never happened, especially if someone who seems therapist-adjacent is telling them that these people are the true culprits, and not remembering anything they did wrong is evidence that they did something wrong. Third, sometimes people have physical illnesses for reasons that are actually completely physical, if hard to fully identify, and if they assume their stomach problems are “the body keeping the score” they won’t resolve the real cause. Given how easy is it for people (even people who are not severely mentally ill) to be convinced they have illnesses they don’t have, or even to recover memories that don’t exist, this is a real problem.

Horoscopes can be fun, but they’re mostly fun if you don’t take them too seriously. And while trauma is certainly realer than your horoscope, you should be careful not to listen to a TikTok healer about it.
