Not to just make this Matt’s Grievance Corner, but recently Natalie Winters published a Twitter thread starting with the line “REVEALED: Here Are the Mainstream Journalists on China’s Payroll” that goes on to name me as one of the journalists in question.

The actual allegation here turns out to be that I took a trip to China in 2009 that was paid for by an entity with links to the Chinese government. This is not what being “on China’s payroll” means. It’s also not a new revelation — the exact same documents were written up five years ago. But also I wrote about this trip at the time! It was not a secret and has never been a secret and has never involved any kind of ongoing relationship. More to the point, the reason you can know this trip is not the secret corrupt reason for my pro-P.R.C. views is that I don’t have pro-P.R.C. views. Winters’s thread includes examples of the allegedly soft on China things that people who went on these tours said, and her one for me is that I made fun of how clumsy the propaganda we were subjected to was.

I wrote a whole book about my dream of re-aligning American politics from top to bottom around geopolitical competition with China. I’ve complained that despite his rhetoric, Trump has failed to engage seriously in competition and instead has gone soft on export controls. My opinions about this are not some big secret.

Esang Wu: Your comment about Tisch and Mamdani’s good working relationship made me curious how long do you think it lasts and why do you think it has sustained so far? I have my own guess having listened to Rahm Emanuel describe having breakfast with Mamdani after the primary. Apparently Rahm tried confronting him at the end of the meeting over not accepting Israel as a Jewish state and all but he said Mamdani was receptive, doesn’t really argue like that or didn’t want to argue about it and made him feel heard even though it was clear he hadn’t changed his mind. My takeaway seems to be that Mamdani is very disarming interpersonally, avoids bringing up conflict topics, isn’t argumentative and is looking to find common ground. But you said somewhere you went to school with Tisch, so what’s your read of their dynamic?

Jessica Tisch and I were in the same class in high school and then went to college together, so while I can’t claim we were close friends or anything, I did know her because we were in the same advanced-track science classes and such. But that was a long time ago. And I have to say that at the time nobody was writing her in as “Most Likely to Become N.Y.P.D. Commissioner.”

Her life and career ended up on an unexpected trajectory that I actually think is notable and praiseworthy. She’s from a very rich family and while there are a lot of kids who grow up in those circumstances and want to commit themselves to something high-minded, very few of them express that by rolling up their sleeves to actually do public sector work, as Tisch has.

That’s just all to say that it was a long time ago and I can’t claim any special insights into her current thinking. But I do think there are a few things in their working relationship worth noting.

Zohran Mamdani has clearly decided that a strong record on crime is key to his larger project and that he more or less trusts her to deliver that. And she is happy to stay in her lane as long as she can do her job in a way that she thinks is appropriate. I very strongly doubt that they see eye to eye on Israel, but that’s not the police commissioner’s job and in fact it’s not the mayor’s job either and there’s something eccentric about how central it is to Mamdani’s personal politics. From the standpoint of the city, though, it’s much better for the mayor to have an extreme view on Israel than an extreme view on crime and policing. I think it is broadly distressing to Jewish Americans, including and in some ways especially including Jewish Americans who (like Rahm Emanuel) enthusiastically sided with Obama over Netanyahu in their clashes about settlements and Iran, that the new vibe in progressive politics is that to be a member of the team in good standing you need to swallow Palestinian nationalist narratives about the conflict whole hog. But this is not actually relevant to the governance of the city, and the latest big announcement Mamdani made about this was in fact him acknowledging that reality.

And that’s the foundation of a good working relationship. Trying to have the N.Y.P.D. enforce International Criminal Court warrants when there is no basis in American or New York law for doing so would be absurd and unprofessional and he’s not doing it — instead, the cops are doing gun seizures like they’re supposed to.

Mar02e: In the comments section of your article about the Odyssey, someone mentioned that they find it interesting that this big cultural moment is happening one week after your article about how we miss big cultural moments.

It got me to thinking -—are we experiencing a vibe shift back toward big cultural moments? I feel like we kind of are, at least compared to pre-COVID. I’d say Barbenheimer and the Eras Tour kicked it off, and then we’re also seeing pretty much all major sports events have record-breaking audiences - the NFL stopped its brief decline/flattening and just got even bigger; the World Cup was huge; the finale of the World Series and NBA finals got massive ratings. Caitlin Clark’s college finale was even a bit of a ‘cultural event.’ And then there’s been a number of blockbuster films outside of the MCU, certainly moreso than we’d had in a while.

Do you think this is a real trend? If so, what might be driving it?

I think the trend you are pointing to is both real and welcome, but it’s not really a reversal of the decline of monoculture so much as it is the residue of what’s left behind as the monoculture dissolves.

The reason that in my piece I dwelled so much on stuff like “The Single Guy” and “Suddenly Susan” is that the connective tissue of mass culture was things that were banal rather than extraordinary. When I wrote the piece, I looked up some clips of “The Single Guy” on YouTube, which got me algorithmically served some additional YouTube content focused on 1990s sitcoms. That reminded me of shows like “Wings” and “Mad About You” that were more successful and better-regarded at the time than the stuff I mentioned but that also ended up not retaining the kind of staying power of “Friends” or “Seinfeld.”

A lot of movies were big widely seen hits without being “Odyssey”-scale events. That’s because back then a lot of people were in the habit of going to the movies on any given weekend. There were, of course, lots of small little-seen independent films. And there were box office bombs. But a famous movie star could “open” a movie — i.e., get a bunch of people to go see it on opening weekend — and if the reviews and word of mouth were okay, it would end up being widely seen.

Similarly, without obtaining anything remotely resembling Taylor Swift’s level of fame and success you could have a song that was a big hit on MTV and the radio and everyone would know it. It’s not that the “one-hit wonder” as a cultural phenomenon was so amazing but the fact that it could even happen was indicative of a time when routine pop culture was just much broader in its reach. If you’re the right age, you know the Marcy Playground song “Sex and Candy” even if you (like me) have never heard another Marcy Playground song. I don’t think mass culture in that sense will come back or even could come back, because the technological substrate just doesn’t exist anymore.

What does seem to be true is that in the wake of total fragmentation, this kind of mega-event is popping up. It’s fun to participate in things with other people, so once an event starts to look like a rare shared cultural moment, there is almost a frenzy around it. I welcome that as an alternative to total atomization. But it’s not the same thing as the old world where you could take for granted that the majority of the content you consumed was also being seen or heard by a large minority of the population.

A New Jerusalem: You’ve previously written about how the left escaped the “velvet pen” (I think that was your term I can’t remember though) during Obama’s second term, with all sorts of implications for the next 3 terms. Do you think a similar thing could happen in the back half of Trump’s second administration? Will anti-abortion advocates, for example, begin defying him and demand much clearer litmus tests for republicans in the 2028 primary?

It was called the “veal pen,” and I think this is the biggest known unknown about the Republican Party and 2028. There’s a ton of furious speculation right now about JD Vance or Marco Rubio, but I think the personalities are much less significant than this dynamic.

Does someone — maybe someone way more obscure than Vance or Rubio — start getting attention on social media by calling for Republicans to actually take action to prevent the legalized killing of innocent unborn children? And if that person does start gaining traction, does that put pressure on whoever Trump endorses as his heir to start making promises in this regard? Immigration is another possible angle. Trump has obviously been taking some pretty dramatic measures on immigration. But he’s also made it cheaper for farmers to hire workers through agricultural guest-worker visas, and some Republicans in Congress are acting at the behest of the farm lobby to push for further expansion of the program. I wouldn’t expect to see anyone explicitly criticize Trump from the right on immigration, but it’s easy to imagine an even more hardcore restrictionist campaign tapping into hostility to guest workers of both the agricultural and H-1B varieties.

Trump has been a very imperfect political leader and he privately loses fights with Congress all the time. But because he so thoroughly dominates Republican politics in public, no Republican politician feels free to campaign against him or to distinguish themselves by criticizing his policies. That particular dominance presumably won’t last, but it’s not obvious what will follow it. You certainly could get something that resembles the 2016/2020 leftward rushes that happened in Democratic primaries — and the 2016 precedent is a reminder that this can happen even if a single frontrunner dominates the whole time.

andrew schein: Jaylen Brown. What’s the deal with his poor advanced stats? Is it a sign he’s overrated, or that the advanced stats have problems, or a bit of both?

I think that people think I’m the kind of basketball fan who’d be super into advanced stats, but I’m actually not, or at least historically have not been. The thing that actually did get me interested in this was the sheer volume of incomprehension about the trade that came from Bill Simmons and other people in the N.B.A. media. They all said they were aware that advanced stats say Brown was nowhere near the kind of player who deserved to be sixth in M.V.P. voting but then didn’t engage at all with this question. I’m not saying people should necessarily agree with the advanced stats take, but if you’re going to spend weeks talking about how mad you are at the trade and going “What was the front office thinking?” it seems actually worth taking a moment to sit with the fact that clearly part of what the front office was thinking was that the advanced stats were right that Brown to Paul George is not that big of a downgrade.

So what do these stats mean?

I’m a Knicks fan. I fire up DARKO, and I see the take that Jalen Brunson is only the third-best player on the team.

That just does violence not only to the “eyeball test” of player quality but really also to the whole way people use language when describing N.B.A. teams. Brunson is just clearly the signature star of the team, the hero of the city, the guy who has the ball in his hands in the most critical moments. You can’t even dispute those facts, so what is the math even saying?

Here’s what I talked myself into. Suppose you’re Mike Brown and you’re told that your eight-man rotation is going to be Jalen Brunson, Karl-Anthony Towns, O.G. Anunoby, and then clones of those guys. What clones do you order up? Well it’d probably be just one Brunson clone, and Brunson2’s job would be to back up Brunson when Brunson needs rest or is in foul trouble. Without appealing to any advanced stats concepts, it would just not be very promising to play a Brunson-Brunson backcourt. It would be defensively weak and the offensive contributions would be redundant. How about Towns? You’d definitely want one Towns clone so that Towns2 could back up Towns. Would you consider playing a Towns-Towns twin-towers lineup? That’s intriguing. I would definitely want one Towns clone and would consider ordering two.

With Anunoby, though, I would want a bunch. I’m very comfortable with the idea of Towns-Anunoby-Anunoby2-Anunoby3-Brunson as a five-man lineup. And then Anunoby4 coming off the bench to back those guys up sounds great. So most likely my ideal eight-man rotation is two Brunsons, two Townses, and four Anunobys. If I got a ninth guy, it would be another Towns. That aligns with the DARKO take that Anunoby > Towns > Brunson, but I think it also just aligns with an intuitive “guy who watches the games” take on how the team works. Anunoby is not the star of the team. He’s not Captain Clutch. But you can almost never have enough guys who can defend multiple positions effectively while scoring efficiently, whereas a team wants at most one undersized guard who’s a subpar defender but scores at high volume.

Max Haas: In the 2016 democratic primary, I remember a debate moment about climate change where Hillary suggested progressive climate regulations, Bernie talked about how corporations were hurting the environment, and then Martin O’Malley gave an answer where he rejected that sort of framing and then spoke about how green energy is an entrepreneurial opportunity for America. Do we need to give O’Malley way more flowers?

You guys know that O’Malley Would’ve Won is one of my core beliefs. But my recollection of the campaign is that there was some tension between the way O’Malley wanted to talk about the issue (which was generally solid) and his specific policy commitments, which largely leaned toward trying to outflank everyone as the maximal anti-pipeline candidate.

Earlier in his career, though, O’Malley was on the correct side of an interesting energy policy dispute when he tried to get a third reactor built at the Calvert Cliffs nuclear plant.

Calvert Cliffs is a big facility that generates 40 percent of Maryland’s power and is far and away the state’s largest source of clean energy. The site is large enough to accommodate an additional reactor, which would significantly increase output and have all the associated benefits. And the French utility company Électricité de France was ready to go on the project when the Obama-era Nuclear Regulatory Commission stepped in to deny them permission on the grounds that it would be a national security risk to have a French company involved in the American nuclear industry.

This seems like an absurd viewpoint. France is a NATO treaty ally and a nuclear-weapon state recognized under the Non-Proliferation Treaty. What is the risk here? France is a country that knows how to build and operate a large nuclear fleet and the United States should welcome efforts to partner with it.

I am always eager to defend Obama and his administration from various progressive slanders, but his stewardship of the Nuclear Regulatory Commission was genuinely bad. The first chair he appointed came out of Harry Reid’s operation and was basically put forward just because Reid wanted someone who would stop Yucca Mountain by any means necessary. In practice, that meant picking someone who was broadly anti-nuclear and was happy to throw whatever wrenches into the works he could find.

Eric H: Do you see much / any live theater? And, do you have a take on proshots (professionally-produced filmed versions of shows)?

I live right by the Studio Theatre in D.C. and it’s been almost 20 years since I attended, so basically, no, I am not a live theater fan. I did listen to a lot of Broadway musicals on tape while driving in the car when I was a kid and have affection for those — I even dragged Kate to see a revival of the “Pajama Game” in the West End when we were in London years ago.

As someone who loves movies but finds live theater a little weird, I find the proshots concept sort of doubly weird — make a real movie! Steven Spielberg’s remake of “West Side Story” is incredibly underrated in my view and shows what can happen when you marry a stage musical that people love to real cinematic craft. I would love to see a film adaptation of “Hamilton,” for example. But people just filming a play? I dunno.

Brendan: I wondered what you thought of this take from David Frum regarding the recent supreme court decision striking down limits on election spending by political parties.

Do you feel any optimism that this will bring balance to the forces pressuring Democratic candidates?

Frum is arguing that America made a mistake by financially disempowering formal party organizations. I used to be very enamored of this take, which you can find in a 2015 book on campaign finance by Ray La Raja and Brian Schaffner, but I’m now pretty sure that it’s wrong.

Their thesis was that if money flowed through institutional political parties, sober-minded professionals who are motivated to win would control the levers of power. La Raja and Schaffner believed that disintermediating the parties had allowed expressive donors to wield undue political influence, leading to extremism and polarization.

I think there are two problems with this.

One problem, as my friends Sam Rosenfeld and Daniel Schlozman argue in their newer book, “The Hollow Parties,” is that institutional political parties lack not just money but, more importantly, social, cultural, and political legitimacy. Haley Stevens’s problem in Michigan is not that she lacks access to money. It’s that a large share of Democrats — and seemingly a majority of media influencers — sincerely believe that Bernie Sanders and Elizabeth Warren have a better track record of political judgement than Chuck Schumer and Gretchen Whitmer. I think both Schumer and Whitmer have shown real flaws in their track records but that this is still an insane assessment.

The second problem, which in my opinion is more damaging for the thesis, is that patronage machine politics doesn’t exist anymore. Many good things happened for the people of Louisiana as a result of John Bel Edwards’s two terms as governor, but it’s not like the leaders of the Louisiana Democratic Party reaped some kind of financial windfall that other state party leaders were jealous of. These are not “bosses” with a concrete stake in winning. They’re the same expressive amateurs who exist in every other part of the political system. When Charlie Baker was governor of Massachusetts, the state party institutions ended up getting taken over by MAGA-aligned true believers who didn’t like him. So I think the idea that empowering formal party organizations will empower winning-oriented pragmatists is empirically wrong.

I’m not sure exactly what the solution is. But I do think part of the answer needs to be some self-reflection on Schumer’s part. On the one hand, I believe that much of the criticism he has taken has been unfair. And I further believe that his most vociferous critics have ideas that are on the whole worse than his. That said, a party leader needs to be able to lead effectively. Throughout the 2021–22 legislative session, Schumer seemed unable or unwilling to impose priorities on his caucus. In the 2026 election cycle, it’s not just that he can’t impose his preferred candidates on key races (no leader has ever been that strong). It’s that having his support seems to be more hindrance than help. He has a large bloc of his caucus actively campaigning against his preferred candidate in Michigan and nobody flying in from out of state to help her out. This doesn’t really strike me as a tenable situation. Politics isn’t always about leaders getting what they deserve; it’s about putting the right people in the right place at the right time, and he doesn’t seem to me to be that guy.

Niketas: What are your latest thoughts on the proposed Paramount-Warner Bros merger? Bad because Ellisons/Bari Weiss/MAGA? Good for Star Trek? Bad because CBS and CNN would be merged? Good because Paramount is otherwise in trouble and a bit crap/Warner also in trouble (similar to a Sprint-TMobile argument)? Thanks!

I’ve got three basic takes on this right now. (1) CNN falling into the hands of a regime-aligned billionaire is definitely bad for America. (2) I am skeptical that there is a bona fide competition problem on the film and television production side of this deal.

And (3) there’s a reasonable argument that Paramount taking over is better for the movie business — and for cinema as an art form — than Netflix taking over would have been. Whatever else you may say about him, David Ellison seems like a guy who really wants to run a movie studio, whereas Netflix has a pretty mixed relationship to the whole concept of movies.
