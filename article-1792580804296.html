Americans need to get out of the house

I’m way more online than I should be, and you probably are too.

Matthew Yglesias
Oct 21
∙

Let me tell you a little about my weekend.

For various reasons, we didn’t have our normal editorial meeting at work last week, so I spent a fair amount of time on Saturday logged on to X, scrolling around seeking inspiration for stories. Dominating my feed were images of the riots in France and, even more so, annoying takes on the riots in France.

I wasn’t even so much seeing people tweeting dumb takes about the riots as seeing sensible people executing good dunks on dumb takes about the riots. But I still had to see both the underlying dumb takes and also the under-underlying riot images. There was also plenty of discourse (some of which I ended up contributing to) in the broad space of “people who agree that Abdul El-Sayed winning is preferable to Mike Rogers winning but are fighting about El-Sayed.”

The France stuff I kind of find entrancing in a doomscrolling kind of way, but El-Sayed discourse is real catnip for me in terms of getting into it with other people. In both cases, though, X engineers are evidently pretty smart and good at their jobs and were successfully executing on their mission of keeping me engaged.

Did it make me feel happy and satisfied with my life? No. Did it help me execute on my actual goal in logging on to X? Also no. But it’s not their job to make my life better. Their job is to keep me scrolling — and mission accomplished.

It’s good to do things

Some other things that happened over the weekend:

I met up with two friends to see the new “Street Fighter” movie. It was incredibly dumb, but I have to admit that it contained individual scenes — from video game nostalgia to hearing White Zombie’s “More Human Than Human” for the first time in years — that brought my lizard brain joy.

It was also good to see my friends, albeit briefly. I like those guys! Later I drove to a middle-school debate tournament where I was supposed to pick up my kid and one of his friends. It turned out that it was running late, so I went in to sit with my son and watch the handing out of awards. In the auditorium, I ran into a writer whose book I like but who I’d only met once before, and we chatted briefly. She introduced me to another dad who I’ve seen around the neighborhood but never met. I said “hi” to some kids who I know slightly, and one of them said something funny. Then I drove up to my kid’s friend’s house to drop him off while he and my son bickered in the back seat like an old married couple. Driving back to our house, my son and I listened to Stan Van Gundy on “The Zach Lowe Show,” and they were saying Boston is going to be good this year, so my son and I talked about how The Ringer spent the whole summer melting down about the Jaylen Brown trade but is now coming around to our view that the Celtics are in good shape for next season and the trade set them up with better flexibility for the future.

Soon after we got home, two adult friends of ours stopped by. They had plans, but one of them has two kids, and we’d agreed with them that the kids could hang out at our house while the adults went out. Our friends’ adult plans turned out to be a bust, so they came home early. All of us adults wound up hanging out while two of the kids played Minecraft and the third read a book. Eventually everyone went home, probably slightly late relative to everyone’s statutory bedtimes. But a good time was had by all.

What really struck me about all of this was that none of it was particularly perfect, or even planned.

The movie was terrible. I was in the auditorium because the debate was off schedule. The drive home from the other kid’s house ended up taking a weirdly long time due to unexpected traffic. The kids coming over was us doing a favor, not a fun plan we deliberately devised. Our friends’ plans ended up being bad. But in the end it was all for the best!

The limits of revealed preferences

Unlike X, of which I am an unhealthily obsessive user, I almost never log on to Instagram. But I did on Saturday after our friends left because someone sent me a link to a post.

Since I rarely use Instagram, its algorithm is terribly tuned and has no idea what to show me. That meant I got an Instagram reel of a young female influencer, Ryan Weitz, talking about why “dating in D.C. is so bad.”

Some of what she says seems normal, like she’s frequently traveling for work on the weekend, which makes it hard to meet people. She also feels that the guys she does meet are often immature. But then she says that while she tells herself she’s going to go out on the weekends when she is in town, more often than not she ends up staying in because she realizes “Oh, I want to like chill and rest.”

I did not find this content particularly engaging in a classic social-media sense — I wasn’t inspired to comment or keep scrolling — but it did give me what I’d been seeking (and failing to find) on X: something to write about!

Weitz is illustrating the difference between stated and revealed preferences but also, I think, the limits of revealed preferences as a framework. Weitz, like most people, says she would like to be in a relationship (stated preference), but she also says that in practice she values her downtime on weekends and dates are often annoying (revealed preferences). While I know men who enjoyed the process of dating — the thrill of the chase or what have you — that was definitely not me and, to the best of my recollection, it wasn’t most people I know. Talking to strangers and having them mostly reject you is not my idea of a fun time. I’d much rather watch dumb movies with my friends or sit home alone trading bon mots on Twitter.

But this is one of those things where you have to engage in a modicum of long-term thinking. I don’t enjoy flossing, but I do it anyway. I am overdue for a tooth cleaning at the dentist, and I believe that statistically speaking I have reached colonoscopy age, and now that I find myself typing this sentence I’m going to go do the grownup thing and book those appointments. A happy life is not an unbroken sequence of joyous occasions. Sometimes, to get ahead, you need to put eyedroppers full of heavy cream in a senior editor’s coffee.

And I think a growing problem in our society is people leaning too hard into the revealed preference for frictionless existence and plenty of alone time.

Not much is going on

A great Data for Progress survey shows how Americans’ social lives have changed since the mid-1990s, and the big shift is toward doing less of almost everything.

I mentioned my disquiet over the rise of remote work online the other day and heard a lot from remote workers who love it about the obvious advantages in terms of reduced commute time and greater flexibility about where to live. It definitely sounds great. And I heard from one friend who lives in the suburbs with three school-aged kids and a wife with a high-powered career of her own, and skipping the commute really does seem great for them.

But clearly, people on average are not having more kids. And they’re also not taking advantage of their increased freedom and flexibility to spend more time entertaining or visiting with friends. And they’re not doing less home-based entertaining because they’re out at restaurants and bars more or joining clubs. There’s been a modest rise in the frequency with which people go see live music or attend exercise classes. But these are still uncommon pursuits compared to the frequency with which people used to go to club meetings.

The big thing is that people are online more and they play more video games.

That’s where, in the aggregate, the extra time has gone. Instead of dealing with the hassles of interacting with flawed humans in the real world, people are spending more time at home alone using the internet. And they do not seem to be better off for it.

A struggling nation

A recent report from Gallup and The Rippel Foundation on whether people are “thriving” or “struggling” in America revealed a well-being ratio that has dipped slightly below the levels associated with the peak of the Great Recession.

I recognize that the energy price spike associated with Trump’s war in Iran is bad, but it’s hard to square this survey with the objective economic data. Both diesel and gasoline are cheaper in real terms today than they were in 2008, for example, and the unemployment rate is strikingly lower.

The well-being decline is also notably stratified by age. Back in 2008, when I was young, people my age mostly said we were “thriving.” Now that we’re middle-aged, we’re doing worse than we used to be. And the new crop of young people that’s come up behind us is doing much worse. The thing preventing total collapse is that senior citizens are doing better.

Some of this is economic in nature. The housing market is rigged against young people, and government benefits for the retired have gotten more generous. But young people were definitely struggling economically in 2010. We were just also young and having fun.

As Halina wrote the other day about “Girls,” you can have a lot of fun as a broke 20-something hanging out with your friends. Going online mostly makes people miserable. You find out that France is on fire and all your political enemies are evil and everyone else is secretly richer and better-looking than you’ll ever be. Every interaction is a minefield of potential problems. And that’s the business model! If you feel inspired to go do something, that’s bad for engagement.

This is bad for everyone.

Derek Thompson has been pushing the difference between “loneliness” (a subjective desire to spend more time with others) and “aloneness” (just being alone because that’s what you want). But I think this is kind of like how a lot of us, if left to our own inclinations, are prone to overeating and not exercising enough. There’s a revealed preference to be fat and lazy, and it doesn’t make us happy or align with what we really want out of life.

I wonder if there isn’t something deep in the evolved history of our brains that just assumes we’ll never have enough personal space or alone time, and we just don’t know how to deal with the possibility of endless streaming video and food delivery.

I just know that for me, getting thrown chaotically into the happenstance of real life tends to generate happy accidents, while the deliberate pursuit of algorithmic pleasures tends — at best — to leave me staying up an hour later than I intended to and feeling a little worse for the wear the next day.
