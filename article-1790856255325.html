How To Find and Maximize Your “Type”

My guide to typemaxxing for both men and women.

A week ago, I wrote about the importance of “typemaxxing,” or optimizing to look your hottest for your particular type (and while the image above is of women, the advice was gender-neutral). Obviously, not everyone cares about looking attractive, but this advice was aimed at people who want to look attractive, but are, like almost everyone, not naturally born into the Ideal Hotness mold. Example: even if a poll of 5,000 men indicates that a blonde cheerleader with an exaggerated hourglass figure is the ideal, attempting to look that way if you are a lanky and angular redhead will probably be less advantageous than playing to your strengths as a lanky redhead, and appealing to men who like lanky redheads.

Obviously, this does not include objective and workable flaws. If you have bad skin, are in terrible shape, or don’t brush your teeth, that is not a “type.” But even at our best, most of us just don’t look like any kind of on-average ideal of beauty. That’s fine. My argument is that we work with the things we can’t control (or the things that would be onerous, costly, painful and perhaps dangerous to attempt to control).

Naturally, many of you wanted to know what typemaxxing would look like if you weren’t sure of your type. In fact, knowing your type seems to be the hardest part of the whole thing. So here’s my guide for maximizing your type, starting with the first step: identifying your type.

One part of typemaxxing is accepting the fact that your type might not be the modal ideal. Some types are more universally loved than others. But that’s okay, because your goal is to attract one person (presumably) or to feel good about yourself, not to become the World’s Hottest Person.

A follower of mine, Marianne, graciously submitted their own before/after of finding her type. She mentioned being born tall with naturally broad shoulders and doing everything she could to look more dainty. As she put it, “Being very tall as a woman, particularly when you don't have a supermodel build and face, is a pretty big dating hurdle. And when I was aiming for a normie body type (as thin as I could be in my frame with my natural tendency to love food), I rarely met guys who thought my height was a bonus, it was more like a handicap they were kindly willing to overlook.”

But eventually, she leaned into what made her unique. She started lifting weights but kept the feminine clothing that fit her personality (I will get into this in a moment, but fitting your own personality is very important even if the attire feels “discordant” with your appearance.) Marianne started working out, embraced her ability to gain muscle, and while her overall dating app matches went down, the men who were into her were really into her. Happy ending: she met her husband when this was her main profile picture:

She put it better than I ever could: “I've always like a fairly retro feminine aesthetic, and I leaned in even harder on that, and basically ended up as the very niche dream girl for men who love girls who wear polka dot dresses and red lipstick but look like they could win an arm wrestling competition.”

Now, most people will not lean into an alternative style, and most people do not have strikingly unique features, such as being a naturally tall and muscular woman. In fact, I’d say the most maddening thing about typemaxxing is how hard it is to really know your type at all if nothing about you is terribly remarkable or unusual. Many people will point to the Kibbe system for women, which is illustrated as follows:

Confused? Yeah, me too.

The Kibbe system works really well for people who neatly fit into these categories—for example, a voluptuous, soft, short woman or a broad-shouldered, angular tall woman. But most of us do not! I have attempted in vain to decipher my Kibbe type and have gotten nowhere. I asked ChatGPT, who determined I was a soft natural. I told my friend, herself a soft natural, who reacted in shock and said, “No way, soft naturals don’t have defined waists.” I returned to Kibbe. I asked Claude. I discovered I was a soft gamine, but was then directed to “dramatic” despite being antithetical to soft gamine. Then someone told me that gamines are short (often under 5’5”) and while I’m 5’5”, I have the vibe of a much taller person (from photos alone, most people think I am 5’9” or so, but as you can see, this is what I look like next to actual tall women:)

TLDR: screw Kibbe and his bullshit, I learned nothing.

As I said, not everyone’s body follows the same pattern throughout, which makes Kibbe typing hard. I have a very narrow upper body, sloping narrow shoulders that cannot hold the strap of a purse, and a long narrow neck. But my lower body is entirely different. I have short legs, genetically shapeless wide ankles that I purposefully hide, and when I gain weight, it almost all goes to my thighs, hips and butt. To be clear, I’m not complaining! I really like my body! But this is not clearly Kibbe-classifiable. Am I delicate or angular? Yin or yang? Feminine or sporty? Ingenue or womanly? It all depends! Even the way people neg my body on Twitter seems inconsistent, with nary a pattern to be found.

There is also the factor of my face, which I won’t post so you’ll just have to trust me. Here are some celebrities I’ve been told I resemble (obviously, they are all much prettier than I am, but see this as my “type.”)

In the Kibbe sense, I have a more “dramatic” as opposed to “gamine” face. I have a thin face, very dark brown hair and narrow, almost-black eyes. I have never looked “girlish” or particularly neotenous. Even as a kid, I was always cast as the mom or older woman in every school play. As a teenager, I could have easily passed as an adult.

I hope that this tendency no longer translates to “looking older” because now I’m thirty-seven and I have no desire to pass for fifty. But my face is defined by more angularity and dark features than cutesy-ness or girlishness, something that has long bothered me but just isn’t going to change. And while I’m not a big fan of my fairly strong nose, I’d rather have this nose than pay someone to break it, so here we are.

When optimizing for type, I have to combine my facial “vibe” with my body “vibe” which aren’t necessarily the same thing. I am not a straightforward bold and imposing type, nor am I a delicate baby fawn, nor am I a voluptuous fertility goddess.

Moreover, there’s my personality! This is probably the biggest defining factor in how I dress—not my body type, not my face, but my personality. I’m a goofy, extroverted, “quirk chungus” type. An improv kid, if you will. Draping myself in dark, dramatic and moody Ric Owens would not work for me, because nothing about my vibe is “dangerous” or “brooding.”

Can you honestly imagine me wearing this:

I may not be Sabrina Carpenter or Betty Boop, but I’m not Tilda Swinton either. When you combine my face, body, and personality, I am The Marvelous Mrs. Maisel or Fran from The Nanny.

Part of this realization involves my hair and makeup, and I can’t show you too much of that while remaining anonymous. But basically: while super-dark or heavy makeup is probably not flattering on anyone, I will also not benefit from bubblegum pink lipstick, super pink blush, sparkly eyeshadow, or anything that reads anime-inspired e-girl. I also do best with my hair my natural dark color as opposed to a lightened auburn brown, as I’ve done for many years of my life. My hair is naturally bone-straight, and any attempts to curl it for more than a few hours have failed. Occasionally, I’ll do some heatless curls, but in general I just embrace having Dakota Johnson’s hairstyle in The Materialists—it could be a whole lot worse.

But the biggest area for opportunity (and the most fun) is fashion. Now, men, don’t zone out, because while this part relates to women’s fashion, men’s fashion is next, and you will still learn from this part.

Now, for a long time, I’ve known that high-waisted jeans or anything with a belt will flatter my shape. Given my thicker lower body (proportionately speaking) and narrow upper body, plus the asset that makes sense to highlight (my waist) I’ve known the shapes that work for me for a while. However, sometimes I can be dressing “for my body” and still things don’t look so great. This is because I’m dressing against my type, so the outfit looks fine, but not “wow.” This is most obvious with overly preppy, “normie” or too-precious looks, where something just feels “off.” See below:

Everything reads more elegant and elevated than cute. The waist is emphasized in all images, but there is no excess skin or cleavage showing, no mini skirts, no short-shorts.

One of my biggest struggles for a while was the fact that, after a lifetime of hearing, “You’re ONLY (insert age here?)” (why do people think that’s a compliment?) I did not want to wear anything that could read “old.” This meant I was basically phobic of anything that could be perceived as elegant and this paranoia pushed me toward saccharine twee. I knew that dressing too elegantly got me confused for an older woman when I was twenty, ergo, I should just never do it. I was afraid that if I wore a dignified blazer at thirty, people would think I looked forty. (Don’t even start with how you’re thirty and “everyone thinks you’re fifteen” and you’re jealous of my struggle.) But actually, dressing like a teenager when you are an adult (with features that are unambiguously non-teenage) looks weirder and possibly even older than dressing to fit the vibe you actually give off.

I also thought, perhaps in error, that Sabrina Carpenter-inspired clothing would best reflect my bubbly personality while also aging me down. I also thought that my age was irrelevant if I remained in good shape. But (and I know this won’t be clear to you without seeing my face) it just looked a bit weird and uncanny, discordant with my overall type, like I was playing the part of a thirteen-year-old girl in a community theater where no cast members were young enough to do it. Granted, this took place during the Barbiecore wave, so this look was slightly on-trend, but some of these outfits just combine too many girlish details—pink, hearts, butterflies, short-shorts, tight, and so on (the jumpsuit is the only one I now consider somewhat acceptable.)

This does not mean I have to abandon this side of my personality—I was onto something! But given my age and lifestyle, I look a lot better when I’m modulating the over-the-top feminine and cute with some darker or more mature anchors. I will wear Mary Janes but not with something cutesy. (Also, yes, I made the comic book skirt and the polka dot coord.)

This rule also works with preppy looks. If everything is too Vineyard Vines or J.Crew, I look like I’m the Jewish woman doing her best (and failing) to assimilate in at the country club, or worse—an “old money” influencer who primarily posts on Instagram Reels:

This is not universal advice! Some women really look great dressed in a very frou-frou, ultra-girly way, bordering on silly. And while my age contributed to me moving away from this, sometimes, you can be the ultra-girly type no matter your age. Jennifer Coolidge is sixty-five, and I’ve always thought she looked best in an over-the-top pink outfit as opposed to something more understated or elegant:

Meanwhile, Lena Dunham looks much more vibrant and interesting when she is dressing for her vibe, which draws on her big and polarizing personality—bright colors, wacky accessories, dramatic dark hair. The neon-green suit and corset bag screams “Lena Dunham” far more than the understated white cocktail dress:

The irony is not lost on me that while Lena Dunham and I don’t look alike, our personalities lend themselves to the same aesthetic tips.

So, “dressing for your body type” is really about 20% of it. Dress for your entire vibe. Your face, your hair, your age, your personality. This also doesn’t universally mean jazzing it up and dressing weird. Some people have personalities that do not pair well with out-there clothing, who truly look their best in something more minimalistic and natural.

Also, your type will change over time! People get very caught up in the excitement of having a “fashion transformation” that they assume they will land on the One True Thing that looks good on them, never to revisit the topic again. The reality is that I wore stuff in my early twenties that looked great on me then, but would look weird on me now even at the same weight, because people (and times) change.

Now, men. Unfortunately men do not have so many levers to pull for fashion, but they do have some. They also have the option to adjust facial hair, which can make a major difference. And the advice is basically the same for men: yes, dress for your body type, but spend more time focusing on the general vibe you give off. Are you a quiet academic type? A lumberjack? A moody musician? A former-fratty dad? A tech bro? A Mr. Big? The list is honestly endless, and since men spend a lot less time thinking about how they appear to others, you may have to enlist some female friends to help you out.

I will now use my husband as an example because I am more intimately familiar with his body and closet than any of my followers (there would be major issues if this weren’t true!)

Now, for men who are still confused, here are some good starting points, or “prompts” if you will (which would also work for women, although I think men might need it more.)

  • Body type (20%)

    • What clothes tend to flatter your body type? For example, if you have short legs, a higher rise might flatter you. Long legs might benefit from a lower rise. Is your waist something you can accentuate, or do you have a more undefined midsection that looks best with something looser? Do you have broad shoulders that can anchor a flowy/drapy top, or do you have narrow shoulders that would be overwhelmed by oversized clothing?

  • Face/coloring (20%)

    • Does your face make you look like a particular “type” of person? Are you boyish/girlish/cutesy, or do you have a more mature and dignified look regardless of how old you are? Do you seem “jolly” or “intimidating?” What are people’s first impression of you before they get to know you? Do you have high-contrast coloring (which might benefit from jewel tones) or low-contrast coloring (which might look better with pastels and muted colors?)

      • This can also tie into how you style your hair, facial hair or makeup depending on your gender. If you have a more “dignified” face, for example, makeup that reads “teenage” could look especially weird on you. If you have a more childish face, heavy or smokey makeup might look bizarre.

        • If you’re a man: can you grow a good beard? If you can’t, you’re best off not trying. Are you balding? (Refer to body type for balding: skinny guys tend to look better with some hair, even if it’s severely receding, whereas heavier or muscular guys can look good with a shaved head.) If you are balding but can grow facial hair, consider a combination of these two things that will be most flattering for your “vibe.” (While some guys can pull off a shaved head and zero facial hair, it does not work on everyone.)

        • Consider how different types of facial hair are “coded.” The mustache used to be frumpy-coded, but now it’s a Gen Z thing, often seen on fratty-looking Gen Z guys in cities and modern-day hipsters. Short beards are a staple among millennials. Keep in mind your own age—if you are fifty, a mustache will not look “Gen Z” on you.

  • Personality (80%)

    • Are you an introvert or extrovert? Have people described you as a “character?” What are some of your favorite things to do, and how does that reflect in the clothes you like on yourself? Are you involved in any kind of subcultures? Are you geeky? Fratty? Normie? Are you a theater kid or artsy type? Do you make a big impression, or are you more understated?
