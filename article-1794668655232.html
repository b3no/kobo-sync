Democrats’ next challenge: talk like people, not politicians

After a win built on authenticity, the new majority has to resist the pull of Capitol jargon, or they risk further alienating the voters who just took a chance on them.

Before Annie Andrews was a senator-elect, she was a pediatrician in South Carolina. She would explain a treatment plan for a child with diabetes or asthma, and the parents would ask a question that had nothing to do with medicine: “How much is that inhaler going to cost?”

That exchange and countless others like it, she told NPR, are what pushed her into politics.

Last week, voters in a state that President Donald Trump carried by almost 18 points in 2024 elected her their first Democratic senator in nearly 30 years.

As a candidate with a career outside of politics, Andrews was not an outlier. Kansas elected Adam Hamilton, a Methodist pastor who leads the nation’s largest United Methodist congregation, as its first Democratic senator since 1932. Montana chose Sam Forstag, a union leader and former smokejumper, for the House. Pennsylvania voters elected Bob Brooks, a union leader and retired firefighter. Voters in North Carolina went for Jamie Ager, a fourth-generation farmer with a Southern drawl from the west side of the state.

Their platforms differed, but their campaigns shared an approach: Each of these candidates — and many others who contributed to the incoming Democratic majorities in the House and Senate — met voters where they were, talked about their lives in the terms that voters themselves used, and let people see themselves in the person asking for their vote.

The question now is whether these newly elected and longtime representatives will take that approach with them to the Capitol or replace it with the kind of insider/outsider language that alienated voters in 2024.

The Democrats have a brand problem

Democrats, who will hold at least 55 Senate seats and 256 House seats in the next Congress, won a victory beyond anything forecast in polls or foretold by talking heads. But that doesn’t mean droves of enthusiastic voters are now pledging their allegiance to the party. The election was, by all accounts, a referendum on Trump, his war in Iran, the cost of living, and government corruption.

The Democratic Party has its own brand problem, even if it isn’t quite as bad as the G.O.P.’s.

A 2025 Pew Research poll found that 75 percent of U.S. adults said the Democratic Party makes them feel frustrated, while 64 percent said the same of the Republican Party. Many cited that Democrats weren’t fighting hard enough against Trump as the cause of their dissatisfaction, but another common reason was that the party was out of touch with voters. The data journalist G. Elliott Morris found the same complaint reported in his survey from 2026. Last year, Politico reported that “working-class voters see Democrats as ‘woke, weak, and out-of-touch.’”

The most frequent complaints about the party across surveys were about its lack of fight, its lack of leadership, and its inability to see voters and understand their concerns.

Being out of touch is just one problem among several. But after a campaign cycle that did away with much of “woke” by putting the cost of living and opposition to Trump ahead of social issues, the next problem that the party can tackle without waiting for legislation or investigations is its connection with voters.

The vocabulary of exclusion

Washington runs on words most Americans never use, and, because civic education has largely been thrown out by schools across the country, the processes of American democracy and government are out of reach. Insiders know what reconciliation, cloture, and BAAJA mean, but while that shorthand might be useful for reducing friction among those who work in or around politics, it’s a signal to most people that the conversation isn’t for them. It makes politics seem removed from its very real implications on American lives and widens the gap between those who make policy and those who are affected by it.

Research suggests that signal is real.

Hillary Shulman, a researcher at Ohio State University, found that cutting out jargon, especially in polling, makes people more trusting. An experiment from O.S.U. found that people exposed to jargon became less interested in the subject, while those exposed to plain language became more engaged. A study from the University of Copenhagen found that when politicians enter office, their language becomes less intelligible and more inaccessible. The shift, according to Frederik Hjorth, is due to the conventions of the job, which make politicians adopt bureaucratic jargon, present bills written in difficult language, and talk more about issues such as “regulation and crisis management — and less about ideologically clear topics like tax and immigration.”

Voters surveyed by Hjorth overwhelmingly favored politicians who used simple language, even when the content was the same.

“Populists often emphasize that the elites have lost touch with the people. This study suggests that the criticism is correct in a sense: more technocratic language is part of the price of government accountability,” Hjorth explained.

Hjorth’s study is Dutch, but its lessons translate to the American dynamic. He explained that the opposition to the party in power has a rhetorical advantage, helping to explain why governing parties often lose support. In 2024, voters reported they swung right because Democrats had lost touch with them.

Pulling back the veil

Beyond simplifying speech, politicians can increase trust through transparency about processes. Earlier this week, Representative Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez took to Instagram Live with a whiteboard to directly explain to her nearly 10 million followers how a House speaker is chosen and what the role entails, amid increasing debate over whether Democrats should back Minority Leader Hakeem Jeffries as speaker. That video garnered significant attention online.

Most people — including me — were never taught in any kind of formal capacity how the job works, and for representatives making a decision about who to support, that lack of knowledge among constituents may result in less understanding about the choices their representatives make, eroding trust.

Ocasio-Cortez, who has risen to party stardom and is a possible 2028 contender, has made this kind of direct-to-camera educational content before. In 2021, she used Instagram Live to walk viewers through the American Rescue Plan.

In an article criticizing Ocasio-Cortez’s livestream this week and similar videos she has made in the past, the Daily Wire snarkily writes that the representative, who, they say, had once failed a “basic civics check” when she referred to the “three chambers of government: the presidency, the Senate, and the House,” tried to give a lesson to her followers who were “apparently unfamiliar with the basic mechanics of their own government.” The critique spends much of its wordspace on a review of studies about America’s declining civic education, proving that, indeed, most Americans are unfamiliar with how their government works.

Education can easily turn into condescension, so this kind of peek behind the veil must be respectful and approached with the understanding that a failure of comprehension is not necessarily the voter’s fault.

The Nation’s Report Card found that only 22 percent of eighth graders were proficient in civics in 2022, and that year brought the first-ever decline in civics scores. By one analysis, state civics requirements are often reduced to a single semester in high school. I was not required to take even a semester of civics in my public middle or high school. A stronger curriculum would teach the basics of American government and, most important, why it matters, such as how a budget decision shows up in school lunches, insulin prices, and at the gas pump. History curriculum also teaches students why this all matters.

Until schools across the country take readopting a civics curriculum seriously, it falls on the people asking for Americans’ trust and support to understand and address the gap in understanding.

Why openness matters

The American people last week took a real chance on Democrats from Maine to Montana to Mississippi. Even though they don’t necessarily view the party favorably, voters offered candidates their trust in a moment when it’s never been more scarce. In 2025, according to Pew, only 17 percent of Americans said they trust the government in Washington to do what is right.

Voters who feel betrayed by their party — whichever one it may be — need to see, and understand, the work.

Concretely, Democrats could adopt a standard of attaching a one-page, plain-English summary to every major bill, explain their votes in plain English in short public videos, and hold negotiations in the open whenever practical. They could also strip away the acronyms that have become ubiquitous in Washington lingo, along with signals of the kind of exclusivity that tells the American people that their government is not for them.

I was at a party last weekend full of Washington wonks. Also at that party were a few Washington residents who don’t work in politics and a few people from out of town who, despite being informed voters, felt like total outsiders in a group discussing BAAJA. One of the attendees told me they’d never felt so stupid in a group of their peers. I told them that the acronym meant the Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act and briefly explained its purpose, and they easily understood what I was talking about, asked follow-up questions, and took a stance on the bill.

That kind of simple transparency follows the logic of the campaigns that just won, and it could go a long way with voters who have put fragile trust in a party that has made them feel alienated.
