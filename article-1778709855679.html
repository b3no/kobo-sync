There’s no place like home

As the market stalls, the dream of homeownership is apparently still alive in Kansas. But because we can’t all live in Wichita, Congress is trying to get housing back on track.

President Donald Trump on Monday called on the House of Representatives to pass the 21st Century ROAD to Housing Act, linking the bill to the housing-affordability demands he made during his February State of the Union address.

“Senators Bernie Moreno and Tim Scott have worked to ensure my call becomes a reality, and have a Bill which has passed the Senate with nearly 90 votes,” he wrote on Truth Social. “I am asking Congress to pass that Bill, the 21st Century ROAD to Housing Act, which would ensure that homes are for people, not Corporations.”

Despite the president’s alleged quiet signaling last week that he did not support the bill’s provision curtailing build-to-rent development, he is holding firm on his demand that Wall Street investment firms be banned not only from buying up single-family homes but also from building and owning new ones. He called on the House to pass the bill as is.

Vice President JD Vance and Republican senators took to X to show their support for the bill.

“The American Dream doesn’t belong to the highest bidder on Wall Street,” Vance wrote. “It belongs to the American people, who work hard, save up, and play by the rules. I applaud President Trump’s leadership on this issue and urge the House to pass this bill.”

In another bit of federal housing legislation news, Representatives Jimmy Panetta (D-Calif.) and Mike Carey (R-Ohio) reintroduced the Workforce Housing Tax Credit Act on May 6. The bill is backed by the National Apartment Association, the National Multifamily Housing Council, and more than a dozen other industry groups.

If enacted, this legislation would bring about the first-ever federal middle-income housing tax credit. It is modeled on the Low-Income Housing Tax Credit, Multifamily Dive reports. The bill would finance roughly 344,000 affordable rental homes, targeting households earning between 60 and 100 percent of area median income — a group that earns too much to qualify for units subsidized by the Low-Income Housing Tax Credit but too little to afford market-rate housing near their jobs.

Industry groups argue this middle-income housing tax credit would fill a critical gap for teachers, nurses, firefighters, and other essential workers. Opponents, including the National Low Income Housing Coalition when the bill was first introduced in 2023, argue that scarce federal resources should instead target extremely low-income households, which face the deepest affordability crises. The tax-credit-driven approach is itself controversial: Research from Professor Evan Soltas indicates that half the value of the original Low-Income Housing Tax Credit, on which this new bill is based, is captured by developers and a quarter is expended on competing for subsidies, leaving only about 25 percent of the face value to actually flow through to low-income renters. Previous versions of the bill have stalled; supporters are hoping the current “affordability” push will improve its prospects.

Spring market slows, and small cities take advantage

The housing recovery many analysts expected this spring has stalled. Existing home sales increased only 0.2 percent in April to a seasonally adjusted rate of 4.02 million units — far short of the 3 percent gain that economists had forecast and essentially flat compared to a year ago, the Wall Street Journal reports. Sales have hovered near the 4-million-unit annual pace since 2023, well below the historical norm of roughly 5.2 million. The national median sales price rose 0.9 percent year over year to $417,700 — an all-time high for any April on record, the Washington Post reports.

The culprit, according to the National Association of Realtors (NAR) Chief Economist Lawrence Yun via the Journal, is the oil shock due to the war in Iran, which pushed mortgage rates back up to 6.37 percent after they had dipped below 6 percent in February, sapping the recovery momentum.

Buyer anxiety about the market has compounded the problem, and the result is the fourth consecutive disappointing spring selling season.

That anxiety is showing up most clearly among first-time home buyers, the New York Times reports. The average age of a first-time buyer today is 40, compared to 2008, when the median age was 30, according to NAR, and those buyers make up 21 percent of home purchases nationwide. This is a historically low share, reflecting the years of appreciation and elevated mortgage rates that have priced out younger would-be owners. Incomes are rising, with some estimates predicting they will begin to outpace home prices nationally sometime this year, but right now the gap between what buyers earn and what homes cost remains wide, particularly for those without existing equity.

But somewhere, the dream of homeownership is still alive. While coastal and Sun Belt cities chase market-choking tech workers and finance professionals, Wichita, Kan., is marketing itself to the middle class, the Wall Street Journal reports. The city’s median household income is around $66,000, and average monthly apartment rent runs roughly $950 — approximately 45 percent below the national average of $1,750. According to Zillow, Wichita’s median home price is about $204,104, up 2.8 percent from last year.

The Journal reports that the city is actively recruiting workers in health care, energy, and manufacturing, with new duplex and small multifamily construction aimed at young families, retirees, and empty-nesters. Chase Billingham, a sociologist at Wichita State University who is quoted by the Journal, explained that municipal officials and business leaders are taking advantage of the inexpensive housing to draw in young talent and new residents who have been priced out of perhaps more desirable markets.

Other cities that have long struggled with brain drain and attracting new residents are also capitalizing on nationwide economic anxiety to bolster their populations. As I asked way back during my first week at Slow Boring: Should we all move to Tulsa?

Local and state policy

Twin Cities miss all housing goals: The Minneapolis Federal Reserve’s annual Regional Housing Affordability Dashboard update, released May 8, revealed that for the first time since housing-affordability goals were set in 2022, the Twin Cities region failed to meet all three simultaneously.

The seven-county metro built roughly 12,000 units in both 2024 and 2025 — about two-thirds of its 18,000-unit annual target. The region also fell short of its targets on affordable housing production and saw Black homeownership rates slip rather than close the gap toward the national rate of 45 percent by 2030.

On the positive side, the Minneapolis-St. Paul metro remains relatively affordable compared to peer cities, ranking fourth-lowest in rent among its 12-city comparison group and second-highest in the share of units affordable to households at 50 percent of area median income. Despite these relative bright spots, the report points to a rental vacancy rate of 5.4 percent — the third lowest among its peers — as a warning sign that prices are likely to rise, absent new units.

A.I. is splitting the Bay Area in two: A Redfin analysis published last week found that the A.I. boom has bifurcated San Francisco Bay Area housing. Since the launch of ChatGPT in November 2022, luxury zip codes (homes in the $3.1 million to $7.6 million range) have seen average sale prices rise 13.4 percent — more than double the 6.3 percent gain seen in the segment just below luxury.

At the same time, homes in the most affordable Bay Area zip codes ($535,000 through $615,000) have declined in value by 3.8 percent.

The metro’s overall median sale price hit a record $1.7 million in March, up 14.4 percent year over year — the largest annual increase in eight years and the biggest gain among the 50 most populous U.S. metros.

Redfin Senior Economist Yingqi Xu called it the effect of “the K-shaped economy taking shape in the Bay Area, with A.I. lifting the fortunes of some households and neighborhoods much more than others.”

The divergence is unique to the Bay Area, Redfin reports. New York, Los Angeles, and Seattle did not see comparable splits between the luxury and affordable housing markets in the same period.

New York City’s pied-à-terre tax: Mayor Zohran Mamdani on Tuesday released a fiscal budget proposal that dropped previous plans to raise property taxes on many middle-class homeowners in favor of a pied-à-terre tax on second homes valued above $5 million. Governor Kathy Hochul announced in April that New York’s state budget deal would include such a tax.

The measure is projected to generate roughly $500 million annually and was framed as a way to close the city’s budget gap without imposing a broad wealth tax, which Mamdani had previously championed.

Hochul’s proposal still requires New York State Legislature approval, and implementation details (including how to assess values and handle properties owned through limited liability companies) remain unsettled. When it was announced last month, Carl E. Heastie, the Assembly speaker, called the governor’s statement “premature,” the New York Times reports, and the Real Estate Board of New York is advocating against it. Brokers cited by Bloomberg argued international cash buyers may shift their activity to low-tax, high-luxury markets such as Miami and Dubai.

Despite pushback, Mamdani signaling support for the measure may come as a welcome compromise to those worried about his prior plans for greater, more widespread tax hikes.

Maine’s NIMBY data-center problem: An investigation from the Daily Yonder published Tuesday uncovered how a data-center dispute in rural Maine unraveled plans for affordable housing and failed to develop the land at all.

In Wiscasset, Maine — a coastal community of 4,000 people — the town received $240,000 in American Rescue Plan Act funding from Lincoln County for an affordable housing project on a vacant 300-acre parcel along the Back River. But when a developer announced plans for a $5 billion data center on the same land in September 2025, town officials shifted their attention. Lincoln County ultimately determined that the “data center is not compatible with their vision of a ‘Great American Neighborhood’” and rescinded Wiscasset’s allocation on March 3 of this year, redistributing the funds to neighboring Waldoboro and Newcastle for their own housing and infrastructure projects.

The story is a local example of a national pattern: Two-thirds of new data centers are expected to be built in rural areas, according to April 2026 Pew Research Center findings, often competing directly with other land uses and public investment priorities. After this kind of competition played out in Maine, the Wiscasset parcel remains undeveloped.

“In Midcoast Maine, a data center stalled,” the Daily Yonder reports. “So did affordable housing in its wake.”
