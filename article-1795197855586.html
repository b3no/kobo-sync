Remembering Jon Yardley (1939–2026)

What I learned from The Washington Post’s longtime book critic

Ron Charles
Nov 20
 
READ IN APP
 

Of course, I’d heard of Jonathan Yardley long before I met him.

But my first personal encounter came in 2005 when the Book World staff took me out to lunch as part of my job interview. Jon regarded his salad with the same skeptical vigor he directed at me.

The next day, I flew back home to Boston assuming the worst. My nervous thank-you email addressed him as “John.” He immediately let me know it was “Jon.”

But a few days later, I was offered a job at The Washington Post and, soon after, received a note from Mr. Yardley that contained such an enthusiastic vote of confidence that I hovered eight inches off the ground for a month.

It was always that way with him. The first thing you noticed about Jon was that he was ferocious. And the second thing you noticed, if he liked you, was that he was ferociously loyal.

During his many decades reviewing books, he brought a blend of erudition and populism to the job that set a standard for the industry. He was just as comfortable tackling a serious work of history as the memoir of a country singer — and both got the same chance to succeed on their own terms. His ability to praise with intelligence was matched only by his willingness to crush with wit.

(One oft-quoted classic: Suffering through Jimmy McDonough’s “truly empty, cliché-littered, bubble-headed” biography of Tammy Wynette on a long flight, he wrote, “I wished the plane would crash, just to put me out of my misery.”)

Once, during a pitch meeting in the office, I mentioned that Stephen King was publishing a novel in which cell phones turn everybody into zombies. Jon immediately quipped, “That’s already happened.”

The most important lessons I learned from Jon concerned how an effective critic should behave. First rule: Resist the clammy allure of publishers’ praise and authors’ friendship. “We don’t work for them,” he would remind me. “We work for The Post’s readers.”

Those readers were served well for decades. So were those of us who worked with him.◆
