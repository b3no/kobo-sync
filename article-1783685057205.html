What Happened to Picky Eaters in Olden Times?

Turns out picky eating existed long before chicken nuggets.

My brother was a severe picky eater, for his entire childhood. Every day, my parents would provide his safe foods for him—cereal for breakfast, often a peanut butter and jelly sandwich for lunch, and either pizza or unseasoned baked chicken breast and steamed broccoli for dinner. I can still vividly recall my mother fashioning a mini baking sheet out of aluminum foil for his nightly bone-dry chicken, a defeated look on her face. And my first thought was, I will never let my kid do this to me.

But parenthood humbles you, and as it turned out, my brother wasn’t just a picky eater. He had a generalized feeding disorder that went beyond stubbornness. He would gag when pushed to try new foods. He had anxiety, gagging, texture sensitivities and found even some typical childhood foods repulsive (to this day, he loathes ice cream cake.) He would have gladly skipped a meal, or even multiple meals, if he found the food intolerably disgusting—and he found lots of foods disgusting. In a moment of weakness, my mom exclaimed at the dinner table, “You don’t like anything!” to which he, about four years old, incredulously responded, “I like fings. Like chips.” (He did not even get chips very often, and he would have made a more compelling case had he invoked the steamed broccoli.)

I later went on to have a child with the same basic issue, and much like my brother, his safe foods aren’t “chicken nuggets.” In fact, my child will not touch chicken nuggets, but loves to snack on extremely healthy (and extremely bitter) cacao nibs. He had another phase marked by an obsession with pistachios. For two years, he would eat up to five scrambled eggs every morning, before one day grimacing at the sight of them, never eating them again no matter how many times they were offered. His picky eating is not driven by a diet of processed sugar. When he started at a school that provided free lunch, I asked his teacher if I should pack him a separate lunch to accommodate his eating issues. She laughed and said, “If he’s hungry, he’ll eat! I’ve never had a picky eater in my class who wasn’t eating something new after a week with me!” I was excited that this woman might actually hold the key to his feeding issues, especially given that we had gotten nowhere with two feeding therapists already. After two weeks, the teacher was urging me to pack him “anything” he would eat in his lunch box, nutrition aside. Apparently, he was the first kid she had seen who would gladly not eat, even when hungry.

To this day, we serve him his few safe foods (which, thankfully, include some healthy things) while consistently offering new foods in a low-pressure setting. We’ve made some progress (he’s eating apples, dried fruits and more varieties of nuts, although, as Trump would say, “not with a lot of enthusiasm”) but most of the time, our goal is simply to make sure he’s eating. And we have the stamp of approval from his pediatrician, which makes us feel a bit better.

I’ve now experienced enough not to judge my parents for “catering” to my brother’s picky eating. It was obvious, in my brother’s case at least, that “you eat this or you starve” would have resulted in his starvation. Perhaps not starvation to death, but eating the bare minimum for survival and having inadequate energy and stunted growth.

But it got me wondering—what the hell happened to kids like this before the advent of “safe foods?” For most of human history, we didn’t have the capacity to procure preferred foods for children, whether that was a processed mango-flavored pouch or a bespoke nut and seed smoothie with Greek yogurt, as my son drinks. “You eat this or you starve” was quite literally our only option for most of human history. So I did some research to try and understand what happened to picky eaters in the days of yore—and I learned some pretty surprising things!

It’s entirely possible that picky eating has an evolutionary component, which lends credence to the idea that it’s not a modern phenomenon driven by over-indulgent parents and asshole kids. Scientists have suggested that children become more picky—specifically around new foods—right around the time they become mobile, as an evolutionary response to avoid new dangers. A child who isn’t mobile isn’t likely to encounter poisonous berries on his own, but a child who is mobile very well could. This is why many people have stories of ten-month-olds joyfully munching on garlic salmon but refusing buttered pasta just a year later. My daughter, who has a much more “typical” eater profile, would eat anything under the sun when she first started solids, but now like any finicky toddler, will randomly decide blueberries are radioactive balls of toxic sludge. Of course, this evolutionary response could have been engineered a bit better. Every parent has a story about a child who refuses to eat carrots but tries to eat a pinecone at the park. My own daughter attempted to make herself a “sand ice cream” at the playground the other day, and joyfully took a bite. Ah, well, nevertheless.

We don’t hear much about picky eaters before the modern era, but I don’t think it’s because they didn’t exist—I think it’s because people just had bigger fish to fry, and children did not have varied-enough diets for “trying new foods” to be a priority. Remember, most picky eaters experience the most issues around trying new foods, not eating in general, and for most of Western post-agricultural history, most people’s diets were basically that of a picky eater. In the Middle Ages, outside of the nobility, people’s diets were heavy in carbs, specifically bread (a picky eater’s dream!) and pottage, which could be anything from a vegetable soup to oatmeal. Moreover, children were often breastfed for a much longer stretch of time than they are today, meaning a finicky toddler could simply survive off milk. Children were not eating a variety of meat, cheeses, fresh vegetables and fruit because nobody was eating this. Perhaps picky eaters existed within royalty, who had access to a variety of meats and other delicacies, but there’s no record of it.

If a child in the Middle Ages had a severe feeding disorder, they might have refused to eat even the daily porridge and bread, despite its blandness and predictability. But we would be too quick to assume, “and everything was fine.” Too often, we look back on ancient times and decide that “everyone was fine” when obviously, a lot of people were not fine. During a period of time when 50% of children passed away before the age of five, a rare feeding disorder causing children to starve to death might have gone completely unnoticed, especially given that many children were already malnourished at baseline. For example, babies who are unable to breastfeed have modern solutions now—bottle feeding, pumping, and baby formula—but before these things were available, a lot of these babies simply died, and this wasn’t particularly noteworthy.

Childhood and parenthood were also just treated differently back then. For example, in the Middle Ages, a colicky baby who screamed too much could be deemed a “changeling”—a demonic fairy imposter—and become subject to various dangerous exorcism rituals or left in the woods. Because babies’ limbs were seen as “soft as wax,” they were often swaddled tightly all day and night without the ability to move around, so that their limbs would mold into the correct straight shape, even if they developed sores or bruises. To keep babies out of harm’s way while mothers went to work in the fields, they were sometimes swaddled and hung on hooks so animals couldn’t reach them. I find it hard to believe that people who harbored these beliefs also felt strongly about “modeling healthy eating.” They had no concept of healthy food for themselves, let alone their kids. Their priority was keeping their children alive and fed, they had no variety of foods anyway, and since young children died all the time, a child dying from malnutrition probably wouldn’t seem so unusual. “Regular” picky eaters probably ate what was given to them since no alternatives existed, and children with severe eating issues were probably dangerously malnourished, only eating enough to survive or not surviving at all.

Alternatively, it’s possible that without variety and emphasis on trying new foods, children who would have had feeding disorders never developed them. This is purely my own theory, but given that people with feeding disorders often still have a few foods they can tolerate, it raises the question of how “safe foods” or “scary foods” would have even materialized if a child only had access to three bland foods in the first place. Still, hardly a “RETVRN” situation. Even kids who ate what they were given were not doing very well!

Since the concept of pediatric medicine didn’t really exist until the eighteenth century, there are very few medical sources on children’s nutrition prior to this era. But there are hints that picky eating persisted through the 1700s and 1800s. Die Geschichte vom Suppen-Kaspar is a 1845 cautionary tale about a little boy who wastes away and dies after he refuses to eat soup. Some of the earliest pediatric medical literature discusses picky eating (using different terms) with a variety of advice, such as refusing to offer alternatives to the family dinner, or even caving completely and allowing a child to eat what they want as long as they eat. French physician Antoine-François Fourcroy said in 1783:

So if they have no appetite, let them eat as they please, taking care only to offer them their breadcrumbs or porridge at the usual times, to often give them a crust of stale bread rubbed with honey, and to let them drink honeyed water at will.

Hmmm…sounds like what all these modern, newfangled, overly permissive and uniquely “American” parents are doing!

I recognized what appears to be a feeding disorder or other form of anxiety-driven picky eating in a 1908 article in a British medical journal, titled, The Refusal of Food in Infancy. This article discusses children who insisted on bottle-feeding, rejecting solid food, after the age of one, and the proposed solution is to stop bottle-feeding, “until starvation compels a child to take from a cup or spoon.” While this treatment appeared to work for some children (who by today’s standards might have been “normal” stubborn picky eaters) the doctor goes on to describe a “neurotic” child, who, by modern standards, would have a severe feeding disorder:

Such a case was that of a boy, aged two years, who had scurvy in the first year of life and later broncho-pneumonia. Recovery was complete, and he was gaining weight rapidly, when the above symptoms [refusing food] came on. He rapidly lost weight, nine ounces in one week and thirteen in the next five days. There was no apparent cause. He seemed quite bright and cheerful, and slept well. On a diet of milk and milk sugar he is improving, and will no doubt return to his ordinary food habits.

It’s unclear what actually happened to this child, or if he ever did return to his ordinary food habits. Clearly, at the point of publication, he still had not! But in this case, the doctor is mostly concerned with keeping the child’s weight up, and less concerned with pressuring him to try solid food, let alone a variety of solid foods. Seeing his feeding disorder as a transient state, the doctor is happy to put him on an all-milk and sugar diet without pushing it further. Moreover, there is almost zero emphasis in the article about “new” foods—just solid food at all. This could be because processed, ultra-palatable foods didn’t exist then (although dessert did!) but rather that eating a wide variety of foods wasn’t an option for most families, nor was it a priority for most parents.

When we think about the history of modern phenomena, we (mostly American and European writers) think of Western history. But the foods available to Westerners in the early 1900s were very different from the foods available to people around the world in other cultures, even today. The Papua New Guinea Highlanders, for example, eat a diet almost entirely comprised of sweet potatoes. Rice is responsible for feeding about half of humanity, and outside of meat, two-thirds of global human food consumption boils down to rice, corn and wheat. 40% of the food eaten by people in sub-Saharan Africa is comprised of roots and tubers. Most of the global population, even in the modern era, is not focused on plating elaborate rainbows of variety in front of eighteen-month-olds. Many people today survive on diets that are more restrictive than that of your average picky eater in America. Imagine the shaming you’d receive on a mommy forum if you announced your child survived on a sweet potato only diet!

Modern Western parents don’t only worry about nutrition—if they did, I’d have no concerns about my son’s diet—but about variety. One scroll through Instagram as mom will have you feeling inadequate as you peruse photos of colorful toddler breakfasts, full of beetroot hummus, mixed berries, crudite, and flakes of cod. While the type of picky eating that relies on ultra-processed, ultra-palatable foods is a recent thing driven by easy availability of snack foods and other junk, our obsession with a varied diet is also pretty recent. The American diet is more varied than ever before, even contrasted with relatively recent history. In 1970, a typical supermarket supplied 9,000 different products. By 2017, that number ballooned to 50,000. In 1970, a parent hoping to instill healthy eating habits in her child would not have had the pressures of such intense variety, only of nutrition.

The book, Picky: How American Children Became the Fussiest Eaters in History lays the blame at the zeitgeist changing around what children could handle. The author Helen Zoe Veit makes a compelling argument that picky eating did not exist prior to the modern era and instead was conjured up in the twentieth century as a response to bad parenting guidelines which assumed children were unable to eat adult foods, and a culture which churned out less nutritious and more palatable “children’s foods.” In one of her examples, the famous Dr. Spock at one point advised parents to assume a child will eventually eat if hungry enough, but later reversed course and declared that pushing children to eat adult food was oppressive and cruel, and that children should be fed whatever they want to avoid undue pressure. This changing mindset is partially why “kids’ menus” exist today, but didn’t exist for most of the twentieth century.

I am not inclined to agree entirely with her premise, although components of it are clearly accurate. There are probably many picky eaters today who would not have been picky eaters in the 1800s, or at least not for long. But “eat what we’re serving or you starve” makes a lot more sense as the default when it’s literally true, not when parents are enforcing it. If a child knows that eating vegetable stew is quite literally their only option, there is no “conflict” over food, because the parents have not set this up as a seemingly-nonsensical (to the mind of a child) rule. It’s no wonder that picky eating exploded around the time that ordinary people gained more access to a variety of foods they could choose, when kids knew very well that there might have been a secret third option other then vegetable stew and starvation—refusing to eat until the next meal, skipping breakfast to sneak vending machine treats at school, bartering lunch with friends. And even if a parent does their best to keep their children from discovering processed foods, they will eventually encounter such a thing at a party or a friend’s house. (I successfully shielded my own child from gummies for almost five years until they were handed out at a Halloween party.)

To understand to what degree picky eating is a modern phenomenon (the answer: somewhat) we should separate the normal developmental stage of picky eating from actual feeding disorders. But more than anything, we should refrain from assuming that “everything was fine” before microwaved pizza entered the picture.
