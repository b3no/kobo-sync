My Own Little Bookstore Is Opening Soon

My wife thinks I'm crazy for wanting to open a bookstore. But independent bookstores are worth supporting — and dreaming about.

Ron Charles
Nov 27
 
READ IN APP
 

My life has been a series of people crushing my dreams.

In college, a weary math professor finally pleaded, “Ron, maybe your talents lie elsewhere.”

At my second teaching job, the headmaster told me, “I used to think of you as principal material, but then I realized, no.”

In every case, I hasten to say, these people were entirely right.

My wife, for instance, has long pooh-poohed my fantasy of opening a bookstore.

I imagine leading eager patrons to undiscovered works in translation and splitting a scone with Elizabeth Strout. But Dawn, whose father owned a flower shop, knows that running a small business is all about renegotiating the lease and unclogging the toilet.

Ann Patchett, the bestselling novelist who owns Parnassus Books in Nashville, took Dawn’s side. A few years ago, when I asked her about opening a bookstore, she brushed aside my romantic notions of what’s required.

“Do I think you need the right bookish attitude?” she said. “No. I mean, if you’re this far down the road, you’ve got the right bookish attitude. What you need is parking. What you need is fair rent.”

But what about my relationship to contemporary poetry?

“The most important relationship you’re going to have is with your landlord.”

After warning me about the impoverishing costs of a snow removal contract — in Nashville, where it rarely snows! — and catastrophes lurking in the old HVAC system, she said, “You really thought I was going to talk to you about books, didn’t you?”

The more I learn, the more awed I am by these feisty literary entrepreneurs. The risk of loss is great; the chance for gain is slim. And if Amazon isn’t training customers to expect books at half price, a new Barnes & Noble is strutting in every week wearing a vintage T-shirt like it’s one of the cool kids.

The challenge is that the benefits of indie bookstores are immense but diffuse. These idiosyncratic businesses funnel revenue back into our communities, they nurture new authors, they host literary events, they defend freedom of expression, and they offer unalgorithmic recommendations.

Throwing all that away is what saving $11 on Kathryn Stockett’s new novel really costs. The calamity club, indeed.

What’s more, no indie bookstore has ever donated $1 million to Donald Trump’s second inauguration or paid $40 million for his third wife’s documentary about her struggles.

Tomorrow, Nov. 28, is Indies First Day, a national campaign to support our independent bookstores. About 1,000 stores will be celebrating with activities such as author signings, story hours, bookstore crawls, raffles, refreshments, craft activities, and special discounts. (To find a participating bookstore near you, check this map.)

Incidentally, my chances of owning a small bookstore are looking better than ever, regardless of what Dawn says.

This fall, LEGO released Bookstore: Book Nook, a dazzling two-story shop that includes balconies, a spiral staircase, a wee café, more than 100 tiny books, and literary allusions to Beauty and the Beast, The Three Musketeers, and many other classics ($149.99, details).

Broken HVAC? Won’t happen. Cruel landlord? I’m my own man here.

But there are other challenges — chief among them, the set’s 1,690 pieces. The instruction booklet runs to 280 pages.

I’m seriously considering giving this to someone I don’t like who has small children.

But I can’t let it go. After all, a little bookstore is my dream.

Of course, in that dream, I always imagined my bookstore would be wider than 16 inches, but a man’s reach should exceed his grasp, or what’s a heaven for?◆

Books Go to the Movies

  • The Hunchback, Soul of Paris, starring Vincent Cassel, begins streaming today on Netflix. This French film, set just before the July Revolution in 1830, was reportedly inspired by the life of a stonemason who worked on the restoration of Notre-Dame in the 1820s. Some speculate that he served as the model for Quasimodo in Hugo’s classic novel set in 1482.

  • If your uncle were Star-Lord, it’s hard to imagine kids would pick on you at school. But suspend your disbelief. Way of the Warrior Kid stars Chris Pratt as an injured Navy SEAL who teaches his young nephew how to defend himself. The movie, streaming on Apple TV and exhaustively outlined in this trailer, is based on Jocko Willink’s middle-grade novel Way of the Warrior Kid: From Wimpy to Warrior the Navy SEAL Way.

  • How time flies when you’re a replicant. All eight episodes of Blade Runner 2099, starring Michelle Yeoh and Hunter Schafer, are streaming on Prime Video (trailer). The series, set half a century after Blade Runner 2049, is the latter-day spawn of Philip K. Dick’s 1968 novel Do Androids Dream of Electric Sheep?◆

How It Crumbles

Losing my paycheck at The Washington Post was hard. Losing access to the Food section’s cookies was devastating.

For many years, Book World editors sat right next to the Food editors, which allowed me to keep a hungry eye on preparations for their holiday cookie feature.

I’ve munched on early versions of Brown Butter Salted Caramel Cookies, Gingersnap and Orange Cream Sandwiches, and even Pistachio Shortbread With Pomegranate Glaze.

Alas, this holiday season, I’m on my own, cast into a howling, cookie-less slough of despond.

All hail Susan Spungen, the founding food editor at Martha Stewart Living Omnimedia. She’s just pulled from the oven a delicious-looking cookbook called Cookie Queen, which is the only form of tyranny to which I’ll willingly submit.

Among more than 80 “irresistible, doable, giftable recipes” are Apple-Walnut Chubbies with Cinnamon Swirl, Caramel-Stuffed Cashew Chocolate Chunk Cookies, Honey-Roasted Peanut Linzers, and Coconut-Pistachio Macaroons.

There’s also a special chapter titled “My Kid Could Do That!” which lays out recipes for young and beginning bakers, such as Jackson Pollock Cookies.

All these recipes glow in full-page color photos that look good enough to eat, but don’t let anybody try that switcheroo.

I’ve taken to leaving Cookie Queen around the house where Dawn might find it — on her dining room chair, in the dryer, under her pillow. I even put an empty cookie tin in the car so every time we go anywhere I can pick it up and say, “What’s this empty cookie tin doing in here?”

Subtlety never got anybody a cookie.◆

Take the book, and eat it up.

I’m totally behind Thomas Jefferson’s claim that he “cannot live without books,” but could we add chocolate to the mix?

Geri Gallas knows what I’m chewing on. She designs fair-trade chocolate bars inspired by famous stories and literary characters. For instance, her Jane Eyre milk chocolate bar with bilberries recalls the moment our heroine flees Thornfield Hall and is reduced to searching for bilberries in the heath.

Reader, I ate it.

It is a truth universally acknowledged that a married man in possession of a Substack must be in want of a Pride & Prejudice dark chocolate bar spiced with cinnamon and flecked with blackcurrants.

Gallas’s company, Open Book Chocolates, also offers bars reminiscent of Treasure Island, Sherlock Holmes, The Count of Monte Cristo, and many more, including a new Odyssey bar with olive oil and rosemary that I would sail 10 years to taste.

A Christmas Carol in dark chocolate contains hazelnuts, pears, and nutmeg, just like the Ghost of Christmas Present would bring.

The bars — about two ounces each — come in elegant wrappers sporting images and words from their related books.

I’ve been recommending these treats for years, and now I’m seeing them show up in some indie bookstores, which makes sense. They don’t come cheap — $12.99 each — but they make a sweet gift or a party favor for book club meetings.

Gallas is swamped at the moment, but she’s taking pre-orders and promises to fill them as soon as she can (details).

Underlined

A passage from something I’m reading.

Since women prepared Knox Gelatine in their kitchens, Mrs. Knox’s vocation appeared to be a natural progression from her traditional female duties. Her ad agency described her as a “practical housewife,” “just a woman, as woman was meant to be . . . [S]he has used all her faculties, developed her power.” They omitted to mention that, actually, at this point she was a grandmother who employed a cook.

Mrs. Knox accomplished two purposes by identifying herself as a common-sense, organized, white middle-class housewife rather than the assertive, wealthy business director that she really was. She helped sell gelatine by appealing, as a peer, to her customers. She also seemed to be less threatening to the vast majority of men and women who still lived within the prevailing gender roles of the time. Those who might object to her power, she disarmed by her approach, leading a reporter to distinguish her “as far as possible from the type known as the ‘aggressive feminist.’”

From “Rose Knox: How One Woman Mixed Kitchen and Boardroom to Remold an American Business,” by Rachel Greenfield (Three Hills/Cornell University Press).◆

Picture This

Tessa Blackham’s new picture book, The Ribbon, tells the story of a dedicated young ballet dancer paralyzed by stage fright. When an older dancer hears her sniffling behind the costume rack, she quells the girl’s fears and gives her a red ribbon.

The performance is thrilling.

Years later, that little girl is grown up and has a chance to pass along those same encouraging words and the red ribbon to another new dancer.

This gentle book relies almost entirely on the repetition and progression of its expressive illustrations. Only a handful of words — including the repeated phrase “Time to go” — appear in these pages.

But the drama of the rehearsals, the excitement of the performance, and the kindness of the dancers play out with lovely clarity in Blackham’s soft-focus illustrations, which were created with charcoal, watercolor, and pressed leaves.

The right little reader will hear the music in The Ribbon.◆

This Week’s Poem

In response to last week’s item about pie, Arden Levine sent me a copy of this poem, published in her chapbook Ladies’ Abecedary.

In our home, we’ve got a new response to stress: “Roll dough.”

The first Sunday after Thanksgiving

she opens a beer and tells everyone

about this year’s pies, about how most of the customers
wanted cherry and not the usual pumpkin or apple,

and how orders flew in like insects through open windows.
Then she says that late Wednesday night she dropped a tray of eight,

watched them shatter their faces against the kitchen tiles, spill ruby innards.

What did you do then? everyone asks.
Roll dough. She places that reply so casually down,

as if she had described knocking over a cup of water in her sleep
and waking to find the floor already near dry; this, and not a story

of toil in making, toil in cleaning, toil in remaking, mourning loss.
Anyway, she claims to have liked the second batch better:

smooth, clean edges. Full.

From “Ladies’ Abecedary.” Copyright © 2021 by Arden Levine. Small Harbor Publishing, 2021. Reprinted with permission of the author.◆
