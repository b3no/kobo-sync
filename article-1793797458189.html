The Worst Girls’ Night Of My Life

At 22, I finally had my first opportunity to go on a girls' night out with cool older women. Surely I wouldn't screw it up.

I haven’t been on many girls’ nights in my life. This is partly because my OCD has, for most of my adult life, made me reluctant to go out and do things without my family (and yes, I am working on it) but also because (probably for good reason) groups of women have not been eager to include me. I’ve done quite a few one-on-one drinks with individual women, but the amount of times I’ve gone on a “girls’ night,” past college, is genuinely less than five.

This true story is about the worst of those five.

I met Jessica after my feet gave out at the Union Street Fair in San Francisco, having reached a level of wear and tear I can only describe as “mangled.” I was wearing five-inch wooden platform heels (most likely from Guess or Bebe), not anticipating that I would be standing, walking and drinking copious amounts of warm vodka for at least five hours. Jessica, a cool older girl (I was twenty-one, she was twenty-seven) hung out with me inside her mostly empty office building while I tried to bandage my feet up with cocktail napkins. 

That day at the Union Street Fair, I was still in college, living across the country, and was only there to visit my boyfriend (now husband) Nick, who had graduated before me and moved out to San Francisco for work. Nick knew Jessica through friends. When I arrived in San Francisco that week, I was a neon magenta bodycon dresses in a sea of Patagonia vests and dirty looks. Sometimes I knew I looked a bit ridiculous, like when we went to a Stanford football game tailgate, and I showed up in a blue and yellow corset with low-rise skinny jeans and stripper heels. (To make matters worse: yellow and blue were the colors of the opposing team. Nick quickly rushed me to the Stanford gift shop to buy me a giant Stanford T-shirt.)

With plans of moving to San Francisco as soon as I graduated, I knew it wouldn’t be hard to alter my attire to fit in. (Surely, I could afford a white T-shirt and jeans!) But I didn’t really want to. I hated the anti-fashion component of being a woman in San Francisco, I liked looking like a C-list Kardashian-inspired club rat, and as much as I wanted people to like me, I had hope that I could accomplish this without changing my style or my personality. As I hung out with Jessica that day, bandaging my feet in her office, I kept expecting her to say something about how out of place I looked, or even just ask me if I was cold. (I was, but did not want to be asked if I was.) But she didn’t. She liked me, and I couldn’t figure out why.

I shouldn’t have been so shocked that Jessica met the low bar of behaving like a normal person, but being a neurotic and petty person myself, I tended not to assume the best of people. I got threatened and jealous easily, so why wouldn’t she? I assumed the worst in people, so why wouldn’t she? (And I was doing it to her!) But Jessica, despite carrying herself nothing like me, and being six years my senior, did not seem to judge me. In fact, she seemed to like me. And she was exactly the kind of person who woudn’t typically like me. She was everything I wasn’t—independent, with lots of friends and a thriving career. As someone who regularly measured my waist/hip ratio and stored it in an Excel pivot table, I was intrigued.

Jessica was an accomplished advertising executive. I looked up her job on Glassdoor, only to discover she made at least $100K, which to me might as well have been, Dr. Evil voice, one hundred billion dollars. As for her social circle, we ran into seemingly two hundred people at the Union Street Fair that day who were close to Jessica, all of whom seemed incredibly cool and mature.

When I graduated and moved to San Francisco permanently, Nick told me I should text Jessica and try to make her my first real adult friend. I did, and we all met up at a few parties. She might have been grown-up and successful, but she loved nightlife just as much as I did, if not more, and she was socially connected enough to always have a fun place to go.

I was about to celebrate my twenty-second birthday. I had always struggled to get people to attend my birthday parties, even as a child, so I wasn’t sure why I thought I’d be able to amass a group in a new city where I quite literally had no friends, but I attempted nonetheless. I told Nick to invite his pals–-whoever liked me enough to tolerate me for a night—to a place called Vortex which had already become my favorite club in the city. We knew the bouncer at Vortex, who always let us cut the line and would funnel us through the back of the club like the club scene in Goodfellas. We never once actually bought anything at Vortex, but we aspired to do it at some point once our budget reached the “no longer buying discarded fish gills in Chinatown” tier.

 Nick encouraged me to invite Jessica, even though it was unlikely she would come on such short notice. And much to my surprise, she came, nonchalantly wearing that same outfit she wore when we met–-teal jacket and flats with her hair in a casual ponytail. This successful, grown-up woman with lots of her own friends attended a last-minute birthday party of an insane twenty-two-year-old in a Wet Seal bandage dress. She even told me she hated Vortex, so she had come specifically because she wanted to see me. 

I had no idea what she liked about me. I got the sense that I was a bit of a novelty for her, but not in a way that felt predatory or mean. I just for the life of me couldn’t figure out what she was getting out of this friendship. Not only did she have a great career, but she had her own well-established social circle, the kind of circle I always dreamed of having, full of similarly grown-up women who I feared would ask her why the hell she wanted to hang out with me. She had a social calendar that seemed incredibly full, and yet she was going out of her way to make room for me.

After we had been friends for about six months, Jessica invited me on a girls’ night out with the rest of her friend group. This was simply not something I did–I was glued to Nick. Two years of cross-country long distance and debilitating OCD made me extremely codependent. To some extent, Nick was also traumatized by our extreme long distance, so we enabled this tendency, hoarding time with each other as if we were starving island castaways hoarding food after being rescued. Any minute I spent without him felt like a waste, but Nick encouraged me to go. He said it would be good for me.

I couldn’t tell how much of my anxiety was about spending a night away from Nick, or about how unlikely it was that I would pass the Socially Acceptable test with all of Jessica’s friends, without Nick to console me if I failed. But I pushed myself to go.

Because I didn’t want Jessica’s friends (who I had only met a couple times in passing) to judge me, I wore the classiest dress I had. This was a plum-colored, ruched infinity dress from Victoria’s Secret that could seemingly be used as anything from a mini dress to an off-shoulder dress to, I assume, some kind of wizard cloak. I arranged the fabric strips in a way that made it a midi-length, low back cocktail dress. Yes, this would be revealing by many people’s standards, but by my standards it was a dignified getup befitting Dame Maggie Smith. We would all be having dinner at a restaurant/club known for having cross-dressing waiters. The entire interior was white and modern, the ambiance swanky but sexually liberated, tongue-in-cheek without being deliberately goofy. Peak Millennial sexless-yet-oversexed-core. BDSM themed bacon cake pop food truck, you know what I mean. It was “Hospital Night,” so all the waitstaff dressed as slutty nurses and carried around giant neon syringes of alcohol to inject into people’s mouths. So this was the kind of thing modern twentysomething women did, not those sticky-floored dive bar parties I attended in college, where Katy Perry’s “I Kissed a Girl” blasted on the speakers and everyone peed outside.

I sat with Jessica and her friends in what can only be described as a giant bed with a table in the middle of it. We all lounged around on what I feared was layers upon layers of dried semen and ordered pad thai and cocktails. I noticed that Jessica’s friends were ordering from the menu with abandon, opting for $50 scorpion bowls and $15 syringe shots. I didn’t want to be a buzzkill, but I knew Nick would be furious if I dropped hundreds of dollars on one dinner with friends. I simply did not have the kind of money these women had, and I feared they made the miscalculation of assuming my parents were supporting me. I ordered a small noodle dish and a glass of wine (I had gotten drunk at home on Popoff’s vodka before arriving and chased it with chocolate milk, to save money, naturally) and asked Jessica if it was okay if I could have a separate check. Her friend Fiona, a regular Coachella attendee and frequent molly-taker in her late twenties, overheard and shouted “Oh, okay, so if I give you a sip of my wine, can you spot me twenty-five cents?” Ugh, I had outed myself as the cheap college girl. I would have to do something–anything–to redeem myself if I wanted to get in with this group.

The lights suddenly switched off and a spotlight hovered over a particularly svelte cross-dressing MC in a patent leather white mini dress with a pink nurse’s hat atop a bright purple bob wig. “Hello all you sexy nurses and doctors!” he said. “Tonight is Hospital Night and to show you all how much we want you to get better–” (at this point some DJ booth played a canned moaning noise) “--We’re gonna treat you right with some medicine from your very own Nurse Tittyfuck.” At that point, he began to prance around the stage, briefly stopping to pole dance against a cement column and perform cunnilingus on the air. “Our first contest is none other than a drinking contest, so who wants to join!?”

A few people from the audience got up to join the drinking contest, which involved sitting on their knees and fellating hollow dildos full of alcohol. Seemed simple enough, as long as nobody took a picture of you and made it part of a LinkedIn post about resiliency. Jessica’s friends were loving this. I suddenly regretted not volunteering to go, because at the very least those people got free alcohol, and I was already starting to sober up. I had initially thought that I would need to act reserved around these women, but they were actually far more liberated than I was, even if they didn’t dress revealingly. They liked this Nurse Tittyfuck shit! I needed to get involved if I wanted them to like me too!

“Now for our next contest, we need some volunteers!” Nurse Tittyfuck said. “Who’s gonna be my sexy nurse or doctor tonight? Anyone?”

I immediately raised my hand, eager to enter some kind of drinking contest, and was escorted down to the main stage by another waiter dressed as a sexy nurse, albeit far less convincingly than Nurse Tittyfuck. I couldn’t believe I was doing this. This felt almost like the time I went skiing in high school and couldn’t stop thinking about the fact that I, someone who would not normally ski, was skiing. Sure, I liked to be the center of attention but not like this. This was delightfully out of character for me. I even got anxiety when magicians asked for audience volunteers! I kept my eyes on the prize. Surely there would be a free jello shot in this somehow and my nerves might ease up a bit if I had more to drink. Perhaps it would be some contest that wouldn’t be humiliating and that I’d actually be good at. Perhaps a “best impression of regional British accents” contest, the prize for which would be a delightful bottle of sauvignon blanc?

Nurse Tittyfuck clarified things. “This next contest is a clothing optional booty shaking contest!” 

The audience went wild. I strained my eyes to see how Jessica and her friends were reacting. Of course, they were cackling. I had to find a way to participate in this contest so that they would think I was fun, but not so much that they’d think I was insane. Was there any socially appropriate, likable way to engage in a clothing-optional booty shaking contest? Dear God. If anyone was badly suited for such a predicament, it was me.

There were three of us in the contest. Me, an average-looking straight guy, and a five-foot-tall thirtysomething married woman whose husband had encouraged her to go onstage. I had no idea what I was doing, but with these odds, there was a decent chance I’d actually win. 

The man went first, and he was a predictably terrible dancer, but did eventually take off his shirt and do the worm, attempting to hump the ground like Channing Tatum in Magic Mike. People laughed and clapped for him and he got to leave the stage having solidified himself with his friend group as a Fun Guy. Good for him. But he wasn’t sexy, and he wasn’t going to win.

I was next. I was not intimidated by the man who had just mopped the floor with his sweaty happy trail. I figured if he got applause for doing something that wasn’t remotely sexy, I could probably get applause for being only a little bit sexy while keeping my dignity intact. Pony began playing. I danced the way I might dance with Nick at a club, gyrating and looking over my shoulder with a smoldering stare at the audience, briefly running my hands over my waist and hips but stopping short of groping myself. 

I heard boos. Jeers. The music stopped.

“This is a clothing optional booty shaking contest!” The MC screeched. “What are you doing!?”

“Dancing?” I giggled. I put my hands over my eyes like some kind of cartoon panda.

“Yeah, sure, but we want sex!”

The audience began chanting “Sex! Sex! Sex!” 

“I’m sorry, I would take off my top but it’s actually a dress and I’m just wearing a thong and no bra.” 

“Even better!” the MC said, and the audience hooted and cheered. For a moment, I actually considered just flashing everyone, as it would certainly move public opinion in the right direction, but would that be too much for Jessica and her friends? More importantly, would that be cheating on Nick, in some weird roundabout way that even I didn’t enjoy? I feared that by dancing at all, I had engaged in some sort of infidelity, but surely Nick would appreciate it if I won him a free bottle of champagne. Was that prostitution?

For some reason, instead of just running offstage like any self-respecting booty shaking contest loser would do, I continued to argue with Nurse Tittyfuck. “My boyfriend isn’t here and he doesn’t know I’m doing this,” I said, with an appeal to the traditional sensibilities of the crowd on Sexy Hospital Night. “I feel like actually showing my boobs would be cheating on some level?”

“Oh my God, you have to be kidding me.” Nurse Tittyfuck was breaking character. 

“I’m sorry,” I said. “I didn’t know you were serious when you said clothing optional.” 

I skulked offstage, which was the only thing I did that night that elicited applause from the audience. We sat back and watched while the tiny married woman got completely nude and won a bottle of champagne, only to be rushed away by her husband covering her with his coat. I still think she was a plant.

I looked around the table to gauge how the cool older women felt about me now. Perhaps it wasn’t even the failed booty shaking contest entry, perhaps it was the fact that I didn’t want to pay for other people’s drinks, or perhaps it was something else about me that they didn’t like, but I saw something in the other women’s faces that solidified my suspicions. It had happened, once again. Maybe Jessica liked me–for reasons I couldn’t comprehend–but I was not part of this group, and I never would be. There would be lunch dates and tea dates with Jessica, but never another girls’ night. Unless you count my bachelorette party, which Jessica roped some of these women into attending despite not liking me very much. But that’s a story for another day.
