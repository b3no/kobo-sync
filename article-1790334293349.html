How to kill housing with paperwork

It takes longer to get permits in Boston than to build a house in Phoenix

Milan Singh
Sep 25
∙
Paid
 
READ IN APP
 

In the time it takes for a developer in Boston to get permission to build a house, a developer in Indianapolis has not only secured permits, hired construction workers, and bought materials — they’ve also finished building the house.

Though YIMBYs have long argued that unduly stringent local permitting processes are choking off housing supply in the highest-demand cities, a lack of good, standardized data has made this difficult to demonstrate with hard numbers.

That’s why the debut of a single, centralized database tracking permitting for homebuilding across the country is cause for celebration. In a new paper, Leonardo D’Amico, Evan Soltas, and Audrey Wang were able to collect data on how long it takes for permits to get approved in 60 major cities and calculate the average permitting duration for different types of projects.

Their findings confirm the conventional wisdom: It takes longer to build in cities with slower permitting, and those cities have higher rents and less building. The authors are also able to disentangle just how much permitting contributes to slower homebuilding. When it comes to housing project completion, “30 to 43 percent of the difference between the slowest and fastest cities is due to differences in the time to receive a permit, rather than in the time to actually build,” they wrote.

In the cities with the slowest permitting processes — San Francisco, Miami, and Boston — permitting alone takes longer than the entire construction process takes in the fastest cities (Indianapolis; Little Rock, Arkansas; and Oklahoma City).

Naturally, the longer it takes to get permits, the longer it takes to build an individual house or apartment building. That, in turn, means that it takes longer for the supply of housing to catch up to demand, keeping prices high. And since housing development is often debt-financed, longer project times mean higher interest payments for developers. Developers then pass on those elevated costs to buyers or tenants to make their money back.

The authors’ findings depict a country that, as a whole, appears to be heading in the wrong direction when it comes to the speed of homebuilding. While average total project duration time has fluctuated from year to year, permitting steadily slowed between 2000 and 2023. That dynamic has reversed in recent years, but it’s not clear why permitting durations fell or whether they will continue to do so.

The optimistic explanation is that permitting durations have fallen post-pandemic as a result of state- and local-level YIMBY reforms. The pessimistic view is that even now, permitting still takes longer than it did in 2000.

The housing holdup, by the numbers

It’s no secret that Phoenix is cheaper than Boston. Comparing rents across American cities has long been possible for economists (and noneconomists, thanks to Zillow). But until now, no one could compare permitting time and total construction duration across cities.

If you want to build a single-family home in Phoenix, it takes just a couple of weeks to get the permits and just under 7.5 months to build the house. In Boston, it takes an average of well over a year to get the permits and well over two years to build such a house.

Perhaps unsurprisingly, Phoenix permitted way more total units in 2024 — 7.83 per thousand people, compared to 2.84 for Boston — and had median rents that were $628 cheaper than Boston’s.

The ability to do this cross-city comparison is one of the key innovations of the new paper. Until now, there was no database that collects and standardizes permitting data from across U.S. cities.

Instead, some cities post their permitting data on websites available to the public, and a bunch of others post the data to online permit portals such as Accela, OpenGov, or EnerGov, which are targeted at housing developers.

According to Soltas, the authors used LLMs to scrape these websites and collate data from 60 cities. They were then able to combine that data with information on neighborhood attributes such as income from the American Community Survey. Thanks to the combined data, it’s possible not only to compare project and permitting times in the aggregate but also to compare projects with similar attributes (square footage, number of units, median income) across jurisdictions.

What their data shows is that, when it comes to building single-family homes, Miami, Boston, and San Francisco have the slowest permitting in the country. The fastest cities are Little Rock, Indianapolis, and Omaha, Nebraska.

When it comes to multifamily homes, Miami; Charleston, South Carolina; and Boston are the worst offenders; the fastest cities are Reno, Nevada; Kansas City, Missouri; and Spokane, Washington.

Cities with longer permitting durations have higher rents and permit fewer units per capita. While the paper doesn’t establish causality, this may be because it takes longer for new housing supply to come online. They also have longer total project durations, which are correlated with higher rents and fewer units permitted.

The charts above group all possible project types into “single-family” or “multifamily.” But even within the same jurisdiction, a project with 50 units in a dense, high-income neighborhood can take much longer to get permits than a single-family home in a middle-income, suburban neighborhood can.

Because the new database includes over 1 million individual permit applications, it’s possible to get a much more granular picture of how long any particular type of new development will take in a given city, based on how many units are in it, how big each unit is, total lot size, density, and neighborhood income.

We know that slower permitting means longer total construction times, which means longer waits for new housing, higher project costs, and thus higher rents at the end of the line — though the paper is careful not to posit a direct relationship between permit times and ultimate costs. But longer average permitting times in some cities could be skewed by outliers: Maybe some permits take up to five years to get approved while others move relatively speedily, resulting in a misleading two-year average.

That uncertainty could also slow construction, since developers have a harder time knowing how long their money will be tied up ahead of time.

Permitting has been getting slower, and it’s not clear why

At this point, there’s been over a decade and a half of research and advocacy arguing that land use regulations affect the cost of housing. The YIMBY movement has even scored some notable policy wins, ranging from state Sen. Scott Wiener’s work loosening zoning laws in California to the federal 21st Century ROAD to Housing Act that streamlines environmental reviews and incentivizes states and localities to reform their zoning rules.

This raises the question: Have such efforts had a meaningful impact on how long it takes to get permits for new developments?

According to the data in the new paper, permitting got slower and slower from 2000 to 2020. It got especially slow right after the pandemic, but that might reflect a holdup of applications that couldn’t get processed during COVID. Since the pandemic, permitting times have fallen, at least for single-family projects. But they’re still higher than they were for most of the pre-COVID period.

Total project duration has followed a somewhat different path. From 2005 to 2015, total duration for single-family projects was mostly flat; it rose from 2020 to 2022 and has since been falling. Total duration for multifamily projects has been increasing since 2010 and more or less flat since the pandemic.

What is striking, though, is that from 2010 to 2020 — when real interest rates were zero or close to it — total duration for both single- and multifamily projects increased. Despite a favorable macroeconomic environment, it got harder to build housing.

The total project duration trend since that time could be driven by many factors: immigration supply affecting the number of workers, inflation and tariffs making materials more expensive, interest rates raising the cost of debt, and more. With permitting, there are simply fewer factors at play: Either the written rules have changed, the way those rules are applied has changed, or both.

There are two possible reasons why permitting duration has fallen since 2023. One is that YIMBY reforms in states like California are making a real difference in speeding things up. The less rosy — though more likely, in my view — explanation is that the drop is mostly an artifact of local permitting agencies working through the pandemic backlog. During COVID, most local governments had bigger fish to fry than issuing permits for new housing, so it’s plausible that permit applications piled up, causing a slowdown after the vaccines were released and the economy was reopened.

Even if the accelerated permitting is the result of YIMBY policies, it takes more than a few good reforms to undo decades of bad policy.

A problematic index

One of the major contributions of this paper is to advance the measurement of land use regulations. Historically, the field of housing economics has relied on the Wharton Residential Land Use Regulatory Index to measure the stringency of local land use regulations. First introduced in a 2006 paper by Joseph Gyourko, Albert Saiz, and Anita Summers, the Wharton Index is built around surveys of local government employees from 2005 and 2018, which ask them questions about how zoning works in their locality.

A lot of academic and policymaking work has been built around the Wharton Index. It’s the basis for a wide range of studies on how land use regulations affect residential segregation, social mobility, economic growth, homelessness, carbon emissions, and even restaurant quality. It’s also a key input in the Cato Institute’s state freedom index and was cited by Jason Furman in a speech on land use regulation when he was chair of Obama’s Council of Economic Advisers.

But the index is an imperfect measure of how strict land use regulation actually is.

A recent paper by Scott Markley, Sara C. Bronin, and Matthew Harris criticized its methodology. The paper pointed out that the index’s survey sample only captures a very small slice of local governments. And it observed that the index sometimes imputes scores for major cities like San Francisco and New York from respondents within the same Metropolitan Statistical Area but not actually within the city itself.

The Wharton Index is also expensive to update. Doing so requires fielding another large survey targeted at a very small universe of potential respondents. This makes it difficult to keep the index current with changes in land use regulation: It was most recently updated in 2021, 15 years after the previous update.

Still, it’s worth noting that the Wharton Index’s limitations are largely a product of the tools available to researchers when it was first developed. Today, we have more advanced tools, so it’s possible to build a better set of measurements of land use regulation.

Conceptually, we can think of land use regulation as having two components: de jure (what zoning rules are actually written down, which a new paper by Alexander Bartik, Arpit Gupta, and Daniel Milo aims to measure) and de facto (how those rules are implemented in practice).

One way to measure the de facto regulatory regime is to look at how long it takes to get the permits for a new housing development. And that’s what D’Amico, Soltas, and Wang set out to do in their paper.

“Our work gives housing policy analysts something they always wanted and that other tools can’t quite provide: granular and up-to-date information on how quickly cities approve and build new homes and apartments,” Soltas told me.

For the first time, we now have a database of estimates for how long permitting takes across major American cities. Now, it’s up to policymakers in the slowest cities — looking at you, San Francisco, Miami, and Boston — to speed things up.
