Welcome to The Weekly Scroll!

The Weekly Scroll is a once-a-week roundup of the funniest, most interesting, and strangest stories from around the social internet.

The internet is not just a strange, hyperselected web of people hiding from reality; in many ways, the internet is reality. What happens online shapes real life just as real life shapes what happens online.

Every week, I sift through social media so you don’t have to, collecting the most notable trends, memes, and online dramas in one place. Think of it as a guided tour through the more insane parts of the web, with commentary about what it all means.

If you enjoy internet nonsense but don’t have the time to doomscroll for hours every day, you’re in the right place.

Levent Alpöge is a mathematician working for the AI firm Anthropic, and this week he casually dropped a disproof of an 87-year-old problem in a tweet, thanking his “close friend Fable” for its help on the problem. The Jacobian conjecture isn’t an obscure problem; it was included on a list of “Mathematical problems for the next century” and has puzzled mathematicians for generations.

This isn’t the first time AI models have made important mathematical breakthroughs. Earlier this year, OpenAI announced that one of its models had disproved the unit distance conjecture, and other mathematicians have used AI to solve Erdős problems. But this is the first time I can remember that someone just dropped a major breakthrough (with proof) in a tweet.

Sure, you might have a Ph.D. in pure abstract math and decades of experience in number theory. But have you considered telling your chatbot “Enough time wasting, just do it”? Why not? Are you dumb?

Meanwhile! The machine gods aren’t just getting better at math. They’re also getting better at breaking out of their cages:

“OpenAI said that its artificial intelligence models were behind an ‘unprecedented cyber incident’ that affected the open-source developer platform Hugging Face, rattling researchers across the industry.

The company said a combination of its models GPT‑5.6 Sol and a more capable model that has not yet been released escaped a sandboxed testing environment, accessed the internet and exploited a vulnerability to gain access to Hugging Face’s systems.”

Apparently the story is that OpenAI was specifically testing two of its models’ hacking ability in a protected “sandbox” environment. The models decided that the best way to solve the test’s problems was to break out of the sandbox and hack Hugging Face’s databases, because someone had probably posted the answers there. And it succeeded!

Katie Porter becomes an influencer

Former congresswoman Katie Porter has announced that she’s pivoting away from politics and toward a new future as an Instagram book influencer.

Sure, what the hell. Why not?

Porter, who grew famous for holding whiteboards in congressional hearings, will shift from using her whiteboards to explain the federal budget to something like this:

Longtime readers of Infinite Scroll will know that my foundational thesis of the internet is that “Posting is the Most Powerful Force in the Universe.” The desire to post can lead people to do insane things, commit bizarre crimes, throw away vast amounts of money, and more. Posting is more powerful than sex, more powerful than money, and I guess now we have proof it’s more powerful than actual political power.

Porter was a three-term congresswoman with a fairly high profile. But she was unable to leverage her large social media presence into higher office, failing in her efforts to run for Senate and governor in California.

Frankly, I’m not sure I blame her decision to leave. She’s always been the kind of candidate who was good at social media, with hundreds of thousands of followers on TikTok, Instagram, and Substack. It’s probably way more fun to post about books than to sit in congressional hearings.

But, man, it cannot be good for society that, rather than using social media to jump into politics, people are now using politics as a launching pad for a social media career.

So, what the hell is going on in Korea?

Last January, a female South Korean YouTuber was convicted of stabbing another Korean streamer, reportedly because she believed they were in a relationship, which he denied. This isn’t the first time this kind of thing has happened in Korea: Last year, Korean influencer Yoon Ji-ah was murdered by a male fan who had donated tens of thousands of dollars to her channel.

I was prepared to read these incidents as byproducts of Korea’s uniquely poisonous gender war dynamics, which make the American gender war look like a Hallmark movie. But on July 13, a South Korean financial YouTuber was stabbed by a viewer who had lost money following his stock tips. The viewer apparently tracked the YouTuber’s location, and attacked him in public. And this week, a Korean YouTuber died after ingesting poison live on stream as viewers called emergency services to try to save him.

I wish I could tell you exactly why this seems to be happening so often, but I’ve got as many questions as you do. My best guess is that, for complicated cultural reasons, the parasocial dynamics of social media seem even stronger in South Korea (and Japan) than in other countries. But if any Koreans want to chime in here, your guess is as good as mine.

Has Netflix lost its mojo?

The world’s biggest streaming giant, which one year ago was flirting with an all-time-high stock price over $130, closed this week around $70. Netflix is still growing, but that growth has slowed, and new data shows that Netflix has major problems retaining viewers for its most popular shows into the second season. According to Netflix’s own data, shows are losing between 30% and 70% of their audiences between seasons 1 and 2.

It’s not a mystery why these shows suffered massive drops. Most of them had a multiyear gap between seasons, and it’s hard to maintain excitement for a show with gaps that big (especially when “seasons” are often now just six to eight episodes). And I don’t think it’s an accident that some of the most discourse-generating and buzziest shows, like The Pitt, actually follow a tight schedule (the first season of The Pitt debuted in January 2025; the second season kicked off in January 2026).

Netflix has also become increasingly tight-lipped about its financial metrics over the past few years. It stopped reporting quarterly subscriber metrics, and regional revenue per user in 2025, and, in the wake of disappointing results this month, it has announced it will no longer report viewership numbers biannually, instead reporting annually.

This is a bold new corporate communications strategy I would summarize as, “Things are going great. Please stop asking for evidence.”

The hits keep coming: Last month, YouTube overtook Netflix in average daily viewing, which probably explains Netflix’s desperate forays into podcasts, gaming, and live events. And beyond all the numbers, the vibes just seem terrible. The Reddit comments on articles about Netflix are almost uniformly negative, and major media analysts are leading with articles like “Is Netflix Washed Now?”

It would be easy to overstate this. Netflix is still growing, still highly profitable, and still by far the largest streaming service in the world. But its cultural power seems to be waning, and the company sure looks like one that realizes it’s becoming the streaming equivalent of Soylent and is scrambling to stay relevant.
