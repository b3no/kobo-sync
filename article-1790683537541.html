America Has a Pit Bull Problem

When must we sterilize them?

50% of the proceeds of new annual subscriptions from this article (for the next 12 months) will be donated to Slow the Flow, a local charity in the Cleveland OH region that provides free sterilization for pit bulls.

I would have chosen a more national charity, but the national charities that sterilize dogs (ASPCA) are also deeply invested in the “rehabilitation of the pit bull image” which contributes to dangerous and aggressive dogs being placed with families and young children. (The reason I am limiting this to annual subscriptions is that I cannot tell how long a monthly subscription will run for a year.)

A horrifying and tragic story broke on my Twitter feed from a well-known Twitter user. I’ll keep the story somewhat brief and anonymized since I did not get a response from the OP about whether they would want to be interviewed: A gentle, elderly dog was mauled to death by a pit bull in a hotel lobby. There were several facts of this story which illustrated that the tragedy was maddeningly preventable: the pit bull was unleashed, the owner was faking a service dog designation with a service dog vest, the owner knew the dog had bitten other dogs before, this owner had a history of adopting aggressive dogs who had been euthanized as a results of their attacks, and she was a regular in the pet-friendly hotel who likely knew about her antics and were familiar with the dog, allowing it to roam free.

And as horrifying as this attack was, it could have been worse. Children have been mauled to death by unleashed dogs, almost always by pit bulls, and perhaps most troublingly, often by pit bulls that belong to their own families—not stray dogs and dogs raised for fighting. Say “it’s the owner” all you want, but I find it hard to believe this nice suburban Memphis family were abusing their pit bull, who mauled the mother while killing the two children after being perfectly pleasant for years. At a certain point, we have to admit that something is wrong with this breed and resist attempts to “rehabilitate” its image, which as far as I’m concerned, is well-earned at this point if not undersold.

While it would be neither humane nor practical to euthanize all pit bulls (I am not suggesting this!) there are plenty of other, far more ethical policies and actions which could be done in America. The problem is, nobody is doing anything, because it is still unpopular (albeit less so lately) to admit that something is wrong with pit bulls and related breeds.

The first time I wrote about the tyranny of ill-equipped and coddled dog owners, I made it breed-agnostic. My main issue was not only with aggressive dogs, but rule-flouting in general (and yes, I’m a lover of rules. A Karen, if you will.) I noticed that after covid, many inexperienced dog owners were adopting dogs far too large for cramped apartment environments, not leashing them, calling them “emotional support animals” and bringing them places where they were explicitly barred. This article gave me a bit of a reputation as someone who “hates dogs.”

But I don’t hate dogs. I don’t love them enough to have one of my own, but I’m often very happy to pet a friend’s dog. I grew up with a dog I loved dearly. I obviously care more about humans than about dogs, but I don’t hate dogs. I’m realizing that my problem is more specific than “dogs,” and honestly, even more specific even than “dogs whose owners aren’t following rules.” The problem is pit bulls.

No, I don’t particularly love having an unleashed yellow lab slobber all over me at a cafe, but realistically that poses no danger. Pit bulls, on the other hand, are basically domesticated tigers walking around town, capable of snapping at any minute (even turning on their owners, for seemingly no reason at all) and we all have to pretend they pose the exact same threat as a golden retriever.

I spoke to a dad the other day who told me he didn’t think much about pit bulls at all until he had children, and now he is constantly head-swiveling for them in public. He saw some stories of pit bull attacks in the news, and realized just how disproportionately dangerous pit bulls are even if the base rate of these attacks is low. While all dogs bite, pits make up the vast majority of dog attacks that end in death or serious injury, especially involving children. Perhaps most troublingly, they can even turn on their own families out of nowhere, even after living with them for years, which is antithetical to what a “guard dog” is supposed to do. I have heard of other dog breeds attacking people who they believe were a danger to their families. It’s rare that I ever hear about a non-pit bull killing a child who has lived with it for years.

Many parents can relate to this anxiety. If I see a pit bull in the general vicinity of my family, even if it’s on a leash, we leave. (The leash is often no comfort to me when the owner is scrolling his phone and blithely walking around, mouth agape, not clearly conscious or sentient.) If any relative of mine were to adopt a pit bull, I would not visit them anymore. This sounds extreme, and it can be a bit disruptive, but these dogs are not like other dogs. They are exceptionally strong, and even leashed, they can escape their owner’s clutch to attack a child at random, as one did on the streets of Manhattan last year.

And if you care about dogs, nobody is more at risk than other dogs. Earlier this year, a beloved pet dog was mauled to death at the Tacoma City Marathon, by none other than a pit bull who was wearing an “adopt me” vest as part of a shelter outreach program. I’m always shocked, but I’m never surprised.

Whenever I talk about these attacks, someone is quick to tell me that they are uncommon, and therefore nobody should care about them. They’ll remind me, smugly, that car crashes are far more dangerous to children. You’ll find no disagreement here, and I don’t think anyone on Earth believes that pit bulls kill more children than car accidents. The problem is that cars, dangerous as they may be, also confer tremendous benefit to our society. Most people can’t get around without a car. You can argue that dogs confer benefits, and they absolutely do, but they do not need to be pit bulls. There is quite literally no reason to raise a pit bull if another breed exists—there are plenty of “guard dog” breeds which are less likely to randomly snap and kill you. We also have plenty of laws designed to make cars safer. You need to learn to drive and obtain a license before owning and operating a car. You could not manufacture a car without seatbelts or air bags. You are held liable for putting your children in a car without proper restraints. Children are required to be in car seats or boosters, sometimes into their teens depending on the state. Cars, because they are more dangerous than other types of transportation, are subject to more rules (there is no law requiring you to use a seatbelt on a bus, or use a carseat in a train.) But in America, pit bulls are generally not beholden to laws which recognize their unique degree of risk.

If cars vanished overnight, society would melt down. If all pit bulls were sterilized en masse and the breed died out in the next ten years or so, nothing bad would happen.

This is what they did in England in 1991 via the Dangerous Dogs Act. The UK government made it illegal to buy, sell or breed any dog that was part of a dangerous breed meant for fighting, which initially included four breeds—pit bulls included—updated to five in 2024 with the import of the American Bully XL. The law also has breed-agnostic laws about owners who allow dogs to be “out of control.” The law mandates that existing dogs that belonged to these dangerous breeds be sterilized, microchipped, tracked and muzzled. When I first heard about this law, I assumed they went door-to-door, saying, “Oi mate, you have a loicense for that dog?” but not so! The law did not require mass euthanasia, pet confiscation, or anything else horrifying.

Critics of the 1991 act cite that dog attacks have actually risen in the UK over the past few years, and deaths remained fairly constant (albeit low) from 1991 to today. But a few missing pieces of information here: the overall dog population grew from 1991 to 2026, so if deaths remained constant, that meant that death risk per dog went down over time. Remember, 1991 did not mark the end of all pit bulls, but the banning of their sale and breeding. Many of the dogs involved in the attacks post-1991 were still pit bulls, so if anything the argument is that the law was not enforced strongly enough, not that pit bulls were harmless. Some of these owners were breeding pit bulls illegally, along with other bull-type breeds such as the Staffordshire bull terrier, which wasn’t included in the 1991 ban but should have been.

Second, the American import breed, the Bully XL, was not covered by the 1991 law and surged in popularity after covid. Although deaths from dog attacks still remained low, they tripled to quadrupled during the time that this breed became popular, and then decreased again after they were banned in 2024.

People who read the tragic story of the family dog killed by an unleashed pit bull in a hotel lobby naturally felt infuriated, and demanded that “all pit bulls be euthanized.” But that’s not going to happen. People love their dogs, Americans love our civil liberties, and exterminating a breed through violent means is neither ethical nor plausible.

But we can make it illegal to sell or breed these dogs. We can mandate sterilization for them, at least at the local or state level. We can ban them from being service dogs or emotional support animals. We can stop insisting that it’s “dog racism” to understand the dangers of this breed. We can stop pushing young and uninformed families to adopt dangerous dogs. These things may not be perfect, but right now the only course of action being taken in America is, as someone in the UK might say, “fuck all.”

We can also enforce laws that already exist! Every time I go to the park, which has a big sign warning owners that dogs must be leashed, every dog is off-leash. I’ve called the non-emergency line a few times about this, and they’ve simply redirected me to the voicemail of animal control, who does nothing. I’ve even called 911, and they never show up. And while most of these dogs are relatively “safe” breeds, I recall a time that an unleashed pit bull was allowed to—and I’m not joking about this—run unleashed through a child’s play structure as its owner played fetch with it, at a park where no dogs were allowed, leash or not. Perhaps worse, none of the parents seemed especially pressed except for me, and they laughed at me when I immediately grabbed my children and left.

And yes, it’s entirely possible a dog who isn’t a pit bull, but kind of looks like one, will fall under a law banning the breeding of the dog or mandating sterilization. So? Literally, who cares? If the law simply requires that this dog be sterilized and not bred, is that really such a big deal? Does your pit bull-looking-but-possibly-not-a-pit bull dog really need to fuck? Most dog owners should be sterilizing and leashing their dogs anyway. And besides, it’s pretty interesting that the pit bull breed is apparently impossible to identify, or doesn’t exist at all, which would eliminate the possibility of targeted laws, but magically the pit bull becomes a real and identifiable thing as soon as it’s time to “destigmatize” the breed and talk about how great with kids they are—the original “nanny dog” as we’ve been told repeatedly.

As it turns out, that claim came out of thin air in 1971, and the man considered to be the grandfather of the breed knew they were not nanny dogs—his own pit bull killed his two-year-old nephew.

That’s another thing we can stop doing—lying about the true nature of these animals. Shelters should prioritize the safety of humans—especially young children—over aggressive dogs with a documented history of aggression. While it’s unfortunate that a dog could go without a home, shelters should not be deliberately mislabeling likely pit bulls as “lab mixes” or “terrier mixes” to attract families. In fact, earlier this year, a seven-year-old boy was killed by his family pit bull, which a rescue activist group had pushed onto the family, reassuring them about its safety, despite a paper trail of the dog being extremely aggressive to other dogs at the shelter. (The family was not informed of this history.) And what’s worse—nobody involved in the deception faced any charges or consequences. Typically, when someone adopts an animal, they sign a contract which releases the shelter of any responsibility. This is great motivation for shelters to lie about dogs’ aggression to get them into homes.

As terrified as some parents may be of unleashed dogs in public, a statistic worth understanding is that the majority of fatal dog attacks on children involve their own family dogs (like the above story) or dogs of another family member or family friend. All the more reason not to “rehabilitate” a dangerous dog breed, lie to families about the dog they are adopting, and guilt families (or prospective parents) into “adopting and not shopping” when the shelters are overloaded with aggressive dogs. Also, all the more reason to be aware of aggressive breeds and not allow your child to be alone with one, even for a short period of time.

There hasn’t been much serious legislation in the US regarding aggressive dogs, but there has been a great deal of activism to guilt people into adopting pit bulls. A few cities have bans in place, but that’s about it. Denver had a pit bull ban in place from 1989 (which brought their overall dog attack deaths down to zero for multiple decades) but this ban was overturned in (big shocker) 2020. Despite the mayor vetoing the overturning of the ban, citing safety concerns for children, he was overruled by the city council.

Given that pit bull advocates love to point out that “it’s the owner, not the dog,” we should probably talk about the type of people who adopt these dogs. Despite the fact that people use this issue as a cudgel for their racial, political or gender-related animus, you cannot pin the pit bull problem on any demographic. Lots of people love these dogs. People of color, white people, men, women, Republicans, Democrats, and so on. The stereotypes I’ve seen have been “inner city thug” and “white liberal woman with suicidal empathy” but it’s just not that cut-and-dry. Some of the most ardent pit bull supporters I’ve seen have been white conservative men. These dogs are everywhere, and cannot be generalized as a pet project (no pun intended) for a particular group of people.

The main problem with pit bull owners, as far as I see it, is that they fall into two categories, neither of whom should own such a dog. The first category is the antisocial “tough guy” who adopts a pit bull to look edgy and isn’t particularly worried about the safety risk it may pose. The second category—who I encounter far more often in the wild—is the gullible (or at least misinformed) rube who genuinely believes a pit bull poses the same risk to children as a beagle. This person may care about the safety of other people, but doesn’t think their dog is part of the problem. They may not even have a specific affinity for pit bulls, but they wanted a shelter dog as opposed to a dog from a breeder, and the shelters were overrun with shitty dogs. They might believe their dog is a “lab mix.”

Then, you have a weird combination of the two types—the type of person featured in the story at the hotel lobby. These are people who likely have oppositional defiance disorder combined with a savior complex, whose identity revolves around “rescuing” and “rehabilitating” dogs who cannot safely exist in public. If other people think their dogs are dangerous, they revel in being “stigmatized” so they can feel like a hero. They love the idea of owning a “misunderstood” dog. They will talk about pit bull stereotypes with the same fervor as one might discuss racial or sexual orientation discrimination, right up until their dog kills someone. In fact, there is an entire subreddit dedicated to these people.

Technically, there is one type of person who would be qualified to own a pit bull—a seasoned dog owner who is acutely aware of the danger these dogs pose and dedicated to keeping them away from children. I have known exactly one person like this—a guy in our neighborhood whose two rescue pit bulls were always leashed and harnessed. If he saw us with our children, he would immediately cross the street. Unfortunately, most pit bull owners are not him.

Given that pit bulls are not strictly owned by a small group of wackos, it’s incredibly difficult to talk openly about the risk they pose. Many pit bull owners truly don’t think their dogs are dangerous, and they believe the world is full of evil Canine Nazis with their evil dog-racist propaganda. And most people just aren’t thinking about this issue at all. Many parents I’ve spoken to have told me that the dangers of pit bulls (or any dogs) didn’t cross their minds until a serious or fatal attack happened in their town, or until they had a close call themselves.

And if breed-specific laws are a problem, let’s meet the activists halfway: criminal liability for owners of any dog who seriously injures another person. These laws already exist to some extent, and they differ by state. But a few problems exist. First, most of these laws require a dog to have a prior incident before the owner faces any consequences for a new incident. Second, many of the owners whose pit bulls attack out of nowhere genuinely thought their dog was safe. Owners who are aware of their dog's aggression may be pushed to be more cautious if they know they can be jailed for manslaughter if someone dies, but owners who think their pit bull is a misunderstood angel will not believe that these laws apply to them.

Still, sometimes the law works, although it’s cold comfort to anyone who has lost a loved one. Earlier this year, a babysitter pled guilty to child endangerment when she left a one-year-old child she was babysitting alone with her teenage daughter and two pit bulls, who mauled the child to death. She’s serving fourteen years in prison, but it’s unclear what the charge would have been if this attack happened while she was home—the case relied on her negligently leaving the house.

No legislation will ever be perfect. But this is not a reason to abandon the issue entirely, especially when mandates around things like leashing, sterilization, and breeding are completely humane and do not require confiscating people’s beloved pets. But in order to acknowledge that this is a problem, we have to notice the fact that pit bulls really are disproportionately dangerous. And even if they weren’t—if it was truly an issue of the “owner”—then we’ll have to agree that some of the worst possible pit bull owners are adopting pit bulls, either apathetic to the danger they pose or oblivious to it.
