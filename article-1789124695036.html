We’re still underrating 9/11’s impact

Flukey events and eccentric decisions changed the world for the worse forever.

Matthew Yglesias
Sep 11
∙

It was an incredibly bright and clear East Coast morning on September 11, 2001. I’ve always been an early riser, so even as a college student, I was awake and at breakfast when I heard a plane had hit the World Trade Center.

As for the rest of that morning, I don’t totally trust my own memory. But I do want to emphasize that quite a lot of us did literally watch thousands of people die on live television as the towers collapsed. And those who didn’t see it live saw it replayed endlessly in the hours and days that followed.

I don’t think anyone who didn’t experience 9/11 as an adult can fully understand how confusing that day was.

First it was one plane, then it was two. The first plane could’ve been an accident, but a second clearly wasn’t. The Pentagon was hit, but there were also false alarms about fires at the State Department and on the National Mall. The Capitol building was hastily evacuated. It was widely suspected early on that al-Qaeda was responsible, but of course nobody knew that for sure. So we were watching, and it was horrible.

And for all we knew, more buildings were about to blow up. A few weeks later, there was an anthrax scare at M.I.T. and an anonymous threat at the Harvard campus libraries. Researchers find that there was a pronounced increase in vacancy rates at the three most iconic office buildings in Chicago because people feared other landmark buildings would be attacked. It was a crazy, panicky time.

A few days after the attack, George W. Bush went to an Islamic center to reassure the country that Islam is a religion of peace and to try to tamp down on an incipient wave of hate crimes, many of which were mistakenly directed at Sikhs.

But while Bush displayed statesmanship and steady leadership on this front, his administration also moved the country helter-skelter into a war in Afghanistan that failed in its main objectives and into a war in Iraq that had nothing to do with the attack.

Critics are often reluctant to admit it now, but these were overwhelmingly popular moves at the time. Invading Iraq had 70 percent approval in March and April of 2003. Which of course doesn’t mean that a “let’s invade Iraq” sentiment spontaneously materialized in the minds of hundreds of millions of people as a result of what we saw on television two years before.

But it was an emotionally distressing time, and the range of possible outcomes was extremely wide.

If Bush had wanted to whip people up into an inward-looking demagogic fervor instead of an external-facing one, he clearly could have accomplished that, too. War on Terror 1 was crazy, and people were prepared to be led in all kinds of directions.

It’s on some levels obvious that we are all very much living in a world that Osama bin Laden made. But on others I think it’s still somewhat underrated how deeply contingent everything was.

An avoidable event

I think appreciation of the contingency starts with observing that the whole idea of hijacking airplanes in order to destroy the World Trade Center is eccentric.

We know Khalid Sheikh Mohammed, named as “the principal architect of the 9/11 attacks” by the official 9/11 Commission Report, is also believed by American intelligence to have been involved in the earlier 1993 effort to destroy the World Trade Center (that time with a truck bomb). So given our knowledge at the time that a group of violent Islamists thought destroying the World Trade Center was a good idea, the emergence of a new plot to do it was fairly predictable. But the details are still pretty weird.

Even if you see the rise of these kinds of jihadist movements as baked into the cake, orchestrating spectacular attacks on American civilians is not an obvious move.

I think that line of thinking gets occluded a little by right-wing types talking about a clash of civilizations and by people on the left talking about terrorism as “blowback” for America’s foreign policy sins. But Latin American leftists, who have a much more deeply entrenched beef with the U.S. government, have never done anything remotely like 9/11. And even though violent Islamist groups are very much still with us in the world, they seem to have largely abandoned this particular tactic. Part of how the Taliban came back to power in Afghanistan was that they credibly convinced the Trump and Biden administrations that they were no longer interested in hosting trans-national terrorist organizations.

And even in retrospect, the relationship between knocking down the towers and Osama bin Laden’s ideological goals is a little hard to parse. He was angry about America’s military presence in Saudi Arabia and our support for “apostate” regimes and Israel. But obviously the political reaction in the United States wasn’t to become less involved in the Middle East or just say, “Well, I guess we should let the people who did the terrorism take over the whole region like they want.”

In other places, he says his goal was to drag the United States into an endless unwinnable war in Afghanistan and bleed us dry economically. That kind of worked, but America is really rich, so it didn’t actually work.

The biggest win for al-Qaeda that resulted from 9/11 is that the invasion of Iraq created a huge new opening for Sunni jihadist operations in the western part of the country and contributed to destabilizing Syria, which created even more new opportunities. In the end, of course, ISIS was defeated and Syria’s al-Qaeda offshoot, like the Taliban itself, gained power in part by disavowing international terrorism.

There’s even more contingency in the fact that it’s easy to imagine worlds in which the attack was stopped.

The Afghan anti-Taliban commander Ahmad Shah Massoud tried to warn American leaders about a spectacular al-Qaeda plot. When Massoud was killed by suicide bombers on September 9, Vladimir Putin called George W. Bush with a similar warning. Zacarias Moussaoui, who likely planned to participate in 9/11, was already in custody, but the F.B.I. couldn’t get a warrant to search his computer. The C.I.A. was aware that Khalid al-Mihdhar and Nawaf al-Hazmi were terrorists, but they were allowed into the country — in al-Mihdhar’s case, multiple times. By August of 2001, the F.B.I. had decided they should actually find these guys, but according to the Justice Department Inspector General’s office, “even in August, the F.B.I.’s search for Mihdhar and Hazmi was not given any urgency or priority, and was not close to locating them by the time of the attacks.” Attorney General John Ashcroft issued a priorities memo earlier in 2001 that did not include any counterterrorism objectives.

I don’t want to exaggerate how easy it would have been to unravel the plot. Massoud was obviously a self-interested party in trying to get America to take this more seriously. Putin’s call was too late to do anything about it. It’s not obvious that a different priorities memo would have led to a more intensive August manhunt.

But certainly the attack could have been prevented.

It wasn’t a black swan event or the work of true geniuses who evaded America’s best efforts. The Bush administration’s response — and especially its decision to frame the problem in grandiose ideological terms — functioned in part to direct focus away from the banal law enforcement, intelligence, and border security failures. And the 1993 precedent shows that even though trying and failing to murder thousands of people is just as immoral as succeeding, the emotional response of the public to the failed attack was wildly different and the intellectual and political response in turn was extremely muted. A foiled 9/11 would have generated some kind of policy response, but not nearly the one we got.

A stack of contingencies

One reason I think today we should be skeptical that bin Laden got exactly the response he wanted out of the United States is that he could not have forecast George W. Bush’s response when the planning began before Bush was even elected. That race, which was razor thin and ended up hinging on factors as trivial as the design of the Palm Beach County ballot, was incredibly contingent.

I don’t really know how an Al Gore administration would have responded to 9/11. I doubt that Republicans and right-wing media would have been as kind to Gore in the wake of an attack as Democrats and the mainstream press were to Bush. I also think it’s possible that with Clinton-Gore continuity and without the early Bush-era decision to downplay terrorism as a priority, the attacks actually would have been foiled. But I’m very sure a Gore administration would not have responded in the precise way that Bush did.

One reason for that is that Bush’s own response was hard to predict based on the information available about him before the attack.

Foreign policy wasn’t a big issue during the 2000 presidential campaign, but to the extent that it came up, the main argument was that Bush accused the Clinton-Gore administration of overstretching American forces with too many nation-building initiatives. Bush’s father’s administration was characterized by realpolitik, interest in multilateralism and a “new world order,” moderate skepticism of the U.S.-Israel alliance, and a lot of interest in courting Arab public opinion. When I was an intern in the very pro-Israel Chuck Schumer’s office in the summer of 2001, we at one point sent out a press release that had something to do with Bush’s efforts to weaken the Iran-Libya Sanctions Act.

James Mann’s 2004 book, “Rise of the Vulcans: The History of Bush’s War Cabinet,” explains with the benefit of hindsight how the administration’s foreign policy turned out the way it did. But this was not how most knowledgeable observers saw this team when they were initially appointed.

More than anything, though, the circumstances of 9/11 created an unusual political situation.

Not only did Bush immediately become super-popular, but trust in the federal government soared, temporarily reversing the secular increase in cynicism that’s been happening since Watergate and the decline of the three-network news oligopoly. This provided Bush with a very unusual lack of political constraint. His presidency also occurred at a time after the fall of the Soviet Union but before China had risen as anything more than a regional power. No country is ever unconstrained in the international arena, but America was less constrained in 2001 than at almost any time in our history. These were the historical and political circumstances under which Bush was able to do some very good things like sell congressional Republicans on funding PEPFAR, which fights AIDS.

It meant that he had the opportunity to become a truly great president in a way that Bill Clinton and Barack Obama weren’t because they governed under much more cramped circumstances.

But … he wasn’t.

The consequences of the bad choices Bush made have been immense, not just in terms of their immediate human impact (though of course that matters a lot), but also in the collateral damage to vague notions of “the establishment” and to the good things that Bush stood for, like a Christian commitment to the brotherhood of all humanity and a belief that America should stand on high-minded principles.

Yet I really don’t believe there was anything inevitable about Bush making the choices he made, and certainly there was nothing inevitable about linking those ideals to the specifics of an extremely half-baked invasion of Iraq. Everything about that awful day and what followed was wildly contingent and hinged on the will of a handful of people.
