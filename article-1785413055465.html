Rejection Isn't Betrayal

Being rejected is part of socializing, and there's nothing evil about not liking someone.

The other day on Twitter, a relatively small account went viral for the following story: she had a Twitter mutual who began commenting and private messaging her very frequently, attempting to escalate a casual Twitter connection into a real friendship. She got weird vibes and worried this was becoming a one-way parasocial thing, so she ignored it. Eventually, the person asked why she didn’t want to be friends. Figuring honesty was the best policy, OP told them that she didn’t feel the friendship was emerging naturally and the one-sided dynamic made her uncomfortable.

I thought this tweet went viral because it just happened to be an awkward story. But in fact, people framed OP as the villain. Not for sharing the story at all (which, yes, might be upsetting to the subject of this story, although she said it happened on a different account) but for rejecting the would-be Internet friend at all.

Some people are neurodivergent, you know. Wow, this person poured their heart out to you and you stomped on it. Neurotypicals be like! THIS is why nobody makes friends anymore. This is “anti-human” language. No wonder we’re in a loneliness crisis.

(OP was apparently autistic herself, leading me to believe that every Internet story involves a minimum of 70% autistic characters. But that’s neither here nor there.)

Either way, you get the gist—because she didn’t want to be friends with this particular person (an Internet stranger) she was “heartless,” and people like her, who have the gall to reject potential friends, are apparently responsible for why everyone is socializing less.

But I argue that people like OP are not the reason that friendships are hard to make. People like OP are doing something very normal which has been done as long as humans have socialized—deciding “Eh, this person isn’t for me.” The problem is that people have become so sensitive to any negative feelings or discomfort that being rejected for a low-stakes Internet friendship has become “getting your heart torn out,” a serious enough degree of trauma to scare you off making friends with anyone ever again.

I am very pro-socialization. I’m always telling people to go out, make friends, throw and attend parties, and as many millennials might say, do the thing. But I am not encouraging this behavior because I believe rejection will never be part of the experience, nor do I think anyone who rejects a potential friend or romantic partner is breaking some imagined social contract where everyone has to like everyone. Have people been stuck online for so long that they forgot it’s actually very normal—if not universal—to occasionally not want to befriend someone?

Rejection is a natural part of socialization, and I say this as someone who has struggled immensely to make friends and has been rejected more times than I can count. Just like any other social skill, rejection is a skill you learn—performing the rejection politely, accepting the rejection with grace, and not allowing it to create irrational anxiety about socialization with other people.

Provided a normal degree of effort has been expended on socialization, you will never meet a single person who has never rejected someone or been rejected. When it comes to friendships, I have been rejected more times than I’ve done the rejecting, in part because I had (have?) pretty bad social skills for most of my life. I would need a centipede’s body to be able to count on any of my hands how many times women have told me we should “meet up soon” and then never respond to my texts attempting to schedule such a thing. And yet, even I, Supreme Weirdo, have rejected people, including people I initially liked.

Sometimes, frequently-rejected people come to believe that rejection is mean and that a nice person would befriend everyone who was interested. They are the equivalent of a child with strict parents, vowing to give their own child unlimited candy and screen time as soon as they become parents themselves (I recall, in 1998, declaring that when I had a daughter, I would get her the life-size figurine of Rose from Titanic because I would be a good mom). When it finally comes time for a frequently-rejected person to reject someone, they feel overwhelming guilt about something which is perfectly natural: not feeling it. They have internalized the idea that “beggars can’t be choosers,” and by assigning moral weight to just not liking someone, they guilt themselves into friendships or relationships they don’t really want.

I wrote a personal essay about the first time this happened to me. I had a long history of being rejected, excluded and even bullied by my female peers. Some of these interactions were just women simply choosing not to be my friend, other times it was mean-spirited. But over time, I combined all these experiences into a folder in my brain titled, “Evil People Who Hurt Me” and concluded anyone who didn’t like me was not just disinterested, but actively antagonistic. In my mind, there was no difference between a woman who didn’t reply to my texts, and a woman who went out of her way to spread rumors about me. If someone made me feel bad, they were a Bad Person.

When I started work at a new company, I struggled to make friends with my colleagues. When one woman showed interest in befriending me, I was initially over the moon. Finally. I jumped head-first into that friendship (going so far as to have her sleep over at my house the night we met after she missed her train home—yes, really) but very quickly regretted it. I just started getting weird vibes. She annoyed me, and something about her was very off-putting. She did not give me much space, and frequently interrupted my work. I felt like things were escalating way too quickly into a forced BFF-ship. I didn’t want to hurt her feelings, but I didn’t want to be best friends either. I also knew that she had good reason to believe we were overnight best friends, because I had been so warm initially.

Obviously, I bore some responsibility for allowing things to progress so quickly in the first place. In the most platonic way, I “led her on.” But I mostly felt guilty for rejecting her at all, because I had been rejected so many times and I had internalized those rejections as meaning something about my inherent value. Surely, someone who is frequently rejected like a dog—and is therefore a stone-cold loser—would be a hypocrite if she went ahead and rejected someone else, right?

But, no. You’re allowed to do that. Even socially awkward, weird, annoying people are allowed to meet someone else and not like them very much. If anything, these experiences should teach us that everyone who previously rejected us was probably not evil, and just didn’t like us. We can learn from these rejections by altering our social skills or behaviors, but the takeaway should not be, “I’m a good person and would never do that to someone!” Because eventually, you will do that to someone.

I have seen some Reddit posts from people who entered into relationships with people they did not even like because they assumed it would be arrogant to reject them. Multiple people have described dating people whose touch makes them cringe just because, well, who am I to say I deserve better? Suffering through bad friendships and relationships is not doing anyone a favor. In fact, it’s doing everyone involved a disservice. And, need I remind you that someone you find insufferable might be an ideal friend or partner for someone else? I can verify: my husband is, for whatever reason, really into the trait of mine that annoys other people the most (my desire to do round-the-clock improv routines.) Can you imagine if some other guy had married me despite considering me the most annoying woman alive? Some other guy massaging his temples and counting down the hours till his death while I walked around gesticulating and saying, “Well, FRANKLY, I think Rosie O’Donnell is a TREMENDOUS LOSER…”

Once you start viewing rejection as a natural part of socialization—the same way not liking particular foods is a natural part of eating—you have an easier time getting over it. Being rejected doesn’t make you a uniformly undesirable person, and rejecting other people doesn’t make you heartless. We can pay lip service to the idea that “you’re not going to be everyone’s cup of tea” but it’s another thing to accept the reality of that fact, both about yourself and others. Of course, if you find that you are getting rejected constantly to the point that it’s a serious detriment to your social life (as I was) it might be worth working on yourself and figuring out what’s putting everyone off. There’s a difference between being an acquired taste, like kombucha, and being an expired jar of Tostitos queso. But it’s also important to remember that sometimes, people like different things, and rejection is not an objective value judgment.

But perhaps today’s young people view rejection differently because they don’t have much practice being on either end of it outside of the Internet. I’ve seen people say that socialization or dating ceases to be “worth it” if rejection is likely to happen, or exaggerating the effects of rejection to be life-ruining (if one girl doesn’t want to date you, she’ll tell everyone she knows that you’re a sexual predator! She’ll film you and post it everywhere! You’ll lose your job because you said hi to a woman!) because the very act of getting rejected at all feels disproportionately catastrophic.

In fact, some people seem to believe that by agreeing to socialize, you are entering into a social contract wherein rejection represents a break in the contract, ergo, anyone who has rejected someone has done something morally wrong. This is why nobody wants to be friends anymore—you might like someone who HEARTLESSLY doesn’t like you back! Okay, that’s life. In moments like these, I almost want to bring back the pickup artists of the 2000s, who regularly engaged in some of the most high-risk-of-rejection social behavior (trying to pick up random women for sex) and became so inured to it that it ceased to bother them.

Of course, most of us (myself included) do not have the resiliency of Mystery the Pickup Artist, nor is modern society built for that type of behavior. Romantic rejections can feel especially hurtful or personal, even when the person isn’t rude about it. I can think of a few times I’ve been rejected, both platonically and romantically, which I still find embarrassing. But there is a big gulf between “this experience made me feel bad” and “this person did something bad.”

So, where do we draw the line between “made someone feel bad” and “did something bad?”

First, you do not owe people friendship or relationships. You do owe people politeness (or at least a lack of rudeness) when rejecting them, which is why slowly fading away is typically the best way to reject a very casual connection, like an Internet friend who is getting a bit too attached, or a coworker who wants to escalate to friendship when you’d rather stay professional (I don’t recommend dumping your girlfriend or boyfriend this way). But sometimes, people will pick up on the fact that they’re being rejected and start demanding answers. This is where it gets tricky. Provided the rejection is about that person, and not actually about your life’s circumstances, providing an honest answer will be hurtful. So I would argue that if you feel you’re being rejected, you should not ask why, unless you’re prepared to hear the truth! On the flip side, if you are rejecting someone who demands answers from you, you aren’t obligated to be brutally honest. You can maintain “it’s not you, it’s me,” or “I just didn’t feel like we had chemistry” no matter how obviously sugarcoated it is, because you don’t really know how genuinely the person wants to uncover the truth, or how they’ll react to it.

People get especially upset over romantic rejections. That’s their right; it makes sense to be frustrated, especially if it happens all the time. But what dating rejections aren’t is a betrayal. It is not incongruent to say you want to go on lots of dates, and then not continue dating most of those people. That is the natural attrition of dating. During an old survey I ran, both men and women said they wanted to be romantically approached by the other sex more frequently than their current cadence (both men and women described not being approached very often at all, with men experiencing fewer approaches than women.) When I released these findings, I got similar pushback from men and women alike: “Oh, they say they want to be approached more, but how would they react if an ugly person asked them out? They’d probably say no!”

This is not a “gotcha.” Assuming they personally were not attracted to this ugly person (remember, “ugly” people still date each other all the time) it’s entirely plausible (and acceptable) that they’d say no, and it means nothing about their desire to be approached in general. Wanting to be approached more often does not imply that you will say yes to every single person who approaches you. You are not a “choosing beggar” if you say you’d like to be asked out more often, and then don’t say yes every time you’re asked out. Because, again, rejection is just a part of meeting people. It’s not always going to be a good fit. And within the dating world, where (unless you’re polyamorous) you can only have one partner at a time, it makes sense that there would be a lot of rejection, even when nothing is horribly wrong.

Of course, one can take this too far. Friend of the ‘stack Lana wrote a hilariously absurd piece about a client who came to her for dating coaching, who claimed his problem with women was being too hideous for any woman to love, when in reality, he was handsome, and his standards required women be 23-31 (significantly younger than he was) and making over $200K a year, along with a litany of other physical requirements. He experienced his dating life as constantly being “rejected,” when in fact, he rejected the vast majority of women right off the bat, for reasons as simple as only being two years younger than him, or only making $100K a year. As Lana repeatedly points out, he’s allowed to have whatever standards he wants. Surely, everyone is. But there comes a point where, if you are rejecting 99% of your dating pool despite also believing yourself to be hideous despite clear evidence to the contrary, some changes must be made, perhaps not to your looks.

I believe some people are like this. They believe they are being rejected because they are only interested in hanging out with a very small segment of the population (either for dating or friendship) but when that tiny segment is unavailable or uninterested, they lash out and see themselves as the rejected party. But most people aren’t like that. Most people have reasonable (yet varied) standards and preferences for friendship and dating, and occasionally it’s just not a good match for whatever reason. It’s important not to experience such natural friction as a series of deep, permanent wounds, unless your rejector is purposefully antagonizing you.

I can only beat this particular drum so many times, but it’s not so much that people are suffering from a lack of socialization, but a lack of social practice, probably beginning in childhood. A lot of people today aren’t even neurodivergent, but just have no social skills because they have very little experience with the natural trial and error (and yes, embarrassment) that comes with developing them. When kids spend time socializing without adult input or guidance, they eventually learn what they like and don’t like, and what pisses off other kids. They do not learn this as easily with adults hovering and forcing all the kids to include each other, and they do not learn this through group chats and multiplayer video games. Similarly, young adults don’t learn social cues and preferences as easily through social media or dating apps as they do through real-life, spontaneous connections with their peers. There is a layer of anonymity to dating apps that make even those rejections far less embarrassing than when you hit on someone at a party, but also far harder to parse or learn from. It’s no wonder, therefore, that people experience relatively mundane, low-stakes rejection as traumatic, because in most cases they have not been exposed to real-life rejection as often as previous generations were.

In fact, I have seen an avalanche of people diagnosing themselves with RSD, or “rejection sensitivity dysphoria,” wherein one experiences disproportionate anxiety and sadness upon being rejected. However, this is not a clinical diagnosis and is absent from the DSM-5. Far be it for me to claim it’s totally made-up, but I have to wonder how many of these people actually have RSD, versus a very low degree of tolerance for any kind of discomfort due to lack of exposure and experience, and therefore relatively unimportant rejections show up in their brains as dangerous or deeply traumatic. Nobody, no matter how neurodivergent, should be “heartbroken” over a stranger on the Internet not wanting to be friends.

When it comes to any disproportionate discomfort with a regular part of life, the only way out is through. This is the framework behind ERP, or exposure response prevention therapy, which is the gold standard for OCD or anxiety treatment. With ERP, the OCD patient exposes themselves to their trigger or fear (say, germs, in the form of touching something dirty) and then chooses not to do their “safety behavior” (ie: washing their hands or any other cleansing rituals.) As someone currently undergoing ERP, I can assure you that it’s very difficult and anxiety-provoking when you get started. But ultimately, the more you put yourself through the anxiety-provoking experience, the less anxiety it provokes. In a way, the pickup artists, encouraging men to overcome a fear of rejection by being rejected hundreds of times over, were ERP practitioners.

I will never tell you not to care or feel something about being rejected. It makes sense for rejection (especially with something higher-stakes than a Twitter mutual) to hurt. But what doesn’t make sense is for you to determine that your rejector (provided they did not go out of their way to be mean) has done something morally wrong. Because one day, perhaps one day soon, the person doing the rejecting will be you. And you’re better off learning not only how to handle rejection with grace, but deliver it with grace as well.
