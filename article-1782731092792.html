The great American math collapse

We've erased two decades of progress in math.

Kelsey Piper
Jun 29
∙
Paid
 
READ IN APP
 

For decades, kids were getting better at math. The best measure of this — the National Assessment of Educational Progress Long-Term Trend assessment — displays a long climb from 1978 until 2012, when 13-year-old math performance peaked. By 2025, we had wiped out over three decades of gains and were roughly back to where kids were performing in the mid-1990s.

A generation of math gains, erased

Relative to the literacy crisis — which has spurred endless think pieces, journalism, and legislative responses — declining math performance has received relatively little attention. But they’re part of the same narrative.

You’ve probably heard some version of the simple story about how America messed up reading:

It starts with a movement of educators arguing that the traditional approach was wrong, that instead of emphasizing phonics — which involves boring things like drilling letter sounds and explaining how letter combinations work — schools should embrace a “whole language” approach to learning.

Instead of doing drills, students were encouraged to look at picture books and guess the words from context clues.¹ The hope was that this would inculcate not just the skill of reading but the love of reading.

This did not work.

Reading scores declined precipitously until word got out. Excellent investigative journalism brought about mass awareness of how reading was (or wasn’t) being taught. Since then, 42 states (and D.C.) have adopted new laws and policy changes demanding that reading be taught in line with best practices.

Math education went through a similar trajectory:

Once again, a movement of educators began to argue that the traditional approach, which emphasized drills like memorizing times tables and lots of practice problems, was wrong — that, instead, math ought to be taught with an emphasis on exploration and discovery.

The educators argued that forcing students to drill their times tables was killing the innate love of numbers that might have flourished if we hadn’t paved it over with too many worksheets. And so they invented a school of thought that is now exemplified by Jo Boaler’s Mathematical Mindsets and the 2023 California Math Framework — intended to make math more fun and meaningful for students.

This also did not work.

Just like many students who are encouraged to learn to read by guessing words will never actually learn to read, many students who are encouraged to learn math purely through discovery will never learn math at all. (There is, of course, a place for discovery in both subjects, but it belongs after mastery; you need the basic skills first.)

Instead, flawed reading practices that were meant to foster a love of reading often fostered anxiety and shame (because students couldn’t actually read). And employing practices that prevent students from developing mathematical fluency do not, in the long run, help them love math — it can make them hate it.

Today, math reformers hope that the parallel they’ve drawn so far will mean that math reform can be on the same trajectory as reading reform: It will get its own Sold a Story podcast, the parents of the world will rise up in rage that their children aren’t being adequately taught, and schools will sweep aside some of the bad instructional practices they adopted. They will go back to teaching the foundational skills that let students become fluent in the subject matter.

Even musical kids need to memorize their times tables

If this analogy strikes you as compelling but slightly too convenient, you’re right. Making the reading and math stories fit so neatly alongside each other requires simplifying both of them.

Let’s get a little messier.

Discovery advocates are often charged with discarding math fluency — that is, the “ability to perform calculations quickly and accurately“ usually due to rote memorization and practice. If you remember doing your times tables over and over in elementary school, that’s the type of skill building we are talking about.

As education policy researcher and former sixth-grade teacher Tom Loveless explained, “Working memory is limited; long-term memory is vast. In that way, math facts are to math as phonics is to reading. If these facts are learned and stored in long-term memory, they can be retrieved effortlessly when the student is tackling more-complex cognitive tasks.”

Discovery advocates vociferously contest the idea that their approach ignores the importance of arithmetic fluency — they say, instead, that they’re promoting “fluency without fear.” But even if this is true in theory, in practice, they do ignore it:

They often advocate multiplication methods that respect the students’ “multiple intelligences” — that is, learning multiplication through song would engage “musical intelligence,” or tapping out rhythms could engage “kinesthetic intelligence.” These are presented not as fun quirks to get kids engaged but rather as options to replace “rote memorization” and timed tests, which Boaler argues cause anxiety.

“Multiple intelligences” are not a real thing,² so replacing memorization with these newer strategies results in weaker knowledge. To be clear, songs and mnemonics are great — they should just supplement practice rather than replacing it. And while tests don’t always have to be timed, how quickly a student can do a problem tells you a lot about how difficult they find it.

But still, it’s a bit of an oversimplification to say that discovery advocates are “against fact fluency” — they just don’t think memorization is the best way to do it.

Education isn’t just a dial you can turn back and forth until good results happen.

In both reading and math, the tug of war between progressive educators — generally suspicious of worksheets and drills — and people who point out that explicit instruction is effective at giving students the needed skills has been underway for a full century. In the 1920s, progressive educators following John Dewey and William Heard Kilpatrick were pushing schools to organize learning around children’s interests and real world “projects” rather than systemic drills.

Much has been laid at the feet of Jo Boaler, a math education researcher who is the most prominent and polarizing figure in American math-reform debate (including by me). But the drill versus discovery fight is not new. Boaler didn’t invent these arguments against drills, and defeating her won’t end them.

A typical math teacher who has been around for a few decades has been through several rounds of this debate. Each curriculum reformulation carries high costs, from producing the new framework to training educators in it — so they would be forgiven for being cynical that the next one will really get everything right. Education isn’t just a dial you can turn back and forth until good results happen.

This inertia born of having been around two or three cycles of the carousel is, I think, a more substantial barrier to reform than people realize. We owe it to teachers to avoid falling into the trap of “the last reform was ill-conceived, so change will be an improvement.”

This is true for reading too. Skipping phonics is a bad idea, and reintroducing it was a good idea. But “inadequate phonics” is definitely not the only thing wrong with reading in America, so it’s not reasonable to expect its reintroduction to solve all of our problems.

And there’s growing evidence schools can overdose on phonics — teaching it too much to the exclusion of building the vocabulary students need to meaningfully interpret text.

In math, the situation is even more muddled.

The best readers have held steady. The math whizzes have gotten even better

There’s another interesting point of disanalogy between reading and math worth digging into.

In reading, top students have mostly held their ground, losing only a little, while scores have collapsed for weaker students. On NAEP tests, Martin West pointed out that eighth-grade reading scores “at the 10th percentile fell by 10 points between 2013 and 2019 and by another 9 points between 2019 and 2024 … During the same period, high-performing students held steady and, despite the pandemic, are scoring nearly as high as ever.”

In math, the divergence is even larger. While the 90th-percentile scores have dropped, the absolute top students aren’t just holding steady: They’ve actually substantially improved. If you’re good at math, it seems easier to become excellent at math than it has ever been. The number of students taking AP Calculus BC has skyrocketed (and the absolute number of students getting the highest score of 5 has too, so it’s not just less-qualified students taking it).

Average and high-performing students are diverging in ability so much because the decline of school math instruction has been accompanied by a massive explosion in free online programs and paid, in-person, extracurricular math programs. The major ones — Kumon, Mathnasium, Art of Problem Solving, and Russian School of Mathematics — have all seen explosive growth in the last decade.

The Russian School of Mathematics now says it serves over 80,000 students across 80-plus locations in North America, up from 22,000 in 2017 — roughly 4x in eight years. Mathnasium has over 1,000 learning centers in North America.

I am glad these programs exist, to be clear. It’s a good thing when kids get a good education, and more people having the skills to succeed in STEM majors is a good thing not just for them, but for the world; they will invent, discover, and improve things for the rest of us, too. But I do find the whole situation sobering.

When I talk to parents in my area of Oakland, California, about the fact that our public school math instruction does not produce college-ready students, I get two categories of response. Some parents are horrified; they assumed that children graduating from California high schools with A’s in math were ready for STEM work at California colleges, and this isn’t true.

But the other group of parents nods knowingly. “Of course,” they say. “I have her in Russian School of Mathematics after school.” Or, as a Globe opinion column observed, “What worries me is that a feedback loop has now kicked in. As more and more kids opt in to extracurricular math, lots of families start to feel that this sort of enrichment is essential.”

The only way out of that feedback loop is to do a better job of instruction in the classroom, but the question of how, beyond drills and accountability, is complicated.

The success of these extracurricular math programs prompts an obvious question: What are these programs doing, and could it be imitated in the classroom as a foundation for a new science of math?

I think we can and should imitate these programs, but I don’t want to underrate how difficult it would be.

The Russian School of Mathematics doesn’t scale

In most subjects, it works tolerably well to teach students who are at a range of different levels: A college student and a middle schooler could both learn a lot from the same lecture on the causes of World War II, and similarly, a class of middle schoolers at widely different levels can all benefit from the same history class.

But in math, this isn’t true. Students struggle profoundly if they are asked to do work for which they haven’t mastered the prerequisites. It’s not a good idea to teach at “grade level”; it’s a good idea to teach at the students’ level.

And that’s one thing extracurricular programs, which typically place students based on their ability, broadly get right, and schools could imitate it.

The other part of their secret sauce, though, I think would be harder. In general, these programs hire teachers who are exceptional at math. Art of Problem Solving’s application for a math instructor prefers a bachelor’s degree in a STEM field. RSM also says all their teachers have a degree in math or a related field.

Public schools mostly don’t require this and often can’t — those degrees are in high demand, and in elementary school, teachers are frequently shaky in their own math abilities.

This is hard to fix! Teaching doesn’t pay as well as other things you can do with a STEM degree. But, of course, teachers who struggled with math themselves will be worse math teachers.

Subscribed

The third advantage enjoyed by these programs is a stronger curriculum. Art of Problem Solving’s (AoPS) Beast Academy is considered one of the most rigorous elementary curricula out there. Could this be adopted in the classroom? AoPS is, in fact, now working on a classroom version of their curriculum … but it looks like it will be slower and less pedagogically rich.

If we were serious about addressing inequities, we would work very hard to ensure that the math education students get in the classroom is sufficient, that we are not allowing STEM education to become a privilege reserved for those whose parents can afford to pay for the prerequisites.

One of the things that finally turned the tables on reading mal-instruction was educated parents who sent their kids to school and were appalled to realize they weren’t being taught to read. If, in math, those parents sigh and sign up for after-school tutoring, what will turn things around?

Teaching math is an art

Passing laws mandating the “science of reading” works because there is a pretty substantial body of research to look to. While that research is flawed, it does point fairly consistently in the same direction on key questions like whether you should teach the sound-letter correspondence or encourage students to guess words without looking at the letters.

There is much less of a “science of math.” There are some widely agreed-upon points, and “fact fluency” — that students need to be deeply comfortable with basic arithmetic operations — is one of them.

But, beyond that, I would worry a lot about mandating that schools follow “the science.” To go back to our reading analogy, even though we do know a lot about exactly how to produce high reading achievement, passing laws that require phonics is far from a perfect fix.

I said earlier that 42 states and D.C. now mandate phonics, but reading performance has not recovered in all of them. The places that are seeing huge growth in reading performance are definitely teaching phonics, but they’re not just teaching phonics; the successful reforms also introduced a lot of testing and accountability, including holding students back in third grade if they can’t read.

In fact, if you were to point to a single turning point in the rise and decline of American test scores, it would have to be 2013, when reading and math scores started slipping across the board. (They slipped further during COVID-19, but that’s less informative about non-emergency policy.)

The Argument columnist Matt Yglesias frequently points to the end of No Child Left Behind’s accountability regime to explain this shift. Accountability, perhaps, is like multiplication tables and phonics drills: Everyone hates it and fears it is sucking the soul out of education, but in its absence, student performance gets worse.

The adoption of whole-language reading or creative math may have been the specific way that instructional quality declined in some places, and it’s probably a good idea to reverse course. But requiring phonics on its own hasn’t done much to reverse the decline, and requiring times tables drills shouldn’t be expected to work any better. It takes a more holistic approach to change things in a way that persists.
