Democratic fields are shifting; G.O.P. bank accounts are growing

From Maine to Wisconsin, Democratic primaries have been upended as G.O.P. incumbents and super PACs continue stockpiling cash ahead of 2026.

Halina Bennet
Jul 20

Even as party leaders in Maine move quickly to unite behind a candidate after Graham Platner’s departure, Wisconsin’s Democratic primary for governor has gone sideways. Meanwhile, fundraising reports from candidates in several key states and major national PACs underscore the enormous financial investments shaping the 2026 midterms.

The status report

Maine Democrats appear to be on the road to recovery. After primary winner Graham Platner withdrew earlier this month amid a sexual assault allegation he denies, a field of would-be replacements assembled within a matter of days. County caucuses over the weekend broke overwhelmingly toward former State Senate President Troy Jackson.

By Sunday afternoon, the Bangor Daily News reports, Jackson had already ensured that a majority of the delegates who will pick the Democratic nominee were pledged to support him. And by Sunday evening, July 19, many of his opponents had dropped out and rallied behind him, including economist and epidemiologist Nirav Shah, Maine Secretary of State Shenna Bellows, beer company owner Dan Kleban, and former congressional staffer Jordan Wood.

This made Jackson the prohibitive favorite heading into the statewide nominating convention in Bangor on Saturday, July 25. The delegates, however, are not legally bound to follow through on their pledges, so technically someone could try to beat him at the convention. But with his rivals dropping out, that vote is turning into a formality.

Meanwhile, Republican incumbent Senator Susan Collins has used the months Democrats have spent choosing and re-choosing their nominee to grow her financial cushion. She closed the second quarter (April 1 through June 30) with $11 million cash on hand compared with Platner’s $1.7 million before he exited the race; he had spent most of the $9 million he raised during the quarter, Maine Morning Star reports. That haul came in before Platner dropped out, and without a clear opponent to fight — and without a clear candidate for Democrats to support — one can assume that Collins has enjoyed the last two weeks of building up her defenses against whoever comes out at the end of the week on the Democratic side.

In South Carolina, Lindsey Graham’s sister, Darline Graham Nordone, has picked up where he left off after being appointed to serve out the remainder of his term. Despite President Donald Trump’s endorsement of her candidacy to replace her late brother as the Republican nominee on the November ballot, Representatives Nancy Mace and Ralph Norman are both still considering bids for the now-open seat, Axios reports. According to Axios, a source close to Mace called it “political malpractice” not to run, regardless of Trump’s stated preference, while Norman texted “Yes!!” when asked by the outlet about whether he still planned to run.

Other names circulating include Representative Russell Fry, Lieutenant Governor Pamela Evette, and former Representative Mark Sanford, who has already converted his old House campaign committee into a Senate one. Candidate filing for the August 11 special primary opens Tuesday, July 21, so do expect news tomorrow.

Texas’s Senate race stands to become more interesting after Attorney General Ken Paxton agreed this week to debate Democratic nominee James Talarico following Talarico’s public challenge.

Separately this week, both campaigns hit the road in South Texas to court Latino voters. Paxton held a rally in McAllen on July 14 while Talarico traveled the border region, each seeking support from a traditionally Democratic constituency that swung toward Trump in 2024, the Texas Tribune reports.

Both campaigns are flush with cash. Talarico raised more than $30 million in the second quarter, bringing his total raised to more than $70 million, while Paxton raised about $9 million during the same period. Now, Paxton is working hard to win the financial support of the G.O.P.’s biggest donors, many of whom have an ungenerous view of the candidate, the Wall Street Journal reports. Those donors appear more amenable now that Talarico is posting such large numbers, with no indication that his fundraising is slowing.

In Michigan, Abdul El-Sayed outraised both Haley Stevens and Republican Mike Rogers in the second quarter, pulling in about $4.6 million and setting a quarterly fundraising record. Stevens raised less than half of that but still finished the quarter with more cash on hand — $3.4 million to El-Sayed’s $2.7 million — thanks in part to heavy outside spending on her behalf. Rogers reported the largest war chest among the three, boasting roughly $5.7 million on hand.

El-Sayed’s campaign has framed the fundraising surge as evidence of growing national progressive momentum, but the Democratic establishment is mobilizing as well. Senator Bernie Sanders and Representative Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez campaigned across Michigan alongside El-Sayed over the weekend, while retiring Senator Gary Peters broke his earlier vow of neutrality to formally endorse Stevens. The primary is August 4, and recent polling has shown the two essentially tied. Early in-person voting begins this weekend.

Wisconsin’s governor race is experiencing its own version of Maine’s upheaval. Lieutenant Governor Sara Rodriguez, who had emerged as a frontrunner after consolidating endorsements from two former opponents (and from Slow Boring), dropped out of the race on July 17 after discovering what she described as serious financial mismanagement by her campaign manager. She said the campaign had far less cash on hand than had been reported.

Her exit reopened the door for Milwaukee County Executive David Crowley, who had left the race less than two weeks earlier and endorsed Rodriguez. He relaunched his campaign on July 18 with a rally in Milwaukee and an endorsement from term-limited Governor Tony Evers, who had stayed neutral in the race until that point.

The rest of the Democratic field looks for now like it is staying put. Crowley is more moderate than the two other frontrunners, whose progressive positions are putting state Democrats on edge with the risk that they could lose to Republican Tom Tiffany even in a favorable national climate for Democrats. The two other leading candidates are former Lieutenant Governor Mandela Barnes, who lost a 2022 Senate campaign after he was mercilessly attacked for his progressive stances, and democratic socialist Francesca Hong, whose momentum is growing amid a string of D.S.A. victories elsewhere — though those wins have come in places that are not nearly as purple as Wisconsin.

The money machine

Senate Leadership Fund, John Thune’s Senate G.O.P. super PAC, reported $238 million cash on hand as of the end of June — more than double what it had at the same point in 2024, Axios reports. S.L.F. and its nonprofit partner, One Nation, raised a combined $140 million in the second quarter alone.

Executive Director Alex Latcham told Axios that the numbers put to rest earlier doubts about whether Thune could fundraise at his predecessor Mitch McConnell’s level. The group said it is prioritizing defense in red states like Iowa and Ohio, but that it is also planning to spend roughly $100 million offensively in New Hampshire, Georgia, and Michigan.

Senate Majority PAC, the Democratic counterpart aligned with Chuck Schumer, did successfully outraise Republicans during that same quarter, bringing in a combined $147 million with its nonprofit partner, Majority Forward. But the Democratic PAC has significantly less banked, with $126 million on hand.

All said, Democrats are pulling their weight with individual candidates, as fundraising numbers show contenders in battleground states outraising Republicans nearly everywhere. (Josh Turek in Iowa is the one exception among nine key Democratic candidates, according to the New York Times. That’s one reason he is high on the list of Slow Boring’s donation recommendations.) The standout numbers suggest record-breaking small-dollar enthusiasm, but the sheer amount of money being put toward national Republican PACs is still hard to overcome. Whichever Democrat secures the nomination for president in 2028 has their fundraising work cut out for them against a Republican nominee who is sure to have nearly unlimited funds working in their favor.
