The right-wing obsession with “The Indian Question”

It’s not good!

Matthew Yglesias
Oct 14
∙
Paid
 
READ IN APP
 

Doing the podcast rounds to promote “One Billion Americans,” I was pleasantly surprised by how frequently I was able to get to yes with conservative hosts about immigration.

I would tell them that I broadly agreed with the view that illegal immigration should be taken seriously and that there are reasonable questions about the fiscal impact of low-earning immigrants.

But my counter was that there are hundreds of millions of people — 900 million in the latest Gallup survey — who say they’d like to leave the country they are currently living in. So it should be easy for a country like the United States to substantially increase the number of permanent residency slots we hand out while making all of the increase selective in the sense of bringing over people with above-average earnings.

I’m not going to tell you that every conservative I talked to loved this idea, but it was generally received warmly as a welcome alternative to progressive anti-patriotism or hostility to merit. I always did wonder, though, if they understood that in practice, this would mean a lot of people immigrating from South Asia.

I’ve been thinking about that a lot since Bo French, the G.O.P. nominee for railroad commissioner¹ in Texas, tweeted that “the problem is now obviously far worse than anyone imagined,” where “the problem” seemed to be students of South Asian ancestry attending a football game at the University of Texas. This remark generated quite a bit of backlash, and by happenstance, Democrats have a strong nominee opposing French in the form of Jon Rosenthal, a moderate veteran of the oil and gas industry who is not a gutter racist and who I hope Texans will elect in a few weeks.

Right-wingers who agree with French have, since that tweet, seemed to feel the need to come up with reasons why South Asian immigrants are bad. Hence the slightly bizarre spectacle of conservative pundits Jonathan Keeperman and Christopher Rufo doing a podcast on what they term “The Indian Question,” which Keeperman promoted by complaining that progressives have been able to “pathologize the desire for homogeneity and subdue any explicit political effort to maintain a white majority in the country.”

A lot of liberals are wondering what’s going on here. The Indian-American population, after all, is tiny. And this right-wing obsession with a small and successful group of people is exactly why my meeting of the minds with these conservative hosts felt so unstable — I think they were implicitly believing that an orderly and selective immigration system would only let white people in.

Conservatives raise a lot of specific objections to various subsets of immigrants — these people came illegally, those refugees are negatively selected, this family unification visa is a fiscal burden — but Indian-Americans overwhelmingly arrive through selective immigration pathways and are an elite group in the United States. According to Pew, Indian-Americans’ median household income is more than $150,000 and 77 percent of those 25 and older have a college degree. This is a great American success story, a clear benefit to the economy and the budget, and something you can’t raise a reasonable objection to other than — as Keeperman says — just objecting to ethnic change as such.

That’s an ugly sentiment that if indulged is going to lead to bad consequences for the country.

A Christian nation no more?

I think Keeperman slightly sold his own side of the argument short by referring to a desire for a “white majority.” What I think is even more true is that America’s identity as a Christian country is deeply important to a lot of conservatives.

Like most American Jews, I appreciate that conservatives tend to be polite about this and use the phrase “Judeo-Christian.” But we can’t help but notice that the mail doesn’t get delivered on Sunday and that schools are all closed on Christmas. The fact that it’s a Christian mass culture and not actually a “Judeo-Christian” one does not particularly bother me, but it’s a notable and real aspect of American life.

I also, as a person who is not Christian, don’t particularly believe in the importance of keeping it that way.

But I understand that others are going to disagree with that. I think that one important reason that Jewish presence in the United States has become uncontroversial with conservatives is that in the wake of the Holocaust and the foundation of Israel, the theoretical maximum Jewish population share in the United States is capped at a relatively low level. It’s not just that Jews are a small minority — we’re a minority that will always be small.

Back in the heyday of Ellis Island this was not the case. The Jewish share of the aggregate global population was much higher a century ago than it is today, and even though Zionism existed as a project and an ideology, on a practical level Jewish people had an overwhelming preference for moving to the United States rather than Palestine. Under those circumstances, Jewish immigration alarmed a lot of American restrictionists, and preventing Jews from coming here was one of the goals of the 1920s immigration clampdown.

Indian-Americans are a tiny number of people, but Indians in India are extremely numerous. I also don’t think the Bo Frenches of the world are very rigorously distinguishing between Indians, Pakistanis, and Bangladeshis, and the population of that whole region is absolutely enormous. Those countries are also poor enough that their residents are highly motivated to leave in a way that Italians or Germans aren’t.

So in a practical sense, even with a rigorously selective immigration system, there are lots of South Asians who’d qualify, and they will grow over time as a share of the population unless the United States either bluntly excludes them or else sharply curtails skilled immigration.

Raising “The Indian Question” is indelicate, but to serious nativists, it’s important because merely talking about illegal immigration or about immigrants who commit crimes or earn low wages or use welfare isn’t going to keep Indians out. Indian-American families are earning good livings, and their kids are doing well in school and attending flagship state universities and going to football games.

You either need to be willing to bite the bullet and say you’re eager to keep good people out or else you need to redefine good behavior as bad.

The turn against hard work

For a segment of the online right, the latter has become the preferred tactic. They argue that Americans are naturally repelled by hard work, that for example, it’s objectionable when Indian-Americans study for spelling bees.

This struck me as a particularly strange example because while natural talent is definitely real, I’m quite sure that studying hard is the only possible way to excel at a spelling bee. Most linguistic communities, as I understand it, don’t have contests of this kind because basically anyone literate in Italian or Finnish is going to know how to spell all the words. English spelling makes for an interesting game because there’s tons of stuff that has to be learned by brute force.

Keeperman on the podcast argued that “what actually makes America so dynamic — it’s not the kid who studies all night for the spelling-bee test. It’s the kid who is the fullback on the high-school football team, who puts his head down and is willing to run through a freaking wall on behalf of his teammates and then spends the night drinking beers at the kegger and wakes up in the morning and gets a 1500 on his SATs, okay?”

I’m a bit at a loss as to who we’re even talking about here.

It’s of course cooler to be smart and athletic rather than just smart. But everyone who’s good at anything works pretty hard at it. When I was in college, I got up unusually early, in part because I’m naturally kind of an early riser but in part because I wanted to spend a few hours blogging each day on top of my classes and the school paper. And the only other people I’d regularly see up that early were jocks. Ryan Fitzpatrick and Angie Ruggiero were way above the American population average in both athletic and academic achievements, and they worked extremely hard. And it’s not like only the very elite behave this way — my son wakes up at 4 a.m. to get to his 5 a.m. swim practices, and in the afternoons when he gets home from school he does extra math.

Far from being a country that places unusually little value on hard work, the United States is a global outlier in the number of people who say yes to the question of whether it’s important to work hard to get ahead in life.

I do want to be clear, though, because some people get a little turned around on this: My point here is not to valorize crazy aspects of grind culture or suggest that everybody needs to be spelling bee-maxxing all the time. I think a lot of immigrant parents are infamously excessively tough on their kids because they personally uprooted themselves to achieve a better life for their family and can be a little neurotic about ensuring their kids take every possible advantage of the situation. It’s definitely true that part of the multi-generational assimilation process is chilling out a little bit.

But in the grand scheme of things, having smart and hard-working people in the country is better than not having them.

A positive-sum world

On some level, this comes down to a positive-sum versus zero-sum mindset.

If you think of America as a fixed status hierarchy, then it’s true that smart South Asian kids can be a threat to your kids’ prospects. There’s only nine Supreme Court seats, and sooner or later, one of them is going to go to a person of South Asian ancestry. If Vivek Ramaswamy wasn’t the G.O.P. nominee for governor of Ohio, some white person probably would have gotten the gig. Indian immigration to Texas presumably does, in fact, make it harder for white kids to get into the flagship U.T. campus.

But it’s a serious error to see these zero-sum aspects of life as typical.

It is better to live in a country with more skilled surgeons, with more well-run businesses, with more smart engineers, and with more diligent middle managers. High-income people pay taxes and make Social Security and Medicare more sustainable. They are customers for other people’s work and businesses. I think most parents put too much weight on peer effects when thinking about their kids’ education, but all else being equal, you’d surely rather have studious classmates than unruly ones. And you’d rather have a school in your neighborhood than see it shut down and have to travel far away.

I’m not going to say you’re not “allowed” to just have a preference for homogeneity that trumps practical considerations.

I, personally, have a preference for diversity. The one summer I spent in Kerrville, Texas, one of the things I missed was having an Indian restaurant in town. But just as I have my preference, people can have a preference in the other direction.

What’s not okay is for them to wildly misportray the economic stakes. If someone is coming here from a foreign country and committing crimes, that’s bad. If they’re crowding out social assistance spending that could otherwise go to a native-born American, I get the complaint. But if not, their presence and economic activities are win-win. If they’re earning income in the top half of the distribution, they’re even making the structure of the labor market more egalitarian.

It’s not like America would instantly collapse without Arvind Krishna and Satya Nadella and Sundar Pichai and Vimal Kapur and Kal Penn and Mindy Kaling and Fareed Zakaria and Ajit Pai and Usha Vance and the other millions of successful Indian-Americans. But in the aggregate, being a place where talented and hard-working people move to is a boon to the United States, and the fact that we have a culture that is flexible enough to change over time is part of our success as a country.

The Trump administration’s ongoing effort to move beyond border security and interior enforcement to crack down on legal migration of skilled workers has been a really under-covered story with bad implications for American prosperity. And as much as it’s fair to get into the weeds of the exact details of program design, this “Indian Question” discourse really gives away the game here.
