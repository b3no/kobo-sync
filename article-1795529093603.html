Mother Love

Daisy Johnson’s haunting new novel, “Long Wave,” follows three generations of women bound by love and fear.

Ron Charles
Nov 24
 
READ IN APP
 

The Tuesday Review

We like to imagine great novelists tucked away in their studies contemplating the taste of a madeleine, but in 2018, when Daisy Johnson got a call that she’d been nominated for the Booker Prize, she was hard at work babysitting and had to fend off a little girl obsessed with her phone.

Now, as a celebrated writer with her own children, Johnson presumably has both more and so much less freedom, which is the subject of her unsparing new novel, Long Wave.

This is a story heated by the cataclysmic fusion of a mother’s love and dread. Over three deeply wounded generations, Johnson traces the recurrence of trauma generated by loneliness, poverty, and depression. I’ve never felt the gnawing fear of caring for children so powerfully set down on the page. Anyone who’s secretly dared to think, “I was a child once and then I had a child and that child killed the child I was” will find a home here. As one mother admits, “I love them but I hate them.”

That sounds harrowing, and it’s meant to, but there’s a tensile line of tenderness strung through this tale that never breaks.

Long Wave opens in such sorrow that even the dog is grief-stricken. Now that the vicar has died, his widow, Edith, must move out of the church’s house where they lived for more than 40 years. “Everywhere there are the markings of motherhood,” Johnson writes, “the markings of child-rearing, the markings of child loss.”

Her loss, it turns out, is literal. Edith’s only child, Ruth, ran away — escaped, really — some three decades earlier and remains missing. All this time, Edith has dreamed of Ruth coming home, where she could once again be bolted into her room.

That offhand allusion to Ruth’s domestic imprisonment will eventually swell into a scarifying ordeal of parental obsession. Determined to keep Ruth safe and unable to control her daughter’s passions, Edith affixed a battery of locks to the outside of the girl’s room and kept her imprisoned there like some latter-day victim in “The Yellow Wall-Paper.”

As in a fairy tale, the exact time and place aren’t pinned down, but Johnson preserves the grim particularity of the girl’s life, her desperate desire for the world outside her second-story window, her knocks on the bedroom wall: “I am here, I am here.” All we can know for sure is that Edith’s surfeit of love somehow curdled into tyranny.

Locked in her room, Ruth wanted nothing more, aside from her freedom, than the chance to visit Bear Island, an uninhabited rock off the coast marked by an abandoned lighthouse. Though she’s never been there, or perhaps because she’s never been there, she imagines the island as an idyll where she could live in complete happiness. Over the years, that yearning ferments into a kind of mania.

She will someday make it there.

It will not be paradise.

I’ll say no more on that evolving mystery, except that it’s difficult to lure you into this remarkable novel without stomping on the shards of its fractured structure. Given the island motif, I assumed the “long wave” of the title was a reference to the ocean, but as happens so often in this craftily engineered book, there is another meaning vibrating through the ether. Late in the story, a young radio host explains to her mother that long radio waves “may struggle to penetrate certain objects or they may bounce off surfaces so that the signal takes different paths and arrives at different times.”

Substituting lines of story for electromagnetic waves, Johnson has created a similarly distorting effect. These incidents, across many decades, take different paths and arrive at different times. We might get to know an abandoned little girl long before we discover what befell her mother; we might wait hundreds of pages to learn the precipitating event of a ghastly marriage. As a narrative tic, such scrambling would be merely irritating, but as an expression of the way time and trauma are folded in on themselves, it’s miraculous.

That chronology sounds intimidating, but Johnson helpfully labels each chapter, and no investment of care in this novel will go unrewarded. Phrases and actions show up again and again like antique jewelry passed down from mother to daughter. The river that runs through the story remains essentially the same and fundamentally different for each generation, for every woman who takes on the unfathomable responsibility of giving birth.

Johnson keeps her footing on the shore of realism — she’s best, in fact, with the gritty tedium of childcare conveyed in a breakneck present tense. But there are moments tinged with the supernatural, with terrifying premonitions, like the woman whom 10-year-old Ruth sees — or thinks she sees — wandering into the water with her baby. (Johnson’s fans may hear echoes of psychological horror from her novel Sisters and wisps of ectoplasm from her ghost stories in The Hotel.) For the women in Long Wave, though, foreshadowing is not always foreknowledge. Overwhelmed by the possibility of happiness, Ruth’s daughter says, “Everything has already happened. Everything is already ecstatic and beyond belief.” And she’s right.

Is it too much to say that reading this novel is something like motherhood itself — its staggering demands, its sloughs of despair, its unspeakable joys?

Ruth’s knocking reverberates through the years, through the generations: “I am here, I am here.” Some will never be heard. But others will.◆

Long Wave, by Daisy Johnson (Riverhead Books, 304 pp., $28).
