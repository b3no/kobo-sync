Twenty-three points on the midterms and the narrative

A sweep that, in retrospect, could have been even bigger

Matthew Yglesias
Nov 11
∙
Paid
 
READ IN APP
 

Today is Veterans Day, a federal holiday and a day off from school, and normally we wouldn’t run a new piece. But this continues to be a critical moment for shaping public understanding of the momentous electoral events that just unfolded, and we didn’t want to leave you high and dry.

I also think it sometimes does a bit of violence to the complexity of events to sweep everything into a totalizing narrative. The overall composition of the House Democratic caucus is shifting decidedly to the center, but you’ll also see the biggest and most empowered insurgent left group in recent history.

So, because many things are true at once, here are 23 somewhat scattered thoughts on both the results and the narrative. We’ll be back with a full article on Thursday.

  1. Most of the candidate quality debate happens among Democrats, so the focus tends to be on the differences between Democrats. But even in defeat, Susan Collins is clearly a stellar electoral performer. Republican Governors Phil Scott and Kelly Ayotte survived a blue tsunami. If you look up Dem-side 2024 wins-above-replacement ratings, you’d think Angela Alsobrooks is a freakishly bad politician, but that’s actually Larry Hogan being a freakishly good one, even though he also lost.

  2. One of Donald Trump’s largest impacts on American politics has been saddling the Republican Party with a bunch of bad Senate candidates. We remember the ones who lost, like Ken Paxton, Dr. Oz, Herschel Walker, and Darline Graham. But Julia Letlow, who won, also seems very weak, and JD Vance was an underperformer in his one election.

  3. I think the left is on the run in the post-election takes game, largely because they got arrogant about their media echo chamber and made the Bond villain play of explaining their plan in advance. The D.S.A. told people they were hoping for a narrow House majority so they could squeeze Hakeem Jeffries, and A.O.C. told a fundraiser that she was going to spin El-Sayed winning Michigan by any margin as debunking all electability concerns, so analysts were ready.

  4. Some people wonder why I’ve been so dogged about this, but doggedness and preparation matter. There was a good Aaron Blake article in CNN about progressive underperformance, and Nate Cohn also explained it well on Derek Thompson’s podcast. Word is getting out because commonsense Democrats are more organized than before.

  5. As well as the midterms went for Democrats, they could have gone better. Imagine if John Bel Edwards had been the nominee in Louisiana, if Seth Bodnar had just run in the Democratic primary in Montana, and if Gary Peters had run for re-election in Michigan and all the money spent there was plowed into Florida instead. I think that’s very plausibly three more seats.

  6. S.N.L. did a cold open sketch over the weekend where the joke was that it’s funny to imagine anyone giving Hakeem Jeffries and Chuck Schumer credit for Democrats’ strong performance — I don’t think that’s fair (they did well!), but it’s a real assessment of where we’re at as a party.

  7. Ultimately, I think Jeffries and Schumer end up being poorly served by not having defeated challengers in order to secure or hold their posts after 2024. If there’d been a contested race where Schumer said, “Here’s my plan, it’s going to work” and then he’d won his contest and then his plan worked, he could say, “See — it worked!”

  8. For that exact reason, I’d love to see an actual challenge this year. It would be great for someone to explain their plan to maximize Democratic gains on the 2028 Senate map and then for Schumer to explain what, if anything, he disagrees with about that plan. Ultimately, I doubt we’d actually see much disagreement, and this whole thing is kind of BS.

  9. When people say the impact of ideology on electability is small, pay attention to the details. My read is that the gap between a progressive and an establishment Dem is small and that the gap between an establishment Dem and a moderate is small but that the prog/mod gap is actually pretty big because it’s a double gap.

  10. Ideology, in my view, is about what positions you actually take, not a kind of ascriptive personal identity. Angie Nixon of the D.S.A. and ex-Republican David Jolly are two very different kinds of people, but they didn’t actually take different positions on the issues except for Israel, so they did pretty similarly. This is what you’d expect — Jolly did better, but not a lot better — if you think ideas matter. It’s not what you would expect if you think “progressive” and “moderate” are fixed personal characteristics.

  11. Halina is too ethical to say this, but Colorado getting Dem supermajorities in the state legislature and electing Phil Weiser as governor is probably bad for the state. Colorado’s residents benefited from a moderate form of Democratic Party governance under Hickenlooper and Polis, and there’s a big risk now of the state adopting some of the more dysfunctional policies of blue states. There’s no electability problem with Weiser, but a more moderate governor would be better on the merits.

  12. Sometimes good politicians are a little bit subpar as electoral performers, and that’s fine! I really like what Kathy Hochul is cooking in New York, but her results were slightly bad. New York is not a swing state, and in fact, beating Nassau County Executive Bruce Blakeman on Long Island might be enough of a political show of force to shock New York Republicans out of their turbo-NIMBY mode. I wish I could factually say she’s a huge overperformer, since I like her a lot. But it isn’t true, so I don’t say it, because I’m not a dishonest person.

  13. I think the question of which people can say accurate things is ultimately more important than these modest differences in performance.

    The signature substantive problem with progressive governance is that you have a lot of smart people over-indexing on coalition management rather than trying to address the issues clearly. Median voter denialism is a downstream symptom of the underlying pathology.

  14. I don’t think Democratic governors won in Texas, Ohio, and Georgia purely because of the war in Iran. Everyone saying that needs to think harder about what’s going on.

  15. I mentioned trying to help with staffing before, and now I have the links. If you’d like to submit your resume to the Blue Dogs go here, to Third Way’s talent bank go here, and to Majority Democrats go here.

  16. I hope that at some point immigration restrictionists will try to explain to me more clearly why they didn’t even try to pass an immigration bill. Did they think they were going to govern forever? They had a real shot to make a durable change in a policy area they care a lot about, and what happened instead was the Department of Homeland Security posted a lot of edgy memes.

  17. When Jeffries was asked post-election about a D.S.A. summer victory rally for their primary candidates at which he was threatened with a future challenge, Jeffries said, “It was very interesting to me, as observed by others, that there was almost zero diversity in that crowd.” Establishment Democrats love to wield this kind of toxic identity-politics rhetoric against their critics on the left, and I think it’s important for commonsense Dems to criticize them for it and also to remind the world that this is in some sense not our fight. The candidates who flipped seats in states Trump won did so by running to the right of Jeffries and Schumer, not by acting as their allies.

  18. All the moderates (but also Bernie Sanders) are warning Democrats not to spend too much time on investigations when they should be helping the American people with their pressing economic needs. I think it’s important to consider what the real tradeoffs are here. If a good affordability bill like the Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act doesn’t pass, it won’t be because Democrats were too busy doing investigations; it will be because progressives didn’t like the bill, because they prefer green purism. I’d love to see our newly elected mods lock in on the bona fide ideological disagreement here and not get sucked into a pointless debate about investigations.

  19. Robert Garcia, who is set to chair the Oversight Committee, said at Crooked Con that “we’re not going to forget” the crimes of former Trump administration officials. “Maybe it’s because I’m gay, but pettiness is a thing.” It’s a decent joke, but I think safe seat members in positions of responsibility need to try way harder than this to not break kayfabe and make sure that everything they say in public is something that helps rather than hurts frontline members. “Do investigations” vs. “pass bills” is a fake tradeoff, but “investigate in a way that’s politically helpful” vs. “get laughs” is a real one.

  20. Ballot measures are a good indicator of policy views separate from partisanship. Conservative initiatives related to trans issues passed in Colorado, Washington, and Arizona. Voter ID initiatives passed in Nevada, North Carolina, and Ohio. Democrats who want to maximize their odds of doing well in 2028 and driving a stake through the heart of the MAGA movement should consider avoiding positions that are too unpopular to prevail in states Trump repeatedly lost. The wealth tax lost in California and a minimum wage hike initiative failed there two years ago — there are limits to the appeal of economic populism.

  21. Ezra Klein wrote over the weekend that “I don’t know any Democrats who are counting on these voting patterns to automatically persist.” I hope that lack of complacency is accurate. But none of the strategists or academics he quotes mention that adopting more conservative views on cultural issues is part of how successful Democratic candidates expanded the map, so I’m concerned that complacency persists.

  22. Krystal Ball says James Talarico talks like Bernie Sanders. Talarico says “I’m a big fan of SpaceX. I’m a big fan of Tesla. [Musk] is a great business leader and entrepreneur and innovator. I want him to stick to those things — I don’t like that he’s trying to buy this Senate seat in Texas. I don’t like that he’s using his wealth to influence our political system. That’s where I draw the line. I will stand up to Elon Musk when he tries to buy our government, but I will work with him when it comes to technology and innovation.”

  23. When I looked at the history of midterms, what’s interesting is that sometimes a big loss prefigures things to come and sometimes it doesn’t. It’s pretty normal for a president to bounce back from a terrible second-year midterm (1994, 2010) and get re-elected. But even a year-six brushback like Democrats scored in 1986 was followed by Republicans’ rare presidential three-peat triumph in 1988. This year was the biggest midterm sweep since 1974, but 1974 led to Jimmy Carter’s narrow win in 1976 followed by basically a failed presidency. I’m not saying that’s what will happen in 2028, obviously, but I do think the collective conventional wisdom keeps failing to appreciate the full range of possible outcomes. What else did we see last week if not that weird stuff happens?
