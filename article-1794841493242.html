Rob Breakenridge: Canada’s harsh hockey diplomacy is a lesson for the world

Russia should not be treated as a favoured nation while its illegal war rages on

Line Editor
Nov 16
 
READ IN APP
 

By: Rob Breakenridge

Ukraine clearly has much larger worries at the moment than whatever the list of participants at international hockey competitions might look like.

Continued throttling of Ukraine’s Starshield connectivity and ongoing shortages of air-defence interceptors have left the country more vulnerable to Russian drone and missile attacks. That, in turn, seems connected to a broader campaign of pressure to force Ukrainian concessions at the negotiating table, thus handing to Russia that which they haven’t been able to capture by force.

But it’s amid that backdrop that Russia was handed an opening for what would have been a massive boost for their campaign to legitimize and whitewash their bloody war of aggression and bring them back into the company of respected nations. Canada’s hardly in a position to bolster Ukraine’s air defences or influence peace talks, but we’re ideally positioned to help deny Russia a major propaganda coup.

Which brings us to hockey.

In a ruling last month, the international Court of Arbitration for Sport (CAS) ordered that Russia be reinstated back into the ranks of international hockey competition. The first such tournament to be potentially affected by this new reality would be the World Junior Championship, set to begin late next month in Alberta.

Canada certainly has its own history of memorable and historic victories over Russian and Soviet teams, and there would be some undeniable satisfaction in yet another. However it would have been a tremendous betrayal and slap in the face to Ukraine — not to mention Canada’s own values and principles — to acquiesce here.

In this realm, Canada has considerable clout. We should be unafraid to use that influence. Canada should not allow any Russian national team to compete on Canadian soil and we should not allow any Canadian national team to compete alongside Russia at any other international tournament. Of what value is international hockey absent Canada?

For as much as these Russian athletes have been worthy competitors and rivals for their Canadian counterparts over the years, there’s no escaping the reality of what it means to be competing under that flag — and the man whose geopolitical agenda they’d be advancing. As it stands, Canada should refuse to take to the ice against them.

Fortunately, the Prime Minister understands this. Following the CAS decision, Mark Carney made it clear that no Russian team would be admitted into Canada for the world juniors or any other tournament. The federal government is also planning to do what it can to slam the door shut on any potential loophole involving junior-aged Russian players already in Canada playing in various major junior leagues.

This stance could be put to the test rather quickly. Russia’s hockey federation has made clear its intent to push for tournaments to be relocated and to seek sanctions against organizers. In the case of Canada and the world juniors, the Russians may even seek compensation for being denied permission to participate.

It’s bad enough to see an international “court” taking the side of a ruthless and lawless aggressor. It would be even more galling to see punishment doled out for the “crime” of standing with the victim of that aggression. Hopefully, we’re prepared to tell the Russians and whoever is demanding we write such a cheque to go to hell.

All of this does, though, serve as a helpful reminder to Canadians that we shouldn’t just hitch our values and our foreign policy to the whims of these international organizations. It’s helpful when those organizations act in a manner which aligns with our own established principles, but the Canadian tendency to be obedient followers of some nebulous concept of multilateralism does not serve or advance our interests.

Canada has taken the morally right and noble stand here, and Canada is not alone, either. Call it a coalition of the unwilling, if you will, as a number of Scandinavian and Eastern European nations have already made it clear they have no intention of competing alongside Russian national teams or hosting tournaments that must include Russia.

Canada obviously has much less say in what other countries do or how other tournament hosts respond to Russian pressure. But if Canada isn’t there, it matters. And as we’ve seen with the World Cup or 4 Nations Face-Off, we can manage just fine without the involvement of these international organizations.

Surely the International Ice Hockey Federation (IIHF) is aware of that, and Canada needs to keep up the pressure to ensure that they do the right thing. So far, that has worked. After a few days of uncertainty, the IIHF acknowledged on the weekend that the Russians would not be able to compete at the world juniors due to Canada’s unwillingness to admit Russian players into the country. (There have also been reports in recent days about a potential IIHF investigation into Russia incorporating teams from occupied Ukraine into the broader Russian hockey infrastructure. That could establish grounds for a new ban on Russian competition, separate from the CAS appeal.)

Of course, there’s still the broader issue of international sport beyond just hockey. The 2028 Summer Olympics in Los Angeles could include Russian athletes, for example, and the current trajectory of the U.S. administration does not fill one with confidence that they’re prepared to take a principled stand on any of this.

But Canadians should be under no illusions as to what Russia is up to and how they’re hoping to exploit this situation. We must not accept or reward aggression, or pretend that we can return to “business as usual” amid an ongoing illegal and brutal war.

To paraphrase a certain world leader, we hold some important cards here. Let’s play them.
