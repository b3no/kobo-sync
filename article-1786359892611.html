The heresy of Hank Green

Thou shalt not use ChatGPT.

Jeremiah Johnson
Aug 10
∙

Hank Green has committed the internet’s newest unforgivable sin: admitting to using AI.

The trouble began when Green, a prolific science educator and communicator with a wildly popular YouTube channel, used the somewhat awkward canned phrase “I appreciate the pushback” in a solo monologue segment of a video.

For frequent users of ChatGPT — or unwilling recipients of AI-generated emails — this was a tell. Many of Green’s fans immediately accused him of using AI to write his script, which Green denied. But he did admit that he’d used ChatGPT to help research the episode. Many of his fans treated this as though he’d admitted to blending baby seals to make his morning smoothie:

Look at the language here: Green is a sellout with “no standards.” He’s a “fraud” whose “credibility is in the trash.” It’s “disappointing” and “incredibly scary.” Make it make sense!

Green subsequently posted a pseudo-apology on Reddit in which he said he was “mortified” to have disappointed people. He admitted that he had been relying too heavily on AI as a research aid while maintaining that he had not used AI to write his script. But he added that he was getting an unhealthy level of dopamine from using LLMs — and essentially soft-canceled himself by promising to pause several projects and reduce his video output in the future.

Some people continue to suspect that Green used ChatGPT for more than just research. His phrasing, after all, seemed odd. Pangram flags the scripted portion of Green’s video as 86% AI-generated, and Green certainly has an incentive to lie if he did write with AI. On the other hand, Pangram can sometimes struggle with false positives. And Green’s reputation for honesty should matter here —he’s built up quite a bit of goodwill over nearly two decades of producing high-quality educational content.

Perhaps controversially, I’d like to argue that whether or not Green used AI is one of the least important things about this entire episode. What’s far more interesting is how a fan base built on a love of science began conducting itself like a dogmatic religious sect, eager to police the boundaries of predetermined good and evil.

Believe science

Hank Green’s entire public persona is built around endless curiosity, optimism, and enthusiasm for science. He frequently highlights new scientific discoveries. But AI itself is one of the greatest technical advances of our lifetimes. In addition to being incredibly impressive in its own right, it’s contributing to breakthroughs in science and math.

So why are Green’s science-loving fans so incensed that he would use AI for research?

To understand the outrage, you first need to realize that for many people “science” functions as a sort of new religion. And this new religion’s adherents have decided that AI technology does not count as science. They’ve decided AI is evil to its core.

There have been many attempts throughout history to turn science and reason into full-fledged belief systems. The French Revolution produced the Cult of Reason, an atheistic substitute for Christianity complete with repurposed ceremonies and churches. In the 19th century, Auguste Comte proposed a Religion of Humanity meant to fulfill the social and cultural roles of organized religion but with positivist reason in place of supernatural beliefs.

The modern incarnation of this instinct isn’t as high-minded as past efforts. It developed in the form of “I Fucking Love Science,” a Facebook page that attracted millions of followers in the 2010s by sharing pro-science memes and news. Those followers weren’t in love with strict adherence to the scientific method. They weren’t dedicated to systematically trying to understand the world through rational truth-seeking. They wore science as a costume, a way of proving their superiority to irrational theists and, in particular, largely conservative theists.

For this group, science was a form of fandom, or a political expression, a tribe they could belong to that let them seem smart and conscientious without any adherence to the norms or difficult work of scientific rigor. The modal “science fan” watched videos where Bill Nye did epic dunks on creationists, spent way too much time on r/atheism, and posted inspirational graphics about how the atoms in our bodies were forged inside stars. They had a Darwin fish decal on their car. They celebrated Pi Day. They were probably overly enthusiastic fans of BBC’s Sherlock and Sheldon from The Big Bang Theory, characters who act as superficial mascots for intelligence.

This fandom overlapped with a particular kind of left-leaning politics.”Believe Science” became a progressive rallying cry, particularly in fights where conservatives were viewed as out of step with scientific realities like climate change, evolution, and stem cell research.

The “Believe Science” movement wasn’t totally without merit. Creationists deserve to be dunked on. Climate change is real. And it’s fine to like corny TV and post inspirational science graphics. But when you divorce the love of science from the practice of the scientific method, what you’ve got is simply a new religion wearing a lab coat.

The progressive-leaning “Believe Science” crowd still holds great affection for science as an aesthetic, and for the moral authority that being pro-science brings. But they’re increasingly divorced from the scientific method of inquiry. Just as creationists pre-determined the answer to Darwin’s theories without ever picking up On the Origin of Species, so too have Believe Science fanatics pre-judged AI. Their objections are rarely considered views about the threat to labor markets, developing nations, or mass death; rather, they treat AI as a contaminant, relying on dated views of AI hallucinations to assume all of its outputs are necessarily bad.

This is why you often see climate and environmental organizations opposing wind power projects or nuclear power. It’s also how Will Lawrence, a cofounder of the Sunrise Movement and now the Democratic nominee for Michigan’s 7th Congressional District, can end up asserting dubious claims as settled fact. He has insinuated that data centers are ruining Michigan’s water, raising electricity prices, and destroying home values. And Lawrence himself admits that some of the same people who oppose data centers also opposed solar and wind energy projects. It’s all the same impulse; claiming the mantle of science when it matches your political commitments while dismissing facts or technologies when they don’t.

The technology writer Jasmine Sun noted this reflexive skepticism when investigating local opposition to AI data centers:

My conversations make me wonder if the technical content of data center deals is mostly beside the point. Closed-loop cooling systems? “I don’t believe them.” Paying millions in taxes? “I don’t believe them.” Creating a thousand jobs? “I don’t believe them.” I hear a reflexive skepticism of every claim.

The scientific approach to these questions is to investigate the facts and try to determine which claims are true and which aren’t. The dogmatic approach is to first decide whether the object of inquiry is good or evil, and choose not to believe any evidence to the contrary.

Opposing AI can, of course, be totally rational: it’s justifiable to be afraid of AI’s impact on the labor market, especially when Silicon Valley CEOs keep telling you you’re going to lose your job to a machine. It’s also possible to be worried about catastrophic risks, increased inequality, or the threat of an authoritarian, Palantir-enabled government. (Indeed, polling from The Argument shows that majorities believe that AI will cause huge problems.)

But it’s only a kind of dogma that explains why so many AI skeptics like Ed Zitron ended up embarrassing themselves with predictions that OpenAI would collapse — and that the AI bubble was about to burst — over and over and over using contradictory reasoning.

Zitron’s readers don’t read him because he has a sterling track record of correct predictions. They read him and love him because he, like they, hews to a dogma approved by the “Believe Science” crowd: that AI is terrible, useless, and a bubble about to pop.

AI as culture war

Commentator Katherine Dee recently speculated that the growing anti-tech backlash will run along a similar arc to “The Great Awokening”: Beginning with understandable grievances, it will devolve into a culture war filled with overreactions, moral panics, status games, and virtue signaling.

This is what the backlash to Hank Green’s AI usage is really about.

During the era of peak woke, it was less important to understand the nuances of policy and more important to simply be on the right side — just like it’s not important how or why Hank Green is using AI, it’s only important that he’s using it at all. It doesn’t matter that the comments on his recent videos have been highly positive, now that the taint of AI has been revealed.

The “Believe Science” crowd isn’t impartially judging a new technology; they’re demanding fealty and purity of belief. They don’t see new technologies as scientific breakthroughs; they see them as tests of moral character. And the anger is so concentrated because the one thing worse than a disbeliever is a heretic. Hank was supposed to be one of the good ones. He was one of the kings of the pro-science crowd — a community that would never use AI for research — which makes his supposed betrayal all the more infuriating. In the end, rather than science making progressive politics more evidence-based, progressive politics has made “Believe Science” more political and dogmatic.
