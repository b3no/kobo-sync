Do moderates still have an electability advantage?

We ran the numbers on 2026. Here’s what we know about the moderation advantage.

Lakshya Jain
Nov 12
∙
Paid
 
READ IN APP
 

Was the 2026 election a referendum on moderation?

In the week since the biggest blue wave in a half-century, takes have flown fast and furious. Moderates like Third Way say that moderation made the Democratic majorities, while more progressive academics say that its impact was near-nonexistent.

I think both of these takes tell an incomplete story.

We won’t have full results for a while; ballots are still outstanding in several states, after all. But current tallies of the 2026 vote give us a fairly comprehensive picture — one in which moderation does seem to systematically advantage a Democratic candidate by as many as 3 percentage points. This advantage absolutely helped Democrats build their majority: In races like the Mississippi and Alaska Senate contests, which are close enough that they haven’t been called yet, a 3-point swing would probably have resulted in a Republican hold.

But while moderation still clearly helped candidates from both parties perform better than they would have otherwise, moderate politicians may have had less of an advantage than ever before.

Share

There’s a metric Split Ticket has developed over the years called Wins Above Replacement (WAR). Similar to its baseball analogue, WAR for elections tells you how strong or weak a candidate was relative to a race where a generic Democrat squares off against a generic Republican.

For instance, take Texas, where we estimate James Talarico’s WAR against Ken Paxton to be in the neighborhood of D+5. That means we would expect a generic Democrat to do 5 points worse against a generic Republican than Talarico did against Paxton.¹

This has turned out to be a pretty robust metric that demonstrates predictive power when we incorporate it into our modeling. It not only significantly improved our 2024 and 2026 projections relative to earlier forecasts — it also improved our past forecasts when we apply it retroactively.

And in every election where we’ve looked closely at WAR, moderate politicians have consistently overperformed by quite a bit, compared to generic Democrats (let alone progressives). Between 2018 and 2024, the most conservative Democratic caucus did nearly 5 percentage points better than the most liberal one (the Congressional Progressive Caucus).

In 2026, however, the effect shrank. The moderate Blue Dog caucus did a little over 3 points better than the progressives. And while candidates supported by the left-wing group Justice Democrats still underperformed the party’s baseline, they also did better than ever before.

And because politics rarely works in only one direction, this effect wasn’t limited to just the Democrats. On the other side of the aisle, mainstream Republicans in the Republican Governance Group had generally done approximately 4 points better than the ideological firebrands in the Freedom Caucus. This time around, that gap was barely 1 point.

I think the Republican shrinkage is easily explained by the fact that there are basically no moderate Republicans left. Per VoteHub, nearly every single Republican legislator in the House has voted with Trump (a phenomenally unpopular president) more than 95% of the time. A moderation boost isn’t going to show up when virtually nobody is moderate to begin with.

The Democratic side is far more interesting, though, and requires some more thought. In my opinion, the simplest explanation for the shrinking moderation advantage on the left is that this election wasn’t about the ideological fault lines that previously dominated the Trump era. The vulnerabilities of progressives — on issues like gender, immigration, and crime — may have faded in the minds of the voters, who were overwhelmingly focused on economic concerns, according to polling. Several populist and progressive economic policies are genuinely popular, and those ideas received more attention this cycle.

In late October, The Argument polled a representative national sample of voters and asked them open-ended questions about who they felt each political party was fighting for. When it comes to the Democrats, responses like “working class,” “everyday people,” and “the people” dominated — and we saw comparatively little mention of the culture war topics from “Woke 1.”

Compare that to the word cloud that emerged when we asked about Republicans. Many of the most common responses contain some type of reference to wealth, like “rich” and “billionaires.”

In other words, when voters thought about both parties, they mostly thought about material politics. That yielded a disastrous outcome for Republicans, because for the first time in the Trump era, their standing with voters on the economy was radioactive.

The economy was simply too dominant in the minds of swing voters for them to care much about the anti-trans and anti-immigration ads that Republicans flooded the airwaves with. And much of the advantage that centrists previously established over progressives stemmed from their moderation on culture and immigration, rather than economics.

Simultaneously, as the center worked hard to establish distance from the left in the fights of the last election, the left began emphasizing economics a lot more. That also made them slightly more palatable to swing voters overall, because economic liberalism is quite popular with the average voter.

It’s worth thinking about what this means for future elections. Ideology clearly matters — for all the economic pivots both sides have taken, our results show that there is a clear and obvious gap between the left and the center in terms of electability. But that gap is also visibly smaller than it was before.

In fact, for the first time in a while, I actually see some overperformers on the left. (More on this once we get our final results, but I’m particularly impressed by Rep. Chris Deluzio of Pennsylvania.)

That isn’t a prescription for Democrats to run confrontational democratic-socialist-backed candidates in Iowa. The effects of the wave that the party enjoyed in 2026 — driven primarily by an eye-popping 13-point advantage on the House popular vote — were certainly enhanced by strategically running good candidates in the right places.

Our early analysis shows that Democrats flipped four extra Senate seats on the backs of candidate quality this cycle: Kansas, South Carolina, and (probably) Alaska and Mississippi.² All of those candidates ran campaigns that were ideologically moderate in one way or another. It would be a mistake for the party to discard an effect that helped them win considerably more seats, even if it’s smaller in magnitude than it used to be.

But if 2026 is a preview of the 2028 presidential contest in both the partisan environment and in issue salience — in the same way that 2006 Democratic gains foreshadowed Obama’s strong 2008 showing — then it is also entirely possible that more ideologically progressive candidates could still triumph in swing states and suffer a much smaller underperformance penalty than they had in the past.

After all, there isn’t any consistently observed level of overperformance that can bridge the gap between an environment like 2024 — where Republicans held a 2-point advantage — and 2026. The former saw Colin Allred decisively lose a Senate race in Texas despite overperforming by 7 points, while the latter allowed Talarico to win Texas by 12 points despite a smaller (though still strong) overperformance. In 2024, then-Sen. Bob Casey was doomed by a 1-point underperformance in Pennsylvania. Though Abdul El-Sayed underperformed by a similar amount this year, he won Michigan by 7 points.

The Argument and Split Ticket plan to release a full table on the WAR for every candidate once the results are fully tallied. But the vast majority of races are locked in now, with virtually complete counts, and the story they tell is clear: Moderation clearly helped, but moderate politicians had less of an advantage in an election dominated by economic discontent and a backlash against MAGA.
