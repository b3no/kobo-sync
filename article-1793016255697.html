I Was A “You People Can’t Do Anything” Person

Leaning into your mental illness and refusing treatment is bad, actually.

Cartoons Hate Her
Oct 26
∙
Paid
 
READ IN APP
 

Conversations around disability feel very different today than they did when I was younger, partially for the better and partially for the worse. Growing up, I believed that the only disabilities that existed were clearly visible: the need for a wheelchair, blindness, and maybe mental or developmental disabilities, but only insofar as they were severe enough to prevent you from living independently. And the main message, at least from the ‘90s media I consumed, was that people with disabilities might not be able to do everything, but they were determined to do as many things as non-disabled people do, and we should accommodate them but never underestimate them. I was shown media of kids in wheelchairs nevertheless playing basketball, and I made sure to include the girl with cerebral palsy in our Spice Girls dance performance at recess, modulating the dance moves so that she could do them.

It definitely did not occur to me that I was disabled.

But, alas, I am, at least by the 2026 definition. I have ADHD (diagnosed as “ADD” in the ‘00s), OCD, and I come across as autistic enough to be referred to as “that autistic woman from Substack” but for full disclosure I’ve never actually sought a diagnosis for autism, so I’m not counting that one.

It wasn’t until my twenties when my disabilities began impacting my employment that it occurred to me that I was disabled at all. It all started when a travel requirement was foisted upon me after working at a company for a year. Traveling alone was (and still is) a huge trigger for my OCD, and I had kept this under wraps to avoid looking crazy, simply avoiding roles that required it. When I finally opened up about it to my boss, his entire demeanor changed. Suddenly, I wasn’t just difficult, I was disabled, and I could be accommodated by the ADA. Perplexed by this description, I found myself blurting out, “What? I’m not like, crippled or something.”

But my disabilities have impacted me in a bunch of ways I initially disregarded, either as bad luck or just being a loser. My childhood was marred with memories of being yelled at for things I didn’t remember doing, often being admonished for “not listening” when I had no memory of what I was supposed to have heard. In school, I was constantly in detention for boring things like being late and forgetting my homework, never for deliberate delinquency. I scored very well on tests and repeatedly won awards for writing, but had a hard time getting grades higher than a B+, and in math I was lucky to get a C. I graduated college with an embarrassingly low GPA. I was unable to keep a job for more than two years. I attempted, and failed, to learn to drive. Up until recently, after deliberately teaching myself social skills, I was unable to hold onto friends.

People like me are in a particularly precarious spot in 2026 disability discourse, which seems to be ruled by our echelon of disabled people—terminally online people with invisible disabilities. Our dominance in online disability conversations probably exists just by default, because, as Freddie deBoer said on the Christianity On The Spectrum podcast, we are the most likely to be able to have a platform in the first place, certainly compared to someone who is non verbal or unable to use social media. And even among the people who are technically capable of building a platform, attractive and likable people—typically women—will have the easiest time gaining followers and spreading their opinions. This means that, even with good intent, one group of people dominates the discourse. It also means that the people who lean furthest into their identity as a “disabled person,” even when their disabilities, like mine, are treatable, can push others toward leaning into their disabilites, believing themselves to be less capable of daily life functions than they are, and rejecting treatment even when it’s available to them.

Because my disabilities aren’t extreme and binary enough to physically prevent me from doing something (walking, seeing, etc.) there is a lot of gray area between what I am unable to do, versus what I am choosing not to do because it would be emotionally or mentally more difficult for me than it would be for someone else. For example, if I got on an airplane without my husband and kids, I would not explode. I would not vanish into a separate entity a la Severance. Unlike a blind person driving a race car, I am completely physically capable of doing this! It would just cause me intense emotional distress, and I’d likely be sobbing my way through those olives and almonds they hand out, although arguably it might serve as exposure therapy and be good for me long-term. (More on that later.)

But because people like me are technically disabled, it’s easy to identify with our disabilities, lean into what we can’t do (think, the opposite of those ‘90s documentaries about amputees who, nevertheless, can do anything) and declare that because we have a disability, nothing can be done about it, and any attempt to push treatment on us is ableist. Otherwise known as the “You people can’t do anything” effect per this viral tweet:

While the first person, who declared Halloween costumes were too was stressful for the ADHD community, might have been half-joking, there are plenty of people who declare that having ADHD or anxiety, or similar disabilities, actually just prevents them from performing basic life functions, to a degree that, if taken seriously, would warrant living in some kind of group home or having a live-in caretaker. Another viral tweet (which I won’t link because this person is clearly going through enough already) recently declared that anxiety and ADHD prevented them from cooking, cleaning, having people over at their house, and “waiting for people to arrive.” (This person clarified that meeting friends at a restaurant was somehow not a trigger for anxiety.) I remember another person who said that their ADHD prevented them from cooking for themselves, extending even to the ability to microwave a frozen meal, and the only way they could sustain themselves was hot food delivery.

First of all, I don’t think these people are faking the disability they say they have. There are two types of often-derided people in the broad and disparate “online disability community” and they’re two different types of people, but because they’re both terminally online and (at least perceived as) attention-seeking, it can be easy to see them as the same type of person. So, yes, fakers for clout exist, but they are a different group of people:

First, let’s talk about the person who is likely faking their disability entirely. This is a person who claims to have a disability (often autism or something like it) but had no apparent deficits or drawbacks from it—in fact, it’s actually a superpower! They’re “autistic,” but they’ve never struggled to make friends, and their “special interest” is having perfect social skills, and everyone likes them. I’ve seen people say that they are so high-masking that they have to consciously try to unmask, which to me sounds like their “masking” is just being born with good social skills, which is, checks notes, not autism. I recall a time that I wrote about some social skills with which I was struggling, and a person responded that I sounded “insufferable.” Someone else defended me by saying, “She has autism*, it’s not her fault” (*their words, not mine) to which my critic responded, “I have autism too, but I’m not annoying! I have loads of friends!”

(I’m not saying that person was necessarily faking autism, but it is odd to “have autism,” a condition that profoundly affects social skills, but then also say you have a great social life, great social skills, and then look down on anyone who doesn’t have good social skills because it’s never been a problem for you. Alternative explanation: this person had poor social skills, but no insight into it—many such cases!)

Then, there are the You People Can’t Do Anythings (and at certain points in my life, I’ve probably been one of them). These are people with real but treatable disabilities who go the other way—instead of insisting their disabilities are actually superpowers and they have no actual hinderance from them, they claim their disabilities are so debilitating that there is no way they could ever possibly receive treatment or manage their conditions, and instead the entire world needs to revolve around their increasingly implausible demands. It’s not just that they don’t have access to treatment (if they don’t, that’s not their fault) but that they’re confident nothing would ever help anyway. They will point to reasonable accommodations, like ramps for wheelchairs, and then declare that their version of this should be free DoorDash for life and being allowed to show up for work two hours late (see: “time blindness” accommodations) because they have ADHD and we all know ADHD makes it physically impossible to buy food for themselves, let alone cook.

See: this person who claims their anxiety makes it impossible to attend work calls before 10 AM. Why 10 AM? Nobody knows. Clearly anyone who has a problem with this just doesn’t believe in her disability and has no empathy for mental health:

The problem with disability discourse being so online is that these groups of people can happily reinforce each other and hype each other up, where no request is too ridiculous and any suggestion that one take some agency is ableist and privileged. When you are surrounded by an echo chamber telling you that your disability is hopeless and you will never get better, you will start to believe it, and for some disabilities (ie: ADHD and OCD) which require conscious improvements in resiliency and tolerance of discomfort, this belief will actually make the deficits worse. I, too, have fallen into this trap. By far the worst disability I have is OCD, and if I could eliminate my OCD overnight there is basically no amount of money I wouldn’t spend. The problem is that “eliminating” OCD can’t really happen, let alone instantly—you can treat it, you can reach some semblance of “recovery,” but symptoms could return if you’re not vigilant. OCD treatment is difficult. It requires lots of work and often lots of tears and emotional distress. The gold standard is ERP, exposure and response prevention therapy, which is basically a less ridiculous version of those people on Maury with phobias who were chased around the stage by a producer dressed up as their fear:

Basically, you need to expose yourself to your fear or trigger, and then not do anything to make yourself feel better. Sometimes this battle will exist primarily in your own head, where the “compulsion” is ruminating and reassuring yourself about why your fear won’t happen, and you have to just stop doing that. It sounds easy, but it’s really hard. Common compulsions for people with OCD include asking other people for reassurance (“If I had a brain tumor, I’d know by now, right?”) or posting on forums (“Hey, did anyone have this symptom and did it turn out to be nothing?”) or…asking AI. Especially if you also have ADHD, you can be deep into a reassurance spiral before you realize you’re doing it, kind of like how you can read an entire page of a book and still absorb nothing. Moreover, sometimes ADHD can make your OCD fear more plausible and screw up ERP progress. For example, people with OCD often worry they’ve left the stove or iron on, but people with ADHD are more likely to have actually done that, so the ERP tactic of refusing to check and tolerating the uncertainty feels reckless when you actually have zero memory of turning off the stove because you have ADHD.

To make matters worse, ADHD and OCD are often comorbid, so this is an issue with which many people struggle!

For years, I avoided treatment because it just seemed impossible, or at the very least, deeply unpleasant. I was in therapy, mostly to tick a box labeled “therapy” so people would stop bothering me about going to therapy. I would show up at therapy, talk about all the things that were upsetting me, sometimes trick my therapist into giving me reassurance, and then continue Googling and asking Reddit and rushing myself to the ER “just for peace of mind.” I was in therapy, and at times even on medication (which didn’t help) but not actually treating my OCD!

Meanwhile, I expected the world to accommodate my OCD. The restriction on work travel was somewhat reasonable (even if I didn’t have OCD, I don’t think I’d enjoy work travel, so I mostly just avoided applying to jobs that involved travel in the first place.) But in other ways, I demanded everyone cater to my treatable disability: I expected my husband to text me as soon as he got to work so I knew he was safe. I expected my mom to pick up the phone every time I called so I could be assured she hadn’t died in a car accident, and I would be angry if she was unavailable. When I had a baby, I expected that nobody ever take him anywhere unless I was also present, and I sent out a big email of “boundaries” to family members that basically banned any kind of contact with the baby. When I was in the hospital with my second baby and my husband and in-laws took my first child out for ice cream after dinner, I was furious that they had done this without asking me first. (I would have been fine with the outing, but, illogically, not without me present, because it was at night.)

This is not someone who was being “treated” for OCD. I was expecting everyone else to accommodate my OCD beyond what was reasonable, because that was preferable to me over actually getting help and doing the uncomfortable exposures.

Similarly, with my social deficits (whether caused by OCD, ADHD or potential autism) I decided that I was not the problem, and actually, everyone who didn’t want to be my friend was just mean. Sure, some people actually were mean to me, but others were just reacting to genuinely bad social skills and deciding they didn’t want to spend time with me. It took me years to get to the point where I realized that the onus was actually on me to improve my social skills. And while my OCD still gets in the way of my ability to socialize (I don’t like going out alone, which is a hinderance) I’ve been pushing myself to do it anyway, because I can’t expect to be able to bring my family to literally everything. Last year, I didn’t go to parent happy hours because we didn’t have a sitter and I was too anxious to go alone. This year, I went. I’ve missed multiple friends’ weddings because I refused to hire a sitter or leave my kids with anyone, and this is not my friends being inconsiderate by disallowing children, but rather, me refusing to push myself and leaning into my disability beyond what is reasonable.

Beyond the demands I made of others, a part of me thrived on the identity I had carved out for myself. The identity wasn’t fake—I genuinely struggled with these conditions and still do—but I was more interested in being affected by ADHD and OCD than recovering. Granted, I still make jokes about my OCD, and I don’t think it’s bad to accept these conditions with some humor, but for a while I could not tell the difference between my mental illness and my actual personality. A part of me was afraid that if I got better, I wouldn’t have a personality anymore at all. Sometimes, family members would accuse me of “getting something” out of my OCD, which wasn’t exactly true—I still would have chosen to erase my OCD overnight had that been an option—but given how much work recovery would require, I was happier to just accept “being this way” than work on not being this way, especially if I had little faith anything would help.

When you lump things like ADHD and OCD in with things like blindness, and insist that they be treated the same way, you eliminate any option of recovery. One does not commonly recover from being blind, but OCD and ADHD absolutely can be treated. Autistic people can deliberately learn social skills, difficult as it may be. This doesn’t mean people with these disabilities should never be accommodated, but rather that leaning into the identity of someone who “can’t” do things is probably not useful. Treatment for these disabilities may be challenging or unpleasant at times, and yes, some people don’t have access to any kind of treatment options, but for those who do have access, there’s really no excuse for leaning into an identity as a permanently disabled person who “can’t” perform basic life functions, when actually, your disability is treatable and there may actually be a path to recovery. Maybe not a complete elimination of the disability, but help. This doesn’t mean nobody should be given accommodations, but the ADA specifies “reasonable” accommodations, and there is such a thing as an unreasonable request.

Despite everything I’ve said here, I reject the premise that anyone currently struggling with mental illness or a similar disability just isn’t working hard enough on treating it. As I mentioned earlier, not everyone has access to the type of therapy that would actually make a difference. Not everyone can tolerate medication, and medication doesn’t work for everyone. And most importantly, recovery often isn’t linear. I have relapses all the time. Luckily, nothing bad enough to make it impossible to care for myself and others, but bad enough to make life pretty unpleasant for me and annoying for the people around me. There is a middle ground between romanticizing and identifying with an identity as a person who “can’t do anything,” and demanding that every mentally ill person be currently in the process of an intense treatment plan, no matter their circumstances, and if they ever have a bad day, they’re clearly failing to treat their illness. I didn’t start taking ERP seriously until my thirties, mostly because I didn’t want OCD to impact my kids. I anticipate that many other people who currently seem complacent with their mental illness will be on a similar trajectory.

But helping those people to reach that point will be easier if we do not reinforce the importance of leaning into these disabilities as an identity and demanding the world around you change—often in particularly implausible ways—to accommodate your lack of treatment. Instead of insisting you can’t do anything, take pride in what you can do, and push yourself to do the things that feel hard. Not because it makes you less annoying to everyone else (although it almost certainly does) but because you will be better for it.
