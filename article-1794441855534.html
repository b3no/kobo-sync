An N.B.A. Player Got Hooked on Sports Betting. Soon, He Became the Bet

Jontay Porter got deep into FanDuel, and in debt to the wrong people. The only way out, it seemed, was to gamble on himself.

By Nick Berardini

Explore the full story, accompanied by interactive graphics and video, at newyorker.com.

When Jontay Porter was ten, his father woke him up most mornings before dawn. He and his siblings piled into the family’s Sprinter van and drove to an empty field house off the highway. Then, while their friends slept, they ran basketball drills with their father. “All I remember is it still being pitch-black outside, and the two dreaded double doors on the side of the building that would always be left open for us,” Jontay told me recently. In the bleary moments just before one of his siblings tugged the field-house doors open, Jontay always secretly wished that they’d be locked. And, one morning, they were. Jontay smiled as he climbed back into the van and drifted off to sleep. But his brother Michael, Jr., who was seventeen months older, was furious. “Michael was the opposite,” Jontay said. “He wanted to go.”

Jontay grew up in Columbia, Missouri, the fourth of eight children. His father, Michael, Sr., worked in Christian ministry, and his mother, Lisa, homeschooled the children. Both had played college basketball—Lisa, as a high-school senior, had averaged a nearly impossible fifty-eight points per game—and they saw the sport as their children’s ticket to college. All eight of the kids eventually got basketball scholarships. By the time Jontay was sixteen, he was six feet ten. In most families, as a five-star high-school recruit, Jontay would have been the main attraction. But Michael was Mozart. In 2010, their father got a job on the coaching staff for the women’s team at the University of Missouri. The men’s coach spotted Michael shooting alone in the arena one night and offered him a full scholarship; he was still in seventh grade. When he accepted it, as a high-school senior, he was the top recruit in the country. His highlight reels had racked up millions of views on YouTube. N.B.A. scouts believed that he could be the next Kevin Durant. “He’s a game-changer,” a league executive told the New York Post.

Big state schools turn their best athletes into local celebrities, but the hype that followed Michael to Missouri’s campus on his recruiting visits was closer to Beatlemania. Fans asked for autographs and took selfies. Jontay, who came along, was seduced by the hysteria. “Frat houses are putting up posters, and he’s literally treated like a king,” he recalled. “I wanted to be a part of that circus.” He skipped his senior year of high school to join the team, too. The brothers were best friends. Still, it was sometimes hard to be one of the top players in the country but just the second best in his family. “He got all the attention,” Jontay said. “I was just kind of his biggest fan.”

For a time that first season, Michael was sidelined with a back injury, giving Jontay a turn in the spotlight. “He’s the best player I’ve ever been around,” Chris Hollender, then an assistant coach at Mizzou, told me. “I’ve always said I thought he was a more complete player than Mike.” At eighteen, Jontay led his team to the N.C.A.A. tournament. “For me to finally get a taste of stardom?” Jontay said. “I was the man. I controlled the narrative.”

Michael went to the N.B.A. draft that summer. Jontay planned to enter the following year. Then, during the final scrimmage before his sophomore season, he pushed off his right leg to explode toward the rim. “I felt, like, every crack in that moment, and just crumpled to the ground,” he said. He had torn the major ligaments in his knee. His teammates lifted him off the ground and laid him on a table in the training room. “He was beside himself,” Hollender said. “Couldn’t catch his breath, he was crying so hard.” It wasn’t from the pain; Jontay knew what his injury meant for the team’s chances that season. “The only thing he said to me was ‘I’m so sorry,’ ” Hollender said. “It was gut-wrenching.”

In 2019, while Jontay was in rehab for his knee, he injured it again. “Everybody’s telling me, ‘You’re going to be O.K.,’ ” he said. “I knew it wasn’t true.” By then, Michael was a rookie with the Denver Nuggets. (He went on to win a championship with the team, in 2023.) But Jontay, watching the N.B.A. draft in his parents’ basement, saw sixty picks go by without a team calling his name. Eventually, the Memphis Grizzlies took a chance and signed him to a contract as a free agent. He loved the N.B.A.—luxury travel, assistants to run his errands, last-minute reservations at Michelin-starred restaurants—but he felt that he was a tourist there. He had to take painkillers just to play, and after games he had to stick needles into his knees to drain fluid, in order to keep them from swelling. After just two seasons, he was cut from the roster.

In September, 2021, Jontay was in Memphis, living like any other twenty-one-year-old kid—except for the money. He’d made two million dollars from his N.B.A. salary, and had received an insurance settlement for another $2.7 million (the difference between his projected N.B.A. earnings before his injury and his actual deal). One Sunday, he invited some friends over to watch football. Sports betting had recently been legalized in several states, and apps like FanDuel and DraftKings, which previously hosted fantasy-sports contests, were suddenly the biggest sportsbooks in America. Jontay had always avoided them; it seemed improper for an N.B.A. player to bet even on other sports. But now he was out of the league, and had started dabbling in sports gambling. He opened up FanDuel and placed a hundred-and-twenty-five-thousand-dollar bet that the Kansas City Chiefs would beat the Baltimore Ravens. He put another ten thousand dollars on a parlay—a riskier bet that strings together multiple outcomes to increase the payout—wagering that the Chiefs would win, that their running back would rush more than his expected yards, and that the Ravens’ quarterback, Lamar Jackson, would run for a touchdown himself.
