Treat OCD Like a Horror Movie Demon

The mental health trick just crazy enough to work: pretending to be possessed by a demon

I basically only write about my OCD when it’s not going very well. Given that my OCD centers around scary diseases (especially my loved ones having or catching them) I did not do very well during the womp-womp hantavirus “pandemic” that Zero Covid Twitter prophesized. And today, I’m writing about my OCD because—shocker—it’s been beating my ass lately.

But the silver lining in all of this is that I can finally articulate the only way I’ve ever been able to “defeat” my OCD (albeit in an ongoing, never-ending way…but thankfully, in a way that anyone can do). TLDR: I treat it like a demonic entity, although to be clear, I am fully aware it is not actually a demonic entity. (Can’t wait for the gossip that appears three months later: “Did you know CHH literally sought an exorcism for anxiety one time? What a millennial white woman thing to do.”)

While traveling this summer I was unable to meet my therapist for ERP (ie: exposure therapy, the gold standard for OCD). Add in a bunch of new locations and triggers, and it’s a perfect storm. I won’t get into details, but this month I became very worried about a loved one having a fatal disease with 1 in a million prevalence. I’m assuming that a normal person would never have Googled enough to discover this disease in the first place, nor would they worry about it after discovering it, but what can I say? I’m not normal. I’m a heckin’ mentally ill chungus.

Anyway, because this disease is so rare, I blew through almost all the medical literature on it in about a day. Still not reassured that my loved one was safe, I turned to AI, but I had to cheat with the free ChatGPT version because nice and ethical Claude refused to “reinforce my OCD.” The more reassurance and research I sought, the worse everything got. What’s worse, I already knew this would happen because it has happened many times before. But I was so worried about this disease, blinded by the degree of anxiety, that I was convinced that if I just read something that could “rule it out,” I would feel better. Narrator: She did not, in fact, feel better.

Once I realized I had to turn this around, I remembered a “trick” I had used the last time I was able to crawl out of the rabbit hole and actually stop my reassurance-seeking and Googling compulsions. This trick sounds a bit crazy, but sometimes you have to fight crazy with crazy. Basically: I simply pretend that my OCD is an otherworldly demonic entity.

I shouldn’t have to add this disclaimer, but just in case: no, your OCD is not literally a demonic entity. I’m sure someone out there has “demonic entity possession OCD” and this is very triggering for them, so without offering too much reassurance, just be aware that I’m speaking metaphorically. People with OCD do not actually hear demonic voices or experience hallucinations and if that’s actually happening, you should see a doctor. Also, I’m just a person with OCD, not a therapist. If you’re looking for an OCD therapist to follow on here, try Jenna Overbaugh, LPC.

Anyway, the last time I was able to essentially put my OCD into recovery (I define this as going days at a time without thinking about my OCD or being very bothered by it) I repeatedly referred to this text from my brother, who told me, during a particularly rough time for my OCD, that I should just ignore every thought I have because I’m insane:

It sounds ridiculously simple, but it’s both simple and hard. Simple because it’s an easy concept to grasp, and hard because it’s very difficult to put it into practice. How do you know which thoughts to ignore? What if that thought is not OCD? So to make things easier, I will reframe OCD in my favorite movie genre: horror.

Imagine you’re the protagonist of a horror movie. Your life is going along normally, until you are randomly plagued by horrific thoughts. These could be disturbing images, worries about things that are technically possible but unlikely, fears that you’ve emailed everyone at your job a racial slur despite never doing anything like that before, or anything else that distresses you but exists (at least as far as the evidence goes) mostly or entirely in your mind.

You discover, from a priest, rabbi, ghostbuster, or some other trusted demon-acquainted authority, that you are possessed by a demon. Luckily, this demon doesn’t feed off anyone’s blood and it’s not going to kill anyone. Instead, it feeds off of attention and its only endgame is to torment you and make your life difficult. If the demon can get any kind of reaction out of you, it becomes stronger. If the demon is unable to spark a reaction, it gets weaker. If you’re able to starve the demon of any attention or reaction for a long and consistent period of time, it will shrivel up and deactivate.

But ignoring the demon is hard, because it is very good at sending you believable delusions, often about the things which mean the most to you. It can hijack your brain and make you believe—genuinely believe, with incredibly urgency—that horrible things are happening and you’d have to be crazy not to try and resolve it. And what if the thing it’s warning you about is real? After all, it’s entirely possible—albeit very unlikely—that you accidentally attached a photo of your hog to that email you sent to your team at work. The demon will try to convince you that you did this, or that there’s even a slight chance you did this. To goal: to get you to check your sent emails to find out if you did it. If the demon can get you to check more than once, it becomes even stronger.

But not so fast! Even engaging with the thought can also feed the demon. If you respond to the demon by saying, “No, there’s no way I sent a dick pic to my team at work because I don’t even have one on my computer and I haven’t taken a dick pic in years,” that’s still feeding it. The only way to starve it is to say, “Not today, Demon,” and then do something else.

The demon might try to trick you into thinking you don’t really love your girlfriend. The demon might try to convince you that you are dating the wrong gender entirely and that you’re in denial about your true sexual orientation. The demon might try to convince you that you’re a dangerous or bad person. The demon might try to convince you that, because a random number popped into your head, you have had a prophecy of something bad happening on a particular year. The demon might try to convince you that you’ve committed an egregious and unforgivable religious sin. The demon might try to convince you that because your spouse left for work two hours ago and hasn’t texted you yet even though no text was promised, that they died in a car accident. The demon might try to convince you that you caught rabies because you don’t technically know if a bat bit you in your sleep, despite the fact that you live in a Philadelphia apartment and have never seen a bat outside of a zoo. And, in my case, the demon will try to convince you that someone you love has a rare disease that will show only subtle symptoms until it kills them, and that you’ll never be able to enjoy your life again until you have 100% certainty that they don’t have the disease.

What the demon is proposing isn’t really that important. Once you stop responding to a particular thought, the demon might try something else. That’s another thing to remember—your demon is as smart as you are, and it knows your brain well enough to trick you really effectively. If you classify your demon as “the sexual orientation demon,” then you will be blindsided when your demon appears with a new thought about how you don’t know for sure that all the windows in your house are locked.

So how do you know when something is coming from the demon? If you even have to ask that question, it is. If 99% of your thoughts that caused this much anxiety in the past—if not 100%—were caused by the demon, the statistics indicate that whatever is going on now is likely also the demon. It’s possible that the demon will warn you of something which also coincidentally happens to be true, but most likely, that’s never actually happened before, or it’s only happened about relatively low-stakes things. If a true emergency is happening, you’ll know, but also, even engaging with, “Well, would I know? What if nobody else notices what I notice?” is further strengthening the demon, you fool!

There is, perhaps, a small risk to ignoring a thought that might be a genuine alarm, but given your demon’s history of making shit up, there is another risk: listening to the demon and continuing to be tormented for your whole life. Are you going to take a 0.00001% risk that a bad thing is actually happening (something you still probably can’t control anyway) or take the 99.999% risk that your demon will become stronger with more attention?

But don’t spend too long trying to figure out if a thought comes from the demon. That alone is providing attention to it, and feeding it. But if you notice any of these themes, it’s probably the demon:

  • (About something which is not actually an immediate, obvious emergency, such as a car accident happening right in front of you) urgency to figure this out NOW, or else you’ll never be able to enjoy life or relax again.

    • Bonus points if you need certainty about something before a big event, like a vacation or holiday. Your demon will convince you that you’ll have an awful time until you know for sure (and you probably won’t know for sure no matter what you do anyway.) Moreover, even if you are somehow able to get perfect reassurance about this thing, the demon will ensure that it brings a different urgent thought to you just in time for your vacation. Do not fall for this.

  • Something that other people have repeatedly dismissed or which sounds absurd when spoken out loud.

  • Something you’ve already worried about a million times in the past (or something very similar to things you’ve worried about in the past)

  • Any fear that you did something horrible and don’t remember it.

  • Any fear that you are a bad or dangerous person because of an unpleasant thought you had.

  • A suggestion that if you only knew exactly how common or uncommon something bad was, you’d be able to stop worrying about it happening.

Basically, if there’s even a slight chance that this thought comes from the demon, it almost definitely does.

The demon is also very good at obfuscating its presence, and if you do things right, there will be a moment where it senses you aren’t feeding it anymore and it will try new things until something sticks. Do not allow the demon to trick you with a secondary fear or thought.

Eventually, things will get better. The first week ignoring the demon will be really hard. You might cry. You will have to just keep reminding yourself that the demon is shrinking, starving and shriveling even if you’re also having a shitty time. Your pain is the demon’s pain. Eventually, you will come out the other end. After a week or two, the thoughts will become less upsetting, and perhaps less frequent. After months of ignoring the demon, you might forget the demon was ever there.

This is where a lot of people (including myself) have gotten tripped up. While it’s important to ignore the demon, you should never—again, no matter how long it’s been since the demon has bothered you—assume it’s not still there. No matter how weak and inactivated the demon is, it can still come back whenever it sees an opening. That “curious” Google search you’re considering, about a topic which isn’t even that distressing but feels mildly anxiety-provoking and you’d rather just know now? That’s the demon trying something new. That reassurance-seeking question about a topic that you know is probably silly, but you’d feel better if you knew for sure? Also the demon.

It’s somewhat easy to reframe OCD as a demon. It’s a bit harder to actually ignore OCD the way you’d ignore a demon. And it’s much harder to stay vigilant about a demon who isn’t bothering you very much anymore. But just remember: the cost of listening to your demon—your OCD—will almost always be higher than ignoring it.
