We need to understand “health care costs” better

Plus multiparty America, a long-lived Lenin, and much more

To just toss a hot take into the permitting reform discourse, one thing that I think we see in these debates is that there is way too much non-park federally owned land in the American West, and that creates some very confusing exchanges about activities happening on “public lands.” It’s good to have parks. And obviously there’s lots of otherwise useful infrastructure that you wouldn’t want to put in a park. But if you look at the federal land portfolio, it’s 12.5 percent run by the National Park Service and then 14 percent run by the Fish and Wildlife Service. A majority of the land, though, is run by either the U.S. Forest Service or else the Bureau of Land Management, and that land is designed for economic exploitation — logging, mining, ranching, and so forth — in a way that just somewhat arbitrarily excludes uses like housing development.

I think we should have more real parks and more real wildlife preservation and then more privately owned land that’s put to its highest and best use by market forces. A lot of what passes for “public land” preservation is in practice a kind of subsidy-masquerading-as-conservation to favored industries involved in natural resource extraction.

Peter: I’m just retired from the tech industry, and need something to do after I finish Slow Boring in the morning. You’ve mentioned in the past that you thought there was not enough analytical work on policy related questions. There have also been examples of someone with a computer providing valuable data to the world, for example during Covid. So, what’s your list of topics that you wish someone would get to the bottom of?

To pick just one example, because it’s a big topic and it’s been on my mind recently, the whole policy community has a very poor grasp of what exactly is going on with health care costs.

That’s in part because both the “health care” and the “costs” parts of the phrase “health care costs” are ambiguous.

Obviously, “health care” is more expensive in 2026 than it was in 2006, but also the bundle of things that we refer to as “health care” changes over time. Some of that is because new stuff gets invented, some of it is because standards of care shift, some of it is because the institutional structure of health care providers is always changing, some of it is because the demographic composition of the population is changing … it’s very complicated. Now, of course, it’s also true that “a mobile phone” in 2026 is very different from “a mobile phone” in 2006. But because it’s so clearly the case that the quality-adjusted price of computer stuff has fallen rather than risen, we don’t sweat these details. With health care, the details of what is in the bundle seem more complicated but also more important to understand.

And then we have “costs.” Does this mean the price of individual services? Or does it mean the cost to consumers of buying an insurance plan? Or does it mean aggregate national health expenditures?

I don’t want to represent that I’m the only person who is making these points or that policy people have no understanding of the distinction. But even the most basic issues often get mixed up in the discourse, and I think there is a genuine lack of granular understanding.

There is a classic 2003 finding by Gerard Anderson, Uwe Reinhardt, and colleagues that “it’s the prices, stupid” in terms of why American per capita health care spending is so much higher than spending in other countries. This is contested in some quarters by people making the argument that international variation in health spending is well-predicted by household income. But that does not per se rebut Anderson and Reinhardt’s claim that the difference is causally accounted for by variance in prices.

To illustrate the difference, American household spending on cars is much higher than Italian household spending on cars. And in a statistical sense, you could say this is largely explained by Americans being richer than Italians. But the way this manifests itself is that Americans have more cars per capita and also our cars are larger on average than Italian cars. It’s not that car prices are systematically higher in the United States than in Italy. And yet, it is true that the average car sold in the United States is more expensive than the average car sold in Italy. The Italo-American conglomerate Stellantis’s bestselling vehicle in Italy is the Fiat Panda hatchback, while its bestselling vehicle in the United States is a Ram 1500 pickup. Italians buy smaller, cheaper vehicles.

The Anderson/Reinhardt contention is that the U.S.-European gap in health spending is not like the gap in car spending — that literally the prices are causing the divergence.

But note that research is over 20 years old at this point! Michael Chernew wrote a piece for Health Affairs in January titled “Growth in National Health Expenditures: It’s Not the Prices, Stupid” making the case that recent changes in American nominal health spending are not driven by inflationary price rises. That does not contradict Anderson and Reinhardt’s claims — which, after all, were about levels 20+ years ago — but it does suggest a different focus for the recent period and is also a reminder that even if it was the prices, stupid, the situation may have changed since then.

Chernew attributes the recent change to shifts in the “volume and intensity of care,” and he breaks this down to some extent. But you can see if you read the piece that there is not that much granular understanding of exactly what’s happening here.

The other thing, though, is that health care is a consumption good that has a quasi-objective purpose. If we discover that people are increasing their volume and intensity of shoe consumption, then I think we figure that if buying shoes is making people happy, there’s nothing wrong with that. Health care is different, though. It’s possible to sell people health care services that aren’t genuinely useful. “Doctor,” after all, existed as a profession long before scientifically reliable medical care was available. But at the same time, treating and preventing disease is so useful that normal people think it is morally impermissible for their fellow citizens to go without useful treatment merely due to inability to pay. So we really want to have some assessment of the price of medical services relative to their usefulness. Is the “intensity” of care increasing in ways that are leading to dramatically better outcomes, or are health care providers just getting better at upselling? Probably both things are happening to some extent. But there is a lot of room for inquiry here.

Ken: I agree the forces pushing members of Congress to be posters are strong. Being a member of Congress, as I have observed, is generally speaking not a super-lucrative job or anything. If we assess them primarily based on their posting, they will try to please us with their posts. Richard Underwood: Alternate history question! What if Vladimir Lenin lived longer, say until 1944? How do you believe his policies would have differed from that of Stalin?

I’m skeptical of the “Stalin ruined everything” narrative personally.

In terms of improvements the purges, ethnic policies and distrust of bourgeois experts would have been less severe in my opinion, otherwise the general direction would have been quite similar.

I suspect the famines would have been just as bad (but Trotskyist are adamant that is rapid industrialization plan would would have prevented the 1930s famine, if so maybe Lenin supports this plan?) but I’m completely unsure if Lenin would have adopted the “Social Facist” strategy leading into Germany’s 1932 election.

I learned all about counterfactual history in a college course on the philosophy of history that Robert Nozick co-taught with Russian historian Eric Lohr, specifically focusing on the Russian Revolution. So I have a lot of thoughts on this.

Nozick felt pretty strongly that once Lenin consolidated power and won the Russian Civil War, the die was more or less cast. One of his sub-takes on this was that it’s a bias of left-wing intellectuals to overemphasize the purges (which impacted a lot of left-wing elites) relative to the mass rural casualties associated with collectivization and the Holodomor and so forth. I tend to agree that due to Lenin’s greater personal stature inside the party, he’d have felt less inclined to engage in so much purging, so depending on how focused you are on that, it might have been a big deal. But the point Nozick would make about Stalin is that he was authentically centrally positioned in terms of the big policy conflicts inside the Politburo in the mid-1920s. Trotsky and his circle wanted to move more quickly to end the New Economic Policy (NEP) — wherein Lenin restored some private enterprise and market competition to save the country from economic collapse — and transition aggressively to a crash industrialization program, which almost certainly would have turned out broadly similar to Stalin’s policies.

Speaking from the standpoint of 2026 rather than 2001, though, I think the relative success of People’s Republic of China economic policy combined with durable Communist Party control raises the question of whether Nikolai Bukharin’s proposal to retain the NEP indefinitely couldn’t have worked. His vision of the economy — state control of the “commanding heights,” such as banking and steel, combined with wide scope for private enterprise — is a lot like 21st-century China. There was a claim associated with late-1990s globalization enthusiasts that it would be impossible to retain single-party political control in a country with meaningful economic freedom, but I think we keep learning that this isn’t really true. So I do think there was another path for the U.S.S.R., though I don’t know that it’s plausible to believe Lenin would have taken it.

Max Haas: Would it actually change anything about our politics if we were a multi-party democracy? And what do you think all the relevant parties would be?

I think the dynamics around this are a little bit odd. The people most inclined to push for proportional representation are people on the far left, because they are annoyed by being junior partners in the Democratic Party. But even though the electoral penalty for being ideologically extreme is real, it’s not so big as to be insurmountable.

“Win a primary and then get a little bit lucky with the national political climate and then Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez becomes president” is not a crazy plan. If you shift to a proportional representation system, then the left itself is going to split into two sub-parties: one that’s inclined to join coalitions with center-left and centrist parties and one that’s more like a pure protest-vote party.

Conversely, moderate Republicans hold extremely little practical power in current American politics because they are regularly outvoted by conservatives inside the Republican coalition. In a P.R. system, something like the Larry Hogan Party would be relatively small but would very frequently control the central seats of parliament. A good amount of the time, it’d be in a position to decide which coalition governs and to set the terms of the policy debate. But I never hear moderate Republicans arguing for proportional representation.

CleverBeast: El Salvador’s intervention in Haiti seems to be going well, and for the time being has local support. International media also seems to be unusually quiet about El Salvador’s actions—in a way I doubt would be the case if any western country were involved.

Nonetheless, as a moral question, should the United States have gotten itself involved before Haiti’s gang problem got this bad? As a political question, do you think there is any appetite left in America for humanitarian military interventions of this sort?

My main observation about this is that it’s hard to really get information about what’s going on in Haiti. I think something that’s easy to lose sight of in the internet era, when it feels like we are more inundated with news than ever before, is that it’s expensive to maintain reporting operations in dangerous foreign places and the revenue model for doing it well has gotten worse.

So when we say that international media is “unusually quiet” about this, it’s not just that it isn’t dominating the front pages the way it might if the United States had sent troops in — I think there are legitimately very few reporters on the ground explaining what’s happening. I’m reminded that every once in a while I try to see if there are semi-reliable casualty or excess-death counts in Sudan, and the answer is basically that there aren’t. It’s not like the media is “ignoring” the civil war there; the broad strokes of it get covered, but I don’t think there’s a ton of audience interest in the coverage, and producing more and more in-depth coverage would be expensive, so it’s not happening.

If you want to be optimistic about the Salvadoran operation, I would start with the question of why Nayib Bukele’s crime crackdown has worked so well while “mano dura” policies have frequently failed elsewhere in Latin America. I sometimes get the sense from following American rightists on Twitter that they think Bukele was the first and only person to ever try a harsh militarized approach to gang enforcement in the Western Hemisphere. But Ecuador’s president was elected on an explicit platform of copying Bukele, and so far at least, it’s not working very well. And Felipe Calderón’s efforts to wage war on the cartels in Mexico failed spectacularly and kind of destroyed his whole political party. This whole topic is way outside my wheelhouse, but the best explanation I’ve found of the difference is that El Salvador’s gangs didn’t really have a revenue model outside of localized extortion. In Mexico and Ecuador, by contrast, the gangs have drug franchises. Because there’s a lot of money to be made in the drug trade, there’s a real tendency for a “next man up” dynamic to emerge when you arrest drug traffickers. That’s especially true because a semi-successful crackdown on drug trafficking actually makes trafficking more lucrative for those who get away with it. This also tends to lead to corruption of the security forces doing the crackdown.

The Haitian gangs, as I understand it, are structurally more like the Salvadoran gangs in this regard. They’re not doing anything particularly profitable except direct predation on other Haitians, so if you can dislodge them, the whole business model collapses and it’s not necessarily the case that anyone takes their place.

On the skeptical front, Haiti is geographically larger than El Salvador and has a higher population. The 2,000 or so troops El Salvador has sent to Haiti are a tiny force compared with what Bukele deployed domestically, and El Salvador is just much too small a country to really impose order at scale in Haiti unless it is able to organize some kind of large indigenous Haitian force to follow its lead. I don’t want to say that’s impossible, but it is a bit of an “assume a can opener” kind of situation. The United States is different and obviously could send a military force that would be very large relative to the Haitian population. There is also a lot of history and failure and ill will there. Certainly, if you look over the broad arc of the 21st century, I would say a large American military intervention in Haiti would have made more sense than a large intervention in Iraq. But on the politics, I think Americans are really deeply and profoundly sick of trying to solve foreign countries’ problems and there would be zero appetite at this point for anything other than very light logistical assistance.

Scott Bieber: During the election, like-minded readers could amplify by supporting candidates financial. Now the discourse has moved to the interpretation of the blue tsunami. What can we do to strengthen the case that cultural moderation, abundance and especiallyheterodox positions on energy overperformed electorally and should be broadly encouraged, rather than merelytolerated.

I think it’s worth just nudging people a little. For example, Annie Andrews’s pollsters did a presentation this week about her race that seems to have included the factoid that “apparently the transgender issue actually didn’t really hurt them too much.”

A lot of Democrats will read that and think, “Wow, even in South Carolina the transgender issue actually didn’t hurt her.” But what happened is she moved to the center on the issue, got attacked for flip-flopping, and in that context the issue didn’t really hurt them too much.

On energy, one point I am trying (with limited success) to convey to people is that not only did a lot of successful Democrats explicitly moderate on energy, but basically every Democrat slammed Trump’s war in Iran for raising the cost of living. That’s great. But everyone who has ever slammed Trump’s war in Iran for raising the cost of living should understand that the mechanism by which that happened was inducing scarcity of fossil fuel supplies to the global market. If you think that’s bad, it has implications for other policy areas!
