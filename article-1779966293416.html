Union enthusiasts should support abundance

You want it to be one way, but it’s the other.

Matthew Yglesias
May 28
∙
Paid
 
READ IN APP
 

Kate Andrias and Alexander Hertel-Fernandez produced a report for the Roosevelt Institute back in April, arguing that abundance-oriented advocates and intellectuals should do more to cater to labor union interests.

When I heard them present a version of this argument at a conference earlier this year, I raised what I thought were some good points during Q&A, namely that to create a labor/abundance synthesis, the change should probably go in the opposite direction. Abundance, as it currently exists and is articulated, has a lot to offer American labor unions on the merits, and people who believe in the union movement ought to make that point to labor leaders themselves and encourage them to adopt the abundance-related ideas that would be good for unions. The authors clearly do not agree, so I’m going to restate my argument here.

Before we get to that, though, I want to flag that my less interesting critique of their paper is that they are not taking the dynamics of pro-union politics seriously enough.

The union’s job is always to ask for more.

If you’re a backbench legislator representing a safe seat, then there’s no particular problem with saying “yes” to every ask from every union. That won’t actually translate into policy because the mayor or the governor or the president or the frontliners in the legislature will say no to at least some stuff. The unions define as “pro labor” the backbenchers who back them on everything, and that sets the bar for what it takes to be a “pro-labor” politician.

The problem is that you can’t actually run a city or a state or a country on the basis of saying yes to everything that every labor union asks for. It doesn’t work. It doesn’t even really make sense, conceptually, because a union that’s getting everything it asks for just isn’t asking for enough stuff.

If you want to formulate a governing agenda, that agenda can’t be “give labor everything they ask for.” This is why the authors don’t really engage with specifics. The Washington Teachers’ Union was assertively campaigning for prolonged school closures during last winter’s snowstorms. Do Andrias and Hertel-Fernandez really think everyone should just reflexively agree with that ask regardless of the broader impact on the city?

I’m skeptical.

But enough complaining! Because the main thing I have to say is that embracing abundance would be good for labor.

The forgotten conflicts

Labor-oriented intellectuals are often eager to pick fights with moderates who sometimes disagree with some labor unions about some public policy issues, but they forget that these conflicts run in multiple directions.

The International Brotherhood of Electrical Workers, for example, loves data centers.

The United Mine Workers of America used to be bedrock progressives, but they haven’t endorsed a Democratic Party presidential candidate since 2008 because they think Democrats are too hostile to mining. They did endorse Andy Beshear for governor in Kentucky, though, and, not coincidentally, Beshear declined to seek an endorsement from the Sierra Club. The United Auto Workers kept criticizing the Biden administration for being too aggressive in its promotion of electric cars. The umbrella group of building trades unions supported the Keystone XL pipeline. The Steelworkers represent people in the oil and gas industry.

The common thread here is that private-sector labor unions sometimes fight the bosses for a bigger slice of the pie and sometimes they align with the bosses to fight for more pie.

The business/labor alliance is sometimes more correct on the merits (like with the Keystone issue) and other times it’s wrong (like with coal-fired power plants). But what I think really matters here is that nobody in the progressive NGO space actually consistently takes the labor side of these disputes.

Which is fine. In fact, it’s correct. Some people think that we should dole out longer prison sentences. But no progressive intellectuals are going to say we should dole out longer prison sentences because that would create good union jobs.

Still, I seriously think labor-loving progressive intellectuals should take more seriously the possibility that abundance is on the right side of a lot of these energy policy disputes and the green groups are wrong.

Labor is a strong voice for nuclear power. The idea of arbitrarily favoring small businesses over large chains makes no sense from a labor perspective. There are just plenty of topics where the abundance side of a factional dispute is more pro-labor, even if abundance takesters are less likely to engage in abstract paeans to labor unions.

Meanwhile, many abundance priorities are important to the future of labor in a way that I think labor unions sometimes underrepresent.

Unions should get to yes on new construction

The history of labor and the YIMBY movement in California is a bit vexing, because it started with a promising idea.

YIMBYs arrived in Sacramento with a good analysis of housing policy but limited political clout. The building trades unions had a lot of clout and an obvious interest in construction issues, so the first batch of YIMBY bills that passed almost all included union-friendly riders. The details are complicated, but generally speaking the partnership makes a lot of sense:

  1. Land use restrictions create enormous deadweight loss.

  2. Upzoning unlocks a lot of economic surplus.

  3. These bills can require union labor and let workers appropriate a share of that surplus to get the bill passed while still leaving plenty of surplus for everyone else.

The problem was that the labor provisions took such a big bite out of the surplus that basically nothing was getting built. Since then, YIMBYs have been fighting for legislation that gives less away, which has created some conflict with labor interests.

But this is a choice for labor interests.

Some of the trades have been a bit more flexible because if and only if you actually get stuff built do you create new union jobs. Of course, whether you actually want to create new union jobs is a question internal to union politics.

Conceptually, a labor union interested in a long-term future needs to organize new people and grow. But in a practical sense, a union is accountable to its current membership and may prefer to stay small as long as its little slice of the universe is protected.

I think that labor-friendly intellectuals want labor unions to grow, but it’s up to them to try to convince unions to actually prioritize this. California and other blue states can have growing unionized construction sectors, but only if the building trades actually work to make that outcome happen — and making that outcome happen requires enough flexibility to make projects economically feasible.

This is even more true on transportation.

The California high-speed rail fiasco is not delivering transportation benefits, so the High-Speed Rail Authority has started justifying it in terms of jobs created.

This has in turn created some backlash whereby conservatives blame labor interests for this turning into such a boondoggle. But to the best of my understanding, labor featherbedding is really not a significant factor in why the cost of American train projects tends to go off the rails. Labor costs are higher in the United States than in Spain or Italy or France or Korea because the United States is richer. But the astronomical project cost differences you see are driven by profound failures of project management and planning.

What is true is that labor has not been a strong voice for reform.

In part that’s because there is some featherbedding happening. But largely it’s because, from the union perspective, what matters is that the project gets built, so they tend to just cheerlead for anything rather than care about planning, project management, and execution.

This is short-sighted, though. If California actually did build high-speed rail from Los Angeles to San Francisco on a reasonable timetable and at reasonable cost, the state would go on to build extensions to San Diego and Las Vegas. There would be more interest in upgrading the Boston to Washington corridor. If that happened, there would be a strong argument for extensions south from D.C. to Atlanta and west from Philadelphia to Pittsburgh, Cleveland, and Chicago. But nobody’s going to do that on the basis of a white elephant stub line between Modesto and Fresno that nobody rides.

The same is even more true for urban mass transit. If America could build with the per-kilometer costs of Italy or France, a ton of projects would pencil out. At our actual costs, the only projects that pencil out are small ones in New York City. The wonks in this space could really use some political help in rectifying this situation, and labor interests would genuinely benefit from better-designed, better-executed projects.

Beyond the building trades

I wish labor would reflect more on the reality that almost all of the growth in the United States is happening in right-to-work states.

Some of that is because businesses prefer to invest in right-to-work states. But that’s not all of it. If it were the case that union-friendly labor laws had just devastated the economies of the Pacific Coast and the Northeast, then those states would be cheap. But demand to live in New York and New Jersey and Connecticut and Massachusetts and Maryland and California remains robust. If it were easier to build houses and apartments in those states, more people would live in them. If more people lived in them, a non-zero number of jobs would move with them. More jobs in states with union-friendly labor laws would create more opportunities to organize.

It would also mean much healthier pension situations for the large public-sector unions in those states.

If a city is growing, it’s relatively easy for it to meet its obligations to retired cops and teachers and firefighters. If it’s shrinking, that’s harder. If a city is growing, it will hire more cops and teachers and firefighters. If it’s shrinking, some of them will get laid off.

I know some public-sector unions fight for narrow, occupation-specific housing set-asides — things like special discounted housing for teachers. I get why that’s where their heads go, but it’s short-sighted and narrow-minded in comparison to just saying “the YIMBYs are right and we should change zoning.”

I don’t expect this to ever be a top priority for unions outside of the building trades. But I want to go on the record as saying it would be impactful.

Dem politicians in blue states hoping for upward mobility in their careers care a lot about getting gold stars from party-aligned interest groups. It would make a real difference if unions said they thought increasing the population growth rate of high-cost blue states was important. By all means, let us YIMBYs do the heavy lifting. Just don’t sabotage us. And acknowledge that we’re correct, because we are.

Correct analysis is important

Of course, the abundance community is not going to advocate for more housing growth because it would be good for teacher pensions or would help prevent the erosion of union-friendly labor law. It’s just true that housing abundance would be good for those things. It’s true that energy abundance is more in line with what labor unions need than conventional green politics is. And it’s true that if our infrastructure delivery in this country were less fucked, we would undertake more projects and create more jobs.

One nice thing about being correct is that when you have a correct analysis of a situation, you can identify stakeholders who benefit and try to convince them to join you.

I think a big problem on the left is that accurate policy analysis has become underrated relative to a kind of coalition slop. So instead of addressing green/labor conflicts, D.C. is now littered with P.D.F.s that say it’s possible to stick with all kinds of orthodox environmental demands and also staple burdensome new labor requirements to them. Or if people seem to be interested in abundance, you churn out a P.D.F. hectoring abundance advocates for not talking enough about unions.

But you can’t get around the need to make choices and set priorities.

When Josh Shapiro got I-95 rebuilt fast, he did so in classic abundance fashion by waiving all kinds of rules and regulations. What he stuck to was using union labor.

And because it worked, it worked. If labor wants to “get to yes” on a more abundant future in the way that Shapiro did there, it should note that he wasn’t taking a lot of criticism from our side of the party. The useful thing would be for the labor-aligned intellectuals — the ones who really do care deep down about the pensions and preventing the triumph of right-to-work laws — to go out and help make the case for abundance, because abundance is already good for labor.
