Welcome to The Closing Argument: our verdict on the news, plus our writers’ key takeaways and everything The Argument published and appeared in this week.

The Verdict by Kelsey Piper

The leading AI companies, Anthropic and OpenAI, have both said that they are focused on achieving recursive self-improvement (RSI). RSI is a possible next stage of AI development, in which humans turn over control of AI development to AIs themselves — and, by implication, making human oversight an increasingly marginal part of the process as AI grows faster and more powerful.

Many people working in AI, including respected senior scientists, think RSI could be a disaster for humanity. But the leaders of the AI labs tell us that they’re hard at work on this in part because, if they don’t do it, someone else will. “We focus OpenAI research towards RSI as we believe it is the only way to remain at the frontier of AI research moving forward,” OpenAI Chief Scientist Jakub Pachocki wrote in a September essay.

This week, we got significant new information about the state of the race toward RSI. Well, sort of: We got a report from the third-party AI research nonprofit METR on this topic, but much of its contents are redacted at the request of Anthropic because they’re “commercially sensitive.”

But what we do know about the report is this: Anthropic says that it expects RSI very soon. Meter estimates, with substantial uncertainty, that on well-specified tasks, progress that used to take a full year now happens in roughly six months. Anthropic and METR see no signs that anything will slow this process down. Crucially, this isn’t just a matter of spending more money, but by the increased ability of AI itself to hasten research.

But we have to take their word for it, because almost all the evidence of this in the report was redacted.

In response, I have a simple request: Congress should get its hands on the original report. If Anthropic has a legitimate case that the report contains trade secrets, fine — Congress already has procedures for sharing nonpublic information with its members. But if billions of dollars in investment are about to result in humanity losing all control of an extremely powerful technology, that’s a legitimate matter of public concern, and our elected representatives are entitled to use all means at their disposal to investigate it.

Furthermore, if the speedups so far are as serious as they are claimed to be, Congress should pass a law banning RSI.

“AI 2027” looks a little too cautious in retrospect

Among the scariest projections I’ve read about AI progress are the ones in “AI 2027,” which was published in April 2025 by former OpenAI employee Daniel Kokotajlo and his team at the AI Futures Project. They built detailed projections of just how rapidly a company with adequate access to computing power and money might be able to hit RSI and build “utopia ruled by god in a box,” as one early Anthropic internal presentation put it.

As the title suggests, they projected that this process could start next year.

The prediction runs like this: More powerful AIs will be more profitable, meaning that there is enough profit incentive to turn AI from the chatbots we had in 2025 into “agents” that act in the world independently. These agents will prove extremely useful for developing the next generation of agents. The companies with the best agents will accumulate more investment money, which they’ll pour into making better agents.

Soon, the AIs will speed up machine learning experimentation, and they’ll eventually run experiments end to end themselves. They will develop new breakthroughs that make AIs smarter and more capable. At this point, progress will be proceeding so quickly that it is almost impossible for humans to offer any meaningful oversight: AIs will effectively be doing thousands of hours of work in every human hour.

We don’t know what comes next, but we wouldn’t be in charge of it.

I originally thought that “AI 2027” — a report warning that AI could trigger a runaway cycle of self-improvement, producing systems far more powerful than humans could understand or control — was right on the mechanics but wrong on the timeline.

And if things played out slowly, humanity would have plenty of opportunities to gather intermediate evidence about whether it was happening at all or whether it was just another fever dream concocted in Berkeley, California (a perennial worry).

The AI safety community does not always strike me as reasonable or balanced; it’s often siloed, prone to groupthink, and full of assumptions that go unargued and turn out ungrounded. I thought that the “RSI in 2027” scenario was likely a consequence of that, and that in the real world we’d probably have a bit more time.

If the new METR report on Anthropic is true, I was wrong. The scenario the AI 2027 team envisioned is playing out faster than they thought.

“This is a much bigger deal than it might sound at first,” METR’s Ajeya Cotra says. “I think this report is the most important update we have gotten about AI risk in the last couple of years — much bigger than the Hugging Face attack. Naively, it points toward much shorter timelines than most external researchers (including the AI 2027 team) have projected.”

If this acceleration keeps compounding, AI progress could become faster than exponential:

Anthropic staff apparently report that they’re now working, on average, about 12 times faster than they would without AI (using models that are more powerful than those they have publicly released).

“I pray and hope there will be strongly diminishing returns before long in this process. But leaders at METR and Anthropic both seem to think there might not be,” concluded Jeffrey Ladish, executive director at the nonprofit AI policy company Palisade Research (and former Anthropic employee).

“If we go through this feedback loop,” Cotra writes, “I expect that the AI systems that come out the other side will be able to very quickly develop novel weapons and very easily overpower human civilization in a conflict. If these systems were seriously misaligned, I think they would likely disempower humanity, and this could lead to human extinction.”

Of course, the report might be wrong, and Anthropic’s employees might be deluding themselves about their progress. So I want Congress to read the unredacted report. I want lawmakers to hire brilliant RSI believers and brilliant RSI skeptics, and have those experts pore over all the evidence and tell us whether these companies are experiencing some kind of mass delusion or actually reporting on an imminent disaster for humankind. And if it’s the latter, I want Congress to ban RSI. Make the companies demonstrate where they’re spending their money, and don’t let them spend it on this.

It’s ridiculous for our elected representatives to be stuck reading the tea leaves from heavily redacted reports about a technology that could determine humanity’s future.

The Road to 2028 with Lakshya Jain

Lakshya Jain, our director of political data, gives us his update on the race to become the first president of the post-Trump era:

Now that the odds of Democrats losing either the Senate or the House in 2028 are slim, you can be sure that the policy debates shaping the fast-approaching Democratic presidential primary will be significantly bolder. With so few plausibly blue-to-red seats up for a vote in 2028, any Democratic president-elect will likely enter office with a trifecta. With all that in mind, look for Georgia Sen. And he has spent months quasi-preparing a presidential run through his Senate reelection campaign optics and speeches, all while taking big swings at Trump and the political system.

Life of the Mind with Kelsey Piper

Kelsey Piper, The Argument’s staff writer reporting on AI, tech, science, education, and politics, tells us what she’s seeing in the world this week:

San Francisco’s local election results look to be nearly a clean sweep for the candidate slate put forth by the YIMBY centrist group GrowSF — and a wipeout for the SF DSA. I’m actually not tempted to interpret this through an ideological lens; after all, the same election gave Los Angeles a DSA-backed mayor. Instead, I think this is entirely about on-the-ground governance.

If the socialists are delivering affordability, public safety, and permitting reform, people will vote for the socialists; if the moderates are doing it, they’ll vote for the moderates.

In one-party states like California, the political establishment can get quite rotten because the other party isn’t a viable alternative. Intraparty schisms do the job of letting an opposition organize itself, so this is often how accountability gets delivered.

🌟Abundance Win of the Week🌟

Milan Singh, one of The Argument’s writing fellows, gives us his pick for this week’s biggest win in the wide world of abundance:

On Tuesday, New York City voters passed five ballot measures, four of which cut red tape. All of them were put forward by Mayor Zohran Mamdani’s Commission on Government Efficiency in defiance of the left-NIMBY ethos that has long dominated city politics.

Amendment 1 changes the City Charter to remove the requirement for public hearings and newspaper advertisements if businesses want to use sidewalks and city streets for things like sidewalk cafes. Amendment 2 directs the Procurement Policy Board to meet regularly and allow it to simplify the questionnaire that city contractors are required to complete. Amendment 3 aims to fast-track the construction of bike and bus lanes and the sale of city property, and Amendment 4 simplifies permitting by creating a centralized hub for construction projects.

All four of the YIMBY ballot measures passed easily, with over 60% of the vote. While Mamdani did not officially endorse the ballot measures, he appeared in a video and accompanying webpage urging New Yorkers to “vote on faster government.”
