Social Rules I Still Don't Understand

I've worked very hard to learn social cues, but some rules still make no sense to me. I'm putting them up to a vote.

As I’ve written about many times before, I have not always understood social norms the way other people do. When I was fifteen, the therapist who diagnosed me with ADHD noted my “missed social cues” immediately in our first session, leading me to wonder (for twenty years and counting) what social faux pas I had committed to a therapist in just a few minutes.

It’s likely that I was born this way, but I was also raised in a slightly unconventional family where being a kind person was deeply valued but following particular rules of etiquette was not as important, or at least not as drilled into us. (There were some exceptions though—when a kid told me I did a good job in the second grade school play and I said “I know,” my parents kindly reminded me that I should have said “thanks.”)

But other rules escaped me until adulthood. For example, I somehow made it to age nineteen before realizing that I should thank people who pay for a dinner. Up until then, almost all my dinners out were with my parents, who, it seemed, did not expect me to thank them for feeding me, especially given that I was a kid. Perhaps someone else would have picked up on the fact that if someone other than your legal guardian pays for your food, you should thank them (and perhaps other families required that children thank their parents for meals). But I did not. It wasn’t until I had been dating my husband for a few weeks when he asked me why I never once thanked him for paying. My only explanation was, “Well, I did offer to split it.” Once I realized my error, I was humiliated and horrified, so immediately the next time I was out to dinner with my dad and he offered to pay, I thanked him in such a formal manner that he looked slightly taken aback.

Aware that my brain was missing either some key information (or some key pieces), I started asking my husband to give me feedback after we had been at a party or gathering, so I could understand if I said or did anything off-putting. He agreed to do this, and there were times when I discovered I was breaking other social norms too. And now, thanks to a controversial Twitter post, I’m discovering that many of the well-meaning things I’ve done to show empathy in the past are actually also bad.

So for this post, I’d like to sort out the social rules that I still don’t fully understand, and have the great people of Substack weigh in to vote on them.

Is it “demanding emotional labor” to give gifts?

Recently, a woman (who I won’t link here because I don’t want to pile on) went viral on Twitter for gifting a new friend a few pages of poetry she wrote, among a few other small things, after hearing she was “going through something.” The friend—who may have been more of an acquaintance—rebuffed this gesture and called it weird, deeply shaming the OP. (My take: they are equally weird and deserve each other. Everyone knows the acceptable thing to do is say, “wow, thanks!” and then slowly fade away if it really freaked you out.)

A great deal of the “poetry lady” discourse surrounded how awkward it was to give something as intimate as original poetry to someone you recently met, and while I agree that was strange, a new theme emerged: it’s weird to give any gifts at all, because gift-giving is really a self-centered treat for the giver, and the receiver has to cope with the burden of “performing gratitude.”

I want to say that everyone should just go outside, because it seems to me that giving a gift, while perhaps unnecessary, is nothing more than a nice gesture. A love language, even! But many people even spoke to their own “no gift” policies—not just in the sense that they don’t want to be overloaded with material items but that they find the experience of gift-receiving to be an emotional burden, especially if they don’t like the gift and have to pretend to be enthusiastic.

I have been the recipient of unnecessary gifts. It was a bit weird, but in my case there was a cultural element at play. When I visited my husband’s family in Greece for the first time on our honeymoon, we were packing to leave their house when they suddenly appeared with a variety of vases and photo frames that we needed to pack in our suitcases. The gifts were nice, but I did feel a little weird because I didn’t have anything to give them, and because ceramic vases aren’t known for being lightweight, travel-friendly items. But were they trying to wring out my last drops of emotional labor? No.

Perhaps I took this rule too personally because, like my husband’s cousins, I love giving gifts. I’m not one of those annoying junky gift-givers who keeps overloading my relatives with cheap Amazon trinkets “just because,” although I did have a regrettable era of repeatedly gifting my brother custom T-shirts with our inside jokes on them (including at one point a shirt that said “Ass Village,” our go-to name for our first city in Civilization). No, these days, I aim to buy people just one nice thing at a good deal, often scouring TheRealReal or indie designers. For Father’s Day, I bought my husband a vintage 2004 Athens Olympics T-shirt. When I was thirteen, I bought my mom a pair of earrings for Christmas, and hid them inside a doll’s chest of drawers which were part of a dollhouse diorama I created as her gift wrapping. Maybe I sometimes go a bit overboard, in case that wasn’t obvious from the fact that I built an entire diorma to display earrings. But in general, I save the over-the-top gifts for people quite close to me, and I don’t give elaborate gifts to people for occasions when they might not expect it, since I acknowledge that can make things awkward if they didn’t get you anything.

But…no gifts ever? Okay, Scrooge! Perhaps I’m taking this rule too literally but I think we owe the generous people in our life the benefit of the doubt that gift-giving is merely one of the ways they show affection, not a narcissistic plot to extract emotional labor. But maybe I’m wrong! Let me know.

It is rude to revert to the previous conversation topic after being interrupted by a waiter?

This is, by far, my most perturbing social question. Often, when I sit down to eat with friends or my husband’s family, we get talking about something really fun (okay, it may or may not be a story I’m telling, and it may be the story of how I built the earrings diorama…but not always!) and then—boom!—“Have you dined with us before? Let me explain the menu to you. Everything is family style and meant to be shared and we recommend about seventeen dishes per person.”

I’ve noticed that even if the exchange with the waiter is brief, nobody returns to the previous conversation, even if it was a very spirited multi-person discussion, not one person’s oration. No matter the topic, it’s dead like a dog. Because of waiter.

A few times, I’ve tried to resume the previous topic but it always feels really awkward and I get the sense that nobody else approves or cares about that topic anymore. This feels particularly nonsensical to me—we were talking about this thing just a minute ago!—but the waiter annihilating the first conversation is such a for-sure thing that I’ve just given up. In one of my worst social foibles, I did something even worse than returning to a waiter-interrupted topic. After the waiter interrupted us, I figured we had a clean slate and I rehashed a discussion topic that had been abandoned before we even left for dinner (I was out with my sister-in-law and brother-in-law.) This was so awkward that my husband actually had to pull me aside to let me know. (The worst part? It wasn’t even about politics, philosophy, culture, or anything important. It was about the 2011 movie, No Strings Attached.)

I asked my husband about this social rule, and he says it’s not rude to return to the previous topic (provided it doesn’t involve Ashton Kutcher and Natalie Portman) but that to seem less awkward, you should jokingly and dramatically resume the conversation after the waiter is out of earshot by saying, “Well, as I was saying before I was interrupted…” but I am hesitant to trust him given his history of encouraging my most annoyingly theatrical antics.

Is it “narcissistic” to relate to other people’s stories with your own?

This one is especially troubling to me because this is basically how I’ve socialized with my own parents and siblings as long as I can remember. Throughout my childhood, my family all just told stories, most of which were tangentially related to things the other person had said, but sometimes not relevant at all. Many of my stories involved my Sims, and I remember spending an entire Christmas season ranting about my Sims’ cat who had a career as an actor and was consequently the breadwinner of the family. Often, my family’s stories would splinter off into various “sidebars,” which, functioning almost as their own waiter-interruptions, prevented the completion of the original stories. I don’t know if my mother and I have ever successfully finished even one story, but we start at least twenty per conversation, most of which detail events we both already experienced.

We also have our “greatest hits.” My dad has told the same story about a kid in his school who fooled the substitute teacher by making a beeping noise with his mouth to throw off the cues for the projector slides, ultimately juxtaposing audio about Thomas Jefferson with an image of a horse. As many times as I’ve heard this story, I will actually burst into tears of laughter when I hear it, alongside my dad. And, on cue, I will respond with my own version—when I was eight and failed to properly learn to play the recorder, my teacher threatened to “not let me play” during the concert and just stand there, and in rebellion I began improvising like Kenny G during a school performance of Hot Cross Buns.

This habit extends beyond my weird little family. My closest friend from childhood also socializes this way, at least when she’s with me. When she visited me last year, she and I told a bunch of unfinished stories about our elementary school gym teacher, through hysterical laughter while our husbands looked on, both a bit confused but happy that we found each other, like the owners of two crazed Dalmatians humping each other at the dog park.

At this point in my life, I’ve consciously made an effort not to tell too many stories, especially if it feels like I’m using irrelevant jumping off points to spew my autobiography. (Nobody really cares about the time I almost got expelled from theater camp—except the sickos of Substack, and that story is coming soon.) It seems to me that listening to someone else’s story, genuinely showing interest, and then building off that theme with your own similar story is a normal way to socialize, as long as it’s not the only thing you’re doing. (You should, of course, be throwing in some Trump impressions where you can, or at the very least, Borat.)

Of course, if your story is intended to “one-up” the other person’s story, that’s something else. I’m talking strictly about stories intended to build off one another using the same thematic elements.

What about if your goal of the story is to cheer them up when they are going through a struggle similar to your own?

This rule, I understand a bit more. If someone is upset about something, sharing a story of when the same thing happened to you can be self-centered, but I would argue it’s not always rude, and really depends on the situation and the person. Perhaps I’m wrong, but my belief is that you should simply ask if a story would help, because it may!

I don’t write about this often, but I had a very upsetting miscarriage between my two children. It felt silly to be so emotional about it because it was very early, but because we had done IVF, we already knew the sex and I had picked out a name. But I didn’t just want to vent about it! I actually wanted, more than anything, to get over it and move on with my life and I was terribly distressed to be stuck in this pit of grief-molasses. Specifically, I wanted to hear about everyone else’s miscarriages (and more importantly, their grief journey) so I would feel less alone. I wanted to know at what point I could expect to live again without waking up every morning in tears, thinking once again about the loss. For once in my life, I didn’t want to talk about my own feelings that much at all.

As soon as I realized what was going on, I messaged friends and family who I knew had experienced pregnancy loss before, all of whom were eager to share their stories with me. It turned out that nearly all my friends had experienced this before—some multiple times, some at much later gestations. Many of them made me feel a lot better! They had been through worse, and were living full and happy lives. None of them made me forget the miscarriage, nor was that their intention, but they all gave me hope that eventually I wouldn’t be quite this devastated, and I would have happy days ahead of me. (It sounds melodramatic, but I genuinely remember believing I’d never be happy again.) In particular, I spoke to one woman—more of a friendly acquaintance than a friend—who had lost her baby at a much later time in pregnancy. She reassured me that eventually, this would become “something sad that happened to me,” and not the theme of every day of my life. Knowing that she had endured something so much worse and still recovered emotionally was extremely helpful.

I have been this person for others, too. When a friend of mine suspected her boss was about to fire her, she reached out to me and said she “immediately knew who to call,” given my, uh, history with this.

I’ve always seen this kind of storytelling as a way to show solidarity and provide hope. If you’re upset about something, isn’t it good to know the person you’re speaking with has gone through the same thing and lived to tell the tale? That said, in moments of hardship, I have experienced people telling me completely irrelevant stories about themselves, with the vague theme of “overcoming a challenge.” Often these stories are boring, unhelpful, and very irritating. But if someone experienced something very similar? Yes, it’s helpful to hear it!

Perhaps in this instance, it’s best to ask someone if they want to hear a story or would rather just vent, but perhaps asking that question is also weird. I don’t know.

If you think someone is mad at you, is it rude if you ask what you’ve done?

Lirpa Strike wrote about this exact social faux pas in a recent article of hers, and she cited a prior article of mine, so here I go again, citing her back.

We both made the “mistake” of asking someone if they were mad at us, but I would argue the way I did it was awkward, and the way she did it was fine. The distinction was the degree of relationship significance.

In my case, I had just met someone—the husband of a new friendly acquaintance—and I (correctly!) picked up on the fact that I had done something wrong during our conversation. I couldn’t figure out exactly what it was, and it was driving me nuts, so I just asked him about it. Shockingly, he answered honestly, and my mistake wasn’t what I had expected: he was offended that I had downplayed my own career because he considered me to be more successful than he was, and he saw it as “false modesty.” Most likely, this guy was never going to be my friend, so I didn’t lose much by asking him such an “inappropriate” question. But yeah, asking him that question was more self-serving than anything else. I barely knew him, and really, I was less concerned about offending a total stranger and more concerned with getting reassurance and validation so I could continue my night without anxiety.

Lirpa’s situation was different. Her close friend stopped speaking with her out of nowhere, and she wanted to understand what she had done wrong. As it turns out, the friend was mad at her, but was inexplicably madder that Lirpa had asked at all, or assumed the radio silence had anything to do with her (even though, as the friend admitted, it totally did.)

I maintain Lirpa Strike was justified in asking this question and I was not. I’m trying to figure out where I would draw the line between situations where this question is appropriate or not, and I’ve boiled it down to the importance of the relationship. If you aren’t terribly close with someone, it’s awkward and intimate to ask them if they’re mad at you, especially because if they are mad at you, it doesn’t really matter. But a close friend who suddenly ghosts? I feel the question is justified, even if it makes the ghoster uncomfortable! (Although perhaps once you’re asking it, you already know the answer—but still, sometimes people need closure.)

“Just listen.”

If you take another look at the image that headed this article, you will see that almost all options for interacting with someone sharing their own struggles are examples of “immature empathy.”

As this image made its way around Twitter, some people felt it was an example of rigid neurotypicals being unnecessarily judgmental, while others believed we were in a serious crisis of emotional immaturity that the “obvious” answer for how to deal with a person expressing their struggles wasn’t being recognized. And that obvious answer was to “just listen.”

Look, I’m obviously not the expert here, given how many people tell me I am “not beating the autism allegations,” and I wrote this article because I have genuine confusion about some social norms, so I’m not saying this advice is wrong, by any stretch of the imagination. But…there has to be a point where listening (and only listening) becomes weird…right?

My husband does this with me sometimes. The other day I was upset about some mom guilt stuff, and as I complained to him, he just said absolutely nothing. When I asked him, probably in a bitchy tone, if he even cared enough to respond, he told me that he thought I just wanted to vent and didn’t want advice or reassurance. I actually would have loved both of those things, especially reassurance in the form of telling me that I’m actually an amazing mom and the things I felt guilty about were silly. In fact, I love to be invalidated as much as possible when I’m feeling anxious! Please, tell me I’m overreacting and everything is great, especially if you have peer-reviewed sources to support your claims! (That last one is more applicable to the transmission dynamics of hantavirus, but you catch my drift.)

But my husband thought dispensing advice would be annoying, and he thought providing reassurance would be more fuel for my OCD. So he said nothing, and I got mad. Perhaps I’d have experienced this differently if instead of staring blankly ahead, he had asked followup questions. But you’d be surprised at how few followup questions one can really ask when someone is complaining! “How does that make you feel” can only go so far, and ultimately if you ask too many questions you sound like you’re interrogating the victim of a crime. “Oh, you lost your job? How much severance did they give you? When you are going to look for a new job? Who did the firing?” I love asking questions to understand more about someone, but peppering someone with questions when they’re upset often feels (at least to me) annoying and not very genuine, plus there is usually a pretty firm limit on how many questions are even possible or appropriate to ask.

All of the rules in this slide make sense to me, if taken too far. But given the responses to the post, these rules don’t seem to be universal. Many people commented that they didn’t want someone to merely listen and be a sounding board for venting. Others said they actually appreciate humor to lighten the mood—and I know I enjoy a break for humor, depending on the seriousness of the problem.

This is one reason why learning social cues and norms is so difficult. Some rules are obvious (such as thanking whoever paid for your dinner, or not talking about yourself nonstop) but others exist in a lot of gray area. One person’s “cheering you up” is another person’s “making it all about you.” And if it’s not too trite to say, I wish we could all extend more understanding to people wading around in these weird gray areas, doing their best to be good friends and partners but occasionally performing the wrong action. These rules are not obvious to everyone, and I’ll go a step farther and say they might not even be obvious to neurotypicals. If these norms are controversial enough to drum up viral discussions on Twitter (admittedly not a place that screams “neurotypical,” let alone “normal”) then obviously they aren’t so clear.

Except one, and I’ll die on this hill—if someone complains about a problem they have, and you have the opposite “problem,” you should probably not reassure them that the grass is always greener because you wish you could trade with them. Most of the time, people who do this are actually just living on genuinely greener grass and complaining that it’s too green.

And now, if you excuse me, the waiter is here to explain the menu to me.
