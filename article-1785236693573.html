A great idea that everyone hates

The simplest way to bring down utility bills is to charge what the electricity actually costs.

Matthew Yglesias
Jul 28
∙

Everyone is angry about electricity prices.

The good news is that there is a very straightforward and well-understood idea that would have a meaningful short-term impact on prices without killing incentives for new investment.

The bad news is that nobody in their right mind even talks about this idea because it’s universally understood to be politically toxic and something everyone would hate.

I’m not here to dispute that diagnosis, just to say that failing to even talk about it is impoverishing the discourse — I keep encountering efforts to essentially recreate it with extra bells and whistles, and I don’t think that anyone coming up with these “new” ideas is taking seriously enough the question of why we don’t just do the straightforward thing.

So what’s the straightforward thing? It’s simple: We could charge household electricity users the spot price of electricity.

Electricity grids must constantly balance supply and demand, which means that on wholesale markets the prices fluctuate wildly based on shifting conditions. On July 15 in New England, for example, it was unusually smoky, thereby putting solar power production below the seasonal average. Then on July 17, there was a weird occurrence where grid congestion led to a period of negative prices in the specific part of Maine where I am.

Residential consumers, however, don’t pay these prices; what households pay instead is a kind of annualized average. That’s a lot more convenient than having an app that gives you an emergency notification that electricity has spiked to quintuple its normal price and you’d better change the thermostat before you go broke. But it also involves significantly higher prices on average. And as more people get electric appliances and cars and as batteries become cheaper and more widely available, the potential gains of forcing everyone to pay the spot price grow — even though almost everyone agrees it’s a terrible idea.

Why this saves so much

The key thing to understand about this is that while most people think of generation costs when it comes to electricity — buying coal to run a coal plant and gas to run a gas plant or paying the large up-front capital costs of solar panels — a majority of the overall price is the cost of the transmission and distribution infrastructure. That’s all the wires and poles and associated equipment that make the grid work.

All of that infrastructure needs to be built to accommodate moments of peak demand.

Say you live in a temperate part of the United States where almost everyone has air conditioning but it rarely gets above 100 degrees. Well, there is still a day or two every year when it gets above 100. And everyone air conditioning themselves comfortably on a day that’s 102 requires more electricity than on a day when it’s 85. So even if 85 is your average summer high, you still need the infrastructure to cope with 102. This is very inefficient — not just in a namby-pamby energy-efficiency sense, but in the sense that it involves very poor infrastructure utilization.

There was a tempest in a teapot recently when Zohran Mamdani put out a tweet urging New Yorkers to restrain their air conditioning to 78 degrees during an unusual heat wave. Some critics claimed this was the city’s comeuppance for electing a communist, but the exact same thing has happened in Texas.

The basic dilemma is that outlier events always exist. If something reliably happens five days a year, you build the infrastructure for it and you pay for it 365 days of the year, even though you’re not using it 98 percent of the time.

But what about something that happens one day every five years?

You absolutely could build the infrastructure to cope with this kind of event. But the wastefulness escalates. Reasonable people can disagree about exactly how much it’s reasonable to pay to have a grid that is robust to rare events, but every actually existing set of political authorities says “enough” at a certain point and sometimes ends up begging people to conserve electricity to prevent blackouts.

One reason we don’t hear about this even more often is that many industrial users are exposed to wholesale spot prices, so they naturally scale down consumption near the limits of the system and curb peak demand.

If you broaden the exposure to the spot price, then a larger set of people will make adjustments to help keep things in check.

A big problem with begging people to set the thermostat to 78 is that of course a huge share of the population is going to ignore you. But if you could guarantee that everyone actually did it, then limiting to 74 degrees might be sufficient. That’s part of the power of prices — they hit everyone.

Prices are also broadly flexible. There are some sickos out there who genuinely don’t mind a 79-degree indoor temperature, and with a strong financial incentive they’d just put up with that and everyone else could ride free. And of course there are other electricity uses beyond air conditioning. A higher price sends a broad and flexible signal that now is a good time to curtail your use. The downside obviously is that people would need to pay high prices sometimes. But precisely because the price is a much more powerful and efficient way of addressing peak demand events, you can get by with less infrastructure and average prices end up being way lower.

Why we don’t do this

This is not a big new idea on my part.

Over in Texas, where they have an unusually innovation-friendly energy market, a retail provider called Griddy started up at one point with a cool idea: In exchange for a $9.99 per month membership fee, you could buy electricity from them at the wholesale spot price with zero utility markup. About 29,000 people signed up for this service reasoning, accurately, that the average prices would be lower.

Then, in February of 2021, Texas had an intense winter storm that dealt a wallop to the utility sector:

  • Most Texans rely on electric heat, oftentimes older, less-efficient radiant heat rather than modern heat pumps.

  • The deep freeze screwed up a bunch of natural gas infrastructure, which can be winterized but mostly isn’t in Texas, where winters tend not to be that cold.

  • Because it was February (not very sunny), electricity demand for home heat soared but supply was curtailed.

So wholesale prices stayed at their legal maximum of $9 per kilowatt-hour for an unprecedented four days. Griddy customers got hit with shocking $5,000 electrical bills, which led to the collapse of the company, lawsuits, and plenty of ill-will.

True believers in variable pricing will note that part of the problem here is that 29,000 people is a small share of Texas’s population. In the opt-in Griddy model, you are exposed to the spot price, but the activities of the opters-in aren’t large enough to actually shift the price.

In the spot pricing ideal, when everyone is getting hammered with prices that high, everyone decides to save money by setting the thermostat to 60 and wearing a sweater. Breadth of impact is particularly important because the relationship of energy use to temperature is non-linear. One household shifting the thermostat by 10 degrees saves less energy than 10 households doing it by one degree. It’s not like if everyone had been enrolled in Griddy, the whole state would have been hit with Griddy-scale surprise bills.

Still, it’s true that for spot pricing to work you need to stick with it even on weeks when the results are very unfavorable. If you do stick with it, it saves a lot of money on average. But nobody wants to do it.

Reinventing the wheel

I bring all this up because I keep getting into tedious conversations with energy wonks about Virtual Power Plants.

Right now, many utilities have demand-response plans that large commercial users (Walmart, say) can enroll in where the utility essentially pays these users to manipulate their thermostat during peak demand events.

The idea of V.P.P.s is basically that modern technology — smart thermostats, rooftop solar, electric cars, household batteries — makes this sort of program feasible on a smaller scale. Or, rather, that a V.P.P. provider could aggregate a bunch of small-scale resources and then sort of sell demand-responsiveness to the utility. Many V.P.P. enthusiasts are ideological opponents of energy abundance coming out of environmental camps like the Rocky Mountain Institute.

But this is just arguing about ideology, not technology. As a proponent of clean energy abundance, I would say that if we can learn to use grid infrastructure more efficiently, that’s a reason to build even more grid infrastructure, not a reason to get by with less infrastructure — sort of like how Madrid builds lots of metro lines precisely because they build much more cost-effectively than New York.

I have deeper doubts about the logic of the position, though.

After all, the technological advancements that make large-scale V.P.P.s (allegedly) attractive and feasible also work for crude pricing. Programmable smart thermostats are widely available and even widely owned, but people don’t actually put them to much use because the features don’t have much value.

If the price of electricity were constantly shifting, though, modern apps would make it trivial for people to vary their use in a fine-grained way. Similarly with electric cars. About 4 to 5 percent of American households have an electric car, and it would be trivial with the infrastructure already on hand to program them not to charge during periods of high prices. Right now, GE will sell you a home backup battery for about $14,000, which is a lot of money but affordable enough that a large share of households could buy one. They’re not very popular because under present circumstances they’re not very useful.

But wholesale electricity prices are quite unstable, and if you can stock up when electricity is cheap¹ and avoid buying when it’s expensive, then suddenly your $14,000 backup battery offers meaningful price savings.

As with any market solution, there are equity concerns — only rich people can afford the $14,000 battery, and E.V. ownership is much higher in affluent households.

But consider the larger chain of events. The first guy who buys a home battery to surf the variable pricing wave is reaping a large public benefit for himself. But as more people buy batteries, they start to actually move the market and prices become less variable. At that point, battery purchasing slows down dramatically. But the upshot is that rich people pay out of pocket to eliminate most of the variability, and low-income households get lower average electricity prices without actually doing anything.²

The question to me is what this V.P.P. aggregation model adds to this that’s not already present.

It uses an opt-in structure, so nobody is forced to use it. But we already saw with Griddy that you can have customers opt in to spot pricing. The main problem is you get a lot less benefit this way.

With the V.P.P. concept, you’re obscuring what’s actually going on, which is maybe better when what you’re peddling is, in fact, a longstanding idea that has been rejected as politically toxic.

But again, to the Griddy point: Thanks to the opt-in structure, it wasn’t toxic ex ante — the toxicity only emerged ex post. The downside (or perhaps upside depending on your perspective) is that you’re creating a new layer of intermediaries who are trying to make money off the system.

You’re also asking people to actually give up control rather than let them say, “Holy shit running the A.C. is expensive today, but goddamnit that extra degree of cooling is worth the money.” That gets rid of one of the biggest benefits of prices, which is that individual preferences and situations genuinely vary. I hate hot weather but don’t mind the cold; others feel the opposite. In a variable price system, the degree to which you pay up versus respond to the incentive can vary flexibly according to your preference (and remember everyone pays less in total on average), while under a V.P.P. it’s left up to some third party.

The true middle ground

This has all been a bit long-winded, and it’s not because I really do think we should try to expose people to electricity spot prices. I’m enough of a cold-blooded technocrat to see the virtues of that approach, but I also understand why people don’t like it. In fact, it’s easy to imagine that if you had a spot price system and there was no way to switch off of it, most consumers would opt in to some kind of “price insurance” plan where you pay a premium but are guaranteed affordable electricity 24/7. You can think of the inefficiency of flat-rate pricing as just an affluent society’s wise decision to invest in peace of mind.

My point is just that I’m skeptical that trying to reinvent this technically-sound-but-annoying solution via third-party intermediaries amounts to much more than hand-waving.

The good news is that even though electricity spot prices vary a lot, the variation is fairly predictable.

An October 2022 working paper by Tim Schittekatte, Dharik Mallapragada, Paul Joskow,³ and Richard Schmalensee finds that you can capture 60 to 70 percent of the benefits of variable pricing by adopting predictable time-of-use pricing.

Subscribed

Under a time-of-use paradigm, it’s not that prices swing to match actual supply and demand conditions. Instead, prices are set to vary in advance in big blocks to match predictable fluctuations. We know that businesses are closed at night and demand is lower but also that solar panels only work when the sun is shining. So in most geographies, you want high prices in the early evening (no solar but people are still awake) so E.V. owners will set their cars to charge in the dead of night rather than when they first get home. People who happen to live in places where the time-of-use swings are large may find it worthwhile to buy home batteries, in which case the fluctuations will smooth out. For way less than half the annoyance of spot prices and without the need to invent whole new business models, customers get significant monetary savings on average thanks to more efficient use of the infrastructure.
