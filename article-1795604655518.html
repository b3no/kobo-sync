What You Son Is Thinking When He Kills a Hooker With a Baseball Bat

In a video game (hopefully)

Jeff Maurer

The video game Grand Theft Auto VI did $3.8 billion in sales on its first day, more money than any movie could make even if the Minions and Batman went to Hogwarts. For those not familiar, the Grand Theft Auto series mixes compelling stories and hyper-realistic settings with violence, swearing, and digital ass rendered with the craft and care that a master woodcarver might devote to a chess set for an emperor. Though some complain that GTA6 is “woke” because you can’t hire a prostitute and then kill her and take your money back (you could actually do that in previous editions), the main character still does things that would cause a real person to be sentenced to death and then have their brain studied by science for a thousand years.

I understand why people find the game off-putting. And I understand why people — especially women — don’t see the appeal. It’s reasonable to assume that playing a game where you act like a violent psychopath reveals a latent desire to be a violent psychopath. And maybe it does in some cases, though the violent video game era has overlapped with a significant drop in actual violence. I think the appeal of games like this is more understandable to anyone who experienced that strange, terrifying period of human development called “male puberty”. But not everyone went through male puberty, so let me relay what I know about what’s happening in those frightened, frantic little minds.

When I was 13, the scandalous video game was Mortal Kombat. This was a fighting game in which you could rip a character’s head and spine off of their body and hold it up for spectators like it’s Punxsutawney Phil. My mom bought it for me for my birthday¹ and was publicly chastised for doing so by some random lady at Target. My mom’s choice was vindicated, though, because 33 years later, I have still not ripped a single person’s skull and spine from their body.

The violence in Mortal Kombat was not incidental; it’s why kids like me liked the game. It was the edgiest, most scandalous game out there, like GTA is now. And that’s why we liked it — it was transgressive. Teenage boys like transgressive things because it’s how we signal that we’ve made the jump from childhood to adulthood.

Childhood is full of rules. My wife and I run our three year-old’s life like he’s in Alcatraz: We tell him when to eat, when to sleep, where he has to go, and what he can and can’t do at all times. If he didn’t learn to follow rules, then he’d become a degenerate asshole who might actually act like the main character in Grand Theft Auto VI...
