In some ways, pessimism is a frequent rhetorical register of Slow Boring and has been since its inception. I’m the one warning people that the Senate map doesn’t get any kinder after 2026, that the median voter in Iowa doesn’t share the concerns of the median Bluesky poster, and that state elections are a constant reminder that public appetite for sweeping change is actually quite limited.

But what I want the dominant register to be is actually something different: agency.

I want everyone reading to see that the choices made by politicians, staffers, aligned nonprofits, and even highly engaged citizens who pay for subscription newsletters or post about politics online make a difference. That politics isn’t just about “vibes” or vertical video skills or superficial rhetorical choices.

And I think this is truly what we’ve seen over the past two years.

The exact same country that delivered a real mandate to Donald Trump in 2024 — a mandate to secure the border and bring down inflation — turned sharply against him because he utterly failed to bring down inflation and embarked on an alarming orgy of corruption. Those choices set the table for a plausible Democratic Senate majority despite the map, and then Democrats ran very good candidates in a lot of key races.

In Texas and Kansas, strong Democratic candidates ran against bad Republican candidates and crazy things happened. Florida is not, in principle, less winnable than Texas, but the Florida G.O.P. put up reasonable candidates and Democrats did not have a Josh Turek or an Annie Andrews in the Florida Senate race. It turns out that in the right circumstances, even South Carolina can be on the map.

But note that part of winning in South Carolina was that Andrews had to align with public opinion on women’s sports teams. Progressives seem to have been happy with James Talarico, and I am happy for them, but he pulled off a win by campaigning for the kind of all-of-the-above energy strategy that post-Obama Democrats abandoned and Slow Boring has argued they should bring back.

The key to carrying all this momentum forward is to remember — and to remind people — that good things don’t just happen because of small-dollar fundraising or campaign gimmicks.

What candidates say they’ll do in office matters, and what they actually do matters even more.

In the context of 2026, that meant Trump’s broken promises were the most important factors in this election. In the context of 2028, his choices will continue to matter but so will the choices of the newly empowered Democratic majority. If they make smart choices, they can spend the next two years putting the boot on the neck of MAGA and the four after that restoring the country’s confidence in Democrats.

If they get sloppy, lazy, or undisciplined, we’ve seen over and over again that large electoral swings are very real and they happen in both directions.

But if the kinds of people who won these tough races dig in and make sure the right lessons are learned and the right momentum is carried forward, we can transform the party, and the country, for the better.

Winning the narrative and the transition

Back in 2018, moderate Democrats flipped critical House seats and gave Democrats a majority. But a handful of left-wing primary winners in deep-blue districts — the Squad — managed to make themselves the story of the midterm.

Common Sense Democrats as a community need to do a better job this time around of focusing on the people who won the tough races and put up the most impressive performances.

Roy Cooper and Josh Shapiro attracted very little media attention over the course of 2026 because their races were blowouts and their prior electoral performance suggested the outcomes were never really in doubt. I get why “dog bites man” stories get ignored, but looking back over the results, winning by such crushing margins is actually really interesting and we should learn from those candidates — just as we should learn from the top Republican performers like Kelly Ayotte and Phil Scott. If even voters in Vermont prefer a moderate Republican check on a progressive legislature, what does that say about the national public’s appetite for sweeping left-wing policy?

Ballot initiatives also give us a sense of the public’s moderate preferences on policy. A women’s sports referendum that was written in a sloppy and draconian way (I wouldn’t have voted for it) looks set to pass in Washington state, and a wealth tax initiative failed in California. These are very blue states — and even many of those voters have significant doubts about major aspects of the progressive policy agenda on both economic and cultural issues. Abdul El-Sayed’s story is inspiring to a lot of people, and his win represents a triumph of good sense in the face of Islamophobia and the ongoing financial might of AIPAC, but it’s also true that an A.E.S.-like performance would not have delivered the win in Iowa or Alaska or Kansas or South Carolina.

On our election night livestream, Nate Silver told me that nobody will care about these small distinctions in candidate quality in the face of a massive Democratic sweep.

But I think a lot of people care what Nate Silver thinks (we certainly had a lot of viewers) and that if he just says the straightforward true thing, it will make a difference to the public’s understanding. A lot of new analytically minded, highly quantitative donors are coming onto the board thanks to the Anthropic and OpenAI I.P.O.s, and they could become a financial base for building momentum behind good candidates and smart politics.

Beyond the narrative, I also wonder about the transition. Joe Biden won the 2020 primary and then spent the next 18 months acting like his job was to win over the Democratic Party staffer class rather than for staffers to learn from his victory.

It’s going to be a real battle over the next several months getting all these newly elected moderates staffed by teams who want to work for commonsense politicians and will spend their time building horizontal networks on the Hill and throughout D.C. to support smart politics. There’s a risk that they instead end up hiring Progressive Establishment types who dedicate themselves to bullying their bosses into orthodoxy.

I don’t totally know how to solve for this, but I think Majority Democrats and The Bench did a great job delivering campaign support to smart candidates, and I know Third Way has been working on these staffing issues. I’d really encourage anyone who reads Slow Boring and wants to help staff aligned candidates in a way that helps them stay aligned to reach out. I’ll do what I can to help make that happen.

Delivering accountability, not infighting

What’s going to be hard once the new Congress is seated is that while I don’t think the Democratic base demands ultra-left policy, they are definitely going to demand a hard line against Trump.

That is good and correct, and I want to push frontline members to run some political risks for this specific cause. It’s true that your voters, at the margin, probably sent you to Washington more to worry about the price of groceries than to worry about the stability of American democracy. But the stability of American democracy actually matters more, and in your heart, you probably know that’s why you ran. If you’re going to run risks, run risks for that.

But I also urge partisan Democrats not to be dupes for leftists who want to weaponize accountability.

The most obvious opportunity is holding hearings to fish around for Trump scandals that embarrass Republicans; that’s a win for everyone in the party. But if every revelation turns into a push for an impeachment vote, you’re taking the spotlight off Trump and onto Democratic infighting.

Similarly, there’s a difference between investigating corporate collusion with Trump in a manner that’s designed to punish the worst actors and reveal the worst behavior by the White House and trying to discredit all of capitalism. The goal of looking at corporate America should be to encourage segments of the business community to flip and rat out the worst actors, not to make them feel like there’s no way to get to yes with Democrats. It’s fine to be angry at some of the behavior we saw from business leaders bandwagoning with Trump over the past two years, and it’s appropriate to demand some measure of penance. But we also need to exert emotional self-control and be willing to forgive people who come clean and tattle on bad acts by others.

The other thing is that Democrats have to be able to walk and chew gum at the same time. I saw polling just before the election showing that voters overwhelmingly prefer bipartisan collaboration on affordability to a relentless focus on fighting Trump.

It would be absurd to take that entirely at face value and naively duck partisan combat.

But Democrats should take it seriously and try to deliver at least a few tokens of earnest policymaking. The low-hanging fruit is to say yes to the permitting deal during the lame duck so everyone will be able to tout a big energy affordability measure.

Candidates — and ideas — matter

Looking ahead to the 2028 Senate map, Democrats have decent pickup opportunities in Wisconsin and North Carolina and then a stretch chance in Iowa. And after last night I suppose we need to consider Kansas and South Carolina as potentially on the map as well.

But I can’t emphasize enough that another D+12 national environment is pretty unlikely and also that there is a type of candidate who can win these states and a type of candidate who cannot.

Democrats’ strong 2026 performance should leave the party with a credible bench of candidates who could run those races.

That said, I think this is undercut by the tendency of moderate politicians to cast their successes as purely personal triumph.

Leftists try to find charismatic candidates and run good campaigns. But they also always explicitly affirm the connective tissue between, for example, Melat Kiros and Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez. They emphasize that these politicians are not just beautiful unique snowflakes but people with shared ideas and common ground. There are, of course, big differences between Cooper and Shapiro and Talarico and Turek and Andrews, and the best campaign for Pennsylvania is not the best campaign for Texas. But doing well in 2028 is going to require a strong national campaign from a strong presidential candidate, and that’s going to require someone who can articulate on an ideas level what compelling politics looks like.

And compelling politics is not just “give in to demands from progressive advocacy groups but then also be nice to crypto lobbyists and try to raise pro-Israel money.”

I think across candidates who did well in tough races — notably including El-Sayed, at times — you see a willingness to emphasize concrete economic issues over post-material identity politics or environmentalism and a commitment to be tough on crime and deliver a secure border.

No politician and no campaign is as good as they look in a favorable national political environment or as bad as they look in a bad one. And most likely, 2028 will not be as good a year for Dems as 2026.

But one key lesson here is that parties and candidates, to an extent, can create their own climates. There’s always the risk of complacency and backsliding after a good result, but there’s also a tremendous opportunity to present an exciting slate of fresh faces to the world and have them actually govern differently from Biden-era Democrats. Democrats in 2027 have an opportunity to move forward in a way that puts this whole horrible episode of American politics in the rearview mirror.
