Mark Carney’s pipeline announcement

Here, from the Prime Minister’s Office, is the text of Mark Carney’s announcement today in Fort McMurray. I’ve translated some passages from French. It’s a big announcement for the federal and Alberta governments. Below, I’ll also post reaction from the Conservative Official Opposition. - pw

Premier Smith, Scott, Treaty 8 Grand Chief Mecredi, Chiefs Adam, Powder, Cheecham, Lépine, and Janvier:

Thank you for joining us on this important day on Treaty 8 territory for Alberta and for Canada.

Great feats often begin with adversity. Fort McMurray knows that better than anywhere. This community has overcome many challenges, including a devastating forest fire a decade ago. It has always emerged stronger.

Carved out of the wilderness at the confluence of the Athabasca, Clearwater and Hangingstone rivers.

This region holds the fourth-largest oil reserve in the world. More than three and a half million barrels of oil leave here every day. To markets around the world, bringing home more than $40 billion to the Canadian economy every year.

Hundreds of thousands of people wake up and go to work every day because of what this community has built. Truck drivers, engineers, welders, and geologists from Alberta — joined by workers across the country.

This bustling city started as a trading post, became a boomtown, and is now a global energy hub. That transformation did not happen overnight, nor by chance. It was steadily built by choice — through grit, determination, and ingenuity.

Long before European settlers arrived, the Dene and Cree peoples collected bitumen from riverbanks – using it to dress wounds and waterproof canoes. Later, explorers and researchers saw economic potential – for fuel and trade. But the bitumen was too thick to flow, and most of it lay too deep to extract.

So, for years, the oil sands were written off. As too costly. Too hard to develop at scale. But generations of researchers, engineers, workers and entrepreneurs kept pushing forward – finding new ways to separate the bitumen, to extract it and unlock what lay beneath the sandstone.

For decades, the Alberta and Canadian governments invested in research, created tax incentives to attract private capital, and even financed oil sands plants themselves.

By the 1960s, a new industry began emerging as this region became home to the world’s first large-scale oil sands operation. Fort McMurray became a symbol of opportunity across Canada and the world.

This is what Canada can achieve when our workers, builders, scientists, and governments work together to seize generational opportunities. This is the legacy we must call on today. The world is facing an energy crisis across three dimensions:

• The immediate challenge of affordability – and, in some countries, availability;

• The structural challenge of energy security; and

• The existential challenge of climate change.

These challenges are forcing countries to think differently about where their energy comes from – and who they can rely on to supply it.

Global oil demand is currently around 100 million barrels a day. Oil supply, often impacted by conflicts, is becoming more volatile. And while the precise path ahead is always uncertain, oil demand will continue to be significant for decades to come. Even under any plausible net zero scenario, the IEA forecasts that world will still need tens of millions of barrels of crude a day.

The question is: who will emerge as the world’s trusted suppliers? It should be us. Earlier this year, the head of the IEA said Canada has a “golden opportunity” to become the low-cost, low-risk, low carbon energy supplier the world needs. In June, G7 leaders echoed that sentiment.

The oil sands are part of Canada’s once-in-a-generation opportunity to become a global energy superpower. To transform our economy. To diversify our trade. To achieve greater strategic autonomy, so we can continue to live our lives as we choose.

The best way to seize that opportunity is to build together. In Canada, we used to know how to build big. The Canadian Pacific Railway. The St. Lawrence Seaway. The Trans-Canada Highway. The oil sands. These major projects in the national interest profoundly transformed our economy. These important achievements crossed provincial borders and demonstrated, again and again, that Canada is bigger, stronger and more prosperous when we are united.

Over time, the way we built became weighed down by well intentioned, but arduous, often duplicative, regulatory processes. To build at scale and speed again, we must do things differently. Our government was elected to change things, to build Canada strong – and we are doing just that.

Canada will remain a country of high standards. But high standards do not require slow decisions. They require a focused government, that gets all the information it needs from all shareholders, shares it immediately with all those who need it, including all stakeholders, and then sets the right conditions to build effectively, sustainably and inclusively.

Investors should know that when Canadians want something built, Canada will get it built. In a more volatile, dangerous and divided world, speed, certainty, and predictability are competitive advantages.

This is why, right after we were elected, our government proposed and Parliament passed the Building Canada Act. This legislation enables the federal government to streamline approval processes for major projects that are determined to be in Canada’s national interest.

To make that determination, the government considers five factors:

• Whether the project strengthens Canada’s autonomy, resilience, and security;

• Whether it has clear economic or benefits for Canadians; Whether the project has a high likelihood of being completed;

• Whether it contributes to clean growth and Canada’s climate goals;

• And whether the project advances the interests of Indigenous Peoples.

Last November, Premier Smith and I signed an MOU to unlock the full potential of Alberta’s energy – both clean and conventional. At the centre of this agreement is a new bitumen pipeline to the West Coast.

In July, the Premier and I announced that Canada and Alberta would share equal ownership of this project, with a meaningful stake reserved for Indigenous peoples.

With this historic partnership between governments, the private sector, and Indigenous Peoples, our government immediately began consultations to determine if this project merited being listed under the Building Canada Act by October 1, 2026.

Over the past three months, the Major Projects Office received more than 100 submissions from Indigenous rightsholders and conducted rigorous consultations with more than 130 Indigenous communities near or along potential routes in Alberta and BC, as well as with federal departments and the Government of British Columbia.

Today, we are ready to move forward for Albertans. For Canadians. I am proud to announce that the Government of Canada is officially listing the West Coast Pipeline, now known as Pacific Link, as a project of national interest.

Over the next year, the Major Projects Office, supported by the Canada Energy Regulator, will lead consultations with all stakeholders on the conditions for this project – the ownership, benefits, environmental protections, and local hiring and oversight. The MPO will then work to finalise those conditions by September 1, 2027 — clearing the way to get shovels in the ground.

This means that as the project proponents work over the next year to develop a final concept — from community consultations to route mapping, ecological surveys, cost estimates, procurement, and workforce planning — they will do so with confidence that the federal government supports this project.

This is an entirely new way of building in a Canada that works. Before the Building Canada Act, proponents would have to spend years and potentially billions of dollars doing this work before even knowing where the federal government stood on the project. For many builders, the risk was not worth it. So, Canada stopped building.

Now, the federal government is making its position clear at the beginning of the process, rather than at the end.

So why is the federal government behind Pacific Link?

A pipeline to the west coast is part of our mission to transform our economy — to double our non-U.S. exports over the next decade, to create tens of thousands of good jobs, to unlock our full potential as a global energy superpower, and to catalyse billions of dollars of new investment into the Canadian economy. To make Alberta and Canada more prosperous, more independent, and to do so, in the right way.

Today, 90% of Alberta’s oil goes to the United States. Pacific Link will materially reduce that dependence by allowing Canada to export an additional one million barrels a day to growing markets in Asia.

That means more customers, more choice and higher prices for Canadian energy producers. And greater long-term security of demand.

Pacific Link will create 140,000 jobs across the country — from the engineers who design it, the pipefitters who lay the lines, the welders who connect it. It will generate over $20 billion in GDP per year and $100 billion in government revenue by 2060 — revenue that will help build the infrastructure and homes Albertans and Canadians need and protect the social programs they rely on every day, from healthcare and dental care to childcare.

This is true nation-building. And it will take a nation to build it. Because today’s announcement is part of a series of initiatives.

Pacific Link needs a route to the west coast, a new port when it gets there, new conservation measures consistent with the higher maritime traffic it will create, new partnerships with First Nations to build shared prosperity, new investments in low-emissions technologies to keep Athabasca crude competitive over the long term.

We will build sustainably. As a prerequisite for the pipeline, Canada, the Government of Alberta, and the Oil Sands Alliance will advance construction of the Pathways Project – one of the largest carbon capture and storage projects in the world.

Pathways will use special absorption towers to capture carbon dioxide emissions before they enter the atmosphere. The CO₂ will then be transported and permanently stored deep underground — making Alberta oil among the world’s lowest carbon intensity crude.

Pathways will help reduce 16 million tonnes of emissions every year — the equivalent of taking 90% of Alberta’s cars off the road.

Pathways alone will create an additional 40,000 jobs and more than $16 billion in GDP. Pathways will give Canada a new competitive advantage in the medium term, leveraging Canadian expertise to create an entirely new industry of carbon capture in Canada. It reminds me of when I was a kid and the oil sands industry was being created by Alberta ingenuity and grit. The same long-term vision, with a similar transformative potential.

To unlock the full potential of both Pathways and Pacific Link, we are working with the Government of British Columbia to expand the Pacific Gateway. The federal government is investing $10 billion to expand the Roberts Bank Terminal which will serve as the landing point for crude moving through Pacific Link.

As we build Pacific Gateway, we are taking major additional measures to protect the environment that surrounds it – because in a strong Canada that works – growth and conservation are fully integrated.

Conservation starts with the pipeline route itself. Pacific Link will follow a southern route which protects British Columbia’s North Coast and avoids highly sensitive ecosystems, including the Great Bear Sea.

And as we expand infrastructure at Roberts Bank, we will strengthen protections for the marine life that call these waters home, including a series of measures to protect the habitats of killer whales during construction.

On Tuesday, we set out more elements of our plan to protect 30% of Canada's oceans by 2030. Today, we are announcing the next step in a pipeline to the Pacific. These are not two plans. They are part of one plan, from one government working with all governments and stakeholders.

We are developing this pipeline with Indigenous Peoples involved from the start — with opportunities to share in the prosperity that Pacific Link will create. To that end, Canada, Alberta, and the project proponent will offer Indigenous communities a minimum of 10% ownership interest in the pipeline – financed by Canada and Alberta’s Indigenous Loan Guarantee Programs. And we will ensure the project creates Indigenous contracting and employment opportunities.

We will build in solidarity with workers. Thanks to the Team Canada Strong initiative, we will create up to 100,000 apprenticeships in specialized trades to train pipefitters, welders, and steamfitters. Many of them will help build this pipeline – and also the ports, railways and other infrastructure for this project. Our unions will train many of these workers.

We’ll build Canadian. To build Pacific Link, we will prefer Canadian materials, including Canadian steel.

And we will build together — with Alberta, British Columbia, and every province and territory – in the true spirit of cooperative federalism, harnessing the full force of a united Canada.

This is a Canada that works. A Canada that recognises opportunity and then seizes it. A Canada where our workers, industry, governments pull in the same direction in the pursuit of a common goal. A Canada where we build together what we could not build alone.

Even in Fort McMurray, it can sometimes be hard to conceive of projects of this magnitude, so let me leave you with this thought.

When I was born just north of here in Fort Smith, the oil sands were a concept – a curiosity to most. By the time I got to high school in Edmonton, they had been transformed into one of Canada’s largest industries.

That’s the scale of what Canada can achieve – and the speed with which we can achieve it – when we work together. Fort McMurray and Alberta are stepping up. And so is Canada. Because the biggest things we have built in this country were never done in isolation. Canada is working.

The Office of the Leader of the Opposition sent this pre-buttal out minutes before Carney’s statement. It’s from a news conference Michelle Rempel Garner gave yesterday:

“And finally, I’d like to give a brief comment on the potential pipeline announcement [Thursday]. A year and a half into Prime Minister Carney’s tenure, we’ve had two signing ceremonies, countless media stories, and no pipelines built.

“I’ll be very honest, we can’t afford more of the same. The country can’t afford more of the same, and my province can’t afford more of the same.

“So, another promise without a clear plan will not help anybody. [Thursday], I want to see, and I know that my colleagues across the country want to see this, anti-investment laws in the energy sector repealed. For example, repealing C-48 would allow more oil to be shipped by rail tomorrow.

“Provinces and taxpayers also shouldn’t have to be proponents for critical energy infrastructure. Rather, the investment climate of regulation should create certainty for private sector to invest. So, Conservatives will push for details on construction, the timeline, costs, and how and when Mark Carney will ensure completion.”
