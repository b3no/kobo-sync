No, the Phonics Decoding Advantage Does Not Persist For the Most Disadvantaged Readers

you can turn a lie into a meme, but it’s still a lie

Freddie deBoer
Nov 11
 
READ IN APP
 

You can repeat something that’s not true as many times as you care to, but that doesn’t make it true; it just makes it a meme. And with the Sold a Story podcast having infected the liberal conventional wisdom, the idea that structured phonics instruction has a large and persistent advantage for struggling has become just that kind of meme - that is to say, the untrue kind.

There’s an awful lot of wild, entirely-unjustified phonics triumphalism out there right now, but I’m not talking about that. (The people engaged in it probably can’t be reached and certainly not by me.) Here, today, I’m talking about those who admit that the generic phonics advantage is limited and fades over time but who assert that phonics instruction can ensure that struggling readers become competent ones. You can see that in Kelsey Piper’s responses in the back and forth she and I recently had in The Argument. This is the “just give them the basics” school of thought, which seeks to preserve the overall form of the old school reform playbook without the messianic excesses that hurt that movement’s credibility in the past. The people I’m talking about have given up the claim that phonics makes every child a competent (and competitive) reader and retreated to the narrower claim that phonics rescues kids from the most basic level of illiteracy, that phonics establishes a floor and evades fadeout in doing so. That’s a more modest position and a more sympathetic one. But I’m afraid that it’s still wrong. The people who advance this reading of the evidence make a specific empirical prediction, which is that the children at the bottom should gain more and keep those gains. The best evidence for that prediction is a few hundredths of a standard deviation - which is meaningless in practical terms - in one of two studied cohorts. That’s just not what their claims suggest.

This is a good time to look at the actual research, which I’m sorry to say many of the most voluble of the phonics triumphalists clearly haven’t done.

The study most often cited in support of the view that phonics can reliably teach struggling students “the basics” is the Machin, McNally, and Viarengo study from the American Economic Journal of Economic Policy in 2018. The authors looked at England’s phased rollout of synthetic phonics; because local authorities adopted the program in waves, early adopters could be compared with later ones. (Why not compare those who got phonics to those who got “whole language” or another approach? You’d have to randomly assign children to condition, with one of those conditions being “pure” whole language, an approach to reading that is almost nonexistent, despite what you’ve likely heard.) The early results were large! Five year olds in the early-adopting schools scored about .25SD higher in reading than their peers in schools that hadn’t yet switched to the structured phonics curriculum. But, alas… fadeout. By age seven, the effect size difference had shrunk to about 0.08SD. By age eleven, the average effect was zero. There was no discernible difference (in decoding) by 6th grade. The authors own read of this is pretty simple: most students learn to read eventually.

Which is true! And good. Mass illiteracy may be a rising concern in the age of screen addiction, but rich countries in the developed world have not really struggled with teaching the vast majority of their population to read. The points to remember though are a) most kids learn to read regardless of method, b) when you go beyond a can read/can’t dichotomy and assess reading ability on a more granular level, there’s no lasting phonics advantage, and c) “most kids learn to read eventually” is a cut against the panicky deficit model that the phonics partisans are so eager to engage in. Those students who remain illiterate into adolescence are overwhelmingly those who face all manner of other personal and social and economic challenges, a cohort of kids for whom pedagogical differences are least meaningful. Meanwhile, the kids who aren’t at the very bottom in all manner of educational metrics eventually learn to read with or without systematic phonics instruction. The trouble is that you can be better or worse at reading, the kids who start out worse stay worse in overwhelming majorities, and phonics has no ability to improve their position over time. I’ m not going to prosecute the case for why you actually very much do care about relative performance here - I’ve made it before - but even setting that aside, we should be clear that we have no reason to believe that phonics instruction results in durably superior readers over time than any other method. Because of fadeout, because of the stickiness of academic ability over time.

Why is it so common to see claims of persistence for the most disadvantaged students? This contention seems to come from a subgroup analysis in the paper. (There are two overarching cohorts in the dataset because England introduced synthetic phonics in stages - in a pilot in 2005–06 and then in a much larger phase-in in 2006–07; the researchers evaluated each wave separately against schools that hadn’t yet adopted phonics.) In one of the two cohorts children who received free school lunch - that is, poorer students - and children who weren’t native English speakers maintained an advantage at age 11… of .06SD. In the other cohort, the free lunch and ELL groups showed no average significant benefit at all. Two significant results out of eight subgroup comparisons seems like thin support for a sweep policy claims, but let’s take the .06SD advantage at face value. The problem is .06SD is tiny. How tiny? Let’s make a useful comparison. The average American male height is about 5’9 with a standard deviation of roughly three inches. How big would a change of .06SD be? Less than a fifth of an inch, about three quarters stacked on top of each other. Nobody would say that a three-quarter-tall improvement in height meaningfully changes the life of a short man. If you would prefer a psychometric comparison instead, you could consider IQ: on the IQ scale .06SD is less than one point. And this is the strongest long-term evidence offered that phonics helps disadvantaged kids catch up.

If you’d like a visual aid, you can see it up top. That’s the distributional difference between poor and ELL kids who got phonics instruction and those who received the old pre-structured phonics curriculum by age 11. That is the difference that has launched ten thousand thinkpieces about how we should just choose to end illiteracy through the power of phonics.

Then there’s the fact that comprehension is supposed to be the point of reading. Phonics teaches decoding, the ability to turn letters into sounds and sounds into words. Decoding is a necessary but insufficient part of the real goal, which is comprehension - the ability to derive meaning from the combination of words on the page. Phonics has never been shown to produce meaningful comprehension gains beyond the earliest grades, and the evidence for that comes from its own champions. The National Reading Panel - whose 2000 report launched the modern phonics revival, more or less - found only “a small, nonsignificant relationship between systematic phonics training and performance on tests of reading comprehension” for students in grades two through six. The federal government evaluation of Reading First, the multibillion-dollar phonics-heavy initiative built on that report, found a modest gain in first-grade decoding but no statistically significant effect on reading comprehension in grades one through three. This is what the research is telling us: phonics shows modest advantages in decoding for the youngest grades, compared to other methods, but said advantage disappears completely within a few years thanks to fadeout. There is no phonics advantage in comprehension and so nothing to fade out.

Please note that in every case here I’m not referencing some sort of alternative research record or crazy theory, but instead, simply accurately summarizing the research that the phonics people cite themselves.

None of this means phonics is useless. It’s a sensible way to teach children to decode and decoding is a necessary prerequisite of understanding. But, for one thing, necessary and sufficient are obviously very different. For another thing, phonics is not some forgotten and forbidden pedagogy! One of the weirdest things about this debate is the dogged insistence by the phonics cultists that, somehow, no one is teaching kids phonics. That’s not only not true now, it’s never been true. Most so-called whole language approaches involve a lot of phonics. And yet people keep saying bizarre things like “They don’t let kids sound words out anymore!” It’s hard to do comparative whole language research because nobody is teaching any “pure” whole language. The fight has been won; it’s just that the difference isn’t really a big deal because, as in all other elements of education, what’s most important is being naturally intelligence. And fadeout rules all. What works in first grade simply has no influence at all on readings gaps in adolescence, which is where the college-and-job market rat race begins and all of this has actual economic impact.

You would like to complain that I am being repetitive in my education writing again. Well, trust me, I wish I didn’t have to constantly do this. The problem is people who should know better keep saying things that aren’t true, and this all has very real policy stakes. (Tell you what: you can leave a comment complaining that I’m being repetitive if you first email Sold a Story at soldastory@apmreports.org and ask a) why they keep saying factually incorrect things and b) why they refuse to debate anyone who is capable of actually calling them on their bullshit.) I have to keep saying this stuff because people keep insisting on spreading lies about vulnerable children and the scapegoat educators who teach them. And the phonics story just doesn’t survive scrutiny. Phonics is now being sold as an equity intervention, a way to lift the bottom of the distribution toward the middle. The best evidence for that claim is a miniscule effect in one of two cohorts on an outcome other than comprehension. It just doesn’t add up. Children who struggle to read at five mostly still struggle at fifteen, whatever method of teaching they receive. That’s the truth, and it’s important, and I’ll keep pointing it out no matter how impatient it makes some people.
