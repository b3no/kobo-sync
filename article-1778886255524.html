You can’t prepare for the next pandemic without confronting the last one

On Covid media, haunting, and hantavirus

Halina Bennet
May 15
∙
Paid
 
READ IN APP
 

First off, some housekeeping: I will be on vacation next week, so instead of my usual evening lineup, please expect discussion posts every day.

Talk to you after Memorial Day!

I flinch when Covid shows up in books, television, and movies. There is an achingly physical discomfort that comes with encountering depictions — both fictional and not — that feel like too invasive a recognition and too fresh a memory.

But I have encountered it a few times, to varying degrees of discomfort.

Ann Patchett’s “Tom Lake” handles Covid gently, as a hazy atmosphere. Netflix’s “Cheer” docuseries crashes directly into it, following collegiate cheerleaders as the most important event of their lives disappears overnight. Even glancing references elsewhere still make my stomach turn. I avoided “Eddington” because of its overt dealing with the pandemic.

This week I read Ben Lerner’s latest novel, “Transcription,” which may be the first pandemic work I’ve confronted that captures viscerally and without innuendo what living through those years felt like. Lerner stays inside the texture of Covid, describing FaceTime calls with dying relatives, the silence of emptied streets, the strange flattening of human contact into screens. He writes about the period’s universality — the collective nightmare shared across the world.

In April 2020, Emily Temple wrote for Literary Hub, “It was only three days into our self-isolation that my husband turned to me and said: ‘Isn’t it depressing that we already know Ben Lerner will write the best American novel about this?’”

In fewer than 150 pages, “Transcription” follows a middle-aged writer attempting to conduct his mentor’s final interview after accidentally destroying the device meant to record it. The novel unfolds through failed transcription, memory, and distortion. It is about consumption — of screens, speech, and stories; by disease — and about a period most people would rather soften than remember.

“​​I wanted to write a book that explains very little and makes a lot felt,” Lerner told Andrew Marantz in an interview for the New Yorker.

“Political situations, especially in relation to Covid, are around the edges of the book. And the questions about why these children are in these deep protests against contemporary life — refusing to go to school, refusing to eat — are inseparable from their inability to imagine a future.”

Covid began during my first year of college. I left for spring break and did not return for nearly a year. Once back on campus, we wore masks, took tests, stayed apart, contact traced. At some point, we stopped doing all those things, but we never really talked about when or why. One day, barely anyone wore a mask anymore, but the world had changed. I was lucky during the pandemic. I did not know anyone who got very ill, I was able to quarantine with family and friends, I took classes on Zoom. But the anxiety and abject grief that swallowed up each day were suffocating, and I’m not sure any of us have relearned to breathe normally.

“Haunting is about distance, the presence of an absence,” Lerner told Marantz. “I think that’s the argument of the book, in a certain way — it’s that the voice that isn’t just totally reproducible — externally, objectively — but is in you, and might be encoded in an artwork, haunts you more intensely, and is ultimately more present.”

The grief we collectively have not yet contended with is still haunting us as we create more distance between ourselves and March 2020.

We have also not come to an agreement about what to grieve: While some Americans mourn the physical interaction they avoided for months, others bemoan the autonomy they lost when institutional overreach closed in on their lives. That mismatch in experience resulted in a national splintering that we have not even begun to address.

Now, an indirect echo of that time has brought that ghost to the surface.

Last month, news came out that passengers aboard the cruise ship MV Hondius had begun falling sick with hantavirus. Some had even died. Within hours, panic spread online far faster than the disease itself possibly could.

Public-health officials responded quickly. The C.D.C. stressed that the overall threat to Americans remained extremely low, and experts emphasized that hantavirus is not remotely comparable to Covid in transmissibility. The Andes strain involved in the cruise ship outbreak can spread between people, but only through prolonged close contact. North American strains do not spread person-to-person at all. Infectious-disease specialists have repeatedly said this is not the beginning of another pandemic, which Matt wrote about earlier this week.

Despite all these facts, anxiety is bubbling up everywhere, among people across the globe who, in many cases, share little in common other than Covid.

The panic surrounding hantavirus is not really about hantavirus. It is about what Covid did to people psychologically — due to the loss of experiences, lives, and institutional trust — and about how little space this country, and much of the world, has allowed anyone to process it since.

Children missed out on years of emotional development, adults lost faith in leadership, and more than 1.2 million Americans died. Researchers estimated that each death left around nine close family members grieving, which means the country contains millions of people carrying some direct experience of pandemic loss. But even that statistic fails to capture what was uniquely brutal about pandemic grief.

Nobody was able to grieve normally. Relatives said goodbye to their loved ones through iPads held by nurses who watched countless people die alone. Funerals were canceled, postponed, or made into livestreams watched in solitude. Families couldn’t gather in homes filled with food and friends and noise. Absent were the ancient human rituals that make death survivable. As Covid killed people, it also dismantled the process of grieving.

A 2020 paper on “unfinished mourning” argued that the pandemic created conditions almost designed to produce prolonged grief, including isolation, interrupted rituals, sudden death, and physical separation from loved ones. The normal social processes that help people metabolize loss disappeared.

And then, before most people had even processed what happened, the country decided it was time to move on.

“[There’s] this expectation that we move on, we push past the pandemic, and yet we have not acknowledged the enormity of the tragedy,” Sarah Wagner, a social anthropologist at George Washington University who studies mourning after mass death, told Science News in 2024.

That interruption in mourning feels central to understanding the hantavirus panic that has been causing the same nagging feeling in my stomach as all reminders of the pandemic do.

Collective trauma often resurfaces indirectly. After a community absorbs a catastrophic event and suppresses the emotional aftermath, it will react intensely to something adjacent but not directly related.

Online, people are already describing the hantavirus coverage in explicitly post-traumatic terms. Reddit users compare their reactions to the first weeks of Covid. Therapists interviewed about the outbreak say patients are experiencing resurging pandemic anxiety. One psychologist quoted by USA Today described the current reaction as the return of “familiar anxiety, fear, and confusion” tied directly to unresolved memories from 2020.

Even the language coming from officials seems to bring up old associations. Health and Human Services Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr.’s assurance that the situation was “under control” landed poorly online because the phrase itself now carries the residue of every premature reassurance from early 2020.

Covid permanently altered many Americans’ sense of stability. Before 2020, widespread social collapse still felt hypothetical, the material of history books or dystopian fiction. Then hospitals overflowed, schools closed, institutions failed visibly, and millions of people realized how fragile ordinary life actually was.

Which is why pandemic fiction still feels difficult to encounter. Rather than nostalgia, it is recognition of something that we have collectively decided is too uncomfortable to remember. The work reactivates emotional states people never fully processed in the first place, like dread, uncertainty, isolation, and the feeling that daily life had suddenly become fragile and provisional.

The panic surrounding hantavirus is disproportionate to the epidemiological threat, but emotionally it makes perfect sense. People are reacting not only to the disease in front of them, but also to the memory of living through years of death, isolation, and uncertainty. Covid may have receded from daily life, but its psychological residue has not.

It is the unavoidable haunting of a collective confrontation with contagion that feels achingly similar, even if this disease today is not a direct reproduction.
