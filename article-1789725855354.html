Plus prepper takes and the end of history

Before getting to the Mailbag, I want to say something about the increasingly salient A.I. debates happening online. Trying to think about policy involves forming views about a lot of contested questions, many of which don’t have immediately obvious answers. That means you necessarily find yourself deferring to an extent to the judgment of other people. Which means that something very important to do in life is decide how to assess which people are reliable sources of information.

The way that I think a lot of people go about doing this is kind of basic friend/enemy politics. They emotionally or intellectually anchor around some particular point and then just try to align with the people who are aligned with them on that.

And I think that’s a mistake. The fact is that for basically any widely held political view, a huge share of the people who hold the view are going to be ignorant, intellectually sloppy, or otherwise badly flawed in their reasoning. That’s just what people are like.

What you ought to do instead is try to see which people casually toss off false statements about things that are easily verifiable. One salient example this week is that I’ve seen a lot of conservative-minded people who decided to align with the White House’s regulatory skepticism on A.I. safety saying that the people who are pushing concerns about the risks of A.I. are the exact same people as “climate doomers” and anti-nuclear activists.

Anyone who is saying this either doesn’t know what they are talking about or (worse) does know what they are talking about and is choosing to lie to you.

That’s not a good person to listen to on this subject. For better or worse, the relationship between the climate movement and the effective altruist community has been very antagonistic over the years precisely because the latter group did not accept the totalizing and apocalyptic narratives the climate people were invested in.

To be maximally generous to Erickson, he was just popping off without looking into it. That’s not ideal epistemic behavior, but I think we’ve all done it (certainly I have) and it can be useful as a learning experience. But if you pop off, get told you’re wrong by better-informed people, and then plow ahead unashamedly, that’s bad. Don’t do that. Don’t listen to people who do that.

The Digital Entomologist: Will Stancil and Parker Molloy got into it on Bluesky. Molloy’s theory seems to be that Talarico showed he’s transphobic, and it acceptable to work to reduce his support which could result in Paxton winning — possibly leaving a 50/50 Senate which remains in the thrall to the Trump personality cult. Her argument seems to be that the election is the best time to try to influence a politician. It is better to defeat the one who is closer to you, than to keep the pressure on for the next 6 years. How does post election influence work? How can one convince trans activists that they are better off talking to Talarico for the next 6 years than being ignored by Paxton?

I certainly agree that vote blue no matter who reduces the influence of those whose votes can be taken for granted during the election, but I also think that those voters still have a lot of influence during the term if they in part of the coalition. Or is that incorrect?

I think the big thing that has gone weirdly missing from a lot of progressive thinking about the political system is the concept of “persuade people that you are correct.”

Bluesky users frequently accuse me of being the one who ignores the possibility that public opinion can change, but that’s just not the case at all. The way the political campaign for marriage equality worked was that the vast majority of politically active gays and lesbians in the United States loyally supported John Kerry and Barack Obama and downballot congressional Democrats in 2004, 2006, 2008, and 2010 because Democrats were on their side on politically feasible gay-rights causes like an employment non-discrimination law. The other thing that L.G.B.T.Q. activists did during this period was persuade the American public that they were correct about marriage. They didn’t browbeat Democrats in red states for refusing to take the unpopular side of the issue. They didn’t even browbeat Democrats in blue states for refusing to take the unpopular side of the issue. They understood that it was their job to convince people and they did.

That was a case of unusually rapid opinion change and it was also unusually clear-cut that the direction of change was from mass opinion to elected officials.

Elite persuasion also matters, though. The housing abundance movement’s big success over the past 20 years has been taking what used to be an obscure academic analysis from Ed Glaeser and turning it into something that is broadly acknowledged as true by the relatively small minority of Americans who bother to care about the details of housing policy debates. The successful elite persuasion around this has been the basis of bipartisan state laws from Florida to Maine to California. It’s why Elizabeth Warren and Tim Scott worked together to enact a small-but-meaningful federal housing-supply law. And it’s why we increasingly have journalists on a housing beat and a growing academic literature exploring the nuances of how the political economy of housing supply really works.

By the same token, I would say the failures of YIMBYism are largely failures of persuasion. Notably, the Republicans of the Northeast don’t seem to agree that the overregulation of housing supply is a major economic problem for Maryland, New Jersey, New York, Connecticut, and Massachusetts. Republicans don’t govern any of these states, but what they think and do matters because voting for bipartisan bills is inherently less risky for everyone, so the lack of YIMBY Republicans makes it harder for YIMBY Democrats to get anything done. If New York Republicans thought about housing the way Phil Scott and Kelly Ayotte think about housing, then Kathy Hochul’s moves toward housing abundance would be much more politically efficacious and the situation would be transformed.

There’s a lot I could say about the sociology of why New Hampshire and Massachusetts Republicans have different views of this, but I don’t think the kind of pressure campaigns that activists like to talk about have anything to do with it. The Massachusetts Republicans just haven’t been convinced!

Closer to the meat of the question, I don’t really like the rhetoric of “vote blue no matter who.” If you compare Ken Paxton with James Talarico on trans issues, it’s just night and day. If the only thing you care about is trans rights, then Talarico is more supportive than Paxton, so you should vote for Talarico because he is closer to your position even if he doesn’t entirely agree with it.

To again analogize to housing-supply stuff, I don’t think I’ve ever heard of a politician who is as much of an extreme YIMBY as I am. But I don’t run around doing takes like “Burhan Azeem threw YIMBYs under the bus by opposing unlimited building height in the city of Cambridge.” That would be insane. He spearheaded an effort to legalize six-story buildings throughout the city and ended up compromising on legalizing four-story buildings throughout the city (plus you can go to six if you meet some onerous inclusionary zoning standards). That’s a huge deal! He’s an amazing YIMBY champion. I would love for even more expansive upzoning to be politically viable but, you know, it isn’t.

Different activist communities, I find, just talk themselves into different levels of sourness about politicians. YIMBYs are, I think, all the way on one end of the spectrum and thrilled to praise everyone from Zohran Mamdani to Ron DeSantis for taking constructive action even though nobody is actually doing the full thing we think they should be doing. The people on Bluesky who are angry at Talarico even though him winning the election would obviously be good for their cause are over at the other end.

But what I really find striking about this is not the attitude toward Talarico per se but the extent to which the movement does not try to persuade people that they are correct. As a person who reads a lot of articles and has certainly read a lot of commentary on this topic, almost none of it is devoted to trying to persuade me that gender self-identification is the right way to assign people to sex-segregated sports teams. The people who believe that have been very successful at creating a situation where lots of left-of-center writers never express any doubts on the merits (think about the many prominent writers who’ve never addressed the topic), but they don’t try to set my doubts at ease. You are allowed, though, to try to convince people to change their minds.

More broadly, a topic that gets kicked around a lot — most recently by Nate Silver, Jerusalem Demsas, and me on a podcast — is the relationship between moderation or centrism as a political tactic and liberalism as a political philosophy. These are, obviously, not the same thing.

But more than Jerusalem I do think they are related things. And this question of persuasion versus coercion as an approach to electoral politics is part of the connective tissue. Part of the spirit of liberalism is to believe in persuasion on the merits as a core pillar of political change. Another part is to believe that political parties will and should alternate in power. If you believe those things, you are going to believe that in practice forging bipartisan consensus is the key to durable change and that electoral pragmatism is how party leaders should address most issues most of the time. So you end up converging on tactical centrism as a worthwhile way of life.

As I’ve said time and again, if you take it seriously, the YIMBY movement is incredibly radical and is a core example of how people who believe in free markets can position themselves as dissenting from the status quo. But it is also true that YIMBYs, in practice, have mostly achieved political success through bipartisan strategies, tactical moderation, and trying to have a cheerful attitude toward winning halves of loaves. I don’t think that’s a coincidence. It speaks to the fact that it’s a deeply liberal cause — it’s about an egalitarian vision of human freedom — in much the way that the gay-rights movement of the 1990s and 2000s was.

Doug Elmendorf: Matt, You explained last week that AI safetyists and super-optimists should make similar financial investments because both groups expect AI to be “incredibly transformative.” But what about other aspects of behavior? A super-optimist might buy more sunglasses because the future is so bright. But should a rational safetyist, recognizing that rogue AI might take down the power grid or allow biological attacks, buy a house on the coast in rural Maine, get a generator and a large storage tank filled with diesel fuel, stockpile clean water, ...?

I do personally own a house in rural Maine that has a generator and its own well, so make of that what you will.

The main thing I think about this is that entirely separate from anything to do with superintelligence, the risk of novel pandemics is structurally increasing largely due to population growth and increased travel. Something that is relatively easy to do to help prepare yourself and your family for the next pandemic is to have reusable elastomeric respirators on hand. They are small and not very expensive and the more people who stockpile this kind of thing in advance the less supply chain stress there will be when the shit hits the fan. I also have a far-UVC lamp that can hopefully incinerate viruses. My emergency supplies box also includes sort of generic stuff like firestarters, a multi-tool, duct tape, paracord, water filters and purification tablets, and so forth. I don’t know that I actually have any necessary skills to make it through a survival situation, but it doesn’t hurt to have basic stuff on hand in case.

Vas: You mentioned in your 9/11 retrospective that Obama and Clinton were constrained in a way W wasn’t, and that gave W an opportunity to be a great president. I agree Obama had constraints W didn’t, but Clinton was in the same framework wasnt he?

Also, I think generally the greatest presidents (Washington, Lincoln, FDR) made their greatness by coming at a challenging time and leaving the country in a better, stronger state after them. To some extent, the constraints allow for greatness.

Looking at your greatest presidents, I agree that they came along at challenging times and left the country in a stronger state. But unlike in your framing, I would argue that all three also took advantage of relatively unconstrained authority.

For Washington that was in part his unique prestige and in part just the fact that as the first president, he had a set of much less settled institutions to deal with than any later president ever would. The fact that he chose to step aside voluntarily after eight years and pass authority to a successor, John Adams, who was broadly like-minded but not a family member or hand-picked crony, had tremendous significance simply because he really could have acted very differently.

For Lincoln and F.D.R. it was enormous congressional majorities plus wartime powers.

Clinton served during a time of very low constraint on the United States as an international actor but of intense domestic political constraints. He had these big ideas on health care and he just couldn’t get them done. He had ideas about welfare reform that were better than the ideas in the bill he eventually signed because he was dealing with an opposition Congress. He can claim credit for sound macroeconomic policy and he signed some good bipartisan bills. But that’s really just making the plays the defense gives you. I’m not a hater by any means — he was in some respects a real political genius, but it’s different from the Bush situation where it was really left to him to explain and narrativize what was happening and he chose to point the country toward Iraq when he easily could have not done that.

Person With Internet Access: How do you think Francis Fukuyama’s “The End of History and the Last Man” holds up 35 years later? What, if anything, from it explains the current Western liberalism struggles?

I wrote and deleted enough answers to this question that I think the right answer is “I need to re-read the book and answer the question properly.” All I’ll say for now is that my recollection of it from about 20 years ago is that it makes a lot of points that are actually very insightful about the current crises of Western liberalism and the thesis is way less triumphalist than it is often remembered.

My analytic doubt about his thesis is how well his treatment of Islamism holds up.

“In the contemporary world only Islam has offered a theocratic state as a political alternative to both liberalism and communism,” Fukuyama wrote in his original 1989 essay. “But the doctrine has little appeal for non-Muslims, and it is hard to believe that the movement will take on any universal significance.”

In Fukuyama’s recent appearance on “The Ezra Klein Show,” he mentions this glancingly but it doesn’t really get pursued. Instead he says that “radical Islamism is not an ideology that’s going to ever conquer the world … in the years since 9/11, it has kind of faded.”

That’s all true but also maybe a bit too flip. Thanks to events in Syria and Iraq, Islamist political parties have more power than they did 25 years ago. The Israeli-Palestinian conflict has taken on a less secular and more religious flavor. In places like Egypt, you see liberals (both Egyptian liberals and foreign policy elites in Western countries) betraying their commitment to democracy to combat a local Islamist movement that while dramatically less radical than al-Qaeda, is still fairly illiberal. You have Western writers like Shadi Hamid arguing that America should deprioritize liberalism in favor of democracy and also that Muslim immigrants shouldn’t face pressure to assimilate. You also have very illiberal forces rising on the right precisely because they believe Muslims can’t or won’t assimilate. I don’t have a fully worked out take on this or recall exactly how (or if) Fukuyama discusses Islamism in the book, but there’s plenty of meat on this bone to chew.
