Of course you’d never fall for a scam. (You will)

In “The Future of Fraud,” Becky Holmes explains why AI is creating a golden age of grift

Ron Charles
May 22
 
READ IN APP
 

Given President Trump’s creation of a $1.8 billion taxpayer-funded slush fund to reward his allies, you might think Becky Holmes’s new book, The Future of Fraud, is already too late.

But you ain’t seen nothing yet. The collapse of government ethics combined with the rise of artificial intelligence heralds a golden age of grift. Vibrating between paranoia and credulity, all of us are at risk of being phished, spoofed, conned, and drained.

“Never before has fraud been so rife in the world,” Holmes writes in this surprisingly engaging little book. “One thing we all know is that AI is going to facilitate an absolute avalanche of fraud.”

Now I wish I hadn’t been playing Candy Crush during those HR training modules.

Maybe I would’ve paid attention if they’d been designed by Holmes, whose previous book was Keanu Reeves Is Not in Love with You: The Murky World of Online Romance Fraud. Although she interviewed more than 50 experts and explored criminal operations around the world for The Future of Fraud, she delivers grim details about all manner of scams like she’s chatting with us over tea and a scone.

The good news is that fraud — “the exploitation of human vulnerabilities” — hasn’t really changed its essential nature for millennia, and she provides an entertaining survey of some of the classics, with Charles Ponzi at the top of the pyramid. “If we had video footage of cavemen,” Holmes writes, “we’d see one of them swindling another out of a big leg of woolly mammoth meat.”

The bad news is that those old schemes still work, and now they’re being supercharged by technology.

For instance, ChatGPT can correct grammar and spelling errors that used to signal a fake email solicitation. In fact, as Holmes demonstrates, AI chatbots can provide instructions on how to commit financial fraud, complete with cover stories and suggested dialogue.

(Lately, I’ve been receiving convincing emails from supposedly major social media companies asking me to partner with them. Fortunately, they all think I’m the Rev. Dr. Ron Charles, who located Noah’s Ark in eastern Turkey.)

AI tools can produce authentic-sounding audio messages — even from your loved ones claiming they need money wired to them immediately. “A lot of modern-day fraud is about speed,” Holmes says, “getting someone to make a decision right now.”

“So many people tell me they are ‘too clever to fall for a scam’ and to those people I say, you’re wrong.” Which is why we need to brush away the shame of being ripped off. If victims didn’t feel so humiliated, they’d report these crimes more frequently and authorities could respond more effectively.

Holmes notes that the global cost of fraud to consumers in 2024 was estimated at $1 trillion — roughly equivalent to one Tesla compensation package for conspiracy theorist Elon Musk.

It doesn’t help that fraud is so lightly punished. “If I was going to be any kind of criminal,” Holmes says, “I would most definitely be a fraudster.” (I’m starting to think I shouldn’t have Venmoed her $875 for that miracle AbMaster that still hasn’t arrived.)

Near the end of the book, she calls for better coordination between agencies that deal with scams, and she castigates major social media platforms for essentially ignoring “acts of fraud that are happening on their watch.”

The Future of Fraud is neither a policy manifesto nor a definitive investigation, which most of us would never read no matter how important we consider the subject. It’s just an hour’s bright conversation with someone who might prod us to come up with better passwords than “password.”

“One thing is certain about fraud education in the future,” Holmes writes. “It’s going to need to be a lot more engaging.” That part of the future, at least, is right here.◆

The Geometry of Language

I did okay in high school English, but I really didn’t learn grammar until the summer before college when I devoted myself to a thick workbook called English 3200. That and my interest in 19th-century theology made me quite the catch on campus.

What I really wanted, though — and could never acquire — was entrée to the occult world of sentence diagramming. The practice began as a cloud of bubbles in an 1847 textbook and was formalized some three decades later with angular branches that spread deep into American education, much to the torment of generations of students.

Sentence diagramming lasted longer in Catholic schools, but at my anti-papist academy, the method was rooted out by the time I learned to keep a participle from dangling.

So it’s with some unnatural delight that I’ve discovered This Is a Book of Sentence Diagrams: A Journey Through the Sharpest, Strangest, and Most Stirring Lines Ever Constructed, by Man Martin.

A former English teacher, Martin eschews schoolmarmish explanations and merely presents an unadorned series of famous sentences diagrammed. It’s irresistible.

James Joyce, Toni Morrison, Pepé Le Pew, even God — all their utterances are sliced and diced into graphic order, some beautiful, some not.

“What sentence diagrams do is they compel you to think about each word carefully,” Martin tells me. “What is the role it plays in this sentence?”

How refreshingly countercultural: to slow down, to look precisely, to appreciate the words as a meaning-making contraption instead of a spool of AI slop.

“Diagrams present the sentences spatially,” he says, “and then the sentence itself presents the information temporally.”

Take Hamlet’s famous “to be or not to be” speech. “When you see that on the page diagrammed, it’s a series of forking paths. It actually is like a visual representation of a dilemma.”

The same clarity comes to Defense Secretary Donald Rumsfeld’s legendary gobbledygook about known knowns, known unknowns, and unknown unknowns.

“When you diagram the sentence, it’s precisely the weird tinker-toy of illogic that you imagine when you listen to it.”

Martin’s elegantly simple book — square in every sense — offers hand-drawn diagrams on every page.

He knows some people are fanatical about the practice, but he’s happy to appreciate diagrammed sentences merely as art.

“I think it’s kind of nice that they’re reincarnated at the present moment as a diversion rather than a discipline.”

A Penny Saved Is a Penny Earned

Steve Martin’s advice for becoming a millionaire: “First, get a million dollars.”

Sheila Bair, former chair of the FDIC, offers more practical advice: Don’t lose a million dollars.

Bair has devoted much of her career to protecting Americans’ savings, and now she’s published a book for young people about avoiding those financial leaks that siphon away future wealth.

I know — as graduation gifts go, this sounds as rousing as Bank of America’s 0.01% savings rate. But Bair’s overriding theme is a real kick in the piggy bank: Ordinary mistakes become extraordinary losses.

Throughout our lives, many of us fritter away small amounts that, if wisely invested, could swell to hundreds of thousands of dollars. And she’s got the receipts to prove it:

  • The average American spends $24 a month on bank fees — money that could grow to more than $150,000 over 50 years.

  • The $7,500 somebody squanders on rent-to-own furniture after getting a first job could grow to more than $350,000 over the course of their career.

  • The average credit card user loses $1,657 a year to interest charges — money that could grow to nearly $1 million by retirement.

Bair warns against risking your money on crypto, putting off saving for retirement, or falling for “buy now, pay later” schemes.

But she’s not entirely Scroogey. She acknowledges that a young person might need a car; she just insists they buy a practical used one. And she knows loans sometimes make sense. But if you pick a fancy-pants college for the bragging rights, borrow a ton of money to attend, and then fail to graduate, Bair will whack you upside the head with a mortarboard.

Committing all the financial sins in this book — a common litany of errors for Americans — would forfeit more than $7 million in potential wealth. That’s a lot of change to lose in the sofa cushions of life.

Bair is pitching “How Not to Lose a Million Dollars” for kids 12 to 17. I’d shift the target audience older — say, 16 to 21. And House Speaker Mike Johnson.

My only reservation is the smell of burnt avocado toast that wafts over these pages. There’s a presumption here that debt is always a foolish choice rather than a tragic necessity. “Building wealth,” Bair claims, “is primarily about establishing a regular savings habit and avoiding debt.” That position downplays structural inequities that for four decades have delivered most financial gains to the richest Americans. But that’s a subject for another book.

P.S. Planet Money: A Guide to the Economic Forces That Shape Your Life, a new collection of true stories and investigations by Alex Mayyasi, is just as fun and fascinating as the NPR podcast.◆

Picture This

Cats may act like they’re on top of the world, but they have dreams, too.

Take the white feline in The Rare Bird, by Caldecott Honor winner Elisha Cooper. This playful four-footed rascal spends a day hoping he’s a thing with feathers.

Darting across the living room, he’s flying through the forest. A dip in the toilet is an excursion into a “gurgling pond.”

“Then he hopped on a rock” — the family dog — “and he sang his morning song to the delight of everyone in the forest,” which is to say, no one in the house.

Even children who can’t read yet will love catching the funny differences between Cooper’s narration and his watercolor illustrations.

Sometimes, the cat’s imagination blossoms into two-page panoramas of the white bird in flight, even cuddling in the nest with a pair of suspicious robins.

As night falls, the cat stretches across a little girl’s lap and listens to a story about “other animals in the great wide world.”

Like an elephant.

This gently adventurous book for ages 3 to 6 is a lovely celebration of the power of imagination to transform an ordinary day — even if you’re prone to hairballs.◆

This Week’s Poem

May 25, Memorial Day, will mark the sixth anniversary of the death of George Floyd.

The title poem in Iain Haley Pollock’s most recent collection offers a harrowing reminder of what was lost that day — and what’s lost every time a life is snuffed out.

All the Possible Bodies

My alcoholic grandfather couldn’t hold his money
& passed a bad check. Hampton / Virginia / after Brown
v. Board but before Selma’s Bloody Sunday. After
my grandmother died but before he moved the family
North / back to the town of his birth. My mother saw
her father cuffed & dragged from the house. The next
day / by some miracle she never understood /
he came home
                   Had he looked at the officers wrong /
acted wrong / spoken wrong / been too familiar / been
a few inches taller / been deeper voiced or darker skinned
& for this had he caught a knee that night to the neck
& not returned / perhaps my mother / needing to stay home
& raise Aunt Joan & Uncle Keith / would have skipped
college. Had she skipped college / perhaps she would not
have gone for a Ph.D. Had she not gone for a Ph.D. /
perhaps she would not have met my father. Had she
not met my father / perhaps I would not be here /
perhaps the boys sleeping upstairs from me now
would not be here.
When you spend 8 minutes & 46 seconds / with your knee /
on another man’s neck /
                                     you block not just the passage
of air / into his body / but block air into all the possible
bodies / dependent on that man /
& his neck / to breathe / breathe / breathe /
breathe / breathe —

The Writing Life

“In small, quiet homes, dreams are folded away like worn clothes.”
                                   — Katelyn F.

Saturday night, 200 people gathered at Arena Stage in Washington to hear young people read their poems and stories.

This Big Book Bash celebrated the publication of Living in the Storm, a collection written by students at H.D. Woodson High School’s Collegiate Academy and published by 826DC, a literary nonprofit. (I’m on the board.)

With great poise, the Woodson students read pieces about poverty, alienation, inequality, racism, anxiety, illiteracy, and gun violence.

The kids are all right — their work is a testament to that — but they’re facing tremendous challenges.

The Young Authors Book Project from 826DC works with students at a D.C. school to help them choose an issue important to them and then raise a call to action through their poetry.

The program reflects a conviction that empowering young people to articulate their concerns in writing and see those words published in a handsomely designed book can help transform their lives and our society.

This year’s students describe in concrete terms problems that too many adults would rather wave away with vague sighs of resignation.

In “Put the Guns Down,” Janeja F. writes,

I reached home after practice with my body feeling like I was
thrown off a building
At about nine o’clock pm
The sun went down, and the Guns came up again
(BOOM, BOOM, BOOM)
Duck down crawling on the floor
Police sirens then reached my ears, I know someone is going to be in tears.

Taylor P. ends her poem by asking, “I’ve wiped my eyes, have you?”◆
