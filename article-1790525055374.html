All In Your Head: You Be My People Power

this conversation needs to happen, this book needs to sell

Freddie deBoer
Sep 27
 
READ IN APP
 

For the first time in 15 years, I’m back in the pages of Playboy, parsing survey data about Gen Z’s attitudes towards sex and romance. I also have a piece up in a wonderful new collection from The Lamp about the seven deadly sins; I tackle pride. And you can find me on C. Derrick Varn’s podcast talking education from last month here. It’s a really good big-picture look at my broader thoughts on education. With promo season here, there’s a lot more where this came from coming up.

Months back I exhorted you to preorder a copy of my upcoming book and promised that I would have only one more post dedicated to that purpose, coming a month before publication. That day has arrived. And while I will surely be posting a link to purchase the book for years to come, and will be sharing promo appearances for the book, this will be the last time I will beg you in this way, as the sole purpose of an entire post. Today, I am begging, because it’s important for me and, as pretentious as it is to say, important for our society. Because my book puts its finger on very real problems that have very real victims, in a way that our media is afraid to discuss thanks to ongoing activist pressure. As usual, I am looking to start a fight, and this one needs to be had. It’s overdue.

Put it this way. If you pay $50 for an annual subscription here (a price which has not increased in this newsletter’s five and a half year existence), I get almost 90% of that $50. If you pay $30 for the book, it only three-fifths as much money and I get something on the order of 10%. And yet despite this grim math, if you can choose to pay for one or the other but not both, I would much rather you buy the book. Buy the book! You can interpret this preference as idealistic or mercenary as you care to - because I invested so much of myself in this text; because I want to continue to be a writer of books and I will only get to keep on writing them if this one sells; because I care about the book and the issues it details and its arguments about them; because if this book does well, there’s a chance I can continue to live and work as a writer and thinker and to potentially get a university job…. There’s also obviously a whole conversation about the fact that he newsletter is digital and something that I control entirely and that is endlessly replicable, while the book is a physical thing produced by another company with a significant financial investment in its success, etc etc. Not to complicate matters - I want you to keep subscribing here, obviously - but if you’re asking me what to do with your scarce dollars, please buy this book.

Look, nonfiction book sales are in a bad, bad way right now. Getting on the bestseller lists, some weeks, can now require selling a third or fewer books compared to even ten years ago. This is bad news for people like me who worship books and live in ideas and who think an engaged and informed citizenry is necessary for democracy. But perhaps it also gives us an opening - with the standards lower, maybe I can sell enough copies of this thing that the media, which is resistant both to me and these ideas, has to pay attention.

You’re more interested in why the book is good, true, and necessary. Fair.

  • Things have undeniably changed. The world of disability, and particularly of psychological, developmental, cognitive, and behavioral disabilities, is unrecognizable from only ten years ago. I have had direct personal, political, academic, and professional investment in these issues for my whole adult life, and I am still regularly shocked at how much things have changed.

  • For the worse. Disability and specific disabilities are now vaguer, less distinct, more subject to fads and fashion, less medically defined and sensible, and less diagnostically useful. Scarce resources are divided up less fairly, the public is increasingly skeptical, authorities that serve necessary gatekeeping functions are too scared to do so, an entire predatory litigation industry creeps around the whole thing.

  • In favor of elites and at the expense of the most-afflicted. The gentrification of disability is real. Changes to disability culture, policy, and law have overwhelmingly tracked with the preferences of the professional-managerial class, with the disability studies and disability rights communities overwhelmingly made up of the educated elite and increasingly shameless about serving that population. The inherent and structural communication and power advantages of the least-afflicted have been exacerbated in innumerable ways. Disability, as a concept in public discourse, has never been more devoted to those who need the least help.

  • It keeps getting worse. For all of the talk of vibe shifts and “Woke 1.0,” in the realm of disability it’s forever 2020. The activist set is more emboldened and influential than they were five years ago, not less; the media is more deferential and anxious to dispute the perceived “more progressive” position on these issues, not more skeptical and confident. The number of people who get pulled into the cult of disability as fashion grows by the day, and each becomes in effect an advocate for these ugly changes.

And, look, I don’t want to belabor this next bit, which I know people find tiresome and self-aggrandizing. But, I must say it: in addition to the loud and energetic defenders of the disability theory camp who reject my perspective on these topics, there are many out there who are eager to impede my ability to sell books, influence debate, and generally enjoy success as a writer. You can read about it here, if you’re a masochist. But please understand that what I’ve described there is not incidental, passive, intermittent displeasure towards me but rather deliberate, active, and constant. I know for a fact that specific people in the industry agitate against any publication running my work. The WaPo review of my second book had to be corrected within a few hours of publication for elementary errors; the NYT review of that book is a bizarre screed from a former editor of the Books section who muscled his way onto the review to prosecute an old feud. My novel was greeted with blatant and unrepentant lies from prominent media people on BlueSky as well as a massive and coordinated review bombing on Goodreads. (The latter has compelled me to swear off the network, after years and years as a dedicated and happy reviewer there.) The disability activist and academic class is already coming after me. I understand that all of this is self-serving, but it’s also all true. True and quite discouraging.

The upshot is this: only you can make this book a success and through it enable me to keep doing this. Quite literally the only thing I have going for me, with this project, is people power. This book and its arguments are not welcome in the social and professional cultures that do so much to determine success in the book market and which still maintain both influence and a very narrow definition of who should succeed and who should fail. It’s also just clearly the case that publishing has set itself up to favor certain voices and certain people; that’s just true, the kind of thing people pretend not to believe publicly and happily admit anonymously. Selling enough copies is really the only way I can force the world to pay attention - not to me, ultimately, but to the very real issues at hand in my book, and through them the many angry people who support my perspective and feel silenced, people with disabilities and their loved ones and those who are forced to navigate our disability accommodation systems with all its broken incentives and bad habits. Again with the caveat that that is all obviously self-serving, I think this book is important and I need your help for it to matter.

I’m a difficult, abrasive person, but then most of us with severe mental illness are. And a big part of what has motivated me, in this project, is my anger about the hypocrisy of the elite set, which claims to “honor” and “validate” the voices of people like me, while trying to police the debate in such a way that everyone who is actually afflicted is marginalized and silenced. I am an imperfect messenger - but that imperfection is exactly why I’m the right messenger. It is nonsensical and hypocritical to celebrate the perspective of hypothetical people with disabilities while working to exclude those who actually have them. And most of us who have actual psychiatric illnesses are as hard to like as I am. That’s the whole point, and I cannot stand the hypocrisy anymore, the hypocrisy of those who say “I stand against ableism, and I lift the voices of the disabled… just not you.”

Simon & Schuster’s landing page has a variety of options for buying the book in the right sidebar, including from independent booksellers. If you’ve not yet preordered but might, I have excerpts up for everyone and for subscribers specifically. I know $30 is a lot to ask. But I’m asking all the same.
