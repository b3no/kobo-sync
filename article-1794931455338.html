Psychiatry’s Syphilis Problem

the field’s basic nature ensures that its problems will always be hard and its solutions provisional and unsatisfying

Freddie deBoer
Nov 17
 
READ IN APP
 

I’m grateful to Dr. Martin Greenwald of Socratic Psychiatrist for laying out the basic reasoning here, which is something I’ve pawed at for awhile but could never really think through or articulate effectively until he explained it to me. I’m talking about psychiatry’s syphilis problem, a kind of branding issue for a field of medicine that is forever struggling to improve its public perception.

Go back in time to an earlier era of medicine, really not that long ago at all - 125 years in the past or so would put you pretty firmly into this period. What were psychiatrists spending most of their time on? Schizophrenia, certainly, and depression (if under another name) and “hysteria,” disorders that are still clearly classified as matters of mental health today. But for much of the nineteenth century, one of the most common conditions in the asylum was a mysterious condition often called general paralysis of the insane, or sometimes general paresis. Patients came in with grandiose delusions, personality changes, mania, memory loss…. Such patients would, over time, decline into a state of dementia, suffer from paralysis, and eventually die. “Alienists,” as psychiatrists were then often called, identified this all as a distinct and terrible form of terminal madness. Doctors at asylums often blamed overwork, alcoholism, or “genetic degeneracy,” all of which fit the moralizing framework that was common to the study of mental illness at the time. In some institutions, this illness made up a tenth or more of male admissions. Clinicians like Alfred Fournier argued for a link to syphilis, but that connection wasn’t proven until 1913 when Hideyo Noguchi and J. W. Moore found the spirochete Treponema pallidum - the bacteria that causes syphilis - in the brains of patients suffering in this way. A disease that had been treated as a disorder of the mind (and for perfectly understandable reasons) turned out to be a bacterial infection of the nervous system.

It’s worth saying that clinical observations and research conducted by psychiatrists were essential to discovering the true origins of “general paralysis of the insane.” And for the record, the first effective treatment of syphilis was developed by a psychiatrist; Julius Wagner Jauregg deliberately infected patients with malaria to induce fevers that killed the bacteria, for which he won the 1927 Nobel Prize. This was obviously an imperfect way to treat the condition, but it worked, at a time when bacteriology was in its infancy. When penicillin arrived in the 1940s it finally made syphilis consistently preventable and curable, without requiring exposure to another deadly illness. General paresis, or whatever other term you prefer, largely disappeared from psychiatric wards. Syphilis is still a problem, especially in the developing world. But it’s a treatable, preventable disease now, and vastly fewer people are having their minds destroyed by it.

If you traveled back in time and told psychiatrists (or proto-psychiatrists) about this future success, they’d celebrate it as a great victory for the field. And yet, today, no one counts the dramatic reduction of syphilis-caused insanity as a triumph of psychiatry. Most people wouldn’t think to connect the treatment of syphilis to mental health medicine at all. And this, I would posit, is a permanent disadvantage for psychiatry, a field with a really awful public reputation and an endless number of critics. One set of those critics blasts psychiatry for failing to meet the evidentiary basis of other fields of medicine, for not finding specific physiological origins for mental illnesses; another set of critics angrily insists that there are no such origins to be found and that attempting to find them is to inherently minimize the role of emotion, culture, politics, economics, and sundry other factors. Psychiatry sits in the middle, by its nature treating conditions where the boundaries are fuzzy and the origins mysterious, taking its lumps. Despite what the antipsychiatry cultists would tell you, someone has to do the job; mental illness ruins lives. And yet the field that treats those disorders remains permanently scrutinized, view skeptically, in part because whenever more traditional medical origins and treatments are developed for given illnesses, those illnesses are handed off to other branches of medicine.

So let’s consider the following hypothetical. Suppose it was revealed that schizophrenia was the result of a previously-undiscovered type of brain tumor. And let’s further suppose that current means to treat tumors, like radiation or chemotherapy, were found to be effective in addressing these schizophrenogenic tumors and reducing symptoms, such that schizophrenia rates were dramatically reduced. This would be a huge victory for humanity. And you could go back and ask psychiatrists in the past, decade upon decade, what they would define as the biggest possible victory for the field, and find that they would say developing effective treatments for schizophrenia. But in our hypothetical world, would curing schizophrenia by treating brain tumors be seen as a triumph of psychiatry? No, it would be seen as a triumph of oncology. And this isn’t just a minor hypothetical issue. The conditions I’m discussing here point to very real problems with psychiatry’s public perception: problems of the mind and how they’re treated are always going to be subject to scrutiny and criticism that are not common to other kinds of medicine, given the fundamental nature of the mind, and those psychiatric conditions that are proven to have specific physiological origins will quickly be handed over to other fields of medicine.

Psychiatry needs better critics. All fields of medicine have problems, but few have attracted as vociferous and constant a set of critics as psychiatry. Unfortunately, these critics are as repetitive and incurious as they are fixated. I devoted the first several chapters of my book to debunking antipsychiatry because those arguments are pernicious and unserious. One core issue is that so, so much of antipsychiatry rhetoric depends on wishing away the most debilitated patients, those who can’t be said to be just living an “alternative lifestyle” by any minimally honest person; this dovetails, in a very unfortunate way, with the gentrification of disability and the online refusal to accept how bad mental illness can really get. And antipsychiatry has always lived in close proximity to pure conspiracist lunacy. But, sure, there are problems with psychiatry, just like there are problems with cardiology, sports medicine, reproductive medicine, nephrology…. Psychiatry deserves and needs criticism. But the only effective criticism is fair criticism, and fair, effective criticism of how we treat mental illness has to take into account the profound and unique challenges that are inherent to mental health.

A field of medicine that achieves victory over its various maladies precisely at that point that those maladies stop being seen as part of its purview is one that will always operated under a profound disadvantage. And psychiatry needs to receive its due credit. Psychiatry saves lives; it saved mine.
