The Verdict by Jerusalem Demsas

The topline finding from The Argument’s latest national poll of 1,549 registered voters, conducted Oct. 21-23, is that Republicans have completely debased themselves in the eyes of the voters.

Democrats are leading by 9 points among registered voters on the generic ballot (and by 11 among the likely electorate), and likely voters say Democrats will do a better job on the issues that are most important to them, 49% to 38%.

Voters think Democrats do better on the "most important" issues
Which party do you believe will do a better job on the issues that are most important to you?

But approval ratings alone don’t tell us what voters think the parties actually represent. Here are some of my biggest takeaways.

Democrats listen to the groups; Republicans are in a cult of personality

We asked who party leaders listen to most when deciding what policies to support. For Republicans, the answer is Trump and wealthy donors, whereas for Democrats the top answer is lobbyists and interest groups. Just 24% say Democratic leaders listen to “people like me,” but that’s a good deal better than for Republicans (17%).

Republicans listen to wealthy donors, Democrats listen to lobbyists and interest groups
Who registered voters think party leaders listen to most when deciding which policies to support. Respondents could select up to three.

The moderation wars continue

The moderation debate within the Democratic Party has pitted folks like The Argument staff writer Lakshya Jain and columnist Matthew Yglesias, who argue that moderation is important in order for Democrats to achieve electoral success, against political scientists like Adam Bonica and Jake Grumbach, who argue that moderation’s electoral payoff has been oversold. Others on the left go further and say Democrats don’t even need to moderate; they just need to be bold.

Our poll can’t settle what moderation does for candidates, but it can tell you in which direction voters think the party has erred.

Among every age group, more voters say the Democratic Party is too far to the left than too far to the right, but there’s a wrinkle: Voters already see Democrats as the more moderate party, 35% to 25%, and they also label Republicans as the more extreme one, 48% to 35%.

If you’re someone who thinks the party has to be moderate to win elections, it’s clear that whatever moderation the Democrats have done (relative to the Republican Party’s antics) has worked. The question, of course, is whether this is sufficient when the issue environment shifts toward issues like crime and immigration or when Democrats are in power.

There’s a complication for moderation-skeptics too: Democrats lead on appearing compassionate and “cares about people like me” while Republicans win on bold and strong.

Democrats are compassionate, Republicans are bold
Share of registered voters who say each word or phrase describes the Democratic Party more or the Republican Party more.

Republicans are seen as the bolder party by a wide margin (41% to 19%) in a poll where they’re losing by 9 points! There is a bizarre strain of thinking among some on the left that in order to be successful, Democrats should emulate a historically unpopular and chaotic administration.

Republicans care about immigration, they think Democrats care about … gay people?

When we ask Harris voters what the Democratic Party cares about most, we get scattered answers: cost of living (50%), democracy (45%) and health care (43%) all land within 7 points of each other.

Trump voters have a … much crisper picture of Democrats: 72% say the party cares most about LGBT issues, and 46% say climate change.

Meanwhile, the picture of Republicans is clear on both sides: 67% of Trump voters and 77% of Harris voters say the GOP cares most about immigration (just 15% of voters think the GOP prioritizes cost of living).

DSA vs. DSA

The Democratic Socialists of America is having a moment, but there’s a striking disconnect between the leadership of DSA and voters who are favorable to DSA. I first noticed this earlier this year when we saw in our polling that voters with favorable views of DSA were the most supportive of free trade — a very controversial position within DSA despite Karl Marx’s famous 1848 pro-free-trade speech where he argued that it would hasten the social revolution.

So, when we got this poll, I decided to look under the hood and find out how voters favorable to DSA felt about the parties:

Among Democratic voters¹, those with a favorable view of the DSA are also more likely to view the Democratic Party favorably: 85%, compared with 65% of voters who view the DSA unfavorably or are unfamiliar with it. This enthusiasm extends to their party’s former nominee Kamala Harris and the one other Democrat we tested in this poll, Jon Ossoff.

Voters who like the DSA love the Democratic Party
Share with a very or somewhat favorable view, among Democratic congressional voters (including leaners).

While cost of living is the leading concern for DSA-friendly Democrats, democracy is substantially more prominent than among other Democrats. Notably, while Palestine is a major organizing priority for DSA, just 4% of DSA-friendly Democrats selected “foreign policy” among their two most important issues, compared with 8% of other Democrats.

Cost of living comes first. Democracy sets them apart.
Top issues among Democratic congressional voters (including leaners). Respondents selected two.

DSA-friendly Democrats are more politically engaged, report greater certainty that they will vote, and consume news across a wider range of media. This isn’t an especially young constituency, either: 18% are under 30, compared with 24% of other Democratic voters. Not much was interesting about their demographic makeup except on income: They are concentrated in households earning $50,000-$99,999 a year — 51%, compared with 37% of other Democratic voters.

In other words, DSA-friendly Democrats are less like a revolutionary vanguard and more like the Democratic Party’s most loyal partisans: Resistance liberals.

Midterm update with Lakshya Jain

Lakshya Jain, our director of political data, uses data from The Argument’s polling operation to inform the Split Ticket 2026 House and Senate models. Here’s his update on the state of play this week:

After adding this week’s polls to our model, the odds of a Democratic flip of the House of Representatives are now at a cycle-high 91%.

In the Senate, Democratic odds have risen to 70% after a wave of extremely strong polls for the party. Those polls show particularly strong gains in Kansas, where Democratic challenger Adam Hamilton is now in a dead heat with incumbent Republican Roger Marshall. (Our model gives the race a 47% chance of going Democratic.)

This is, frankly, the most confident our model has ever been about a blue wave. What exactly materializes on election night is anyone’s guess. But according to every signal, the real question is not whether Republicans will lose on Tuesday, but by how much.

Life of the Mind with Kelsey Piper

Kelsey Piper, The Argument’s staff writer reporting on AI, tech, science, education, and politics, tells us what she’s seeing in the world this week:

We know that open-source AI models are approaching the hacking capabilities of Mythos Preview, the Claude model that Anthropic refused to release to the public in the spring due to cybersecurity concerns.

So far, I would say we’re at... 5/10 on the cyber-apocalypse scale? The only reason it’s not higher is that emergency patches are being announced at a frenzied pace. As stressful as that is for everyone in cybersecurity, it’s a sign that our defenses are holding up.

However, there have already been successful hacks that have broken through. For example, on Thursday, billions of dollars were stolen in a hack of the cryptocurrency exchange Binance and millions were siphoned out of Polymarket. It seems likely that these platforms were targeted because it’s easiest to monetize a cryptocurrency hack, rather than because the platforms have poor cybersecurity defenses.

The best guess of the people I’ve spoken to is that we are in for a rocky few years.

🌟Abundance Win of the Week🌟

Milan Singh, one of The Argument’s writing fellows, gives us his pick for this week’s biggest win in the wide world of abundance:

On Tuesday, the Pittsburgh City Council voted to approve a major zoning reform package. The legislation, which has been in the works since 2024, makes it easier to get permits to build accessory dwelling units, such as small backyard homes or rental units in basements or attics.

The package also eliminates minimum parking requirements for new developments. (These requirements make it more expensive to build, in turn making it harder to expand the supply of housing.)

The original proposal included mandatory inclusionary zoning, which would have functioned as a tax on new development. Instead, the final version contains a voluntary program in which builders can obtain development incentives if a certain number of a new building’s units are kept “affordable” for 35 years.
