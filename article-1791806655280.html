When “I Don’t Owe You Anything” Culture Collides with Affirmative Consent

Some sex is consensual but harmful.

READ IN APP
 

Something happened at my college in the olden days of the late 2000s which still lives rent-free in my mind. A fraternity developed a game where you won points for sleeping with girls who were on the extremes of attractiveness. Sleeping with an average-looking girl was minimally rewarding, but you would be greatly rewarded if you bagged a 10 or a 1. Of course, these boys eventually found it was much easier to bag a 1, so they began scouting campus parties for the “ugliest” girls they could find, often bringing them back for sex and then recording the girl’s ugliness on whatever system they were using for their scoring. I still don’t know who won. This was the type of game that yielded primarily losers.

As far as I know, the girls consented. They weren’t drugged. No legal cases came out of this, not even a singular rumor of sexual assault. But clearly, what the boys did was wrong. The girls did not know—at least not until later—that they were playing the “ugly chick” in a morally repugnant game. They likely wouldn’t have participated if they knew. You could make the argument that they should have been more discerning about why a much better looking boy wanted to sleep with them, but is the onus not still on the boys for engaging in sexual bullying, the same way the onus would be on a group of girls who had sex with ugly boys for the sole purpose of ridiculing them? (I’m aware some ugly boys wouldn’t care and would simply enjoy the sex because men do not feel as embarrassed about such things—but I maintain it would still be wrong!)

If you were around for the 2000s-era time of liberalism being the “cool” South Park laissez-fiare young person philosophy that would live and let live, smoke a joint and leave gay people alone, then you might recall saying, or hearing, that “what two consenting adults do in their own bedroom is nobody’s business.” And hey, why stop at two, and why limit this to the bedroom? As long as everyone is consenting and not a minor, nobody can judge. And if you do judge, you are kink-shaming, slut-shaming, uptight, and not minding your own business.

I said this plenty of times. I probably said it last week! Liberals were pushing back on theocratic right-wing sentiment that the only acceptable sex to have is within matrimony and for procreation. And, hello, it’s 2026. People will date, and by virtue of dating not always ending in marriage, you’re probably going to sleep with a few people before you get married. Some people are gay. Some people (although I think it’s a very small percentage) are polyamorous. I’m sure plenty of people can have a threesome or an orgy and come out feeling just fine about it. And because we don’t live under the Taliban, it’s not our government’s job to police consensual sexual activity.

Now, while policing and judging this activity should not be the domain of law enforcement, things spun out of control when we decided nobody was allowed to make any moral judgement unless a law was broken. This put us in a strange situation when confronted with a sexual act that was consensual but indisputably harmed someone. We created a framework wherein consent alone determined whether something was morally wrong or harmful (I cannot take credit for this observation, I first heard it on Feminine Chaos) and if something was harmful, it was either actually non-consensual in some way that might not be apparent to anyone, or it was fine, and the person who was harmed needed to take accountability for their own bad decisions and entitlement. Did you really think someone you slept with owed you anything other than deigning not to rape you? Grow up!

This, my friends, is the combination of affirmative consent culture meeting the secular, therapy-speak idea that you don’t owe anyone anything. As long as everyone consented and there was no problematic power imbalance, you were not (at least in my liberal milieu) allowed to judge sexual behavior that was deceptive, selfish, or hurtful. It was “nobody’s business” if you cheated, knowingly slept with a cheater, used sex as a manipulation tactic, treated a sexual partner poorly, or lied to get sex. If you took issue with this, the rebuttal was the same: well, it was legal. This also meant that if sexual behavior was consensual but immoral or obnoxious, there could be a kneejerk instinct to brand it as a form of non-consent, even if it fell nowhere near the legal definition. The problem is that cheating, using sex to manipulate, lying to get sex, or being pushy and obnoxious about sex, are immoral acts. But we lost the language to talk about sexual immorality that has nothing to do with consent and is totally legal, especially from a secular point of view. Under the new framework, unless you were assaulted, you should not, and cannot, expect any decency or moral code from anyone you sleep with.

One problem is that one person’s sexual immorality is another person’s “live and let live.” I do not care if people are gay, polyamorous, kinky, whatever. I understand BDSM is hotly debate (especially by radical feminists) and sometimes regarded as something that only ever degrades women, that no woman would ever choose without a man pressuring her, which is always abusive even with consent but…man, I disagree. This may be true sometimes, but also, plenty of people are just into weird shit—shit way weirder than being spanked and dominated. Nobody would ever postulate that an executive who pays a dominatrix to step on his balls was as victim of sexual assault or intimate partner violence. Sometimes, people just want to get their rocks off, and they’re into stuff that other people would find abhorrent. This is where “it’s not my business what consenting adults do” is perfectly valid.

I draw the line at sexual interactions that involve deception and manipulation—ones where consent might be withdrawn if one party understood what the other party was thinking or planning on doing next. Some people have tried to re-brand these interactions as a form of non-consent—see the infamous 2018 NYT Modern Love article called, He Asked Permission to Touch but Not to Ghost. As silly as it is to declare, “I was non-consensually ghosted,” this author was probably trying to grapple with the fact that sex can be immoral, deceptive or hurtful without being illegal. Framing it in the language of “consent” was ridiculous, because ghosting is not about consent at all. You cannot be inverse-raped when someone has sex with you but then doesn’t commit to you. Ghosting may not even be immoral, provided the ghoster did not make false promises with the intention of getting sex, knowing full well they planned on ghosting. What if you like someone, and then stop liking them? Must you ask permission to stop liking them? No. Such is life. But deceptively lying to get sex and then ghosting is harmful, and if “consent” is our only framework for whether something is harmful or not, people who believe such a thing will reframe unfortunate but consensual situations as non-consensual.

The year of “I didn’t consent to be ghosted,” was also the peak of affirmative consent culture, which famously told young people that no might mean no, but yes doesn’t necessarily mean yes—that women might verbally consent and have sex with a guy but secretly this could become non-consensual at any time, and the woman was not obligated to tell the man when the consent had expired (I’ve been sexually assaulted, and I found this framework needlessly paternalistic—unless you are deeply intoxicated or under some kind of threat, an adult woman is completely capable of just saying no, and if she isn’t, she’s not mature enough to have sex.) While our definition of “non-consent” became a lot broader, we loosened up about everything that fell under the umbrella of consent. If everyone consented, it was fine, and if anything bad happened within that consensual encounter, or as a result of it, then only a conservative church lady in a floral hat would have anything to say about it.

I found myself getting into an argument about this on—wait for it—the infamous Jezebel comments section. Jezebel, for those blissfully unfamiliar, was an online feminist publication in the 2000s-2010s which had a comments section that was notorious for being especially toxic, filled with both misogynistic trolls and intra-feminist dogpiles. (On a separate occasion, I had gotten into it with someone who referred to Jews as “higher in the privilege hierarchy than whites” and therefore in need of more significant repentance.) Anyway, on this particular day, I had made some comment about how it was morally reprehensible to knowingly sleep with a married person (implied here was that the spouse of the married person didn’t know, and wouldn’t be okay with it.) I thought this was a pretty standard-issue opinion. Sure, perhaps the person breaking their marital vows was doing something worse, but knowingly engaging in an affair is also quite bad.

Although I made it clear that my judgment was gender-neutral, I was immediately accused of being a misogynist incel troll who was “slut shaming” women. Once it became clear that I was a woman, they accused me of being an uptight virgin who probably couldn’t even get a man. I didn’t understand why these feminists were so eager to go to bat for people who were facilitating adultery, which harmed women just as much as it harmed men. And then it hit me—they were all operating under the moral code that they “did not owe anyone anything” and that all sex was fine if everyone consented. The cheater’s spouse’s lack of consent was immaterial because nobody was forcing sex on that person. The cheater’s child’s wellbeing was irrelevant because they weren’t being molested. The other spouse’s sexual health being at risk was also not part of the equation because none of those things fell under the umbrella of rape.

Some commenters conceded that if the cheated-on wife was a close friend of the other woman then a line had been crossed (they refused to acknowledge that all genders could be involved, and repeatedly framed it as “other woman” versus “wife”) so presumably, they owed something to their friends, but not to people in general, which was a surprisingly libertarian view for a bunch of leftists to have. (Imagine making this same argument just a few years later, for why you didn’t have to wear a mask!)

I went back and forth with them on this for far longer than I should have, repeatedly asking why they found it morally permissible to knowingly be complicit in harming another person—not in a “buying pasta from a problematic retailer” way, but in a “one degree of separation, ruining someone’s life” kind of way. Their reasoning always boiled down to, “I don’t owe anyone anything, and it was legal so who cares?” Eventually, someone told me that my priorities were all screwy. They were a good fucking person, and they cared about “real issues” facing women like street harassment, not stupid pearl-clutching stuff like adultery.

This comment was highly upvoted, and I remember finding it the most bizarre. How many women, if faced with the options of: A.) having their husband cheat on them and B.) getting catcalled on the street, would actually choose the former? How was street harassment a “real” issue but adultery wasn’t? They had decided that the only party worth any concern was the person receiving unwanted, non-consensual sexual attention. Any harm that came from any other type of sexual misconduct was legal, and therefore nobody’s business—even if it was magnitudes more damaging.

There are many other scenarios which are damaging and hurtful but still technically consensual, and they cut across gender lines. I recently watched the Louis Theroux documentary about polyamory, where the term “liberal enclave” is immediately denoted with this award-winning image. No notes:

Anyway, one of the throuples featured on the show involved a woman with two boyfriends. (Keep in mind I can only report what I saw in the documentary—it’s possible they were given a bad edit.) The woman and her first boyfriend started out as monogamous, but one day she told him she strongly desired a relationship with another man (who later became the second boyfriend.) She already had feelings for the second guy; he did not exist as an abstract concept, so at the very least she had engaged in some kind of emotional infidelity. Now, technically the first guy consented to entering into a throuple after this conversation. But his consent came from the fact that his girlfriend told him that she needed this second boyfriend because of her history with severe depression and her risk of self-harm. When Louis starts probing further about the woman’s mental health, the first boyfriend breaks down crying.

Technically, everyone here consented! They’re all adults! But clearly, from what was shown in the documentary at least, this guy was manipulated into this agreement which did not really benefit him. He was not sexually involved with the other man, and as far as the documentary showed, he did not have an additional girlfriend. To add insult to injury, he reported that the second boyfriend lasts “hours” in bed, and when they tried to have a threesome he had to leave and get lunch after climaxing because he got bored. And because he loved this woman and feared for what might happen if she became too depressed, he stayed in this shitty relationship.

One could say this guy was a gullible idiot deserves what he got, and that he should be held accountable for his own decisions. Play stupid games, win stupid prizes. And yes, he’s an adult and he was not forced at gunpoint to enter a throuple. But he entered into it, believing that the woman he loved would become dangerously depressed if he didn’t comply. Is this a legal issue? An issue of non-consent? No, but I still think she did something wrong, and the blame should not lie with the manipulated party!

You could make a similar connection to Lindy West’s polyamory origin story, which I (like any other genderslop writer) already covered. This was not a story of non-consent. But Lindy’s spouse also guilted and manipulated her into polyamory by saying monogamy (and the expectation of monogamy) was racist, that her desire to be his only partner was codependent, and then he tried to flatter her (will full awareness of her physical insecurities) by saying that his girlfriend had a crush on her and wanted to see sexy photos of her. (Up until that point, Lindy always publicly identified as straight.) Now, just like the guy from the Louis Theroux documentary, Lindy West could have kicked her spouse to the curb. But it was probably clear to him that she wouldn’t, because a great deal of her writing has been about her low self-worth based on her weight, and her longstanding belief that being fat made her unlovable. No crime was committed here, and everyone involved was a consenting adult, but there is a reason people reacted so negatively to this story. Just because nobody involved is breaking a law doesn’t mean ethical boundaries haven’t been crossed, or that it’s stuffy and conservative to point that out.

(Of course, as I said before, plenty of people are happily polyamorous, although I think it’s a pretty small minority of people. I still think that plenty of people are manipulated into polyamory when they don’t really want it. Again, not an issue of consent but an issue of ethics, and I will not blame the manipulated party, even if that person is an adult.)

As a society, we are not very sympathetic to people who are harmed within consensual sexual interactions. This is, we insist, just the business of dating and sex, and sometimes things are messy. This is true in the sense that sometimes people have rough breakups, sometimes feelings are unrequited, and sometimes people aren’t as nice to their partners as they should be. But sometimes, there is deliberate harm (or at least obvious, preventable harm that someone has selfishly ignored). And still, people have a tendency to either say it’s not their business, or victim-blame the person who got played.

I think people have a kneejerk negative reaction to victims of this sort of harm for several reasons. First, there is nothing we can really do. The reflexive reaction is, “Well, it was legal,” because the assumption is that by calling adultery immoral, they are trying to jail all the adulterers. Typically what victims of consensual but immoral sexual behavior want is an acknowledgement that someone has harmed them, and the ability to openly judge that behavior without sounding like a prude, but that’s a lot mushier and harder to pin down. And surely, some people do agree with them, even if others will tell them it’s all their fault.

At this point you might be wondering what I’m really suggesting here, if I’m not suggesting legal consequences for being a jerk. And, as I said before and as you likely know, there is no shortage of people who judged Lindy West’s spouse and would be even quicker to judge a straightforward cheater. My argument is not that this type of behavior should be illegal, or that nobody judges it as-is. But often, when someone is treating another person poorly in a way that is still completely legal, other people in their lives stay out of it and say it’s not their business. If your friend starts a hookup-buddy relationship with someone who is clearly interested in romance, you probably won’t say anything about it, especially if you have no connection to this other person. If your friend is sleeping with a married person, you might say, “Well, it’s not your vow!” and excuse it. Of course, you could openly disapprove of your friend’s behavior and they might go ahead and do it anyway. But surely, it shouldn’t be considered prudish overstepping to voice your opinion about legal but hurtful sexual behavior.

The second reason that we often feel unsympathetic towards the people who are harmed from consensual sex is that these are typically people who expected something that was not given to them, as opposed to people who received something they didn’t want. The woman who expected commitment from a guy who willfully misled her and ghosted. The guy who thought that the much-younger woman was actually interested in him and not just using him for his money. These are typically not sympathetic figures because more often than not, they are attempting to punch above their weight in the first place, and many people see their downfall as a natural consequence of expecting too much. Who amongst us has not laughed at the absolute rubes getting scammed on 90 Day Fiance, gender aside? All of this could have been avoided if they had been slightly less credulous and just dated another dowdy middle-aged person in Kansas. And in a worst case scenario, people are all too eager to dogpile and blame the victim as long as what happened was consensual. What did you expect? Why didn’t you leave? Do you not have agency? This is the refrain frequently leveled against women who stayed in very long relationships with men who said they would propose but never did, whittling away their girlfriends’ fertility window. Yes, these women could leave, but if they are repeatedly being told that a proposal is around the corner (when it actually isn’t) is the onus not at least somewhat on the lying party?

We need to establish some degree of secular ethics beyond what should or shouldn’t result in jail time. Harassment and assault are not the only ways sex can be unethical, and something can be both legal and wrong. Of course, we will never agree on the specifics. My attitudes might be a bit more liberal than others, who believe all group sex and all casual sex and all kink is wrong. But at the very least, we should find common ground somewhere. And surely, “finding the ugliest girl to have sex with for my scorekeeping Ugly Game” should fall in that place.
