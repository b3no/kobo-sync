The Buck-a-Book solution

Following up on the Authors Guild study

This newsletter is about libraries. A lot of readers get agitated when I write about libraries, call me names, and attribute ideas and opinions to me that I don’t actually hold. It’s been suggested I must have been kicked out of a public library for watching Internet porn.

Some people seem to think it’s mean-spirited to question anything about public libraries, which they love, as do I. But even good things can have unintended consequences, and that’s what this piece is about. Stay to the end—I propose a solution that everybody should be happy with, including librarians.

First, some background. I first started writing about public libraries six years ago when I realized that I’d made a significant miscalculation about book publishing. I’d looked at the record industry and newspapers and magazines and concluded that books had done a better job than these other media of holding their prices through the transition to digital. Album sales were getting killed by streaming, newspapers and magazines were closing as the Internet absorbed their advertising revenue, yet books were still mostly selling in the range of $20 or $30 a copy.

Turns out I was half right. The book industry hadn’t been seriously disrupted by digital technology, but that didn't mean it had protected the economics of selling books. I hadn’t accounted for the massive expansion of public libraries and their lending practices over the previous half century. It was kinda laughable. I’d gone into the book publishing business without ever really considering the implications of there being more library branches in North America than McDonald’s and Starbucks outlets combined, all of them making available for free the same products I’d be attempting to sell.

Public libraries lend something on the order of 1.5 billion books a year, print and digital, in the US and perhaps 225 million to 250 million in Canada. BookScan says 783 million physical books are sold new in the US annually, while BookNet data suggest Canadians buy roughly 63 million books across formats. The measures aren't perfectly comparable—very roughly two to one borrowed over bought in the US, and four to one in Canada. But the scale is unmistakable: library borrowing is enormous relative to commercial book sales.

Public libraries have been ubiquitous since the nineteenth century. However, they’ve evolved over time. The original premise of the public library was to ensure that even people of limited means had access to knowledge and were able to self-educate. This idea was still alive in 1959 when Philip Roth wrote Goodbye, Columbus with its subplot about an underprivileged Black youth who discovers the life-changing paintings of Gauguin in a book at the Newark Public Library.

Beginning in the postwar decades, libraries and the bodies that funded them increasingly measured their success by circulation and usage. They adopted merchandising techniques familiar from bookstores, expanded their branch networks and stocked more copies of recently released and bestselling books—the books readers most wanted.

Over time, they systematically removed the costs and inconveniences associated with borrowing. Membership charges largely disappeared, branch networks expanded, holds and renewals became easier, and eventually many systems eliminated late fees. Ebooks and audiobooks made it possible to borrow without leaving home.

Various library systems began running advertisements during peak book buying periods to remind people that books can be borrowed for free and needn’t be bought. The American Library Association built a calculator to show people how much money they could save by borrowing rather than buying.

The upshot is that public libraries in North America have a much larger and different footprint today than they did when Roth was writing Goodbye, Columbus. In the US, borrowing per capita increased from 4.0 in 1955 to 8.3 by 2010. Canada leapt from 3.1 books borrowed per capita in 1960 to 8.5 by 1994. The nature of the books in the library changed: today you can find many more copies of recent bestsellers than texts that are taught in classrooms. And the patrons changed, too: according to their own published surveys, public libraries cater disproportionately to college-educated people with good incomes who read for their own entertainment.

So an impressive job of expansion and innovation by the libraries. I like to think that if I’d been running one, I’d have followed the same path. I’d have wanted more people using my services and more money from governments to run them.

But I’m not a librarian. I’m a publisher and author. When I see libraries increasing their borrowing statistics and budgets, I can’t help but notice that author incomes and publishing revenues have been declining for decades. According to a 1987 article in the Los Angeles Times, the Authors Guild reported that the median income of its members in 1980 was US$7,900, or about $31,000 in today’s dollars. In 2017, the Authors Guild reported the median income of its members was $6,080, or about $7,700 in today’s dollars. The Writers’ Union of Canada reported that the inflation-adjusted income Canadian authors earned from writing fell 78 per cent between 1998 and 2017. Obviously, libraries didn't cause all of that decline. Amazon, retail consolidation, digital disruption, and plenty else happened along the way. But as publicly subsidized consumption of books expanded enormously, the incomes of the people producing those books moved sharply in the opposite direction. It seemed worth asking whether the two were related.

It’s not a question that gets asked a lot, let alone studied. People who work in the book trade love libraries—we all grew up in them and we all understand the original promise of them. It’s difficult to see them as hostile to our interests so we look the other way. Librarians haven't shown much interest in exploring whether their lending behaviour undermines book sales. Their standard responses to the question are that libraries encourage reading generally, that library borrowers are big readers who also buy books, and that libraries help publishers and authors by first buying the books they lend out.

There’s some truth in there. But just because libraries encourage reading doesn’t mean they don’t discourage book sales. Just because borrowers sometimes buy books doesn’t mean they don’t buy fewer books than they would in the absence of borrowing. And, yes, libraries pay for the books they lend. But they pay once for a copy that can then supply dozens or even hundreds of readings. The purchase compensates the publisher and author for the sale of one copy; it doesn’t compensate them for whatever additional consumer purchases that copy may displace. Library purchases themselves account for maybe 3 percent of publisher sales while, remember, far more books are being borrowed than bought.

Six years ago, I did my best to figure out exactly how much book buying was displaced by book borrowing. Did every book borrowed represent a lost sale? Believe it or not, librarians often use that measure. The American Library Association’s calculator values each book borrowed at the retail price of the book. And library systems in search of government money claim to deliver nearly $6 of economic activity for every $1 in funding they receive, a number arrived at by counting each book borrowed as “economic value” equivalent to the purchase price of the book. But it seemed unlikely to me. Did every four books borrowed represent a lost sale? Every six books? I couldn’t say. I was nevertheless convinced there was some relationship between borrowing and sales. When libraries closed during the pandemic and publishers enjoyed record sales, I felt confirmed in my view.

The fact is that if even ten borrowed books represent a single lost sale, that’s 150 million lost sales in the US annually, or about 20 percent of physical books sold in the US. In Canada, by our rough borrowing estimate, it would mean 22.5 million to 25 million lost sales, possibly as much as 40 percent of all books sold. Those are huge dents in the publishing industry—huge dents in already fragile author incomes and publishing revenues.

And now, finally, we have some real data. The US Authors Guild recently published an examination of how library lending relates to purchasing behaviour, using a national panel of 1,998 American adults who had read at least one book in the previous month and four in the previous year.

It traced the source of all print books and ebooks actually read by the survey respondents—whether they were bought new, bought used, borrowed from a library or a friend, whatever. The survey found that 29 percent of books read were borrowed from libraries while only 19 percent were purchased new and another 6 percent obtained through paid subscriptions (presumably mostly Audible and Kindle Unlimited).

The survey also found that active library users read 16 percent more books than non-library readers, but bought 42 percent fewer new books than non-library readers. That is difficult to reconcile with the standard library defence that library users are great readers and therefore great book buyers. Only the first half turns out to be true. It also undermines the proposition that library use has no material effect on book sales.

The dynamics are similar when it comes to digital lending. Library ebook readers consume 6.3 volumes per month and buy 1.3 new. Non-library ebook readers consume 5.9 books per month and buy 2.2 new. Both groups are consuming enormous quantities of books, yet the non-library group buys 73 percent more new books. Library users here, too, are far less likely to buy.

The survey also confirms that the most active library borrowers are disproportionately well educated and affluent suburbanites. They mostly have university and graduate degrees and full-time jobs with incomes in excess of $75,000 annually. In fact, people earning below $75,000 are substantially less likely to use the library. And the affluent suburbanites aren’t reading for erudition so much as entertainment: active library users are disproportionately likely to read major brand-name authors such as James Patterson and Colleen Hoover.

The survey can't tell us how many sales are displaced by library borrowing. But it renders the proposition that the number is zero unsustainable.

Let’s return to the basics. Governments have made a policy decision that books should be available to everyone through public library systems without charge, regardless of whether the reader is poor, rich, or somewhere in between. That decision is presumed to produce an enormous public benefit—an informed citizenry, the democratization of knowledge, etc. And public libraries aren’t going away because generations of voters have been raised to believe in this enormous public benefit, and people love getting their reading for free and they’ll be noisy if politicians mess with that. Nor will they stand by if librarians try to reinstate user fees or only purchase one copy of each bestseller. So let’s all agree that, as a practical matter, the system must continue as it is.

What remains to be determined is what we do with this strong evidence that the expansive world of free reading has shifted some portion of book consumption from a marketplace in which writers and publishers are paid according to consumption into a public system in which they largely aren’t. Is it fair that they should bear a substantial part of the burden for this enormous public benefit we’ve decided to give everyone? Roads and classrooms are also of enormous public benefit, but we don’t expect road-builders and school-builders or educators to provide their services on the cheap.

The solution is what’s called a public lending right. About thirty-five countries around the world, including most of Europe, Australia, New Zealand, and Canada, already recognize the principle of the public lending right, which compensates authors whose books are held in and/or borrowed from public libraries. The gold standard is Denmark, which devotes more than C$40 million annually to its lending right despite having only six million people. Relative to the number of books borrowed from Danish libraries, that works out to roughly a Canadian dollar per loan—and it all goes to creators.

Each country approaches its public lending right differently. Some are generous; others, including Canada, are cheap. Some compensate authors only; others compensate both authors and the publishers who finance the production of books and are also harmed by widespread lending. The US does not have a public lending right. It needs one, and Canada needs a much better one.

A public lending right shouldn’t start by pretending that each book borrowed represents a lost sale, as the librarians sometimes do. Let’s make it really simple, taking a cue from the Danish model. A buck a book. Every time a book is borrowed from a public library in the US or Canada, the federal government should compensate the author and the publisher, $1 to each. A buck a book.

A new trade book typically retails for $20 to $30. Against that, a dollar for the writer and a dollar for the publisher is modest compensation for the public use of a commercially produced work. The library pays nothing. The library patron pays nothing. The book remains as free and accessible as it is today. Payments follow the actual circulation of individual books—their contributions to the public benefit—which is fundamentally fair.

A public lending right should probably be limited in the US to US authors publishing with US-owned publishers. It should probably be capped at a reasonable level—$20,000 for individual authors and $2 million to 5 million for publishers—so governments aren’t writing cheques to rich authors and publishing companies. The total cost might be somewhere between US$500 million and a billion.

The Canadian program should probably be limited to Canadian authors publishing with Canadian-owned publishers. Ottawa’s explicit policy is to encourage Canadian ownership of its publishing sector. It can’t start writing cheques to the multinational houses that currently dominate the Canadian market. If Ottawa is going to spend public money on the book industry, it should use it to strengthen the Canadian-owned players. The cost would probably be under C$50 million.

Those costs are minimal when you consider what governments spend on public libraries. The US spends more than US$15 billion a year operating public libraries and Canada more than C$2 billion. America’s public libraries employ roughly eight times as many people as the Authors Guild has members, and a typical library employee earns several times more from library work than a full-time author earns from books. And then there’s capital spending. Calgary dropped C$245 million on its spectacular new central library; Ottawa’s new library and archives complex is budgeted at more than C$300 million. Omaha’s new building cost US$158 million. Pasadena, population 135,804, is spending US$186.2 million just to renovate its central library. Against numbers like those, fairly compensating the people who create and publish the books isn’t extravagant.

The details need work and my cost estimates are back-of-the-envelope. But the principle is simple, affordable, and potentially transformative for authors and publishers. It would allow both to continue to contribute to the enormous public benefit that libraries are universally recognized as bringing.

Librarians should see an opportunity here. Instead of arguing with authors and publishers about whether free lending hurts book sales, they could join them in demanding that governments fund both sides of the bargain: free access for readers and fair compensation for the people who produce the books. A buck a book costs libraries nothing. It gives authors and publishers a stake in expanding library use and turns an increasingly adversarial relationship into a common cause.

Everyone wants free access to books. That’s a perfectly reasonable thing for government to provide. It’s also perfectly reasonable for governments to properly compensate the people who provide the books.

A dollar for the writer. A dollar for the publisher. Free for the reader. No cost to the library.
