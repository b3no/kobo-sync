The party can’t fire you

Considering controversy and the consequences of stubbornness in Senate races

Halina Bennet
Jul 10
∙
Paid
 
READ IN APP
 

There is a particular kind of helplessness that settles over a political party when a candidate it needs to win becomes a candidate it cannot defend. Early this week, that was the condition of Maine Democrats — and of national Democrats focused on winning the Senate.

This week Graham Platner, the oysterman and military veteran who has spent nearly a year campaigning as progressive Democrats’ beacon of antiestablishment authenticity, was accused by a former girlfriend of sexually assaulting her in 2021. The following three days brought about as fast and total a political collapse as modern politics can produce. Bernie Sanders, who had all but adopted Platner as a protégé, recommended he step aside, and Ro Khanna withdrew his endorsement, calling the allegation a “red line.” Elizabeth Warren, Ruben Gallego, the Democratic Senatorial Campaign Committee, the state chair, and a Rolodex of other party figures all abandoned Platner less than 72 hours after the scandal broke.

On Wednesday night, Platner suspended his campaign. He said he was not admitting guilt, instead maintaining that the political establishment had used the accusation to strip away the money, the voter data, and the infrastructure he needed to keep running and that continuing without those things would be pointless. Whatever the merits of that framing, the result is that Maine Democrats now have until July 27 to pick someone else.

The party never had the power to simply make Platner go away. Party leaders’ endorsements are a courtesy. Once a candidate wins a primary, the party has no formal institutional authority over its nominee. Leaders can withdraw support — money, surrogates, infrastructure — and apply public pressure. But the party cannot, in Maine or almost anywhere else, strike a name off the ballot. All it can do is make continuing to run so untenable that the candidate withdraws before the legal deadline (5 p.m. this coming Monday, under Maine law).

This dynamic is of course familiar to anyone who recalls the 2024 presidential race, when intense pressure from Democratic leaders pushed Joe Biden to step aside in favor of his vice president. It is also familiar to anyone who followed Missouri’s 2012 Senate race, when Republican nominee Todd Akin refused to withdraw despite overwhelming pressure from his own party after he said in a television interview that women’s bodies could somehow prevent pregnancy in cases of “legitimate rape.” The G.O.P.’s reaction was close to unanimous. Then-presidential candidate Mitt Romney, Mitch McConnell, and five of Missouri’s own Republican senators, past and present, called on him to withdraw during the roughly 48-hour window before he would need a court order to leave the race. National money fled the race almost immediately, but Akin let the clock run out and then launched a bus tour.

National Republicans, who had been counting on flipping then-Senator Claire McCaskill’s seat, gave up on it entirely rather than fund a nominee they couldn’t remove. McCaskill beat Akin by more than 15 points to hold a seat that Missouri Republicans had previously considered to be the Democrats’ most vulnerable, with Akin leading in most early polls. Fortunately for Maine Democrats, their tainted candidate — whose earlier comments about rape had already drawn comparisons to Akin — stepped aside with time for them to replace him. Missouri, by contrast, showed what can happen when a party has no legal way to remove a candidate who refuses to quit.

Five years later in Alabama, Roy Moore was the darker version of the same lesson. A month before the special election to replace Jeff Sessions, at least four women accused the Republican candidate of pursuing teenagers when he was an assistant district attorney in his thirties. The Republican National Committee pulled its support, only to restore it after the race tightened and Donald Trump, then in the first term of his presidency, endorsed him. Moore lost by less than two points anyway to Doug Jones, a Democrat. While Republicans held on to their Senate majority in the 2018 midterms, the loss of an open seat in one of the country’s reddest states was a consequential and panic-inducing reality for the party.

Although Republicans lost in both Alabama and Missouri, what made Moore’s case resolve differently from Akin’s was the thing Platner himself said was the mechanism used against him: the coordinated withdrawal of the infrastructure a modern campaign needs to survive.

Withheld money is a blunter instrument than removal from the ballot, but it has real teeth. A nominee who can’t pay staff, buy ads, or access the party’s voter file is functionally finished even while legally still the nominee. Sanders, Warren, and Khanna pulling their endorsements and the D.S.C.C. and Senate Majority PAC declining to invest in the race as long as Platner remained the nominee made his candidacy untenable. Akin stayed in a hopeless race. Moore’s loss was significantly slimmer than Akin’s after the establishment and the money came back around his way, but his scandal proved insurmountable.

The cases when a party successfully replaces its candidate are rare, and the cases when the new candidate actually wins are even rarer. In fact, it is almost certainly more common for a controversial candidate to maintain the support of their party and eke out a victory — as the current president did in 2016. The New York Times identified nine of more than 500 Senate races over the last 30 years in which a major party replaced its nominee. Only two of those nominees won.

Most recently, in New Jersey in 2002, former Senator Frank Lautenberg stepped in to take over the nomination vacated by incumbent Democrat Robert Torricelli, who abandoned his re-election bid after the Senate Ethics Committee busted him for campaign finance violations. Lautenberg came roaring back to the Senate, winning by 10 points after just over a month as a candidate. Helpfully, the New Jersey Democrats were holding a seat, not trying to flip one, and their replacement was a familiar name who had been in the Senate until just one year earlier. State parties are not usually so lucky.

Now that Maine Democrats can stop holding their breath about whether or not their spoiled candidate will bend to their will, the fun of finding a replacement begins.

Maine’s state committee has already voted to hold a nominating convention rather than a traditional primary because there isn’t time for the latter before the July 27 deadline. The nominee will be picked not by a broad electorate but by a convention of roughly 600 delegates: the hundred or so members of the state party committee plus 500 additional people selected by the county parties. What was mostly rumor and speculation earlier this week is now happening in public. With the downfall of Maine’s golden boy, the field has belatedly become crowded.

Troy Jackson, a former State Senate president and onetime Platner ally, launched his bid within hours of Platner’s withdrawal video — having filed exploratory paperwork the day before. Shenna Bellows, the Maine secretary of state (and the Democratic nominee against Susan Collins in 2014), officially announced her candidacy on Thursday. Nirav Shah, the runner-up in this year’s gubernatorial primary and a former state C.D.C. director, jumped in at the same time as Bellows. Beyond the big three, Jordan Wood, a former congressional staffer who already pivoted out of one House race, stepped in. So did Dan Kleban, the brewery founder who was already a candidate in the primary before Governor Janet Mills got in. State Representative Valli Geiger also said she is considering a bid after Platner allegedly asked her to run.

Unfortunately for “Grey’s Anatomy” fans, Patrick Dempsey has already declined to run. So long, Senator McDreamy.

While the new contenders vie for the nomination, every day spent on an internal convention fight is a day Democrats aren’t spending on Susan Collins. And no matter who emerges from the July convention, that nominee will have been chosen by an inside process rather than by the primary electorate that gave Platner overwhelming support in June. Claire McCaskill, in Collins’s shoes in 2012, publicly defended Todd Akin’s right to stay on the ballot, correctly guessing that a wounded, isolated nominee chosen by voters was an easier opponent than whatever replacement Republican elites might have preferred. Fortunately for Maine Democrats, they no longer have that problem. Instead, they have something that has historically been difficult to pull off: the task of proving by November that a nominee picked by 600 insiders for a race that started as a referendum on the establishment can still look like the will of the state’s voters.
