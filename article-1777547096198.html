Technological progress isn’t always good

The Luddites were wrong, but that’s not a universal law.

Apr 30
∙

If there’s one thing that everyone knows about human history, it’s that new technology always generates fear and anxiety but, after the initial disruption, it always turns out for the best.

It’s easy to muster some amount of human sympathy for the skilled artisans who became the machine-smashers of the Luddite movement. But the fact is that we all ended up better off for the use of productivity-enhancing technology. And that’s life! People lose jobs and face displacement as a result of innovation but, on average, it leaves people better off. And over the long term, everyone benefits from technology and progress.

Of course, when I say everyone knows this, tons of people obviously don’t “know it,” and that’s why techno-optimists restate the basic point over and over again.

And in the vast majority of cases, the optimists are correct.

It’s certainly possible to make an individual better off by blocking any productivity-enhancing technology that happens to be bad for his income. But you cannot make society better off that way. What makes society better off is the rising tide of broad-based technological improvement.

But the optimistic take is only correct because the vast majority of disputes that people have in mind when making this argument are specific to the dynamics of the past 250 years and to the internal disagreements of specific political communities.

The Industrial Revolution was a massive disruption with a lot of stresses and downsides, but it also made people better off, and since then the process of technological change in industrializing and industrialized societies has been basically the same.

If you widen the lens, though, you can find plenty of counterexamples. The invention of boats capable of sailing across the Atlantic, for example, was only bad for the Inca and many other groups. There’s no “in the long run it all worked out” because their entire societies and cultures were destroyed. Of course, there also isn’t some moment in time when Inca Luddites had the opportunity to prevent the emergence of transatlantic sailing vessels, so it’s not like there’s a group of Inca techno-pessimists who were vindicated by history.

That still doesn’t change the fact that it would be correct for an Inca to be extremely pessimistic about the advent of the Age of Sail.

The paradox of agriculture

For the vast majority of humanity’s existence, people lived as hunter-gatherers who extracted resources from the land in a manner that was low-productivity on a per-acre basis. This low productivity meant that population density had to stay low, and also that most¹ human groups had to be mobile. High mobility led to lots of intergroup conflict and warfare. It also suppressed birthrates, because it’s hard to maintain mobility with closely spaced children — an adult can carry one baby, but most children needed to be able to handle long walks by themselves. Needless to say, the medical care was also bad.

Farming produces way more food on a given parcel of land, allowing for higher population density, and it ameliorates some of the impediments to population growth.

Societies that adopt agriculture can also grow enough food to support a specialized caste of full-time warriors along with craftspeople who make things like weapons. This bundle of technologies is useful for pushing hunter-gatherers off of desirable land by killing or enslaving anyone who wants to stay in places that are suitable to growing crops. Nomadic lifestyles remained dominant for a long time in places where crops won’t grow — the desert, the Arctic, and notably the Eurasian Steppe — but agriculture won everywhere else.

In some sense, though, while agriculture “created jobs” far in excess of the hunter-gatherer jobs it destroyed, the associated outcomes were overwhelmingly worse.

People in agricultural societies lose much of their autonomy and are stuck with a more limited range of foodstuffs. They’re exposed to zoonotic disease by mingling constantly with livestock. And you can see the impact of this in the shorter stature and worse overall health of farmers. Part of the issue is that the class of permanent warriors and craftspeople isn’t like the benign professional military of a modern democracy. They are stationary bandits who expropriate all the surplus wealth for themselves and for the even narrower ruling class that commands them.

And for thousands of years after the dawn of agriculture, the cycle repeated: Technology improved. Productivity rose. The population grew.

But Malthusian dynamics made sustained growth in living standards difficult, and excess wealth went into things like palaces and zero-sum military competition.

Patricia Lambert reminds us that these short, often ill farmers with bad teeth are exhibiting superior Darwinian fitness compared to the tall hunter-gatherers with varied diets and fewer cavities. In this case, you can also say that it all worked out well in the end because people today are much better off than hunter-gatherers. But it genuinely took thousands of years for this long-term benefit to show up. Some kind of Mesopotamian tech bro circa 8,500 B.C.E. telling you it’ll be way better to live in villages and just harvest wheat seasonally would have been selling you a bill of goods.

Animal farm

Market transactions that take place in a climate where the rule of law prevails tend to be positive-sum in a way that makes technological transformation positive. But many market transactions do not take place in law-governed societies.

Even when not performed by chattel slaves, pre-modern agricultural labor was often done by workers who were not fully free to come and go as they pleased in a modern sense. Labor mobility and freedom of contract are important because employers are constantly coming up with economically valuable things for people to do that are in some way worse or less pleasant than the things they are currently doing.

In a market for labor, the only way to get someone to do jobs like that is to either pay them extra² or to come up with offsetting ways to make the work more pleasant. If you’re a serf, this dynamic is not in play.

Agriculture, of course, also uses a lot of non-human animals as production inputs.

Wild animals are subject to an enormous amount of suffering, and I think it’s likely that the historical rise of farming had a more benign impact on livestock than on humans. It was bad, of course, for the wild animals who lost their habitats. But the farm animals who replaced them were not only more numerous and more fecund than wild animals, but they plausibly lived in better conditions.

However, recent history has seen explosive growth in the number of animals (mostly chickens) raised for meat. This is overwhelmingly due to technical innovations in livestock-rearing — it’s not like planet Earth is suddenly covered with cute chickens roaming in open pastures. That wouldn’t be workable.

The upshot of these innovations is very bad for the birds. Chickens are kept in such crowded conditions that cannibalism and feather-pecking are serious issues, so they cut off the animals’ beaks without anesthesia to reduce their ability to injure each other. Comparable issues plague other kinds of livestock.

Innovation in this sphere has clearly had some large benefits for humans, who don’t have to hunt for meat (which is difficult) or go without (like our growth-stunted farming ancestors). But it’s been extremely bad for animals.

I am a resolute techno-optimist and think this problem will be resolved one day. Either, as Bruce Friedrich hopes, we will develop new ways to manufacture meat that doesn’t involve raising animals, or else a future of radical energy abundance will make hyper-efficient vertical farms economical, leaving plenty of space for pasture.

But we’re about a century into the era of steadily increasing innovation in the field of animal cruelty, and there’s no reason to believe that’s going to turn around any time soon.

Beyond economics

These are, by design, all marginal situations relative to mainstream political debates. Because that’s my point: Weird stuff happens at the margin.

I started with the Inca, who are of course just one of dozens and dozens of peoples of the western hemisphere who were more or less wiped out by European disease and invaders. The same thing happened in Australia. It doesn’t play into the same politicized framework so we don’t hear about it as much, but something similar happened to the nomadic peoples of Siberia.

In every case, some of this is due to deliberate malice and cruelty.

But a lot of the devastation was wreaked by infectious diseases that hit isolated populations who had no acquired immunity. The Columbian Exchange was, of course, a two-way street. But while the western hemisphere picked up measles, influenza, typhus, bubonic plague, diphtheria, scarlet fever, whooping cough, chickenpox, and mumps, along with smallpox, really only syphilis moved in the opposite direction.

The greater disease density in the Old World is thought to be downstream of the greater concentration of domesticated farm animals, but we’re still talking about a pretty contingent situation. Nothing about the history of technology would have needed to be dramatically different for the transatlantic crossing to have brought some terrifying new illnesses back to Europe where they would have killed millions. To an extent, the Europeans just got lucky.

It seems plausible, by the same token, that it was just the heroic action of one man that prevented a nuclear war in 1983. If he’d acted otherwise, I think the survivors would have felt that techno-pessimism was pretty well-vindicated by events.

In the real world, a general disposition toward techno-optimism encouraged a widespread belief that the internet would democratize access to information and that this would undermine authoritarian governments. And in free societies, the internet really has dramatically reduced the informal power of traditional gatekeepers.

This has itself proven to be a mixed bag for society. But the larger error is that in authoritarian societies like China, the internet has proven compatible with censorship. Better information technology has simply facilitated surveillance and control on a level impossible for 20th century dictators, while Hannah Arendt and others observed that the kinds of regimes assembled by Adolf Hitler and Joseph Stalin would not have been possible at earlier stages of development.

I am a big fan of economists and conventional economics, and I generally think policymakers should be less innovative and more orthodox in their approach to economic policy questions.

But what I really want to emphasize in all of this is that while optimism about the impact of technology is all well and good within the bounds of what economics studies, the functioning of market economies is actually a pretty small subset of the experience of planet Earth. It is completely consistent with human history for technological progress to have catastrophic downsides or for us to avoid catastrophe through narrow luck.
