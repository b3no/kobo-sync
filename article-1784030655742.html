"The Male Gaze" Was Never About Wanting a Boyfriend

Trying to attract a partner is a normal part of any sexuality, but "the male gaze" has become a subject of anxiety and debate.

I’ve noticed something a bit weird about participating in your own heterosexuality as a woman: openly admitting you want a partner, and doing things to your appearance with the intention of attracting a partner (while being honest about your motivations) is not received very well. By anyone.

And yes, I know, straight people are not oppressed, discriminated against, or subject to “anti-straight” violence, and no, the existence of the gay purple Tellytubby doesn’t count (that reference, I fear, falls into the “too late” category). Yes, there will be a handful of radical gender separatist feminists who ask why straight women cannot simply “stop dating men,” as one could boycott a problematic retailer, but this is obviously a niche worldview that does not equate to systemic discrimination. Even within the most lefty enclaves of Portland and Brooklyn, just being a heterosexual woman isn’t a shameful thing.

Nobody is criticizing women for being straight, but people are disproportionately criticizing and analyzing straight women for the particular ways in which they “do” their straightness, particularly the degree of effort they expend on it—not just among wacky far-left college students, but among the normies and conservatives as well, just with different languages and words. The bipartisan message we receive: straight women can be attractive—in fact, it’s great if they are—but they shouldn’t have to try, and it should never be explicitly for men. (But men? Yeah, go ahead and talk about how you work out for the ladies, it’s fine.)

Look no further than the widely-inaccurately-used term “male gaze,” which has come to be short hand for “striving to look attractive to straight men” (and yes, I am also guilty of using it incorrectly despite knowing better! Listening and learning.) The term was originally an academic concept to describe media, but has become an undue burden on straight women to avoid one of the most fundamental and normal parts of any sexual orientation: trying to attract desired partners.

Apparently, women are not the only ones warned about “pandering to the male gaze” (aka, openly trying to look more attractive). With the new definition of “looking good in a way men like,” even looksmaxxing influencer Clavicular—according to The New York Times—is pandering to the male gaze. And to its credit, Cosmo (which, yes, still exists!) has picked up on how the “male gaze” as a term is now being used, ironically, to police women’s behavior more than anyone else’s.

For anyone of any gender and sexuality, having a sexual orientation usually involves a few things: finding a particular gender (or genders) attractive, wanting to have sex and/or relationships with the aforementioned people, and taking action to be attractive to people from your desired gender(s). Assuming you aren’t homophobic, you naturally assume that every sexual orientation does these things, and it’s fine, because what else would you have them do? On the face of it, you may also say it’s fine for straight women to do these things. But, in practice, there is a uniquely straight-woman taboo about visibly expending too much effort on attracting a partner, especially with her looks. If there is effort expended, she must deny it had anything to do with men—something that is not demanded of any other gender or sexuality.

Just to make sure I wasn’t overstating the matter, I asked my LBGTQ followers if it was taboo or embarrassing to admit that you were deliberately taking actions to become more physically attractive to your desired partners, such as dressing a certain way or losing weight. The answer, it seems, is no, with the exception of lesbians and bi women being steered away from improving their attractiveness in ways that read heterosexual. So basically, trying to look more attractive to attract a partner is fine, as long as that theoretical partner is not a straight man.

Let’s say a straight man wants to get a girlfriend. Many such cases. He might work out—perhaps even dedicate lots of time and energy to his physique. He might get a haircut, and start pushing his sleeves up a bit or wear a nicely-fitted suit jacket, and will not be afraid to admit this is for women. When he eventually gets married, his wife might tell him she really prefers him with a certain style of facial hair or lackthereof. Provided he doesn’t have strong opinions either way, he might adhere to this preference and openly joke that he keeps his facial hair this way because his wife likes it. (How many men’s razor commercials directly state that men shave their faces so a pleased women will materialize behind them and start stroking their chins?) And most people will have absolutely no problem with a man admitting to this. I have never heard a straight man insist, repeatedly, that he only shaves his back “for himself” and nobody would ever demand that of him. And nobody—unless they were trying to be funny—would accuse him of “pandering to the female gaze,” even if he explicitly admitted that he tweaked his appearance to attract women.

In part, this is because there is no “female gaze” the way there is a male gaze. The male gaze is more accurately described as a framework for media or depictions, where the assumption is that the media is being produced for the eyes of heterosexual men, with women as sex objects. A great deal of our culture surrounds the male gaze in ways you might not even notice. Actual examples of the male gaze might include attractive women being used as objects to sell items that have nothing to do with women or sex—beer, cars, soap, what have you. The default audience is heterosexual men looking to at women, who are being utilized as sex objects. Another frustrating—but less obvious—example of how the male gaze shows up in our society are the men complaining about the existence of Lena Dunham, Amy Schumer, Lizzo or any other woman who is not thin or conventionally attractive—not because she isn’t talented, but because she is not attractive enough to justify existing. If a woman cannot be a sex object for men, what else could she possibly be doing?

As I said before, I’m guilty of using the term “male gaze” incorrectly. I shouldn’t have made that mistake because I took a bunch of courses on this in college like any good libcuck, but the male gaze does not literally mean “a straight man is looking at something and his peanus likes it.” For that matter, there are some sandwiches and couches out there who “pander to the male gaze.”

But anyway, given that the male gaze is not about individual men, or dating at all, an individual woman cannot “pander to the male gaze” unless she is producing media. Wanting to attract men is not pandering to the male gaze, nor does it necessitate subjugating yourself unless you are going to self-harming lengths to do it, anymore than it’s embarrassing or debasing for a straight man to do things to attract women, for a gay men to do things to attract other gay men, for lesbians to do things to attract other lesbians, and so on and so forth.

I recall engaging in a Reddit thread about women’s fashion during the dark times of the 2010s, and I joked that if my husband had his way, I’d wear high-waisted jeans and crop tops every day. Immediately, I was downvoted, seemingly by every woman on the thread, and a woman responded to me, warning me that my boyfriend was “using the male gaze,” as if he was using heroin. By her account, what gaze was he supposed to be using? Eventually the women of the thread unanimously agreed that men should not only abstain from voicing opinions on their female partners’ clothes (even when asked—and God, why would you ask?) but that a man was only a non-misogynist if he genuinely had zero opinions on them at all. I asked them if it was okay for me to have opinions about my husband’s clothes and was accused of trolling and/or being emotionally abused. One of them asked me if he “allowed” me to wear what I wanted, which was meant as a taunt but was probably more relevant to my sexuality than she thought.

At this point, you might say, “That was Reddit! Reddit is full of losers!” and while that may be true, I regret to inform you that even in real life, women who openly admit they are doing things to attract or encounter men are not seen positively…by anyone. Don’t get me wrong—being an attractive woman is not perceived badly, nor is being a well-groomed or stylish woman, but admitting that you are doing these things to be attractive to men, or going to any effort to achieve this, is viewed badly, and not just by Reddit feminist harpies. You can be attractive, but only in a seemingly-effortless “Oh, this old thing?” kind of way. Desiring men is not the default, being passively desired is, especially if you can claim you are doing your best to avoid male attention entirely. Deliberately doing things to provoke male desire is seen unfavorably even among non-libs, for a variety of stupid reasons which are, dare I say, misogynistic.

Perhaps, among less lefty audiences, these “male-gaze-pandering” women are not given a Vassar-educated lecture about the patriarchy, but they are at best seen as desperate losers or sluts, because straight women shouldn’t have to try. Everyone else can try, but not straight women. Take, for example, the entirely right-wing controversy over Evie Magazine’s sun dress. The dress itself was normal-looking, and left-wing feminists weren’t paying much attention to the drama anyway. It was conservative women who took issue with the dress (explicitly marketed to fulfill men’s “sun dress fantasy”) for being desperate, whorish, and overly focused on men. A brawl erupted between the conservative Evie women who weren’t afraid to admit that they were wearing clothes to attract men, and the conservative women who believed such behavior was bad, not because it was “pandering to the male gaze” in those words, but because it was indecent and hussy-coded. Other conservative women used this controversy as a jumping-off point to brag about—you guessed it—not having to try, flaunting the fact that men want them even when they’re wearing mud-covered dungarees. Only a woman who is not only a slut, but ugly, would do things to deliberately provoke that attention!

And let’s not forget the drama over women’s gym attire that seemed to dominate the 2010s. Somewhere along the line, certain men started claiming that if women wore skimpy clothes at the gym, they were asking for sexual attention from all men, everywhere, all the time, and they were hypocrites and teases if they complained about these advances. But instead of saying, “Wanting to look attractive isn’t the same thing as entering into a free use contract with the entire world,” women insisted that no woman, anywhere, would ever wear butt-scrunch booty shorts for—gag—a man. No, in fact, women are only dressing this way at the gym because it’s comfortable, and men should ALWAYS leave them the fuck alone!

Look, I agree men should leave women alone if they make it clear they don’t want to interact, and no matter what a woman is wearing at the gym, men shouldn’t behave inappropriately. But must we deny, obtusely, that these clothes are ever worn to look attractive? If anything, that argument makes it all worse! By saying “We don’t consent to harassment, because these clothes aren’t for icky men,” you make the argument that there is no difference between wanting harassment and dressing to look sexy, and that wanting to attract a man implies not only desire for, but consent for, all men. The argument also fell flat because everyone knew that these clothes were not necessary to get a good workout. True, working out in an Amish-looking dress would be impractical, but there is nothing about ruching along a butt crack, or booty shorts with a 2-inch inseam, that makes a workout more comfortable. I concede plenty of women wear sexy clothes to the gym for reasons unrelated to men, such as wanting to admire their own bodies or feel good about themselves, but given how many singles’ events take place at fitness classes, it’s obvious that attracting a man—not every man ever—is sometimes the goal.

But I understand why many feminists cannot separate the “wanting to attract men” part of female heterosexuality from the unfortunate reality between men and women. Straight women are the only people who are exclusively attracted to a gender that has been uniquely dangerous for them. Yes, strife can happen between gay men and between lesbians, but there is no inherent or historical power imbalance based on their genders alone. If your view is that men are uniformly (or at least mostly) dangerous for women, then it’s understandable that any kind of “pandering” to men—even done by an individual woman with the perfectly reasonable goal of getting a boyfriend—could seem like radical political statements of patriarchy-endorsement, or in a best case scenario, being an insecure loser who’s obsessed with dick at the expense of her dignity and safety.

The author of The Last Straight Woman and the Internet’s resident expert of openly desiring men, Phoebe Maltz Bovy and I have repeatedly gotten this particular piece of feedback when we write about the reality of most straight women wanting sex and companionship with men—not that we’re dick-obsessed losers (although, maybe? haha) but that we are making a political statement by saying that straight women are attracted to men, and that doing things to attract men is normal, not something that must be obfuscated or denied. All too often, people misunderstand this statement as a jubilant endorsement of all men, everywhere, and a defensive declaration that we need some kind of straight pride for women. They will say, “In light of domestic violence, sexual assault, and other issues facing women at the hands of men, how can you possibly tell women that the state of heterosexuality is so amazing and that men are so great?”

But this very accusation completely misses the point. Women’s heterosexuality (and related actions taken to find partners) cannot ever be viewed in a neutral light the way other sexualities can, for a reason that isn’t even women’s fault! Phoebe and I have never suggested that being straight is really fun all the time, or that all straight women should want boatloads of male attention and express gratitude for every man who has ever looked in their direction. Only that being straight is…a thing. And that thing involves many of the same desires and motivations that apply to all other sexualities.

Phoebe and I don’t believe female heterosexuality exists because men are fantastic anymore than gay men exist because men are fantastic. We do not believe women should “pander to the male gaze” because men deserve it for being so amazing. Women are free to opt out of trying to attract men—and plenty do, happily! But the desire to attract men and the effort expended on such a thing is pretty normal (just ask gay men) and not worth a bunch of debate, anxiety, and mental gymnastics.

The one feminist concept which (perhaps unintentionally) agrees with me is the idea that “nothing happens in a vacuum.” Basically, if a woman tries to explain away a “male-gaze-pandering” choice (say, wearing makeup or heels) with the explanation that she “just likes it,” or does it “for herself,” some feminists will be quick to remind her that part of why she likes it is because she lives in a patriarchal society that has enforced the prioritization of female beauty, and tsk tsk for not “unpacking” such a thing. Then another brand of feminist will push back against this statement and insist that akshually, doing things that make themselves sexier to men in a very typical and mainstream way are radical and empowering decisions, if not downright “subversive” in a way men will never notice. (Man gets boner, subversion achieved, I suppose.) The most important part of their position is that these things are NOT FOR MEN even though men coincidentally happen to like them. (Note to straight women: “ironic hyperfemme queer camp” doesn’t really come off as ironic on you.)

I am more inclined to agree with the first group (no, wearing a push-up bra is not “subversion” unless you are Harry Styles, and even then…) but I resent how much handwringing is involved in all of this in the first place. Why is this discussion even happening? Why is “trying to attract men” an accusation instead of a fact of life for straight women, the way it is for gay men? A great deal of onus is being put on women to avoid—or at least feel weird about— doing one of the main things that comprises any sexuality: trying to attract a partner. I concede that more pressure is placed on straight women than any other gender to look a certain way, and pushing back against these expectations is reasonable, but demanding that women pretend they don’t do these things for men seems counterintuitive.

It feels ridiculous for me to say, “being a straight woman who likes men isn’t political,” because even saying that implies some kind of politics—the type of politics that encourages people to say inane things like, “Can we please normalize loving your children??” but at the same time, straight women’s desire is just treated so differently from the desires of every other gender and sexual orientation for no good reason. Straight women are, of course, allowed to hold that sexual orientation but warned against actively participating in it, or having any real desire for it. One must only be a straight woman reluctantly, no matter your politics. Liking men too much and too openly is either “male gaze pandering” or, if you ask some conservatives, whore behavior.

There is a degree where attempting to attract men goes too far—where you not only want a boyfriend, but live and die on whether or not men think you’re attractive. I don’t know where this line is, and I’m not going to issue a decree about the point at which primping becomes body dysmorphic looksmaxxing. But I will definitively say this: seeking out desired partners and trying to attract desired partners is normal, no matter your gender or sexuality.

And no, Clavicular is not pandering to the male gaze.
