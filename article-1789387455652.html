Nobody Owns Disability

and if adult academics and activists presenting formal papers at a conference are not fair targets of good-faith criticism, who possibly could be?

Freddie deBoer
Sep 14
 
READ IN APP
 

In fairness, the panelists that Kulwin and Johnson are defending here were just ordinary folks, speaking offhand and in private. Oh wait, no! The literal opposite! They were academics and activists reading prepared remarks for a publicly-accessible conference that they knew was going to be livestreamed. My bad. I’d hate to see these socialist thinkers who have deliberately attempted to introduce their ideas into the public conversation be shielded from that conversation by condescending able-bodied white men who think that they’re too widdle and weak to be criticized. I mean, why else does someone go to a conference and present contentious arguments on issues of sensitivity and consequence, other than to inspire someone else to react negatively? What do Kulwin and Johnson think we do all this shit for?

One more time: the dominant left political strategy, here in 2026, is avoidance. Here you have a certain vintage of leftist practicing a particularly bold version of it. Kulwin and Johnson can’t or don’t want to defend the argument that exercise is fascist, but they feel intense tribalist impulses to attack critics of that argument anyway. So you get these weird, explicitly empty quasi-defenses here, which operate on the most nakedly associative logic imaginable. Both Kulwin and Johnson suggest, on Twitter, that these people are somehow exempt from criticism. But they were at a conference called Socialism 2026, which is (unfortunately) a very prominent event in the world of the American socialist left, and we all have to answer for ideas associated with our movements even if we would prefer not to. Am I to believe that, if the Heritage Foundation ran a conference called Conservatism 2026 and there was a panel titled “Child Sexual Abuse is Cool!,” Kulwin and Johnson would not try to hang that around the necks of the entire conservative movement? Of course they would, and everyone would understand it to be fair game. It ain’t beanbag. You stand up there and share your perspective and the world shares back, and nobody knows that better than me.

With this book and its topics, I am in several binds. The first is that, as is often the case, I’m trying to insist that a position broadly associated with the left is not leftist at all. The cultural and social quasi-political beliefs we broadly refer to as identity politics are not in fact socialist ideas, and in saying that I’m part of an actually quite old and unremarkable tendency within the left. So many of the beliefs that are now associated with the left-of-liberal left are bizarre, tortuous bits of “theory” imported from academia, cultural studies gobbledygook totally disconnected from the lived reality of actual oppression and liberation. With this book, I’ve taken my considerable history in left activism and my considerable education in disability theory and history and my considerable personal experience with these topics and articulated an argument about why contemporary disability discourse is broken. That argument, fundamentally, is a left-wing argument: I believe that what I’m saying is ultimately liberatory, that my position and not the position of the Ivy League activists I’m opposing is the one that’s most consonant with freedom and justice. Of course some people will disagree, that’s fine. What I hate is the attitude evinced in these tweets - that left and right are decided by pure tribalism, that it really is all rooting for laundry. I’m not making conservative critiques of left disability politics. I’m making a left critique of bad disability politics.

The other bind regards standpoint theory: I reject it, but the fact that I have a disability certainly comes in handy in these debates, in a way that cuts directly against that rejection. Standpoint theory is the notion, popularized in academia, that only people who are part of a given “marginalized group” should have the right to speak on issues related to that marginalized group. Some people will insist that there are more nuanced versions than that, but as a lived discursive reality, that’s what standpoint theory actually is - “you’re not allowed to talk about this” is the ubiquitous and unmistakable argument in many disability discursive spaces. Standpoint theory can be found all over the Yelling Social Justice world, but nowhere is it more rigorously enforced than with disability; the autism slogan “nothing about us without us” has been invoked against me again and again, as a way to insist that I don’t have a right to an opinion on the topic.

My immediate response is to say fuck you, I don’t have to live under the burden of regulations I don’t agree with, and I decline to follow your rules in this regard. Maybe more importantly, “nothing about us without us” should immediately prompt the question, who is us? The suggestion is that “us” encompasses everyone with autism, but of course there is literally nothing that everyone with autism shares, especially not opinions. As Jon from Christianity on the Spectrum has said, “In reality there is no autistic culture, there is no autistic community, no one group of people speaks for us, and there is no consensus about what we think, believe, or want. We are fractured, divided, and actively want mutually exclusive things.” This is the most essential critique of the very concept of standpoint theory: what is its purpose, once we acknowledge that “identity” groups have no shared standpoint at all? What do we do when members internal to these communities disagree? Pragmatically speaking, the answer is “the activist class gets to make the law because they are the loudest, the most shameless, and the most aggressive.” I know autistic people who were deeply offended when “neurodivergent” Harvard student activists had a tantrum and got a panel on treating autism shut down. But my criticism of those activists is routinely regarded as being “ableist,” because in practice the people who get to define the standpoint are just the loudest voices.

The bind is that I reject all of this shit - it’s contrary to my most basic values - but I do benefit from an inchoate version of standpoint theory, where I am extended more right to talk about these things because I Have A Disability. Certainly, the extent to which All In Your Head gets reviewed in the months ahead will be influenced by this dynamic; book sections of legacy media tend to be the most conventionally social justice-y these days, after all, and routinely decline to review any books that do not come from the most narrow radlib point of view. Well, I wouldn’t choose for others to bless me with a right to an opinion because of my disorder, but if this is what it takes for the arguments to get out there, so be it. What I would like to call attention to is the broader current running all of this, one which haunts the social justice set and thus the American left: who gets to speak? Who is blessed with the right to engage? In a world where disability is (well, was) unchosen, who exactly gets allowed into the fraternity of people who are allowed an opinion? Because I can tell you for a fact that the people in the activist community tend to have very narrow and self-interested answers to the question.

I’ve said before, and it’s true, that I started writing about the topics that turned into this new book because I did a podcast with three mothers of adult children with schizophrenia. Even in the few hours I interacted with them, I could feel their sheer fatigue, the absolute exhaustion that comes from keeping a child with a psychotic disorder alive and out of long-term confinement in a psychiatric institution. And after we recorded, one of them said that they were thankful that I was saying the things I was saying, because as mere parents they were not extended the right to speak, according to the activist class. If you think this is not a big deal, you’re wrong - I have engaged with activists of all kinds in my life, as an activist working with them myself and as a writer in an antagonistic position towards them, and these conflicts can get very nasty. And yes, some of them are shameless enough to insist that family members of people with disabilities shouldn’t get a voice. That’s not a strawman. It’s a real, direct conflict. And who gets to speak has stakes! It’s well known, for example, that the Autism Self-Advocacy Network - whose adherence to the standpoint theory model is found in their name - had a major influence on the 2013 revisions to the DSM, which has serious consequences for research, treatment, and advocacy.

So the question for Adam Johnson is this: expanding the circle of solidarity to include whom? He seems to think that there is only one answer to this question, that the options are bigger or smaller. But me, personally? I would much rather expand that circle to include a bunch of exhausted, broke, marginalized parents of severely disabled children than to make the circle even cushier for the activists who shut down debates on what’s best for disabled people they don’t like. I would rather expand the circle to include people the activist class doesn’t want to hear from. Johnson’s free to favor the views of whoever he chooses, but he’s not free to say that I’m not trying to expand the circle. I’m fighting to expand it to people who have been marginalized and pushed out of the conversation by those who call exercise fascist and who throw around terms like “dysgenic.” Would Beatrice Adler-Bolton, the woman whose panel talk has attracted a lot of scorn, include people I want included in the circle of solidarity? Not sure. But I’ll listen to her, and I’ll subject her arguments to adult review, and for that very reason I won’t do her the disrespect of hiding how I feel: “Being healthy is eugenics and fascist” is an incoherent, ridiculous opinion and not one that could ever win.

I’m not going to prosecute the case against that claim here, nor make the broader argument against disability theorists and activists, because I wrote all that down in my book coming out on October 27th. It has already made its way into the hands of some people in the broad “disability rights” community; would you believe they’re not big fans? Perhaps the people at Socialism 2026 will read and react to it, although that community has a strong internal social culture mandating against reading arguments said community is predicted to disagree with. It’ll be a shame if they don’t, but either way, I have made my case, have been making it for years. Because that’s how this works - you go on panels to make arguments that, you hope, other people will argue against. When people come back at me and take their swings against the book, no one is going to try to shield me from criticism. Nor would I want them to. This is the deal, man, this is how this all goes, this is how it works. Solidarity grows out of the soil of argument.

As I so often do, I will ask you to consider what respect actually is, what it actually means. If you’ll forgive the hypocrisy inherent to this, let me say: I’m here, as someone who has read thousands of pages of disability theory, who has gotten his hands dirty in this fight for many years, and who lives with the consequences of these debates in a very material way. And I’m saying that Adler-Bolton is wrong. Kulwin and Johnson are saying that she’s not even wrong; they’re suggesting that her position and her status do not rise to the level of deserving actual adult review. That, to me, is the opposite of adult respect, and respect is the necessary precondition of all true solidarity.
