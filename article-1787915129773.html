The Great Awokening started in 2014, not 2020

Plus price controls, a lot of Maine stuff, and what a 2028 anti-MAGA insurgency could look like

As someone who is interested in monoculture events in general and the movie business in particular, I suppose I should have something to say about “Spider-Man: Brand New Day,” which is a huge hit and stands a decent chance of becoming the top-grossing film of all time in nominal terms.

I saw it and I thought it was … okay. The third-best of the Tom Holland Spider-Man movies? The third-best film Zendaya has appeared in so far in 2026? Which is just to say I sort of don’t get it. I don’t know anyone who is super enthusiastic about this movie but also nobody is outraged by it. For a big hit movie to also be sort of mid and unremarkable is I guess the kind of thing I used to expect from America’s old movie culture, when a much larger fraction of the population was in the habit of “going to the movies” pretty regularly. Because “Spider-Man” strikes me as a fantastic “going to the movies” kind of movie. Everyone basically knows who Spider-Man is and the movie will make perfect sense even if you are not up to speed on the latest M.C.U. lore. It has that kind of well-rounded quality where there’s a romantic plot and plenty of action but no hyper-violence and the Punisher is funny rather than alarming so anyone could enjoy it.

But isn’t this sort of “Hey, let’s go to the movies!” vibe exactly what’s gone away in post-pandemic America, with its endless streaming and short-form options? Not that I’m complaining, mind you. I love going to the movies. But that’s why I’m still the guy showing up for stuff like “Crime 101” and “Tuner” — random cool genre movies that would have done at least okay in the 1990s but kind of drop without a trace these days. Not that I’m complaining; I’m glad people are enjoying “Spider-Man.” But I think it’s a little weird.

Siddhartha Roychowdhury: I’m seeing an attempt by the media/Democratic politicians to redefine the Woke 1 era by compressing it from Covid/Floyd in 2020 to 2024. I’m pretty sure that DEI in hiring, dumbing down of curriculum, trans stuff, etc. goes back to around 2014/2015 at least. Are you and others noticing this as well? Should that concern those who don’t want a Woke 2 when Democrats are back in power?

The whole “Woke 1” discourse is a good joke, but it obviously lacks a certain kind of precision.

I wrote a big feature for Vox about what I called the Great Awokening back in March of 2019, which obviously was before Covid and before George Floyd. So while the social phenomenon I was talking about clearly reached its apogee 12 or 24 months after I published the piece, the underlying shifts were visible before that. There are a bunch of different ways to characterize what these shifts were, but I think if you want a single clear data point to focus on, I would look at the share of the non-Black population that says “yes” to the question “On the average Black people have worse jobs, income, and housing than white people. Do you think these differences are mainly due to discrimination?”

While 2014 feels like a long time ago now, when I wrote my Great Awokening piece in 2019, it was only five years in the past. And you can see that as recently as 2014, a majority of non-Black Democrats said “no” to this question. The numbers then surged among non-Black Democrats and non-Black independents in the 2016, 2018, and 2022 surveys while Republicans had small increases in 2016 and 2018.

Lots of stuff happened as a downstream consequence of this, and some of that stuff was good and other stuff was bad. You can sort of try to cabin off whatever you think the worst excesses were and call that Woke 1 and then say that whatever you thought was good doesn’t count as woke or is what’s going to be brought back during Woke 2.

If you want to understand how I was thinking about it in 2019, my views were heavily influenced by my understanding of William Julius Wilson’s work. In this view, the big hope for progress in America was that we could secure political support for a race-neutral program of uplift for what he called The Truly Disadvantaged. Late in life, Martin Luther King Jr. had some socialist economic policy ideas that I don’t think worked, empirically, but shared this moral vision of a program that “would benefit all the poor, including the two-thirds of them who are white.” King specifically described his ideas as “not a ‘civil rights’ program, in the sense that the term is currently used.”

The blockage here, though, was that I recall very clearly back in Barack Obama’s first term that Rush Limbaugh would specifically describe the Affordable Care Act as “a civil rights bill” and “reparations” because delivering health care to low-income families does disproportionately benefit African Americans. That’s a classic mode of American politics, right-wingers using divisive racial rhetoric to undermine white support for the social safety net. My optimistic read of the Great Awokening was that declining anti-Black sentiment in the white population was going to undermine this style of right-wing politics: that rather than viewing anything that helped African Americans as inherently suspect, white Americans were going to be increasingly open to seeing their fellow citizens as fellow citizens regardless of skin color and wanting to help people in reasonable ways.

Now that we are in the post-woke era, I also think it is important to recall that overt advocacy for racial discrimination as a law enforcement strategy used to be pretty mainstream in American life. People would say that, well, if it’s statistically accurate that Black men are committing crimes at a higher rate than white ones, it is correct and appropriate for law enforcement to target people on that basis. This is, I think, a very demeaning, dehumanizing, and socially corrosive way to manage public spaces. And a clear implication of the Great Awokening was that we should move away from it.

To an extent, I think those good things have in fact happened and that’s good. But during “Woke 1” we also got a ton of turbocharged, totally thoughtless disparate-impact analysis. The most infamous example was the argument that if African Americans are disproportionately represented among the population of people who get arrested, that shows that the practice of arresting people is racist and so we shouldn’t have police. This style of thinking had, obviously, disastrous consequences for people who lived in crime-prone neighborhoods and delivered a disproportionate negative impact on precisely the Black communities it was intended to help. But the bad inference about crime and law enforcement was not the only example of this kind of sloppy thinking. And, unfortunately, I think a structural increase in sloppy thinking due to viral content distribution is very much still with us even though this specific mode of thought has become unfashionable.

Incidentally, for the young people out there who don’t even remember what pre-Awokening Democrats sounded like, here’s Obama at a Black church in 2008 talking about how fathers are “missing from too many lives and too many homes,” scolding parents about how they need to not “just sit in the house watching SportsCenter” and “don’t get carried away with that eighth-grade graduation; you’re supposed to graduate from eighth grade.” As Julie Bosman wrote at the time, this kind of thing made frequent appearances in Obama’s rhetoric:

The address was not Obama’s first foray into the issue. On the campaign trail, Obama has frequently returned to the topic of parenting and personal responsibility, particularly for low-income black families. Speaking in Texas in February, Obama told a mostly black audience to take responsibility for the education and nutrition of their children, and he lectured them for feeding children “cold Popeyes,” a reference to a chain of fried-chicken restaurants, for breakfast.

“I know how hard it is to get kids to eat properly,” Obama said at the time. “But I also know that if folks are letting our children drink eight sodas a day, which some parents do, or, you know, eat a bag of potato chips for lunch — buy a little desk or put that child at the kitchen table. ‘Watch them do their homework.’”

This was a frequent bone of contention between Obama and more progressive African American intellectuals. Ta-Nehisi Coates really didn’t like it and Nia-Malika Henderson wrote an insightful piece about Obama shifting away from it in his second term as the Awokening was gathering steam. The change, obviously, isn’t that now Democrats think cold Popeyes and potato chips are healthy. But it used to be that a majority of non-Black Americans favored willpower-based explanations for disparate racial outcomes and support for that collapsed, especially among Democrats, starting after 2012.

I think this whole phenomenon is somewhat under-discussed. A common thing that people say about me is that I am too inattentive to the possibility of public opinion shifting on issues. My standard reply is that when you look at big shifts in public opinion, they are rarely attributable to high-profile politicians leading the charge. Obama and the Great Awokening seems like a perfect example to me. He clearly pivoted away from this kind of rhetoric after it stopped being popular (and through initiatives like the My Brother’s Keeper program he still keeps it alive on some level) rather than leading the charge. So who did lead the charge? I have some guesses, but I don’t think it’s widely understood.

Incidentally, in both of those charts above I only presented non-Black opinion — that’s because I think the Great Awokening is almost by definition about shifts outside the African American community. You can, though, see that Black opinion has not really moved all that much — the key thing is that white Democrats’ views have shifted considerably.

The most recent data points on this are all from 2024, and I will be interested to see what the future holds in terms of further shifts.

theeleaticstranger: Regarding the bad policy temptation of price controls—are there any sectors where more regulation/scrutiny of prices would have minimal expected supply/innovation impacts? Suppose a future President Warren imposed price controls on streaming services, or required that all NFL games be offered on a single, affordable monthly subscription with no cable subscription or bundle required? Is there any bad thing that would happen? If so is it safe to indulge populism in a few select areas, or is it always going to be a slippery slope?

I would think about this more broadly than price controls.

There are a lot of areas where there is an impulse to impose various consumer-friendly regulations, but then there is a counter-impulse that says these kinds of regulations will undermine supply and ultimately be counterproductive. So, for example, nobody likes to get fucked over by an airline delay. But if you impose steeper financial penalties on airlines for delays, you’re going to raise the cost of providing routine air service, and there will be fewer routes flown. That doesn’t mean you never want a pro-consumer regulation, but you need to tread carefully.

The considerations look different when the product category as a whole is not really one where abundance is desirable.

Gambling websites strike me as a really clear example of this. Most of them, as I understand it, will just kick a person off if he wins too much. The companies are trying to make money, not actually serve as optimal information aggregators. This is unfair, and I think there’s an obvious populist appeal to saying you shouldn’t be allowed to do that. The “downside” is that preventing gambling sites from screwing over their customers would make the whole sector less lucrative and probably cause it to contract. But that’s fine. You could say it’s win-win. And I think it might be reasonable to apply this kind of logic across the digital entertainments sector. Trying to do stuff like directly tax the consumption of compulsive video services might be hard because people like them too much. But implementing user-friendly rules that actually make them less lucrative and therefore less ubiquitous could be a feasible option.

GMD: Matt recently said that Bernie’s Medicare For All bill is “nothing like the current US Medicare system” and “nothing like European universal systems”. It would be helpful to get an explainer on the technical differences between various current systems and the Bernie proposal. Something like what Ezra did ~20 years ago, but updated and maybe a bit more thorough?

The main thing is that what Bernie’s legislation is trying to promise people is health care that’s not just free at point of service but available in unlimited quantities.

Some Yale professors did a study recently claiming that Medicare for All would save $1 trillion per year but also save 114,000 lives. Obviously every point of that estimate can be disputed. But the main idea on the lives-saved front is that with cost barriers eliminated, people who are currently uninsured or underinsured would consume more health care services and those services would be useful and save lives. But there’s no mechanism in the bill to identify services that are currently being provided and don’t have the same life-saving value that could be redistributed to those who need more services. Instead, the researchers are clearly contemplating some kind of large expansion in the total volume of health care received by the American people.

One can ask oneself whether it’s credible to envision this large expansion in health care volumes happening at the exact same time that cuts to administration and provider payments are reducing aggregate spending by $1 trillion. But apart from the accounting, my point is just that I don’t think there is any national health system that works like this. What real countries do is some mix of prices and administrative rationing to constrain consumption while attempting to target resources to the highest-value areas. That’s where you land based on generations of dealing with an actually existing system and various tradeoffs.

Azareal: Matt, you’ve mentioned several times that you have a vacation home in Maine. I’ve been struck since living in DC how there isn’t an obvious summer destination that’s a reasonable drive from the city the way that other East Coast cities like NY, Philly, and Boston have, and then once you are on a plane or road trip, the number of options becomes a bit overwhelming. How did you land on Maine for your family? What do you all do when you are there and how has that changed over different kid ages? Is it a destination you’d recommend?

My grandparents bought a house in Brooklin, Maine, before I was born, so I’ve been spending summers up there my whole life except when I was in sleepaway camp in a different part of Maine.

President Camacho: As someone who spends your summers in Maine, is there any culture shock when you leave DC for small town New England or is it more palpable upon your return to the city?

The main thing that I always re-forget before switching is how different the age structures are.

Maine has the oldest median age of any state, while D.C. is younger than every state except for Utah. And even that sort of understates the difference because D.C. (unlike Utah) does not have a particularly large number of actual children. It’s just a city that contains relatively few senior citizens by American standards and a lot of twentysomethings. So I walk around my neighborhood in D.C. and I feel old. I feel like most of the people I see on the street or at the Coffee Bar or wherever are younger than I am. And in Maine it’s just the reverse. A fortysomething couple with an 11-year-old isn’t part of the old squares in Maine; my family’s the fresh blood.

Obviously the two places are very different in a million ways. But a lot of it is obvious. I don’t go to Maine and then find myself surprised that the coast is beautiful or that it’s eerily quiet at night. I don’t come back to D.C. having forgotten that the available restaurants here are a million times better. Those are the things I think about consciously. But I find myself shopping at the Hannaford in Blue Hill or going to the Y to work out and being taken aback that there are all these seniors everywhere. I’m not used to seeing that many elderly people on a day-to-day basis in D.C. And then I come back here and it’s like “Wow! Some people are 27!”

Will U: For Yglesias: favorite towns in Maine other than Brooklin?

Well, just in the basic Brooklin neighborhood, Stonington and Castine are both great, and I also have a real soft spot in my heart for Bucksport, which I firmly believe should erect a memorial to the Red October that was (fictionally) scuttled in the Penobscot Bay just outside of town. To range a bit further afield, the ominously named Winter Harbor is great, and the one time when I was a kid that the family drove all the way east to Lubec and Eastport, I found that far Downeast region fascinating.

But there’s also plenty to be said for the quirkiness of inland Maine.

We’re very clearly living through a period of flattening and homogenization where every place increasingly resembles everywhere else. And the people who choose to live north and west of I-95 in Maine are people who are very deliberately opting out of that whole vibe. Greenville and Millinocket are very much not places that are just like everywhere else. The whole Pat’s Pizza chain exists primarily in places that don’t have chains. There’s a whole part of Maine where people are shopping for groceries without going to a supermarket. I’m not really a hunter or an outdoorsman, so I can’t pretend that I am spending tons of time in the North Woods or its environs, but going there is a very special and unique and genuinely alien experience in a way that going to the coast isn’t.

BJ: Polymarket currently gives JD Vance a 47% chance of being the 2028 GOP nominee, and Marco Rubio a 20% chance. (Donald Trump is currently third at 2.3%.) Personally, I’d bet the field - Trump seems a lot more likely to be at 25% approval than 45% approval in 2028, and I don’t really get why the GOP voters would want to nominate the second- or third-most powerful figures from a failing lame-duck administration. So my question is, if it isn’t Vance or Rubio, would you bet on the GOP nominating someone saner-seeming, such as DeSantis, Youngkin, or Kemp? Or do you think they’d go full crazy with someone like RFK Jr or Tucker Carlson?

ETA — one other reason I’m highly skeptical and Vance and Rubio — to call back to your columns about Kamala, it seems highly likely that they would need to shake the etch-a-sketch to win a primary (not to mention a general) and there’s just no way Trump let’s them get away with that.

I disagree with you on taking the field. Even if Trump is at 30 percent national approval, that would still translate into an enormous amount of intra-G.O.P. clout, and I think the person he prefers will probably be the nominee.

But if it doesn’t shake out that way, I think the logical alternative is neither electoral pragmatism à la Kemp nor total zaniness à la R.F.K. Jr. — it’s ideological rigorism. It’s someone making the pitch that Ted Cruz tried to land in 2016 and saying, “Hey, look, the problem with Trump is that he’s not right-wing enough.” Abortion is, I think, the obvious possible hook here, possibly paired with other social conservative ideas like “we need to crack down on pornography.” But you might also be saying to conservatives, you know, we need to get serious about spending and entitlements.

It’s not that I think these are savvy ideas that it would make strategic sense for Republicans to embrace in 2028. But these are clearly ideas with powerful constituencies behind them in Republican Party politics, constituencies who’ve been fairly beaten down by Trump’s personal clout.

If that clout fades, then I think the Republican version of “the groups” starts rising again. Not in a way that is directly critical of Trump or anti-MAGA, but just a sense that if we’re going to get our mojo back, we need to tackle the big problems of society.
