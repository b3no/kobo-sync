Are You About to Get Fired?

Tips and tricks for reading the writing on the walls

Cartoons Hate Her
Sep 16
 
READ IN APP
 

This is an archived article (originally paid) made free for all subscribers, free or paid. Expect more of these every Wednesday.

If you haven’t read my firing lore, let me fill you in: I was fired a lot during my previous life in tech, a tenure that lasted about thirteen years. I was not a disastrous employee, just a below-average one (although to be more specific, I was probably average in product and below-average in sales, where my incompetence really shone through.) I’ve only been fired for actual performance reasons twice, but been abruptly let go for no cited reason several other times, and laid off several other times too. I think, all in all, my employment was involuntarily terminated six times.

Part of the reason was that I hated quitting, so I always stuck around as long as I possibly could, even when nobody else would have, even when my employer was clearly about to go out of business. The other part is that…well, I’m a bit weird and always have been, so even at “normal” companies, I was bound to piss everyone off without trying. It’s one reason my most successful stints have been remote jobs that didn’t involve client interactions, where it’s much easier for me to cloak my weirdness in the shadows.

One question I got a lot after publishing my history of getting fired was, “How do I know if I’m about to get fired?” Or, “What do I do if I know I’m probably getting fired soon?” Well, you’re in luck because the expert in Getting Fired is here to give you all the deets!

Signs You’re Getting Fired

If you’re anything like me, you’ve had a lot of moments where you’ve worried about getting fired, but it doesn’t actually happen. This means that when you actually are in danger of getting fired, it’s really easy to tell yourself that you’re just imagining it.

At one company, during orientation I broke my work computer with a game of Sims (I was playing outside of work hours on a work device.) I was convinced it was over for me, and sheepishly showed up to IT ready to confess the sin of starting a Sims dynasty who were going to own a family farm over the generations. As it turned out, the computer crashed for reasons unrelated to Sims, but IT laughed and said I wouldn’t get fired for that anyway. But every mishap after that point led me to think I was going to get fired. It got so annoying that my boss actually had a meeting with HR to discuss how my fear of being fired was paradoxically impacting my ability to do my work. Of course, the fact that she met with HR did not feel reassuring. Blame the OCD, but every month or so, a new thing happened that I was convinced would be my end. And ultimately, I did get fired! Just not for any of those things. Or at least not explicitly- it’s possible all those other things, even if they were inconsequential, created the perception of me that I was overly anxious and incompetent.

If you’re like me, and have had lots of false alarm firings, then it can be hard to get a straight interpretation of your situation from anyone you ask. Most likely, your family and friends will roll their eyes and say, “Weren’t you worried about this last month too?” But having anxiety doesn’t preclude you from ever being fired for real—in my case, it often contributed.

Having been fired several times I can think of several surefire signs that distinguish the real firings from the delusions:

1.) You get put on a PIP

This one is the most obvious, but don’t worry, I’ll get into the lesser-known clues later on. If your manager puts you on a formal PIP (Performance Improvement Plan- common in the tech sector) then you are probably going to get fired regardless of what you do.

I’ve heard of people surviving PIPs (supposedly 50% do) but I would estimate it to be much lower than that. Especially if you work at a large company, a PIP is more or less a cover-your-ass thing so that you can’t sue for wrongful termination later.

Most likely your PIP will include several requirements that you need to meet before you can graduate from it and remain at the company. If these requirements seem doable, or if your boss is making genuine efforts at helping you meet them (for example, using company resources to train you to improve your knowledge) then it’s likely a “real” PIP and not just a pretense for firing you. However, if you notice that a lot of the PIP is stuff that’s so vague you could never prove you’ve completed it (I was once on an informal PIP which included the goal “be more strategic”) then you should assume you’re getting fired no matter what.

2.) You get put on an informal PIP

You might think that if there’s no PIP, you’re fine, but there’s actually something else companies like to do called “documented feedback.” This is where they cover their ass by writing you long summaries of your performance on a regular basis, including prompts for things you can do to improve, which are often purposefully unclear and impossible. At one company, I felt like my manager (who I think desperately wanted to fire me, and had felt that way for a while) was giving me extremely vague feedback. I told him I needed more clarity about what he wanted, because it felt like whatever I did to meet his expectations was somehow the wrong thing. His response was to start having hour-long 1:1s with me every week, where I received twice as much vague feedback.

Then he began documenting the vague feedback in long emails (sometimes as many as five paragraphs.) Every time I made any mistake at work, even after he had spoken to me about it, he would send me some kind of email about it (including the ill-advised “Game of Thrones themed” training deck I put together, which was apparently “very unprofessional.”) I think this kind of behavior is actually more of a bad sign than a PIP because at least sometimes, PIPs are meant for actual improvement.

As an aside, if you work at a company with an “expectations grid” and you are repeatedly told to refer to it when you ask for detailed feedback or advice, this boss does not actually care about helping you improve and just wants to get rid of you.

3.) Your boss suddenly starts bugging you a lot

This is something I noticed when I was let go from a company but not for performance reasons, only redundancy. I was actually doing really well, with only stellar performance reviews. It was my first remote job, which helped—I didn’t have to focus on my “perception” or faking it with my coworkers. I could simply do my work and have my performance speak for itself, although the main problem—which of course, led to my layoff—was that the job itself did not need to exist and there was very little work that I did not conjure up for myself. I managed this for three years, my longest ever tenure. I was also a well-liked employee because in lieu of having to socialize with coworkers in the office, I appointed myself the go-to person for pooling birthday and holiday presents, virtual baby showers, and other remote festivities.

My boss liked me on a personal level. We were around the same age, we were both moms, and I considered her a “friend.” I knew, of course, that she could fire me (likely not without approval from her boss, though, who I also had a buddy-buddy relationship with.) But *Obama/Shapiro voice* let me be clear: THEY DIDN’T GIVE A SHIT! Even your buddy-buddy boss is mostly in it for his or her own interests. She was no exception.

As I mentioned, this role did not need to exist, so laying me off (via eliminating my role) was a perfectly fine business decision. But of course, naturally, it was a situation I wanted to avoid. The first indicator of this removal was that my boss became a bit…annoying.

Suddenly, she was messaging me constantly, asking me if things were done that weren’t even due yet. If I was one minute late to a meeting, she would message me and ask if I “still planned on joining.” I also noticed that she stopped liking my posts on Instagram (was it weird that we were friends on Instagram? Yes! But it was her idea!) I got into the habit of checking the Instagram posts of another coworker, and sure enough, my boss liked her photos every day. I hadn’t done anything to alienate her, but she was almost purposefully sabotaging any “friendly” vibe to our relationship, because laying off your friend is extremely awkward.

4.) Your boss leaves you alone

After the aforementioned Buddy Boss replaced Instagram likes with breathless Teams pings, she entered a new state where she kind of ignored me. She canceled a lot of our 1:1s and claimed to be busy with other things. I also noticed the small amount of work I had really dried up, and I was being asked to train a more junior employee—the CEO’s nephew—in some of my more menial tasks. This guy would eventually absorb my entire job, but I was led to believe that I would be managing him as part of a re-org that never happened.

5.) They start asking you for lots of weird information

Before my role was eliminated at the company from points 3 and 4, something happened that in hindsight, was an obvious sign that I was getting let go. My boss started asking me to forward her the invites to all the calls that I regularly had. She claimed it was because they were starting a new “shadowing initiative” and she might need to join them in the future. At the same time, another employee told me that I needed to assign him certain levels of permissions to software that only I used because of a “new project.” To me, it felt like all the signs were coming together, but because I had been wrong about this many times in the past, my husband doubted me. Of course, I was right.

6.) You get an ominous invite on your calendar

At this point it’s obviously too late, but a surefire way to know if you’re about to be fired is an unscheduled invite. I’ve heard people say that if this happens, HR will be on the invite. That’s true, but you won’t see that they’re invited until they show up, so it doesn’t really matter.

At one company where I was fired for performance issues, it was an invite for a regular 1:1, but in a weird location in the office, a floor where my department didn’t typically work. They did this so I wouldn’t “make a scene” in front of my team, and so security could remove me from the premises if necessary. Presumably, this was just a policy, because that’s the last thing I would do. In fact, while I was being fired, I asked the HR lady if I was making it easy enough for her and whether or not I was on the smoother end of firings.

At the company with the Instagram Friend Boss, where I was made redundant but not fired for performance, it was actually pretty soul crushing because there was an elaborate web of lies that led up to the layoff meeting: the promise to “hire someone under me,” the requests for my calendar and user permissions with phony “initiatives” that warranted these asks…one could say I was being gaslit, because I actually asked point-blank if I should be concerned about a layoff (I had been turning down inbound job opportunities at that time! It was a great job market!) and I was repeatedly told that I was not getting laid off.

By the way, here’s a piece of advice: never ask if you’re getting fired or laid off. They’ll say no or deflect. It won’t matter. Although I do have to give credit to one boss I had at a Japanese company where I failed to bring in any revenue all quarter. When I asked him if I was going to be fired, he looked sadly into his lap and said, “I don’t know.” This also meant yes, of course.

Anyway, back to the redundancy story: I got an invite with my boss’s boss (who I also considered a close “ally” at work.) I actually think he tried his hardest to keep me around as long as he did, because he was the one who hired me and he liked me a lot. But I have it on good authority (conversation with a disgruntled former member of C-level leadership) that the decision to get rid of me came from the CEO, who had never wanted to hire for my role in the first place. So while the idea might not have been my boss’s boss’s (or my Instagram Friend boss’s) they were responsible for the intricate D&D fantasy world-building that was done to make me believe my job was safe.

Anyway, he put a call on my calendar which was dubiously labeled “Sync.” Nope, don’t like that at all! I texted him to ask what it was about or if I should prepare anything. He said he was “too busy” to give me any details but that I didn’t need to prepare anything. Sure enough, I joined, and his camera and sound were both off. “Sorry, WiFi issues!” he texted me. Then, like a horror movie jump scare, I saw some mysterious woman materialize on the screen. Oh hell no, it’s HR! At this point, the “WiFi issues” resolved and my boss’s boss went camera-on to let me know my employment was terminated.

Despite the fact that Instagram Boss had clearly known about this for a while (and helped to orchestrate it) she sent me a text saying she had “just heard about what happened” and she “couldn’t believe it.” Girl, bye.

7.) Your attempts at being friendly are rebuffed

Obviously, I’m used to rejection. I write about it all the time. But one glaringly obvious sign I was being made redundant was that my attempts to be fun and friendly went unreciprocated. This was at a company where my boss’s boss had said he saw himself as a “father figure” to our team, and that watching us come up with new ideas was like seeing his own children take their first steps. It was a pretty emotional, effusive place to work!

I knew something was off when he was giving a presentation to me along with a few other people, and he showed a slide that he had shown me on my first day at the company. He asked us what we thought of it, and I said, “I like it, but maybe I’m biased because it reminds me of my first day. Awesome memories!” (I’m sure what I said was less awkward than this—actually, maybe it wasn’t, but this particular boss was corny enough to find this endearing.) BUT…he did not find it endearing. In fact, he just pretended he didn’t hear me.

So if you want to very passively test the waters, maybe see how your boss or boss’s boss reacts to your attempts to be friendly in a work-appropriate way.

8.) General company changes

If a new C-level person comes into your department, or there is a broad sweeping change with a lot of other teams, it may affect your team as well, even if you haven’t done anything wrong. One of the things that happened before I was made redundant was that a senior executive who had previously been left to his own devices without much work (probably off gooning all day) was suddenly brought back into the daily grind, and he didn’t like me very much. I don’t think it was his decision to lay me off but I think him taking over our department made it easier for that decision to happen.

Now…what about the signs that you’re not going to be fired? What are some indicators that this actually is all in your head? There are several!

1.) The company uses their resources to help you.

As pointless as many “seminars” and “trainings” might be, they actually cost a lot of money. I once worked for a company that brought in this wacky new-age motivational speaker to do an 8-hour workshop (which involved writing complaints on pieces of paper and then shredding the paper “cathartically”) and then bragged that this one workshop cost $40,000 so we better take it seriously. If your boss has negative feedback for you but is spending company time trying to help you, by signing you up for various trainings or sessions, it’s not a sign that you definitely won’t be fired but it does mean that the feedback they’re giving you is genuine and they actually want you to improve (instead of using the documentation as a precursor to fire you.)

2.) You’re being asked to do stuff in the future.

If your boss is talking to you about planning initiatives that won’t come to fruition for another six months, you’re probably safe for the meantime, unless a big part of that planning involves delegating those tasks to other people or training people to do your work.

3.) The troubling stuff that applies to you also applies to other coworkers.

If you’re noticing a change in how your boss is handling things (for example, more formal 1:1s) try to do some subtle sleuthing to see if the same thing is happening with everyone on your team. For example, if your boss suddenly starts tracking all your progress in a 1:1 shared document when previously nothing was documented, this could be a red flag, but it could also just be something he’s doing with the whole team. It’s hard to check this kind of thing without coming off as nosy and paranoid, but if you have coworkers you consider close friends, you can probably do it.

So what now??

Let’s say you’ve read through all this and you’re fairly certain you’re going to get fired soonish. There are several paths you could take:

1.) Quit immediately.

Give them the whole “You can’t fire me, I quit.” This is what you might do if you have a lot of dignity and don’t want to be taken for a fool. I’ve never really done this because it seems risky, although I’ve fantasized about it. The problem with this is that you miss out on money. Every day you work is more money (and getting fired often means severance.) Plus, it’s easier to find another job if you already have one, so you might also make things harder for yourself in the job search by quitting too fast.

2.) Find another job secretly.

This is probably the best thing to do if you can swing it, but if you work at a company that fills your calendar with various “recurring syncs,” it can be hard to do. You also don’t want to rush to find the first thing that’s halfway passable because you’re on a deadline of wanting to find a job before you get fired.

The dream situation actually happened to a boss of mine: she knew she was going to get fired so she started looking for another job, and then she took a job offer at a big company that was willing to pay her more. She shared this information with me, and then later in the day told me to keep it a secret because she had just been fired as of an hour ago, and she didn’t want anything to jeopardize her ability to negotiate for more severance. QUEEN.

3.) Wait it out.

This is what I typically do, although I have attempted option 2 (it’s harder than it sounds.) Severance is nothing to scoff at, and you often get it even if you are fired for performance. You almost always get it if you are let go for reasons other than performance, or laid off. The only situation in which you are almost guaranteed not to get it is when you’re fired for gross misconduct, but that’s not common and it’s pretty easy to avoid.

Sometimes after a really draining experience at a job, it can be nice to have a little time without the slog of work. Granted, if you have financial concerns (as I have before) then this time isn’t very relaxing, so it all depends on your situation. But if you have the means to take a little time off (even if you are spending that time exploring the job market) it can be a nice reset.

Some people want to know how they can explain being fired in a job interview after the fact, if they wind up unemployed in between jobs. The truth is, you don’t have to. There is no law mandating that employers tell people that you were fired. Employers often have nothing to gain from sharing this information anyway, and I believe some of the time they aren’t even allowed to share it (not sure on the state laws here.) All that matters is there is someone from a previous company that can be a reference, and if you were simply let go over performance or redundancy, there is someone who would say something positive about you. And if that person doesn’t exist, just pull a Borat and say that he’s dead:

You should absolutely never lie about your employment dates, your title, or anything else that can be verified. I don’t even think it’s a good idea to lie about being fired, it’s just something you don’t need to share explicitly using those words. Consider saying:

  • “We got to the point where we realized it just wasn’t a good fit on either side”

  • “I realized this wasn’t a good role for me, because I struggled with XYZ. In my next role, I pivoted to XYZ which was a much better fit for my skillset.”

  • “I was looking for another job because I wasn’t happy with XYZ. In hindsight, I should have just left the company to focus on the job search full time, because balancing work and the job search meant I couldn’t do my best work, and that became an issue.”

Anyway, I hope that you don’t even have to use these talking points because you’ll never get fired, ever! But if you’ve gotten this far, you’ve probably been fired before. In either case, it’s usually not the end of the world. Onwards and upwards!
