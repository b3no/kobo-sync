Mark Zuckerberg’s race to the bottom

The Meta CEO’s views on AI are even worse than you think

Kobe Yank-Jacobs
Nov 9
 
READ IN APP
 

In September, OpenAI CEO Sam Altman, Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei, and xAI CEO Elon Musk all called for “pacing” AI development amid growing fears about the technology going rogue. One voice was conspicuously absent from this chorus: Mark Zuckerberg.

Altman and Amodei spent the 2010s publicly worrying about the right way to safeguard against runaway AI. Whether you consider them compromised by their business interests or not, they at least have a long record of concern.

Zuckerberg is a different case. He spent that decade turning up the dial on political extremism and addicting teens to his social media platforms with little apparent thought for the consequences. At various times, he would justify his decisions with high-minded rhetoric, depending on which way the political winds were blowing, pivoting multiple times between an avowed commitment to policing misinformation and vague paeans to “free expression.”

Now Zuckerberg is bringing the same flightiness to an even more consequential technology. In September, he argued that markets create perfectly fine incentives for AI companies to keep their products under control. And in an August essay, “The Future is for Everyone,” Zuckerberg reached for uplifting rhetoric about the decentralizing forces of technology without any sense of irony about the fact that his company is one of just five that own two-thirds of all the compute in the world.

His support for a freewheeling approach to AI oversight might have been inconsequential at an earlier time, when Meta was seen as lagging behind its AI competitors. But Zuckerberg has since spent billions of dollars to reorganize the company’s AI division, and those efforts have just started to pay off. In September, Meta released one of the first popular AI personal assistants. And last month, Meta’s newest model release — Muse Wonder — was rated one of the top models in terms of capability.

Zuckerberg has also had the ear of the second Trump administration. In fact, he was the author of the toothless AI safety statement the lab CEOs signed at the White House in September, which President Donald Trump called “morally binding” at the announcement.

AI progress is, for better or worse, a race. For years, Anthropic’s strategy has been to make it a “race to the top.” By developing the best AI systems with the highest standards, Anthropic is betting that it can make safety a competitive standard that other companies have to match. If a major rival can gain ground by prioritizing speed and scale over safeguards, companies that try to commit to safety risk falling behind.

Meta is now capable enough to create precisely that pressure, and its unscrupulous founder seems poised to pull us in another direction.

Zuckerberg’s war of all against all

Whenever Zuckerberg muses about high-minded concepts — like free expression or the decentralizing force of technology — it always seems to carry the whiff of both teenage naivete and minimally concealed business interest.

He began 2018 with a promise to fix Facebook by taking a more active role in cleaning up abuse and defending the platform from foreign political influence. By 2019, he had already pivoted to a different argument: that the real threat to free expression was platforms like Facebook taking too much responsibility for deciding what people could say. He retroactively rationalized this about-face in a rare speech at Georgetown University, in which he laughably recast the company’s roots. Instead of Facebook arising out of Facemash, a site Zuckerberg designed as a Harvard sophomore to allow his peers to rate each other’s attractiveness (the true story), he claimed the company was inspired by his feeling that open debate might have stopped the Iraq War (what?).

“I remember feeling that if more people had a voice to share their experiences, maybe things would have gone differently,” he mused vaguely.

Now, in the thick of the AI era, Zuckerberg has latched onto a new stock phrase from the democratic lexicon to justify Meta’s actions: “checks and balances.”

“People and institutions with competing interests naturally check and balance each other to lead towards positive outcomes,” Zuckerberg wrote in “The Future Is for Everyone” this August, asserting that “the best and most realistic path to building a positive AI future is by delivering superintelligence to everyone.”

In other words, what will keep us safe is the fact that everyone — good, evil, or ambivalent — will have something like a machine god in their pockets.

As with his 2019 “free expression” speech, there are at least two ways one can read Zuckerberg’s new essay: as a cynical cover for his business interests or a sincere but misguided expression of confused values.

Start with a cynical reading and you’ll see there are about a half-dozen ways that this freewheeling conception of AI advantages Meta’s interests: For one, Zuckerberg argues we should create protections for distillation, which is the technical term for the process of training one model on another’s outputs. Bloomberg has previously reported that Meta relies heavily on the practice. A second and more glaring example is that his proposed solution to worries about uncontrolled, self-improving AI is to build more compute — the very comparative advantage that Meta has already staked in the field.

He also called for all this “personal superintelligence” just weeks before releasing Muse personal agents, which makes the document appear to be more of a soft product launch announcement than an earnest vision.

It looks even more like a marketing document when you see how often he aims to frame his vision as a contrast with his competitors’. He says, for instance, that his view on AI-driven job loss is “distinct from others in the industry” who want to automate work — but he doesn’t offer any specific way that Meta’s vision for AI will prevent this. Instead, all he offers is a shallow reading of how he hopes the economics will play out: People will start more businesses, and even if each business has fewer employees, the net employment effect will be positive, he argues, citing nothing.

By far his flimsiest attempt to cast himself as the true man of the people in comparison with his competitors is his clumsy straw man of Amodei’s views: “The notion that AI is so dangerous that the only safe path is an extreme concentration of power seems inherently problematic.” But it’s Zuckerberg’s own company that appears hell-bent on concentrating power by laying claim to a huge share of the world’s total compute.

That’s the cynical reading. But how would his world of “personal superintelligence” work out if it were not just a front for Zuckerberg’s business interests?

In his stated view, the best way to achieve AI safety is to have bad actors countered by good ones armed with the same technology — the same way that gun advocates like to say “the only thing that can stop a bad guy with a gun is a good guy with a gun.”

This is most apparent in Zuckerberg’s take on alignment. Alignment means, to put things simply, that an AI acts the way humans want it to act, rather than pursuing unknown objectives that might have been an accidental product of its training. The issue at hand is whether you can ask an AI for “X” and it actually does “X” while not also pursuing its own goals secretly.

Zuckerberg calls for “a new way of thinking about alignment” that’s about aligning the technology to each user, not to the company that creates it.

“A healthy balance of power is to ensure that there is no singular centralized superintelligence,” he wrote, “but instead as many people and businesses as possible with different superintelligent agents aligned to their goals that check and compete with each other in the ways our natural economy behaves.”

But this elides the most pressing aspect of alignment: whether an AI system reliably follows human instruction at all. That part is largely baked in when models are trained. It doesn’t change just because AIs interact with many people with many different values.

This misrepresentation shows that Zuckerberg is not interested in using terms the same way as everyone else. “Checks and balances,” for instance, is usually understood as powerful institutions counterbalancing each other’s power, like Congress and the presidency. In the AI realm, you might think of some sort of oversight body squaring off against the industry.

Instead, Zuckerberg’s version involves some hazy libertarian utopia where everything works out fine if we just take our hands off the wheel and let everyone use AI however they wish. He seems blithely undeterred by the fact that when he tried radical libertarianism with Facebook, it led to political extremism that was organized first on the platform and the spread of anti-vaccine conspiracies.

But experts across various domains are skeptical. In biosecurity, the same AI that helps a scientist troubleshoot her experiment could help a would-be terrorist develop a biological weapon. In cybersecurity, AI could help defenders strengthen their networks and hackers write better phishing emails. While large institutions might be able to use AI to defend their systems, will your grandmother have the same protection? The benefits of making everyone more capable don’t necessarily accrue equally to everyone.

If Zuckerberg’s vision founding Facebook really was about the turn-of-the-millennium dream of the internet redistributing power, that failure should have taught him something for the AI era. A more democratic society may well be good, but getting there requires detailed thinking about hard trade-offs, not empty shibboleths.

“Natural incentives” never stopped Zuckerberg before

Even beyond their basic incoherence, the high-minded points in Zuckerberg’s essay are belied by his own decisions in Meta’s pursuit of frontier AI.

After his peers called for slowing AI development down, Zuckerberg countered that companies have a “natural incentive” to make safe models; he argued that this is because customers don’t want dangerous AI. And he said that even if someone released a dangerous model that consumers adopted, there are already product liability laws on the books to handle any problems.

The first obvious flaw in this line of thinking is that safety is an issue long before any AI products go to market. The July incident in which OpenAI lost control of hundreds of AI agents that hacked into the company Hugging Face happened while one of the models that powered the agents was being tested.

AI is not a single product that remains inert until it rolls off an assembly line: There are many checkpoints in the development of a model that could be dangerous. For example, when Claude accidentally got onto the internet and tried to deceive an engineer into accepting malicious code, it was because Anthropic’s vendor hadn’t set up its testing environment properly. (Zuckerberg does propose sharing certain intermediate checkpoints with the government, but it appears that his vision is that compliance is totally voluntary.)

There is a precedent for the kind of process oversight we’ll need for AI: With airplanes, the Federal Aviation Administration doesn’t check only the final aircraft. It also inspects the factories the aircraft are made in to ensure they conform to the safety design. With restaurants, we don’t just wait for customers to get sick as a signal to warn off others. We have professional health inspectors check the kitchens and put a rating in the window.

With potentially world-ending technology like AI, a rating in the window is the very least we could ask for.

Meta’s own corporate behavior demonstrates the futility of “natural incentives” and even liability law in sufficiently constraining the consequences of profit-seeking behavior. If natural incentives were enough, why did Meta knowingly foment political extremism, harm teens, and addict the mass public to its products over the past decade and a half? In August, it agreed to pay $18 billion to settle a child safety lawsuit.

Zuckerberg has cited the delayed release of Muse due to safety concerns as evidence that market incentives work, and that labs will slow down of their own volition if they need to address safety issues. (During this time, a safety lead in the company had had her own emails deleted by an earlier version of the agent.) But reporting from The New York Times shows that as soon as a competitor, Instinct, developed a similar product, Zuckerberg rushed Meta toward its own release. According to reporting from 404 Media that cited a Meta source, “security teams were asked to push hot fixes to these bugs as quickly as possible and in a way that wouldn’t delay Muse’s launch, leading to what [the source] described as ‘half-baked protections being rushed out to enable the launch.’”

Slowing down doesn’t count if you press ahead in a rush as soon as a competitor releases a competing product! Imagine if you’d left your child at home for a while and told them not to go in the cookie jar, and when you came home, you found that they had eaten all the cookies. Then imagine they told you that they had actually waited most of the time — they only ate the cookies just before you got home. Would that make it OK?

No, of course not. The point was to not eat the cookies!

We can’t afford to ignore Zuckerberg’s optimism

Ever since most of the AI CEOs called for “pacing” AI development, they have been accused of holding disingenuous beliefs about the dangers of the technology. They have been accused of attempting regulatory capture, or of trying to hype up investment by making embellished claims about the end of the world. In my view, their long track record of concern about AI’s dangers absolves them of this (as does the fact that making such claims would be an absurd business strategy).

But Zuckerberg really does make sense as a target of this criticism! He has spent over a decade shape-shifting his values around Facebook’s and later Meta’s real-time business interests. That’s why his version of the case that AI is a “normal” technology rings so hollow.

“I’m much closer to the belief that AI is a technology that can be engineered with normal guardrails and built responsibly than the anthropomorphic vision of AI,” he said in a recent interview, “which is that it is just going to go do its own thing.”

Until we have regulation, each lab will be making decisions about how fast to move, and they have even weighed making those decisions together. AI, for better or worse, has become something of a prisoner’s dilemma. Zuckerberg, so far, seems like he’ll always be the one to defect.
