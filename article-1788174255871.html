You’ll Never Know Who The High Heels Are For

Why it’s almost impossible to know if a beauty or fashion choice is for yourself or for men--and why that might not even be a question worth answering.

I’ve always been fascinated by beauty standards and how women execute on them, specifically the openness to which women admit they are grooming, primping or dressing a certain way “for men.” Or not for men.

This topic is so heavy for women because we are raised, basically from day one, to have a really concrete idea of what men find attractive. Even if the men in your life don’t say anything about it, awareness of male preferences is inescapabale. A big part of red pill and black pill manosphere ideologies is the devastating realization that women care about shallow things, and will not dole out their attention based on which man behaves the best, but by who they find the most attractive. But for women, this is not a revelation because we already know. I cannot remember a time in my life when I was confused by, or oblivious to, the idea that boys would treat me better and be more interested in me if I was pretty.

I don’t say this to blame “men” for this in any kind of vengeful or Male-Tears-mug-coded way. As I’ll cover soon, many women hold themselves to exaggerated versions of conventional standards which most individual men would never impose on them. But I also don’t blame the women for this perception. These expectations are everywhere around them, to the point that they become second nature, even if the individual men in their lives aren’t putting any pressure on them.

Because women are steeped in these male-preferred beauty standards so deeply and so early, they become a sort of muscle memory for many of us. Most women do not consciously think, for example, about the specific waist-hip ratios that men prefer. They don’t have to think about it. They can look at every Disney princess and well-known sex icon and have their answer even without a numerical value (I’m aware some man will remind me that boys are aware of ultra-muscular superheroes, and there’s no disagreement there, but I maintain that assessing your own attractiveness against a conventional standard is more ingrained in a young girl’s mind than a young boy’s.) Most straight women, therefore, are surrounded by male preferences and often adhere to them in a way that does not feel conscious or deliberate. Sometimes, adhering to these standards may even feel completely self-driven, if not feminist, because being seen as attractive can feel “empowering.”

This is not a unique concept that I invented. The idea that “nothing exists in a vacuum” has been circulated among feminist writers much smarter than myself for ages. See, Sandra Bartky in 1988 or Naomi Wolf pre-vaccine-quackery in 1991. But I’m interested in where we draw the line, or if the line can be drawn at all. If beauty standards are so subconscious, how can we really know—and does it even matter—if our fashion, grooming, or primping is “for men” or for ourselves?

I wasn’t even planning to write about this until a particularly interesting comment from Eurydice on one of my articles last week sparked me to revisit the topic. She noted a few seemingly inconsistent gaps in my belief system. I have repeatedly said that almost everything that straight, conventionally feminine women to do look “better” is actually driven by what men find attractive, even if they claim they do it for themselves. Yet, I’ve also pointed out that some beauty rituals mean nothing to men, and women who say men are imposing these standards on them either have incorrectly calculated what men care about, or are using men’s standards as a cover (“girl tax!”) for doing something they just happen to enjoy which mostly appeals to other women (manicures, designer handbags, etc.)

I have also said that some things exist which men claim they don’t care about, but only because they just don’t see those things. Per my most famous cartoon of all time:

Obviously, this all sounds a bit contradictory and could potentially give, “Everyone is lying except me, the smartest and most self-aware person ever.” I can assure you I don’t believe that—in fact, I have also found myself confused and conflicted over whether I was doing things for myself, for a general male preference, for women, or for a specific man. That’s probably why I write about it so often.

As I noted earlier, the subconscious nature of conventional beauty standards means that a lot of women do things to adhere to these standards without even realizing it. If they are ardent feminists, this might make them uncomfortable because they find “looking reasonably good to attract a mate” to be akin to “pandering to the male gaze” so they will seek to explain it away, perhaps just to themselves: Men have NOTHING to do with this! I just really love ripping out all my pubic hair because I have sensory issues, and I like uncomfortable stiletto heels because they make me feel empowered! Some may go a step further and insist that their conventional beauty is actually really countercultural and rebellious, if not meant to be anti-man and if you can’t tell, that’s on you. Most of the time, even if these women have not really examined why they prefer what they prefer, I don’t think they are consciously lying to save face. They may really believe that any choice a feminist makes is a “feminist choice,” and therefore countercultural on the basis that a feminist decided it was countercultural.

The opposite dynamic can emerge too, and I’m guilty of this one: a woman will sometimes undertake an onerous beauty ritual and claim she needs to do this to survive in a patriarchal society or find a boyfriend, but it’s something so extreme or superfluous that no man would ever care or notice, and often something far too expensive and time-consuming to be a requirement for all straight women. Even if she observes no real-life romantic upside to this ritual, some kind of innate desire to “optimize” towards an extreme version of a conventional ideal will keep her focusing on it despite zero tangible benefit. Over time, it becomes her own personal special interest or compulsion, which is actually more “for her” than she thinks. My foray into this behavior was waist training. The male version of this behavior is quite apparent in male looksmaxxers, or men who believe you need to look like a Bully XL on steroids to find a girlfriend.

Very few men, on the other hand, do things to appeal to women while denying it out of political or personal embarrassment. In fact, we can expand “men” to “everyone who is not a straight woman.” When I asked my LGBTQ followers if they felt conflicted about doing things explicitly to appeal to desired partners, most said no! This seems to be behavior fairly unique to straight women (or bisexual women who date men). My initial theory, when I first noticed this contradiction, was that because of feminism, straight women are embarrassed to admit anything is for men, and that they consciously make up excuses to other women so they don’t appear dick-obsessed. But I think there’s another layer to it, which is that male beauty standards are so second nature to us that often our own preferences can genuinely feel like our own, even if they’re strongly influenced by conventional male preferences.

So first, let’s name the most obvious conventional male preferences—the things women grew up seeing all over the place, the things we clock immediately as “hot” without consciously thinking about the details: a slim (not underweight) BMI, average to large breasts, a low waist-hip ratio, clear skin, perky if not ample-sized butt, long hair, and the general appearance of youth (I say “youth” here to mean “twenties,” not underage.) Generally, this can be expanded to include feminine clothing that accentuates the body, often including high heels and form-fitting clothing.

The vast majority of women will not be able to embody all of these traits. Also, plenty of men will diverge in taste here. I have met men who were nuts about short hair. It’s not hard to find a man who loves chubby women. I have small boobs and a very anti-neotenous face, and have never suffered for lack of male attention. But when we think about the conventional ideal, it’s basically what I described above.

Generally, most of those things are determined by genetics (or just the linear passage of time) but women can pull some levers: diet and exercise, hair care, skincare, makeup, clothing, and maybe a few cosmetic procedures. And most straight women, even the ones who are not especially vain, will do a few things to come closer to this ideal. It’s rare to find a woman who has never once fretted about her weight, breast size or skin.

But it’s not always so clear-cut. Because men’s tastes vary, if your goal is simply to attract one particular man, you do not need to adhere to every conventional standard, especially not if it’s especially cumbersome to do so. This is why I pushed back against a directive for all women to get breast implants. But most straight women have internalized these standards so that occasionally, they will derive pleasure from meeting one of these standards, while their male partner might either not care, or actively dislike it. At this point, who is it for? The only answer is: me, him, society, and aaaaah I don’t want to think about this.

A great example here, at least in my own life, is high heels. The other day, I posted a note which was intended as a teaser for an upcoming article about “ugly-cool” shoes. (Coming soon, my fashion followers don’t wanna miss it.) But I uncovered an interesting dynamic in the comments:

I’ll save you a click: men overwhelmingly preferred the heels, and women overwhelmingly preferred the ballet sneakers. And, even less surprisingly, only women noticed that incredibly cool purse made of telephone wires (STAUD, 2017, thank you very much.) Regarding the shoes, there were maybe 1-2 outliers on each side. This is not surprising; men will claim they don’t care much about high heels (“Ladies, why do this? It looks so uncomfortable!”) but really, they do care. They see a woman in heels and note that she looked more polished and feminine, and her ass looks perkier, despite viewing heels in the abstract as cumbersome and painful, the ultimate “women be like” punchline. The white sneakers come out, and suddenly, we look a bit sloppy.

However, my husband does not care for heels, and not in the “doesn’t realize he cares” way. He really does not care for them. In fact, I showed him both shoes with this outfit, and he said he preferred the sneakers because they looked cooler and more unexpected. He said the heels looked pretty, but were too typical-polished and not interesting. The umbrella word he’s looking for is, of course, “millennial,” which has come to embody a certain pretty-but-uncool aesthetic we all recognize in Emily in Paris.

My husband’s remark tracks with what he’s said about all the other shoes I’ve worn around him. He will show interest if a shoe is “cool” in some way, but “cool” often does not equal sexy. He has plenty of typical male preferences, but high heels just don’t crack the list at all. But if you had to ask me which shoe I thought looked better, I would say the heels, hands down!

In this case, I have the “male” preference and my husband has the “female” preference. (This is the world liberals want, I am informed.) To dress to appeal to my husband, I should wear the sneakers, even though I actually think the heels look better. (The sneakers are, of course, far more comfortable, which is worth considering!) But my preference for heels is not completely random. I prefer how the heels look because I have been conditioned to want to optimize my appearance for conventional female beauty. The heels elongate my legs and make me feel more feminine. When I wear the sneakers, I don’t feel cool and young—I feel a little dorky and, dare I say, orthopedically challenged.

If I were to wear the heels instead of the sneakers, would that be for “myself?” Yes and no! They would adhere to my visual preferences more than that of my husband’s, but my visual preferences were formed in an extremely pungent stew of male preferences, starting from childhood onward. Plus, I can acknowledge that if I lived in an all-female commune, those heels would probably have been donated long ago.

Now, let’s explore the opposite phenomenon, which is commonly referred to the “girl tax,” (not to be confused with the “pink tax,” wherein woman-designated version of items are nonsensically marked up). In the case of the girl tax, a woman will undertake some onerous or expensive beauty routine or purchase, say she doesn’t really want to do it, but explain it away by saying the patriarchy is making her do it and it’s the only way to live in a world dominated by male preferences. These women will frequently cite the “cost of being a woman,” and “being a woman” for some reason includes microneedling, lasers, expensive mani pedis, and $15,000 worth of clothing every year. Let’s just say the vast majority of women are apparently in debtor’s prison.

Don’t get me wrong, I love to shop and will never demonize a woman for doing the same, but this is actually not the cost of being a woman and has nothing to do with male attraction. Yes, men are attracted to clothes that look good and flatter the body, but those clothes can be purchased for far cheaper, and I’ve never met a man who complained that a woman’s wardrobe was not luxurious or varied enough. In this case, a woman may be purchasing clothes that make her look attractive to men, and she is absolutely being influenced by the society in which we live and the beauty standards of that society, but on some level she is enjoying this too (why else would she be shopping that much? It gets to a point…)

And yes, it’s true that men might gravitate towards a woman who looks polished and is wearing nice clothing, but most straight men cannot tell a Banana Republic black skirt from a Khaite black skirt. I’m sorry! Even most women cannot tell the difference!

But clothing falls into a uniquely interesting territory because so much of conventional women’s fashion is about male-preferred beauty—nipping in the waist, making you appear slimmer, making you appear more hourglass-shaped. Therefore, if you spend a lot of time buying clothes with that goal in mind (even if you genuinely enjoy shopping and buy far more clothes than any man would require) it can feel like you are being pushed by the patriarchy to place that $700 order at Shopbop.

But, at the same time, quite a bit of fashion is…not that. If you’re really into fashion, you’ll find that the coolest and most stylish pieces will directly fly in the face of the slim hourglass ideal and may even make you look a bit weird. As someone with cankles, I am particularly peeved at the fact that most modern-day “cool” fashion seems focused on making your ankles look as wide as possible. But someone really into fashion will gravitate towards clothes because of the mood and vibe they give off. Sometimes that mood is sexy, but most of the time it isn’t. A pair of breezy “coastal Grandma” linen pants can be incredibly stylish, but not particularly sexy and certainly not form-fitting. So what can we say about women who love fashion but wear the exact type of clothes that would send men running for the hills? The aesthetics which may fall under the bucket of…man repeller? After all, if a woman is spending lots of money on oversized mohair sweaters, Dad sneakers and barrel leg jeans, can anyone in good faith say she is paying any kind of “girl tax?”

This is why we cannot make any clear declarations about who fashion is for. Even the man repellers might occasionally enjoy a silhouette that’s more fitted and sexy, because it would be odd to be so experimental with fashion and then specifically avoid anything a man might like. In a way, that is still centering men! In fact, even the OG Man Repeller, Leandra Medine Cohen, has been playing around with fairly revealing and tight clothing, albeit in her own quirky way.

I don’t think this outfit is “for men.” But should a tight cocktail dress only be designated “not for men” once a woman has established herself as a serious man repeller, and has worn an adequate number of fuzzy shapeless coats that make her look like Grimace? I don’t even think it matters, honestly, and I don’t think we’ll ever be able to know how much certain silhouettes are for men or for women because it’s all connected anyway.

A woman’s preference for a flattering cocktail dress that makes her waist look snatched is, I’m sure, genuine. She probably feels great when she sees herself in the mirror looking that way. She may wear such a dress just to get drinks with a group of female friends, even if she knows she won’t encounter any men. She may wear such a dress and not even want men to look at her. She will probably prickle at the accusation that the dress is “for men” because strategically, it isn’t—she just wants to get drinks with her friends. But it’s no coincidence that the dress she likes on herself happens to make her look more attractive to men.

This used to confuse me, because women would say they were dressing for “themselves” despite dressing exactly the way a typical man might want them to dress. When I was younger, I assumed they were deliberately lying to save face, especially since my open admissions that I often was dressing for men tended to be viewed negatively by these women and earned me labels like “desperate,” or “pick me.” I was probably oversimplifying things by assuming they were lying—I think they genuinely felt that their fashion choices were for themselves and just weren’t thinking about how they were influenced. But at the same time, I think I was right to be irritated that conscious male-preference-adhering was seen as more worthy of condemnation than subconscious male-preference-adhering. If a man couldn’t tell the difference as to which one of us was “pandering,” why was this distinction so important? Why was “absolutely, positively, not for men” the gold standard?

Not to argue with myself here (okay, I’m doing it, I’m arguing with myself) you could say that it’s degrading to dress or groom in a way you don’t actually like because a man wants you to, or because you think men in general would like it. And, fair, I agree. But earlier on in my adult life, I realized that the way I liked to see myself was so inextricably tied to the way I imagined men would see me, that I could not fully decouple these things. Why did I like outfits that defined my waist, and did not like outfits that made me look stumpy or boxy? Given that I was not a straight man, why was the accentuation on an hourglass shape so important to “my” preference? Moreover, I knew I would dress differently if I was never planning on leaving the house, and I knew I’d dress differently if I were living on an island full of women. With that in mind, it seemed dishonest to claim that any of my dressing was really “for myself.”

But I think I might have been taking everything more literally than these other women. By saying they were dressing for “themselves,” they were saying that they weren’t dressing with a particular romantic goal in mind, and that they were wearing clothes they found aesthetically pleasing, which was likely true. If they were, in fact, completely denying that conventional male preferences influenced their own preferences, perhaps I’d say the ladies doth protest too much, but maybe that was never what they meant.

Anyway, I can understand all of that now. My issue is not women who adhere to conventional beauty standards without mulling over it enough. Who could blame them? It’s no fun to second-guess all of your choices, and I’m only doing it because I’m insane. If I have one actual pet peeve in this arena, it’s women who adhere to conventional beauty standards, benefit from them greatly in very conventional ways, but then go a step further and claim that actually, they’re doing these things to be transgressive and to subvert beauty standards. It’s kind of like getting married to a man and claiming you’re doing it to “subvert heteronormativity.” If nobody could possibly notice that you’re subverting something, you aren’t really subverting something. Subversion cannot be achieved by doing the thing, but harder and better.

The best example (and I’m sure many of you know who I’m going to say) is the model Emily Ratajkowski. Now, Ratajkowski has had a tumultuous relationship with her, frankly, genetic lottery tier body, and with men’s inappropriate and often misogynistic reactions to it. She has written extensively about experiencing sexual harassment and assault during her early years as a model. Obviously, this is bad! There is no excuse for harassing or assaulting women, no matter how naked or sexy they may be.

But her ethos does not begin and end with “Being sexy is not consent to being touched and harassed.” (On this, we would agree.) For a great deal of her career as an activist, she has framed her nakedness and sexy selfies as feminist rebellion, even though there is nothing remotely subversive about them. She’s well within her right to post thirst traps without being a “bad feminist,” (it’s fine to simply want attention, especially when that’s how you make money) but can she really claim such behavior is challenging the patriarchy? Can the patriarchy tell it’s being challenged?

Ratajkowski has even framed conventionally attractive women showing their bodies as somehow more controversial and counter-cultural than conventionally unattractive or plain women showing their bodies, even though many men want to see the former and far fewer men want to see the latter. In a Harper’s Bazaar interview in 2016, she said of a sexy selfie posted by fellow oppressed hottie Kim Kardashian:

You know, when Lena Dunham takes her clothes off, she gets flack, but it's also considered brave…But when a woman who is sexual takes off her top, it plays into something.

“Something” in this case being society’s condemnation, not an incredibly successful career as a beauty icon and arguably a setter of more unrealistic beauty standards. I have to say, Lena Dunham was perhaps not the best example here of a woman whose naked body was so beloved and accepted by society! (Thank you to Phoebe Maltz Bovy and her book, The Last Straight Woman, for alerting me to this quote.)

I’m not going to hound Ratajkowski for unwise things she said in 2016, especially because she has at least somewhat softened her stance on this and has admitted to the fact that her attractiveness has opened doors for her that other women did not get. But her statement here isn’t unusual. There are plenty of women out there who want to enjoy the benefits of adhering to conventional beauty standards, but still want the credit for “subverting” them. The problem is that to subvert conventional beauty standards, you can’t achieve that by just being really hot, all the time, and then declaring, “I subvert thee!”

But anyway, I don’t say all of this to insist that the only good way to be a woman is to adequately subvert male preferences or completely ignore them. This would quite obviously be hypocritical, but also just wrong. It’s perfectly normal to want to look attractive, and it makes sense that if you’re a heterosexual woman, wanting to look attractive would carry the subtext of “for men.” There is no reason women should be shamed for doing what every other gender and orientation can do without all the hand-wringing about “who it’s really for.”

Instead, I say all this to point out that “for men” and “for myself” is not so black and white, and therefore, it’s best not to confidently condemn one and venerate the other, or make big declarations about how trench coats are for women (what if they’re belted? What if a man has a trench coat fetish?) while push-up bras are for men (what if a woman likes how she looks in a push-up bra more than her husband does?) If a beauty or fashion ritual is onerous, painful, or just unpleasant, we don’t need to ask ourselves who it’s for, because that answer will likely be too complicated to parse. What matters is whether it’s worth the trouble (and the vast majority of the time, if it’s really that much of a pain, it won’t be.)

It’s a shame that these things have to become so complicated and anxiety-provoking for women, when men never sit down and mull over whether or not they’re lifting and shaving for women or for themselves. And I suppose that’s one of the most annoying things about conventional beauty standards—not that they’re unattainable, because most of us come to terms with that eventually—but that they’re everywhere, and they infect our own belief systems to the point that we don’t even know what “for myself” means anymore.
