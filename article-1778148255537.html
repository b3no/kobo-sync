Political commentary should be boring

Old man yells at podcast clouds

Before the New York Times culture section dropped their strange microlooting podcast episode, I’d been thinking (as one does) about urban planning in Los Angeles.

L.A. is a funny city. It sits by the coast and is home to a major port, but its downtown is surprisingly far away from the ocean, apparently because the original Spanish settlers chose a segment of the Los Angeles River that was sufficiently inland as to be safe from pirates.

Pirates are not a major concern in contemporary California, though, and the odd location lends the city a genuinely uncanny urban form.

I was wondering how history might have played out if the city had been established near where Ballona Creek joins the Pacific. In that case, downtown might be roughly where LAX is. What’s currently Marina del Rey might have been developed (as the Army Corps of Engineers once proposed) as Southern California’s main port. L.A. would be a much more conventional city, with the most desirable residential neighborhoods on the beach near downtown.

Alternatively, if the original settlement had been at the mouth of the Los Angeles River (as is the case for most coastal cities), the whole conurbation would be centered around what is today Long Beach and San Pedro, with balanced coastal development going both up toward Santa Monica and down to Newport Beach.

This is the kind of stuff I like to think about. I loved SimCity as a kid, and if contemporary Los Angeles were a saved game of mine, I would delete it and start over again with the same map.

Which put me in the mind of a Bratmobile song that I loved in high school “Polaroid Baby” off their 1993 album, “Pottymouth.” The lyrics consist of the repeated incantation “burn to the fucking ground L.A. / whitey’s gonna pay / whitey’s gonna pay.” The text is racial hatred, but the eerie tone of the singing sounds bored and the voice clearly belongs to a young white woman. I find the effect sort of entrancing (my 11-year-old says it’s “creepy”).

I also think the song is basically funny even though the subject matter is incredibly serious.

They’re singing, of course, not about my urban planning fantasies, but about the riots that came in the wake of the acquittal of the L.A.P.D. officers who beat Rodney King.

Needless to say, I do not in fact think that rioting is a constructive, useful, or moral political response, even to a genuinely outrageous¹ turn of events. And yet I happily consumed pro-rioting and anti-police music, even at a time when the overall tenor of politics was far more culturally conservative. I just felt there was a vast difference between politics-flavored entertainment and time spent actually learning about political issues by reading The Economist or The Nation.

But a signature development of our time is that the lines that traditionally separated these things are blurred, and it’s increasingly difficult for anyone to tell what’s meant to be taken seriously and what’s just people mouthing off.

Oh, you thought this was going to be a classic Slow Boring piece about urban planning? Sorry kids, not today.

When panics were moral

Bratmobile and several of the other Olympia-based riot grrrl bands were socially and professionally associated with D.C.-based hardcore bands like Nation of Ulysses, who had an album called “13-Point Program to Destroy America.”

To the best of my knowledge, Ian Svenonius and the other guys in that band are sincerely left-wing but do not actually want to destroy America.

This stuff is all at least a little bit tongue in cheek, an effort to ironically reflect back the moral panics of the mainstream culture. These punk bands were generally too obscure to be the subject of moral panics, but this was a time when a lot of mainstream cultural and political figures were vocally unhappy about heavy metal and especially rap music.

In more recent years, Michael Hobbes pioneered the use of “moral panic” to describe basically any journalistic trend he disapproves of.

But I think the critical element of a proper moral panic is that the thing it’s panicking about is genuinely (and usually uncontroversially) immoral.

The people panicking about gangsta rap were saying that the attitudes toward women, drug dealing, and violence expressed in the songs were bad. And that is correct! I don’t think anyone is going to stand here and say that Dr. Dre lyrics are a fantastic guide to living an upright and moral life. The nature of the panic was that old squares were freaking out about these songs, and were setting themselves up against believers in freedom and fun and the inherent rebelliousness of youth and the integrity of art.

In retrospect, the panickers seem pretty ridiculous.

We know now that crime dropped enormously over the course of the 1990s, and that there was no rising generation of teens inspired into a life of violence. Dre became a wealthy headphones entrepreneur. Snoop Dogg is an NBC Olympics correspondent and friends with Martha Stewart. Ice-T, of the widely-criticized-at-the-time 1992 song “Cop Killer,” became one of America’s longest-running television-cop heroes.

On the other hand, even though the Tipper Gore side of the argument seems to have been discredited by events, in another sense I feel like the culture has shifted in her direction.

Contemporary sensibilities are much more direct, literal, and moralistic about culture. Celebrities have more control over their public image than ever before, but it also seems more important to audiences to believe that artists are thoroughly good people upholding their values. People got very upset that Taylor Swift didn’t weigh in on the 2016 election, fret over Chris Pratt and/or Sydney Sweeney possibly being Republicans, and worry that “Euphoria” characters are behaving badly. There’s generally a sense that both performers and the roles that they portray ought to be upstanding and correct, and if they’re not, that should be very clearly stated in the work itself.

As an old person, Bratmobile is a refreshing throwback in this regard. Whatever the real person is like, the Charli XCX character portrayed in songs is not a role model for contemporary youth. She seems like somebody who might steal for fun and not bother to rationalize it in political terms. But that kind of work — with multiple levels of irony and humor and deliberate transgressiveness — is the exception rather than the rule.

Oh the irony

Rage Against the Machine’s 1999 album “The Battle of Los Angeles” is another reference to the Rodney King riots. There was a verbal conceit in far-left circles at the time that these were not “riots” at all but a genuine act of revolutionary combat.

This is both a factually inaccurate description of what occurred and also I think a rather obviously silly vision of possible political change in the United States.

Achieving political change through the electoral process is hard. But it’s much easier to win elections in a progressive jurisdiction like Los Angeles than it is to win pitched battles with the police and National Guard. There’s no rational basis for specifically encouraging this as a preferred mode of engagement. At the time, I greatly enjoyed Rage’s music — in part, I think, because of a layer of ironic distance. It was fun to talk about Subcomandante Marcos and reappropriate Ronald Reagan’s Evil Empire rhetoric, but these guys were playing sold out shows at the Worcester Centrum, not actually organizing a guerrilla uprising.

But the band really did make serious efforts on behalf of Ralph Nader’s destructive 2000 presidential campaign.

That indicates perhaps more political seriousness than I was originally inclined to attribute to them but, on another level, it confirms the idea that these guys are not genuinely interested in violent revolutionary politics.

The whole question of the extent to which far-left political music is just a gag is explicitly addressed in Against Me’s 2002 banger “Baby, I’m an Anarchist,” where Laura Jane Grace sings “you thought I was only joking / when I was screaming ‘kill whitey!’ / at the top of my lungs / at the cops in their cars / and the men in their suits.” And yet again, we are left with the fact that the singer and author of the song and most of the band’s fans are in fact white and, as far as I know, not actually committing acts of political violence.

There is something about the anarchist politics that is sincere, notably the rejection of meliorism and electoralism and the strong belief in individualism.

The most admirable part of the song is its reference to the anarchist/Stalinist split during the Spanish Civil War: “you fought for Stalin / I fought for freedom.” But then the part of me that’s more interested in political theory than punk rock wants to say that the actual politics of human freedom are tedious liberalism, not the politics of smashing windows and throwing molotov cocktails.

And now you do what they told ya

Part of what’s fun about this stuff is that young people are spending time figuring out who they want to be and what they’re all about. When you’re a little kid you are, if properly parented, taught that it’s a good idea to follow the rules and listen to your teachers and other authority figures.

And then you start to discover that these authority figures are themselves of decidedly mixed quality. Teachers say things that aren’t true. Your parents screw up. Doctors make diagnostic errors. The police arrest the wrong guy.

Major elements of received wisdom are contested and contestable. Lots of people think your parents’ political opinions are wrong, and those people might be correct. There are whole books out there describing radically different ways to arrange the basic functions of society. Lots of the people we’re taught to see as heroic figures were understood by their contemporaries to be dangerous radicals. Others were complicit in monstrous crimes.

Under the circumstances, who² wouldn’t want to rock out and chant, “Fuck you, I won’t do what you tell me”?

But this process of trying to understand the world shouldn’t end in your teens or 20s. And I think continued reflection often leads to the recognition that there is something fundamentally unworkable about this brand of liberatory politics.

It is true that the people in authority are a lot more flawed than they would like you to believe, but it’s also the case that a world without rules and order is not going to be a utopia — it’s going to be a place where the strong systematically exploit the weak.

Nonetheless, in a world where the lines between different forms of content have become much blurrier than they were when I was a kid, I’m not totally sure I can tell when I’m being the wise elder who can correct youthful excess and when I’m being the old fogey who just doesn’t get the joke.

I also think that downstream impacts can be hard to predict. The much-feared collapse of public morality in the face of the onslaught of gangsta rap never happened. But I do think the wave of anti-police sentiment that swept American progressive politics five or 10 years ago was driven in part by a cohort of white millennials whose ideas about African American public opinion came from listening to hip-hop rather than church ladies or old heads at the barbershop.

My real concern, though, is that the dynamics of the internet are causing us to lose context.

When I was in college, I read Randall Kennedy’s book “Race, Crime, and the Law,” a nuanced and thoughtful treatment of issues ranging from racial profiling, biased juries, and police brutality to underinvestment in securing Black property and lives from criminals. Kennedy makes the point that in the aggregate, the latter form of bias has been the dominant factor in American history.

I have no idea how you’d turn those ideas into a song, though, whereas “Fuck Tha Police” is a banger.

The way a traditional news bundle worked was that the news coverage was earnest and boring. Its function was to make subscribing feel like a worthy civic endeavor and to suggest to advertisers a high-toned product they wanted to associate their brand with.

The fun parts of the bundle were just totally different: cartoons and astrology and garrulous old sports columnists and gossip.

In today’s world, where everything more or less stands on its own, you can try to do something serious for a niche audience (thanks, as always, to our paid members). But anything meant to reach the masses is supposed to be fun and entertaining in a way that strongly incentivizes irresponsible takes. There is some reason to think that the rise of frontier large language models as a source of political information will counteract this trend. But for now, we’re living in a world where political edu-tainment is on the rise, and it’s bad.
