Congress waits, cities experiment

Trump leaves a bipartisan housing bill unsigned while cities take matters into their own hands.

Halina Bennet
Jul 1
∙
Paid
 
READ IN APP
 

More than a week after it passed Congress, President Donald Trump has still not signed the 21st Century ROAD to Housing Act. His stated reason is that the Senate has not yet acted on the SAVE America Act, a bill he has made a priority that would require proof of citizenship and photo ID for voter registration.

On Monday, Trump dismissed the housing bill as a “big yawn,” grumbling that it was “very bipartisan.”

“Nobody knows more about housing in the history of the presidency,” he told USA Today. “Nobody did well like me in housing. I made a lot of money with housing. But when I look at that bill, it’s a bill. But when I look at the SAVE America Act, it’s about saving America.”

House Speaker Mike Johnson spent the back half of last week trying to talk him down. That culminated in a two-hour Oval Office meeting Monday, after which Johnson formally transmitted the bill to the White House. If Trump neither signs nor vetoes it, the bill automatically becomes law after 10 days, excluding Sundays. Johnson has been telling reporters flatly that a veto isn’t coming.

“He won’t veto the bill. We already know that,” Johnson told USA Today. “He’ll either allow it to just go into law, or he’ll put his signature on it and take partial ownership, and I hope he does the latter.”

It’s a strange coda for a bill that both parties spent months negotiating and that Trump himself had previously endorsed. Practically speaking, the final outcome is unlikely to change. Unless Trump reverses course and vetoes it, the ROAD to Housing Act will become law by July 10 whether or not he signs it. But the episode is a sobering reminder of how ineffective Washington under Trump has become, even on the rare occasions when both parties are aligned in Congress.

Seattle bets on government-owned housing for the middle class

Seattle offered the week’s most interesting experiment in how to build affordable housing for low- and middle-income renters. The Seattle Social Housing Developer, a city-created agency tasked with building and buying social housing, closed this month on its first purchase, an eight-story, 150-unit Belltown property it bought from a private owner for $61 million, CNN reports.

The pitch is explicitly modeled on Vienna, where roughly half of residents live in some form of government-subsidized housing that spans a wide range of incomes. Seattle’s target is households making too much to qualify for a housing lottery but too little to comfortably afford a market-rate unit near Amazon’s headquarters.

Existing market-rate tenants in the building are guaranteed a two-year rent freeze and no evictions; as units turn over, they’ll be filled through an income-restricted lottery (up to 50 percent of area median income, which is $65,000 for a two-person household). The effort is funded by a voter-approved payroll tax on compensation above $1 million per employee. The Seattle Social Housing Developer’s stated goal is to acquire or build more than 1,500 units over the next five years. That is a small slice of Seattle’s housing stock, but it will be a useful test of whether the public sector can operate rental housing at scale without running into typical public-housing dysfunctions.

New York’s budget fight ends in a CityFHEPS truce

Mayor Zohran Mamdani closed his first city budget with a $125 billion deal that resolves a standoff over New York’s rental-assistance voucher program, City Fighting Homelessness and Eviction Prevention Supplement (CityFHEPS).

Mamdani campaigned on expanding CityFHEPS and dropping the legal fight over a 2023 City Council package that would broaden eligibility. Once in office, he did neither, proposing instead to cut $500 million from the program.

The Council, led by Speaker Julie Menin and Housing Chair Pierina Sanchez, refused to pass the budget without an agreement to expand rental assistance. The final deal, which pushed the budget through on Tuesday, commits $175 million in fiscal year 2027 and baselines another $125 million starting in fiscal year 2028 for a new, capped rental-assistance program administered by the N.Y.C. Department of Housing Preservation and Development that could reach up to 30,000 additional New Yorkers facing eviction or homelessness, according to the Council.

In exchange, Mamdani will drop the city’s legal challenge to the 2023 eligibility expansion once the Council passes the enabling legislation. CityFHEPS costs the city roughly $54 a day per family, versus up to $270 a day to shelter that same family — a fact Menin and advocates leaned on hard.

Separately, the Mamdani administration this week rolled out a set of supply-side tools aimed at homeowners in historic districts. A new interactive map, an accessory-dwelling-unit fact sheet, and a dedicated Landmarks Preservation Commission team are meant to help homeowners use the accessory-dwelling-unit provisions legalized under 2024’s City of Yes zoning reform. The rollout also includes new guidance making it easier for landmarked buildings to sell off unused development rights to fund housing nearby.

It is a small-bore complement to the big fights over vouchers and public housing, but it is also a sign that the administration’s Block by Block housing plan intends to squeeze density out of every corner of the city, including the ones protected by historic-preservation law.

Cities scramble as tech blows up California’s coastal markets

Governor Gavin Newsom signed a balanced state budget that he says will leave California with a zero-dollar deficit. The deal still finds nearly $1.7 billion for housing and homelessness, including $900 million in grants to help local governments address homelessness and $500 million in state low-income housing tax credits for 2027. Separately, an $11.25 billion affordable-housing bond (Senate Bill 417) is headed to the November ballot.

Meanwhile, Southern California localities are attempting to halt deepening affordability crises:

  • The old World Trade Center tower in downtown Los Angeles is being converted into 512 units of deed-restricted affordable housing, with rents starting around $937 a month. The conversion is part of a roughly $200 million partnership between Jamison Services and Kennedy Wilson. It is the first project in a partnership aiming for 4,000 affordable units citywide via adaptive reuse and new construction, helped by Mayor Karen Bass’s expansion of the city’s adaptive-reuse ordinance.

  • The San Diego City Council created a new $8.5 million Affordable Housing Preservation Fund, administered by the San Diego Housing Commission. Its purpose is to buy up “naturally occurring” affordable housing before it converts to market rate, the San Diego Union-Tribune reports. This is a smaller-scale, more targeted version of what Seattle is attempting.

  • SpaceX’s record-breaking $75 billion I.P.O. this month minted thousands of new millionaires among current and former employees, with real estate agents already fielding calls on multi-million-dollar properties in Manhattan Beach and Redondo Beach, the Los Angeles Times reports. Analysts expect the real buying surge to hit once stock lockups expire around December. Once again, we are reminded that tech wealth shocks remain one of the more reliable ways to blow up a coastal housing market.

Manufactured housing gets a policy push in three states

In Florida, Jacksonville Mayor Donna Deegan and Housing and Urban Development officials signed a memorandum of understanding to launch the “Jacksonville Starter Home Initiative,” which will use five out of 17.5 acres of city-owned land in Northwest Jacksonville to install manufactured homes — up to 10 per acre — aimed at buyers making between 80 and 120 percent of area median income. HUD officials called it a potential national model, with one HUD deputy noting that manufactured housing is already one of the country’s largest sources of affordable single-family homes that do not rely on subsidies, the Jacksonville Daily Record reports.

In Oklahoma, five tribal entities received a combined $1.6 million in housing grants from the Federal Home Loan Bank’s Native American Housing Initiatives program. The grants will support a range of efforts, including tiny-home construction, down-payment assistance, and rental aid for tribal elders and veterans.

And in Maine, lawmakers wrapped up what is likely the most aggressive state-level crackdown yet on private-equity ownership of manufactured-housing communities. A new law (LD 2149/SP 867) builds on a 2025 package that gives residents right of first refusal when their community goes up for sale, imposes a per-lot fee of $10,000 on purchasers with net worth over $50 million, and creates tax deductions worth up to $750,000 for owners who sell to resident cooperatives or municipal housing authorities instead of institutional buyers.

The District of Columbia’s stadium-adjacent housing plan

D.C.’s Office of Planning released a draft master plan for the 180-acre Robert F. Kennedy Campus, the future site of the Washington Commanders’ new stadium. The plan calls for 5,500 to 6,500 housing units, with at least 30 percent designated affordable. Public comment runs through August 14, with the D.C. Council expected to take up the plan this fall and, if all goes well, adopt it by the end of the year.
