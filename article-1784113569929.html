No, 2.8 million Americans are not "screaming and vomiting"

The junk marijuana stat that fooled The New York Times

Eli Richman
Jul 15
∙
Paid
 
READ IN APP
 

The Washington Post, The New York Times, and many other news outlets want you to believe that 2.8 million people are regularly screaming and throwing up because they smoke too much weed. But, unfortunately, each of these outlets has been fooled by the same faulty study.

Last Friday, the Post ran an article about cannabis hyperemesis syndrome (CHS), a rarely diagnosed condition that causes heavy weed smokers severe nausea. The disease regularly presents with patients throwing up multiple times a day and feeling relief only when taking hot showers.

CHS is sometimes called “scromiting” (screaming and vomiting), mostly in a sensationalist effort to give the problem a more memorable and alarming name, but partially because “CHS” does a bad job encapsulating how debilitating this condition can be.

The Post uncritically reported that “about 2.75 million Americans experience CHS each year.” It also speculated that this figure may be an underestimate since “CHS can be hard to recognize.”

Certainly sounds alarming. That would mean almost 1% of Americans are smoking so much weed that they have become unable to lead regular lives.

I’m open to believing that marijuana legalization has come with some unintended consequences. I’m even willing to believe the drug is causing severe nausea in some heavy weed smokers. What I’m not open to believing is that nearly 3 million American stoners are effectively unable to work because they can’t stop vomiting.

Share

In February, The New York Times’ editorial board published a piece reconsidering the paper’s past support for marijuana legalization and advocating for new federal regulations and taxes. It led its section on the dangers of heavy weed use by noting that “Each year, nearly 2.8 million people in the United States suffer from cannabinoid hyperemesis syndrome, which causes severe vomiting and stomach pain.”

Multiple other outlets have also cited the same figure.

The problem? This figure is built upon a single 2018 paper with a poorly sampled study group and an outrageously broad definition of CHS.

In a sample gathered by research associates walking through the emergency department of a single public hospital and surveying whoever they saw, researchers ultimately approached 2,127 patients. They did bedside interviews with patients who happened to be in this hospital, and, after refusals and exclusions, they found a group of just 155 patients who admitted to smoking marijuana 20 or more days per month. To make matters even worse for this sample: This was all taking place in a location where weed was illegal. Of those 155 patients, 51 met the researchers’ criteria for CHS.

At this point, the researchers took this percentage (32.9% of frequent cannabis users) and concluded that “If this is extractable to the general population, approximately 2.75 million (2.13–3.38 million) Americans may suffer annually from a phenomenon similar to CHS.”

Pretty big “if”!

To their credit, the researchers did acknowledge their study’s limitations: “The sample size of this survey cohort was relatively small and consisted only of patients presenting to our one urban, public ED, decreasing the generalizability of the results to the overall population,” they wrote.

“It is likely that the number of daily users is under-reported due to either stigma or concern of legal repercussions,” they added.

However, those acknowledgements are paywalled, while the abstract (which is the only thing most people, including reporters and, apparently, Times and Post fact-checkers, read) prominently reported the figure.

Still, the finding that scromiting affects nearly one-third of heavy marijuana users who attended the ED is still pretty concerning, right? Except that’s not what the researchers found.

The study abandoned previously employed definitions for CHS because they were too restrictive. Such definitions “described only those cases severe enough to seek care,” the authors wrote, meaning the existing criteria are “likely missing more mild versions of the phenomenon.”

So, the researchers decided to focus on the “relieved by hot showers” part of the diagnosis instead of the “severe nausea” part. They simply listed 11 treatments to relieve nausea and asked respondents how effective each was on a scale of 1 to 10. Anyone who ranked hot showers at 5 or higher was defined as having CHS.

That’s it. No, you’re not missing something. The patients defined as scromiters did not even need to have problems with vomiting frequently — in fact, the researchers specifically excluded patients visiting the ED for nausea, vomiting, or stomach pain. The patients in the “CHS group” only needed to smoke a lot of weed and self-report finding hot showers a soothing relief for whenever they happened to feel nauseated.

This definition is so broad that it could easily include lots of people who don’t suffer from nausea on a regular basis at all. You could be “vomit free since ‘93” and just enjoy taking hot showers when you get the stomach flu, and these researchers would still define you as having CHS — as long as you also happen to smoke weed.

The researchers acknowledged this limitation beneath the paywall as well, before running with the dramatic number in the abstract. “[F]ollowing either … stricter proposed criteria would have resulted in a smaller estimate of prevalence,” they wrote.

Media abhors a data vacuum

Scromiting isn’t the only side effect of marijuana legalization making the rounds: CNN has run piece after piece after piece banging the drum about marijuana’s risk of causing chromosomal abnormalities or birth defects. Meanwhile, Fox is very, very concerned about the impact high-potency cannabis can have on mental health.

The problem is that for many years, marijuana was categorized as a Schedule I controlled substance, which made it extremely difficult to conduct research on the side effects of marijuana use.

But that problem is particularly acute for CHS, given its clinical criteria, which are not really workable for researchers. CHS is so new and poorly understood that it didn’t even get its own diagnostic code until last October.

In that kind of an information vacuum, it’s easy to see how this authoritative-sounding statistic could quickly become the definitive figure on the issue:

  1. 2018: NYU researchers publish their article estimating 2.75 million Americans could be suffering from scromiting. They begin promoting this stat to the media: The lead researcher says it “blew our socks off.”

  2. 2019 to 2023: The stat makes the rounds in academic literature, appearing in several notable journals, albeit often with caveats like “Figures on [CHS’] prevalence are yet unclear, ranging from 350,000 in the USA to an extrapolated estimate of 2.75 million based on data from one emergency department survey in New York City.”

  3. 2024 to 2025: Having received some degree of legitimization in academic media, the stat makes the jump to mainstream media. JAMA publishes a patient education page about CHS that cited the stat, and it also appears in a report in the Times. The Times compounds the faulty stat, using the researchers’ estimate of scromiting affecting 32.9% of heavy weed users and extant estimates of heavy weed use to conclude that around 6 million Americans could be suffering from the condition.

  4. 2026: The Times leans on the statistic to reconsider its stance toward marijuana legalization. The stat also appears in The Hill and, most recently, the Post.

Once a stat makes its way into conventional wisdom like this, people begin forming beliefs and policy designs around it. We can already see mainstream outlets like the Times and media commentators across the political spectrum expressing hesitancy about the merits of legalization.

Like most red-blooded millennials, I supported weed legalization (and still do).

I’m open to believing that marijuana legalization has come with some unintended consequences. But it’s incredibly important to be clear about what those consequences are and how bad they are.

It’s not enough to know that a legal framework could hurt someone, somewhere. In order to make informed policy decisions, we need good evidence on how bad the problem is and how it compares to alternatives.

Some evidence is now starting to emerge: New studies are coming out every day on weed’s health risks with stronger sample sizes and better methodologies.

Those are the types of studies we will need to learn more about the true risks and benefits of weed. And they’re the types of studies that would become more difficult if we revert to our previous zero-tolerance approach to marijuana.

That’s how, before you know it, the hot water preferences of 150-odd stoners in a single hospital come to define the severity of a country’s marijuana problem in the leading papers of record.
