If you’ve seen Moneyball (or read the book), you’re probably familiar with the concept of Wins Above Replacement (WAR). In baseball, WAR tells you how much value a player adds to their team by quantifying how many “wins” the player contributes over a generic replacement-level player at the same position.

As part of our work at Split Ticket, we’ve spent years calculating WAR for political candidates. Today, we’re proud to introduce our WAR model — exclusively here at The Argument — for the 2026 midterm elections.

Share

Here’s how we calculate each score: For every single contested two-party race, our model compares the final result to the expected result of a matchup between a generic Democrat and generic Republican in the same district. The difference between the actual result and the generic baseline tells you whether the Republican or the Democrat in the race was stronger — and by how much.

For instance, let’s consider Mississippi’s U.S. Senate race, in which Democrat Scott Colom narrowly prevailed over incumbent Republican Cindy Hyde-Smith. Because baseline voting patterns in Mississippi are so Republican to begin with, a “generic” matchup between an incumbent Republican and a Democratic challenger probably would have produced something like a 6-point Republican win — even in a blue wave year like 2026.

Instead, Colom won by roughly 1 point. Thus, the WAR for that race is D+6.6 — meaning, in our estimation, that Colom did 6.6 points better against Hyde-Smith than you would expect a standard Democrat to do against a standard Republican incumbent.

A model like this is incredibly hard to make, especially in a midterm year. Many basic analyses simply compare 2026 results to 2024 presidential margins. But a Democrat outperforming Kamala Harris by 30 points in Houston isn’t nearly as impressive as the very same 30-point overperformance in rural Mississippi would be.¹ That’s because you have to take into account factors like the demographics of the seat, incumbency and outside party support, and the statewide environment.

This is the most complex and comprehensive modeling exercise we’ve done yet. Let’s dive into the findings.

Do you remember when, in the wake of the 2020 elections, the only topic anyone could talk about was the looming Senate disaster for Democrats? The worry was this: Following Donald Trump’s realignment of the Republican coalition, a full 62 Senate seats were in states to the right of the country, and Democrats sat at just 50 with a worrying pair of Senate maps to come in 2022 and 2024.

Today, the fear looks quaint: Now that the dust has settled, the party controls 56 Senate seats, and there is a real (if remote) chance of assembling a filibuster-proof 60-seat majority after 2028.

Here’s what I want to stress: This wasn’t an accident, and it isn’t normal. Our WAR modeling shows that Democrats won four extra seats thanks to candidate quality advantages in Kansas, Mississippi, Alaska, and South Carolina. (We know that because the WAR scores in those states are larger than the margins of victory.) By contrast, the only Senate seat they lost that they (maybe) should have won is Florida.

But what stands out to me is just how remarkably strong Democratic candidates were across the board,² even in races they were “expected” to win.

The ability to flip unfriendly seats on the fundamentals is one thing. Maine, for instance, was probably always going to go blue in a D+15 national environment. But it’s quite another to put up the margins of 5+ points that Jon Ossoff, Roy Cooper, and James Talarico netted in states won by Trump in 2024. That’s because these were all considerably above-average candidates.

All of these candidates also carried their downballot House candidates to victory. It’s not as if most of the Democratic House candidates in Texas or North Carolina were especially strong; however, that didn’t matter as much, because Talarico and Cooper won by so much that their coattails carried candidates who were never expected to win (and probably wouldn’t have otherwise) across the line.

Large Senate majorities are built on winning races you should win, while also flipping a few races you shouldn’t. In each of the last few cycles, Democrats have watched Republicans blow seat after seat by nominating candidates who no serious political party would have considered — think Ken Paxton in Texas and Darline Graham in South Carolina — and have largely taken advantage of it.

The final result is this: Come January, Democrats will hold 18 Senate seats³ in states Trump won in 2024. Republicans, meanwhile, will hold zero in states carried by Kamala Harris.

Without moderates, Democrats would likely have won 4 fewer Senate seats

With a larger dataset and a fuller set of results to analyze, it remains true that moderate Democratic politicians overperformed progressives. However, the advantage that the moderate Blue Dog caucus held over more left-leaning Democratic groups is smaller than it has ever been, and the ideological gaps in performance among Democrats have shrunk — two conclusions independently substantiated by both Nate Cohn of The New York Times and Michael Caley of Expecting Goals.

For Republicans, it’s also true that members affiliated with moderate groups had a smaller advantage than they did in previous cycles.

But while some have taken the above chart to demonstrate a negligible moderation advantage for Republicans, I don’t think that’s the right way to read it. Instead, the right conclusion to draw from the chart above is that there are no Republican moderates left.

Virtually none of the candidates belonging to the Republican Governance Group or the Main Street Caucus have a record of substantively breaking with Trump. It would be unreasonable to expect voters to give them credit for joining a more “moderate” group in name when their voting record for this Congress is virtually identical to that of the Freedom Caucus flamethrowers.

That’s why I think it would be incorrect to look at the midterms and say that “moderation doesn’t matter,” even though it’s clear that ideology played less of a role in these elections than it has in the past.

Scroll through the list of races with the highest WAR, and a common theme emerges: Most of the incumbents and winners who did well were still pretty moderate, while most of the ones who did badly were on the fringe of the ideological spectrum. Three of the top four Republican incumbents or winners from this cycle were Brian Fitzpatrick, Mike Lawler, and Susan Collins. All of these are moderate Republicans from Harris-won seats, and even though they all lost, they each overperformed by more than 8 points.

The underperformance penalty at the ideological extremes holds regardless of party: Democrats like Pramila Jayapal and Ilhan Omar underperformed a lot, as did Republicans like Anthony Constantino, Tom Cotton, Chuck Gray, and Lauren Boebert.

At a macro level, it is clear that moderation had less of an impact than it has ever played. At a micro level, though, moderate politicians continue to have an edge. Without that edge, Democrats would probably be looking at four fewer seats in the Senate.

What does this tell us for 2028?

This election gave rise to perhaps the most interesting type of Democratic candidate I’ve seen in recent times: the Berniecrat. This type makes it a point to de-emphasize (or even outright moderate on) cultural issues and immigration, while also running as an unabashed progressive on economic matters.

Much has been made of James Talarico’s pivots to the center on matters like energy, immigration, and gender. But Talarico’s rhetoric was unabashedly and unapologetically progressive (or outright populist) on many economic matters. In fact, Talarico’s campaign took a more progressive, populist turn on economics than virtually anyone could have thought possible for a victorious Texas campaign, whether on data centers, billionaires, wages, or taxes — including a denunciation of trickle-down economics as a “theft.”

This was not an easy gamble to take in a state like Texas — even against an opponent as historically weak as the scandal-plagued Ken Paxton. It’s a different approach than what prior Democrats took in the state: Both Beto O’Rourke and Colin Allred posted sizable overperformances in their races, but neither of them sounded this economically confrontational in their campaigns. Yet, in the end, Talarico won by a whopping 12 points — and overperformed by 5.

Similarly, in central California, Randy Villegas seems to have considerably overperformed against a very strong Republican candidate; incumbent David Valadao has historically held some of the highest WAR scores of any Republican candidate in modern memory.

It didn’t matter: Though Villegas ran on a significantly more left-wing economic platform — one that scared establishment Democrats enough to actively intervene against him in the primary — Villegas ended up posting a preliminary⁴ WAR of D+4.8, which is several points better than any candidate has ever managed against Valadao. If there was a progressive penalty to be paid here, it’s really hard to see it in the initial results.

I could go on: Sam Forstag, a “Berniecrat” progressive who was also endorsed by the Blue Dogs, put up a very strong performance in his 3-point win in a Montana district that Trump won by 12, posting a WAR of D+7. Tom Perriello, the ex-congressman who posted a D+2 WAR in a close loss of a very red seat, was a darling of the progressive establishment. And Chris Deluzio, the Pennsylvania congressman openly mulling a challenge against incumbent Sen. John Fetterman from the left, has now posted three straight cycles of overperformance.

Though these candidates might not be left-wing according to everyone’s standards, it wouldn’t be accurate to label any of them as “moderates” in the traditional sense of the word either. Their campaigns didn’t try to distance the candidates from the progressive positions espoused by the Democratic Party at large. They didn’t engage in left-punching in the way that more traditional moderate House Democrats like Tom Suozzi, Hillary Scholten, or Josh Gottheimer have made a point of doing.

WAR isn’t simply a metric I advise people to “plug and play” with — in other words, a high WAR doesn’t automatically mean you should take a shot at higher office. The more conventional moderate, left-punching strategy of folks like Jared Golden and Marie Gluesenkamp Perez plays very well in smaller electorates and quirky districts (and earns them high WARs). But it has little chance of scaling up in a higher-profile election, like for a Senate or presidential run. That’s because these candidates almost certainly wouldn’t be able to get out of a highly contested primary.⁵

The “Berniecrat” is basically the only flavor of Democratic candidate I have seen who can combine substantial electability benefits with the types of stances preferred by the wider Democratic electorate, which is more liberal than ever before. And even the more successful, high-profile campaigns pulled from this playbook: Jon Ossoff attracted considerable scrutiny for his more conservative votes (including for the Republicans’ Laken Riley Act on immigration) early in the cycle. But his actual campaign rhetoric was extremely economically populist, with a focus on marrying affordability with anti-elite messaging.

With an incumbent Republican president sitting at 37% approval, Democrats might be favored to win the presidency regardless of who they nominate. But the magnitude of the win matters; a good environment could see them hit 60 Senate seats and a filibuster-proof Senate majority, while a poor one could still make the House a dogfight. To the extent that Democrats want to find lessons for the future, the Berniecrats may provide useful clues.
