The death penalty is a gender issue

When polled on crime, women are generally less punitive than men — more supportive of rehabilitation and more open to redemption. On capital punishment, specifically, there is a longstanding gender gap, with men consistently more supportive than women.

This pattern aligns with preexisting intuitions around gendered behavior: the empathetic woman who pities the marginalized and misunderstood and the retributive man, steadfast and unyielding.

But there is something not quite right about the simple notion that men are tough on crime.

In our survey of 1,516 registered voters fielded from April 20 to 23, the conventional gender gap held up when the death penalty was abstract. Asked whether they support the death penalty for “serious crimes,” men were 11 percentage points more supportive than women. But when we asked about specific crimes, a different picture emerged.

Sixty-nine percent of female respondents agreed that the death penalty should be an option for child sexual abuse. Only 61% of men said the same, although 72% of them were in favor of capital punishment for “acts of terrorism.”

The gender gap also reverses for rape, with 56% of women and 50% of men supporting capital punishment as an option. Notably, the reversal is driven by men becoming less punitive, not women becoming that much more punitive: Compared to “serious crimes,” there is a four-percentage-point increase among women and a 13-percentage-point drop among men.

Punitiveness isn’t a stable trait. The context that seems to matter most is who voters picture as the victim and who they imagine being executed.

Since 2008, when the Supreme Court ruled 5-4 that capital punishment should be reserved only for cases that resulted in someone’s death, this debate has largely been moot.¹ But a growing number of states are seeking to overthrow that consensus.

In 2023, Florida passed a bill that allows for the death penalty for the aggravated sexual battery of a child under 12, even when the victim doesn’t die. The lawmakers’ goal was to set up the circumstances to overturn Supreme Court precedent. Decisions that split the court 5-4 are always fragile, but the court has lurched right since 2008, leaving this consensus particularly vulnerable to attack.

Tennessee, Arkansas, Idaho, Oklahoma, and Alabama have all passed similar laws. The Trump administration has signaled support for this growing movement to reinstate the death penalty for non-capital crimes.

Tough on whom?

The simplest explanation for why gendered support for the death penalty varies by crime is that men and women’s ability to imagine themselves as victims also varies by crime.

Women are considerably more likely to face sexual violence. According to the CDC, roughly 1 in 5 American women experience attempted or completed rape in their lifetime; for American men, the rate is 1 in 31. We also know that around 82% of reported child sexual abuse survivors are female (although male victimization is presumably undercounted due to stigma).

Conversely, men are more likely to be victims of the crimes for which they are more likely to support capital punishment. In the U.S., men are three times more likely to be murdered than women. In 2024, the FBI reported 12,144 male and 3,538 female victims of homicide.

The numbers are less stark on terrorism, but because men are disproportionately represented in roles with high exposure, such as military, law enforcement, and security, more men are killed in direct attacks on government buildings or public spaces.

The flip side of the gender-of-victim story is, of course, the gender-of-offendant. A well-documented bias in jurors is “similarity leniency,” or the tendency for people to have more sympathy with defendants who share their gender, race, and class.

Men are considerably more likely to be the perpetrators of all violent crime: murder, terrorism, and rape. Yet high levels of anxiety about false allegations may make men particularly wary of supporting the death penalty for sex crimes.

In our February poll, more than half of male respondents thought false reports of sexual misconduct were either very or somewhat common. While there is little evidence to support this concern, it presumably contributes to the comparatively low levels of male punitiveness for rape and child sexual abuse measured against other crimes.

The death penalty accounts for less than one-tenth of 1% of total prison sentences in the United States. It is an exceptionally rare sentence.

Yet opinion polling on capital punishment is a useful temperature check on broader punitiveness and a revealing proxy for underlying attitudes toward crime. According to Gallup, polling support for the death penalty has been on a steady decline since the 1990s (likely tracking declines in the crime rate).

The carceral feminist electorate

There’s an enduring tension on the left between feminist demands for accountability for sex crimes and skepticism of incarceration and capital punishment generally. Given our polling, it seems that a slim majority of women are comfortable resolving that tension toward punishment. Our sample suggests that this holds across race, with 60% of nonwhite women being in favor of the death penalty as an option in cases of rape.

It is sometimes argued that the punitive impulses within feminism — carceral feminism — is a top-down imposition by feminist organizations that allied with the police lobbies and tough-on-crime Democrats in the 1980s and 90s.²

This account argues that this was a bad trade for women: Harsh punishment failed to reduce sexual violence, and criminalization has fallen hardest on the poor.

However true that is, our polling complicates this top-down narrative. The punitive position on sex crimes is the median view of women voters across the nation. Skepticism of capital punishment in these cases belongs to a minority.

Of course, it’s possible that if the debate over reinstituting the death penalty gets even more specific, views will shift. Justice Kennedy’s 2008 majority opinion was in large part motivated by the concern that victims would be less likely to speak out if they feared their abuser — in most cases a member of their own family — could be put to death.

But either way, the left must contend with the fact that despite half a century of feminist anti-carceral advocacy, large numbers of American women would like the state to kill the people who hurt them.
