Welcome to The Weekly Scroll!

The Weekly Scroll is a once-a-week roundup of the most interesting, strangest, and funniest stories from around the social internet.

This week: the biggest media release of all time, the video games hijacking our brains, more Pangram denialism, and the rise of Nick Fuentes.

GTA VI cometh

Grand Theft Auto, the video game series where you can commit enough crimes to warrant a trial at The Hague and then evade capture by changing your shirt, released its latest edition this week. Why make note of a single game’s release? Because Grand Theft Auto VI may be, without exaggeration, the biggest pop culture event of the decade.

Video games are still sometimes treated as a niche category, a disreputable hobby obsessed over by teenagers with Cheetos-encrusted fingers. This stereotype is decades out of date, and many people don’t realize the staggering scale of the video game industry today.

In 2024, the entire global box office was around $30 billion, but video games brought in over six times what the film industry did — around $200 billion. Grand Theft Auto V has sold nearly 235 million copies in the 13 years since its release. It generated around $11 billion in revenue.

For reference, the most successful film of all time is Avengers: Endgame, which grossed about $2.9 billion over the course of its theatrical run. Grand Theft Auto VI did nearly $3.8 billion in sales on its first day.

Share

As best I can tell, this will be the largest launch for any piece of media in history. Around 1 in 5 salaried workers may have taken off work Thursday for the game’s release. There are job listings for GTA VI Researcher. For non-gamers, it is hard to overstate how large a cultural event this is.

To add to the already substantial hype, the game may end up being one of the best-reviewed games of all time. At the time of writing, the game has an absurd 99/100 score on Metacritic, making it tied for highest score ever. Reviewers are giving near-religious quotes like “15 minutes in and, been playing games for 45 years, I’m at a loss to explain what I am even seeing” and “been making games over 30 years … cant even find words for what i’m playing now.”

Amusingly, there exists a rump of right-wing gamers who are mad that early in the game you buy flowers for your girlfriend and do chores with her — Clavicular called the game “insanely cucked.” But as with the right-wing reaction to The Odyssey, there’s no broader impact to this whining.

Mobile games are slot machines

Ayo Omolewa is a creator who reverse engineers the code for mobile apps like Monopoly Go!, Candy Crush, and Block Blast to figure out exactly how they work. Turns out, they’re as predatory as you’d imagine, if not more so. They deliberately script wins at the beginning to hook users, lower player odds right before important game milestones to drive spending, and use the same internal code as actual slot machines to psychologically manipulate you.

Anyone with a bit of online savvy knows that mobile games are designed to extract as much money as possible from their players using microtransactions. But I think few of us truly realize how pernicious these apps are.

This feels like a weird thing to write down, but these types of games are probably unhealthier than the run-over-pedestrians-and-beat-up-hookers simulator that is Grand Theft Auto. The evidence that violent video games are linked to real-world violence is remarkably weak. And for all its sociopathic violence, at least Grand Theft Auto VI tells you a complex story with a single purchase and no microtransactions, rather than a carefully engineered series of dopamine hits meant to extract the maximum amount of cash until your brain turns to mush.

Pangram drama returns

Last month, The Atlantic published a piece titled “Yet Another Literary Prizewinner Seems to Be AI.” It accuses Simbarashe Steyn Kundizeza, the author of “The Blue Fighter” and winner of the Caine Prize for African Writing, of AI writing: An analysis using the AI-detection tool Pangram found that Kundizeza’s story was almost entirely AI-generated.

This generated some controversy, but the discourse didn’t truly explode until last week, when the Caine Prize organization said that an “internal review” had found no evidence of AI writing. Max Spero and Edward Tian, the founders of Pangram and its competitor GPTZero, respectively, both posted that the Caine Prize’s statement clearing Kundizeza was also entirely AI-generated.

This set off a firestorm of controversy, in part because this keeps happening to prestigious literary awards. And it’s not only literary awards. AI writing is spreading everywhere: in nonfiction books, in a column in The New York Times, in dozens of articles in The Guardian, in schools (from both students and educators), in a horror novel, and more. People are fed up with the deluge of AI-generated work.

On the other side of the argument are those who don’t trust AI detection tools. One highly salient fact is that many (but not all) of the award-winning literary works being caught are by nonwhite authors. This has led to widespread accusations of racism. The linguist and AI skeptic Emily Bender wrote that the larger story is “about racism, about the use of software sold as ‘AI’ to clothe racism in a veneer of objectivity.” Others have complained about the “crime of winning while African” and called the results “racist, Afrophobic witch hunts.”

Are those accusations fair? The strongest version of this argument goes something like this: People learn English very differently in non-Anglophone countries, and they use different words and patterns of speech than native speakers do. It’s possible that AI detectors are more likely to flag work by that kind of author because it’s not the style of writing they are used to.

It’s a thoughtful, interesting hypothesis that happens to have been pretty thoroughly disproven. In her article in The Atlantic about the Caine Prize, journalist Vauhini Vara ran the previous 25 winners through AI-detection software and it labeled them all as 100% human written. Professor Tuhin Chakrabarty analyzed 187 African books, and only one scored as more than 1% AI-generated besides Kundizeza’s “The Blue Fighter.” And just looking at “The Blue Fighter,” the story showed clear signs of AI: It overindexed massively on sentences that start with “Not,” words like “silence” “quiet,” and “gentle,” and other common AI tells.

(Source: Tuhin Chakrabarty)

This ended the controversy, and everyone accepted that AI detectors are not racist — haha, no, they accused Chakrabarty of also being racist and are organizing to sue him for conducting the analysis.

Now, look. AI detection tools are not perfect. But the documented cases where they struggle are almost always edge cases: a poem about a whale that repeats the same phrase 4,000 times, human writing that is put through AI translation into another language, or a writer deliberately trying to sound like AI and use AI constructions.

The false positive rate on longer texts is so low that it’s effectively zero: Pangram’s audit of more than 15,000 essays from English-as-a-second-language students found literally zero false positives. Another audit of nearly 2.9 million words of never-before-digitized text found zero false positives.

Spero, the Pangram CEO, has offered a standing cash bounty for any pre-2020 text of 750 words or more that Pangram mistakenly flags as AI generated — and to date the only winner is the whale poem. When leading AI detection programs like Pangram or GPTZero flag a long work as 100% AI-generated with high confidence, it is overwhelmingly likely that the work is in fact AI-generated.

So why, then, are so many people emotionally invested in denying that AI-detection software works? I see this as a stance based on in-group loyalty rather than evidence. Whether you want to classify the in-group as “fellow nonwhite people” or just “fellow writers in the literary prize community,” many people will simply choose to believe the word of people inside their community over any accusation from outside the community, no matter how well-supported.

That’s why many prize committees, like the Caine Prize, have chosen not to rescind their prizes. You don’t have to take my word for it — here’s what Ellah Wakatama, the chair of the Caine Prize’s board of trustees, told The Atlantic before a decision had been made.

“As an African,” she said, “my first stance is, if I ask you, Are you telling the truth? and if you say yes, I am going to believe you — especially because the accusers are not coming from amongst our own people.” [emphasis mine]

The biggest shame in all of this is that there are plenty of human authors being boxed out of these prizes (and the cash and recognition that come with them) by AI-generated slop. And it’s worrying that institutions that claim to care about preserving the role of human-generated stories are so resistant to actually testing their stories — and even more worrying that they often seem to prefer AI-generated text to human work.

How much power does Nick Fuentes have?

I sincerely regret to inform you that we need to have a conversation about Nick Fuentes.

The Republican Party is extremely disorganized right now. Even Vice President JD Vance, the obvious successor to Donald Trump, may choose not to run because the environment will be so bad for Republicans. Virtually everything about the party is up in the air, and all the important players are still trying to figure out where they stand. The only wing that is defined, organized, and has clear leadership is the far-right Groyper movement, which is why Nick Fuentes is having a moment.

You may know Fuentes as an authentic ideological racist, sexist, and fascist. He has repeatedly praised Hitler and suggested women should be put in gulags. He is also, to the world’s detriment, a great entertainer. He can be extremely charming and funny, easily moving between irony, outrage, absurdist humor, and sincerity without losing his audience.

This is part of what makes him dangerous — he has the ability to introduce extreme ideas in a lighthearted, charismatic, semi-ironic way. And it’s working; Fuentes spoke at the NXR (New Christian Right) conference last weekend, where he commanded the largest crowd of the entire event. And so for those of you with much healthier online media diets than I have, it’s probably time for you to learn a bit more about him.

Fuentes has a large audience. He’s got 1.3 million followers on Twitter and more than 700,000 followers on the streaming platform Rumble. But his actual livestreams aren’t as widely watched as you would expect for someone with that follower count. Ryan Broderick has documented how Rumble inflates view counts, explaining that one of Fuentes’ apparently popular streams only got a few hundred comments, a low number for someone of his stature.

In other words, he’s authentically popular — but not that popular.

Share

Where Fuentes shines is his ability to manipulate the clipping economy. He’s very clippable and very good at going viral from moments on his stream. And the size of the potential audience from clipping is 100 to 1,000 times as big as the actual audience that watches livestreams. He’s constantly being discussed and debated, which gives him immense influence on the right. But that influence is due to a smaller, dedicated core audience that spreads his clips algorithmically — not because his audience is massive in and of itself.

Still, that dedicated core has led to Fuentes followers infiltrating Republican institutions. Kai Schwemmer, a Fuentes ally, is now the political director of the College Republicans of America. Fuentes actively urges his fans to seize organs of the party, and conservative commentator Rod Dreher (himself fairly far to the nationalist right) has relayed a claim that around 30% to 40% of Gen Z Republican staffers in D.C. are Groypers.

The exact extent to which Groypers have percolated through conservative institutions is up for debate, but they are absolutely there. Fuentes knows that he’s unlikely to win a majority of Americans with his message, but that won’t matter if enough Groypers land in important jobs so that they effectively run the country.

In a party with no clear idea what to do next, Fuentes knows what he wants. He wants a Republican Party built around white identity. He wants America to disengage from foreign conflicts generally and from its allyship with Israel specifically. He wants an even purer and more extreme form of MAGA.

Fuentes was one of the first people on the right to break with Trump, declaring that “Trump is canceled” as early as March 2019. He takes the tone of the insurgent, accusing Trump and the majority of the Republican Party of betraying the MAGA movement. He encouraged his followers to sabotage the party by voting Democrat in 2026, and he’s threatened that Republicans need to follow the Groypers’ lead or face further sabotage.

In short, he is positioning himself as an active player in Republican politics. And as one of the few voices on the right with an actual, definable viewpoint, he’s likely to be extraordinarily influential for the next two years.
