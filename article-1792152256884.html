The Vicious Cycle of the Socially Awkward Pick-Me

And the self-fulfilling prophecy of the woman who only hangs out with guys because women are “too much drama.”

The pick-me is easy to hate. She comes in many forms, but the underlying theme is the same: she prioritizes male attention over female attention and puts other women down to obtain it. Or, at the very least, she thinks less of other women and this mindset seeps out in her behavior.

While a woman who “panders” to men in a particularly sexual or histrionic way might earn the most pick-me accusations, a woman who isn’t feminine enough could fall into this category too, especially if she considers herself more intelligent and worthy than “shallow” women who like fashion and pop music, and even if the only male attention she seeks is platonic. Technically, this archetype is more of a “not like other girls” than a true “pick me,” but either way, the alienation from and condemnation of women is the theme that connects the “not like other girls” and the “pick-me.” For an example of what I mean, here’s my comic on the topic:

I knew a particularly egregious pick-me, a woman on the periphery of my friend group in San Francisco who repeatedly flirted with her friends’ boyfriends, jumped on their shoulders ‘as a joke,” and hyped up the fact that she watched football and drank whiskey. It helped, of course, that she didn’t look like a whiskey-guzzling football fan. Many years earlier, at a teen summer program, a similar girl talked nonstop about how all her friends were guys because the guys were so much less drama and girls were so catty, but the only thing she ever said to girls was that she would love to wear their clothes except her boobs would “bust out” of them.

Yeah, we all know the type.

And yet….

I get the pick-me accusation all the time. At one point, it was probably totally true, both in the “how it looked” sense but also in the less flattering sense. I know this is not hard to believe, but in college, I became an honorary member of the geekiest fraternity on campus and even got my headshot on their fraternity composite, so my alliance with socially awkward men was solidified long ago. (Before you assume this was some kind of group sex thing, I was actually “just friends” with them. I only dated one of the brothers, but…still.)

But why? Why do women go this route? Why did I decide that hanging out with a group of twenty guys (who, I should add, did not actually include me in all their social activities and treated me as a sort of second-tier friend) was a better use of my time than just getting into a group of loyal girlfriends?

Women like me (and others who may engage in more insidious forms of pick-me-ism) become that way for a reason. Typically, it doesn’t begin with “hating other women.” They may not hate other women at all, even at the depths of their pick-me rock bottom. While I know I field the accusation quite often, I have never hated other women and I imagine the same is true for other pick-me’s. Instead, this path comes from a very dangerous combination of being socially inept in a way that specifically hinders your ability to read more subtle social cues that exist within groups of women, while being just friendly and cute enough to pass the much lower bar for male attention and male friendship.

What results is a woman who, by virtue of her own social deficits, becomes a “pick me” because it’s the easiest way for her to have a fulfilling social life. But this, of course, makes it worse—being a pick-me (even if only in the “only hangs out with guys” way and not in the “I hate other women” way) makes women like you even less. And once a woman experiences vitriol directed at her from the very women she perceives to have rejected her long ago, she reacts in the only way she can: she becomes even more of a pick-me.

By now, if you’re at all familiar with my work, you should be aware that I was born with some social deficits. I may have autism, I don’t know, but I would say at the very least I am “clinically annoying.” A therapist once diagnosed me with “missed social cues” a few minutes into our session and I have no idea what I could have done during that time period to be socially inept (and in case you’re wondering, no, I did not jump into a Trump impression, although I’ll have you know it was only 2006 and I had already mastered one, based on his feud with Rosie O’Donnell.)

In any event, I always found female friendships difficult to manage, or even find in the first place.

In elementary school, my preference was to make girlfriends only, because I was very girly myself and didn’t think I’d have much in common with boys. A boy, I assumed, would have zero interest in the fact that I received Felicity Merriman’s horse for Christmas. But my efforts with girls were unsuccessful time and time again. In third grade, I remember leaving adoring notes for a little girl I wanted to befriend (she, rightfully, found this creepy).

At day camp, when I was about nine, we made “friendship frames” at arts and crafts, and this craft involved taking a Polaroid photo with a friend and adorning it in a homemade frame. Nobody would agree to be in the photo with me, so my counselor offered to step in, and for some reason this humiliating photo is still displayed in my mom’s kitchen.

In fifth grade, taking adults’ advice to “just go up to people and make friends with them,” I recall hovering around the perimeter of a ring of girls at recess who did not create an opening in the circle for me despite full awareness that I was there. I made my best efforts to enter the conversation, short of physically pushing my way through, and later unsuccessfully tattled on them for “snubbing” me. (This was when I learned that you actually can’t force people to like you, and even in grade school, people aren’t required to have a good reason for excluding you.)

I had a few female friends, and often had very close one-on-one friendships, but the minute another girl entered the picture, all bets were off. I was utterly devoted to my best friend Caroline in fifth grade, until two inexplicably gargantuan blonde twins decided they wanted her as their shared best friend and successfully pulled her away from me, then catfished me for a while for seemingly no reason. I wrote a satirical newsletter mocking them and printed it out for my parents, but a few months later I later forgot about the segment regarding the twins (the newsletter covered many topics) and foolishly printed out twenty of these papers to pass around the school. Then I got in trouble for bullying and “I actually wrote this for my parents” was not a valid defense.

My most notorious girl-group fumble was when I finally solidified a group of girlfriends in middle school and they ceremonially (quite literally in a ceremony) voted me off our shared cafeteria table, inspired by the reality show Survivor. This was such a traumatic experience that almost every girl involved tracked me down at some point (the last one to do so was in 2018) to apologize.

I’m sure there are a million reasons that these girls didn’t like me, but “hating other girls” wasn’t one of them. I know this is not exactly a flattering comparison, but it’s kind of like how people tell incels that the only reason they can’t get laid is because they hate women. They may hate women, but their lack of success with women likely started with something else entirely (sometimes physical flaws, but more realistically, terrible social skills or crippling anxiety.) Over time, these men become embittered, they get hoovered up by the lucrative ragebait industry, and then they start talking about women in a way that warrants an FBI tipoff. (Then, there’s the other type of “incel” who hasn’t actually tried to talk to women but has preemptively determined he stands no chance as a cover for his social anxiety.) But either way, “women don’t like you because you’re misogynistic” isn’t really the whole story.

I continued to make girlfriends, maybe even the occasional girl group, but up until my mid-twenties or so, the groups would invariably fall apart. This was not the fault of all girls everywhere, and was entirely my own fault (to the extent that being socially inept for whatever reason was my fault) for struggling to parse subtle and not-so-subtle social cues. Boys were simply much easier to understand. Sure, I experienced breakups and rejection, but I never had to worry that all my crushes were secretly hanging out without me to make fun of me, or that a guy broke up with me because of some unspoken rule I had violated. Sometimes, boys were crude—they’d reject me because they just didn’t think I was hot enough—but at least I knew where I stood, and in a way I found the uncertainty so much more daunting. So I decided to focus more on what was easier for me: boys. And not just for dating. I also had a feeling I’d have an easier time making friends with boys.

I even received this advice from trusted adults in my life. I had a therapist tell me that “girls like me” should simply make friends with gay guys because they had all the interests of girls, but would not be sexually competitive and would be “less drama.” (I now know a few gay men who would be great datapoints against the hypothesis that “gay guys can’t be dramatic.”) But that was a big thing I heard: men are less drama. As I experienced “drama” with other girls (ie: other girls not liking me, and sometimes being mean about it) I was told that perhaps there was something about me that was better-suited to friendships with boys and men, who were for whatever reason guaranteed to be less volatile and likely to enjoy my company more.

When pick-me’s say that “girls are so much drama,” they are telling on themselves, because girls are only “so much drama” if they don’t like you. Now that I’ve learned how to socialize with women, I actually don’t find them dramatic at all, and my female friendships are incredibly low-stress. To some extent, this is because women in their thirties are a lot less drama than teenage girls, unless they happen to be contestants on a Real Housewives spinoff. Plus, as you get older, you aren’t beholden to high school cliques and if someone is a pain in the ass, you don’t have to interact with them. But yes, in my youth, I experienced behavior from other women that could unfortunately only be described as “catty.” The Survivor incident aside, I was abruptly exiled from a sorority I was pledging after experiencing a sort of gray-area sexual assault at the hands of an ex-hookup of one of the senior members (I went to a very small school and it was nearly impossible to date anyone without knowing their ex.) I should have surmised that “I don’t care if you go out with him, we barely dated” was not a serious statement, and I also should have read between the lines of, “Oh my God! What happened? We’re here for you!” (They were not, in fact, there for me.) To add insult to injury, one of the sisters later attempted to sabotage me the night I met my husband by pulling him aside and warning him that I was a “slut.” Sadly, the poor man still suffers to this day.

At this point, you might find it odd that I would be sexually assaulted, socially ostracized as a result of it, and only internalize that women can be mean, not that men can be violent. But this incident did not occur in a vacuum. I had interacted with many men and boys over the course of my life, and 99.99% of them did not assault me. But it seemed the majority of women (at least from my perspective at the time) were quick to exclude me, ignore me, or straight-up bully me. So the women’s reaction added to my existing narrative, while the man’s behavior (while worse on the face of it) seemed like more of an anomaly. (I’m sure my experience would have been different if I had been raised in an abusive environment where most of my suffering came from men and boys.)

Of course, “all girls” are not catty, and even girls who do catty things to people they loathe might be perfectly fine most of the time to everyone else. But my experience was a persistent pattern of girls and women excluding me, spreading rumors about me, and making fun of me. And although I had a few very negative experiences with boys, the vast majority of my experiences with boys were positive, especially when it came to platonic friendship.

This is not exactly surprising. If you are a reasonably attractive young woman, it will be shockingly easy to make male friends. Sometimes, this is because they’re all trying to have sex with you, but I find that reasoning oversimplified and quite frankly, untrue. The reality is that even if you’re taken, and even if they’re taken, a lot of men just like being around attractive women. Plenty of men have told me they are like this, so it’s not even my theory. Perhaps there are female versions of this—women who would gladly have a purely platonic friendship with a hot man just so they can look at him—but I see it far more often the other way around.

Male social cues are also easy to parse because they’re out in the open. I’ve been in a group chat with my brother and a mutual male friend for more than thirteen years. On several occasions, I’ve made a bad joke or been annoying, and they will simply tell me so, but the infraction never results in long-simmering resentment or any kind of secret side-text thread about how toxic I am (at least I hope! lmao.) I’ve probably been insulted and mocked more on this group chat than anywhere else in my life (minus the Internet) and yet, it doesn’t offend me. (My brother has called me the “Michael Jordon of being annoying” and told me I exist right in the space of mental illness below “needs to be sectioned.”) I see my brother and our friend rag on each other like this too. Sometimes I join in. If subtle, unspoken social cues are hard to parse (and for me, they are) then the overt teasing and banter that occurs within a guy-group can be far easier to understand. And even when guys are saying insanely mean things to each other, it’s often meant endearingly.

This, of course, does not mean that men are better. A thing is not better just because it’s easier to understand, which is something I learned the hard way when I posted on r/TwinPeaks saying I didn’t like the show because I “couldn’t figure out what the hell was going on.” But for a while, I expected the world to revolve around my own social difficulties instead of learning social skills so that I could better function in society, and thus pushed me towards men. Yes, some women are naturally catty, but what appears to you as “catty” behavior could also be the behavior of women who simply don’t like you, because you’re annoying, off-putting or unintentionally rude. To make matters more complicated, “changing who you are” to appeal to anyone, even female friends, is often seen as a grave social sin—you are not being yourself. If people repeatedly tell you to be yourself, and that women who don’t like you are “just catty,” then the natural response is to continue being socially inept and make friends with men who won’t care.

I can’t exactly blame socially inept women (myself included) for taking the path of least resistance. Why would you put more work into making people like you (who repeatedly show you they do not like you) when you could instantly make five friends just by showing up and being able to take a joke?

The only problem is that these friendships—where a socially inept woman befriends only guys—create two further problems for that woman, even if the men all act like perfect gentlemen. One problem is she will begin to believe that women don’t like her because she’s hot. Plenty of people will probably reinforce this belief (see: my former therapist) because they will look at the evidence: cute girl has no social troubles with men, lots of troubles with women, ergo…women are jealous. As I’ve written before, hotness is basically always an asset in all facets of life, but a woman’s hotness will help her far more with men than with women, and her social detriments will harm her far more with women than with men. While a woman who is both hot and socially skilled will likely do quite well with female friends, a woman who is hot and socially inept will suffer because her social ineptitude will read as arrogant or narcissistic and therefore less forgivable than just seeming “awkward.” In my case, whether I was actually that attractive or not, dressing in an attention-seeking way combined with bad social skills was especially poisonous for making female friends.

Then, the second consequence for the only-guy-friends woman—she will read as “potential trouble” to other women. In part, this is because a woman who only has male friends appears to be the kind of person who thrives off sexual attention and might be dangerous to have around husbands or boyfriends. But even if she doesn’t seem like the type who would seduce partnered men for sport, she might just seem like a bad match for female friendship. I don’t even blame women for this assumption. Given that you are under no obligation to give everyone the benefit of the doubt, why should you go out of your way to assume the best about a woman who doesn’t even seem to like women very much? You don’t know her full life story, and there is no incentive for you to learn it. Frankly, if I met a woman who only had male friends I might not necessarily think she was a pick-me, but I might assume she wasn’t interested in befriending women. If she went around talking about how women were “too much drama,” there’s about a zero percent chance I’d want to befriend her, which would then further her perception that “women are so catty.”

Thus, the pick-me’s perception of other women (and other women’s perception of her) is a self-fulfilling prophecy which initially had nothing to do with misogyny, internalized or otherwise. But of course, the end result looks a lot like it: a woman who doesn’t trust other women, doesn’t want to be friends with them, and believes she’s better than them because she’s “less drama.”

Of course, there is only one way out of this: realizing the real reason women didn’t like you was because you were unlikable, and that this tendency is still there with men, but doesn’t affect you as much. Perhaps it’s still on the pick-me to decide if working on this is worth it. Now that I had a great number of wonderful female friends, I think it is. But I didn’t get there until my early thirties, so for now I will excuse the shameless twenty-two-year-old pick-me’s who think women are just too much drama. At least until they start shimmying up to my husband and talking about how much they love beer and basketball. Then it’s time for a war crime tribunal.
