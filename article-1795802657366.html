The Authors Guild this year completed its rollout of what it calls Human Authored Certification. It allows an author to pay a $10 fee (free to members) to certify, without independent verification, that: “the text of the book was written by a human and not generated by AI, with the exception of minimal uses such as AI applications that check spelling and grammar or for research.” Already, 6,500 titles have been certified. Canadian and British authors groups have launched parallel programs.

This month, Tor, the flagship science fiction imprint of Macmillan, withdrew the cover of an upcoming novel after readers identified AI-generated artwork. The publisher’s art contracts and the authors’ contract prohibit AI use. This follows Hachette’s cancellation of a novel by Mia Ballard over suspected AI use, and an announcement by Quebec’s Éditions du Boréal that it would not reprint Thélyson Orélien's C'était ça ou mourir for the same reason.

The Giller Prize and Writers’ Trust awards in Canada have prohibited AI-generated writing. The Society of Children’s Book Writers and Illustrators has firmed up its policy outlawing generative AI in books submitted for its programs and awards. Bloomsbury last year announced a blanket prohibition on AI-generated creative content. Penguin Random House has added notices to books prohibiting their use for AI training.

What all those initiatives have in common, apart from strong resistance to generative AI, is that they’re occurring in the world of trade publishing—the world in which authors and publishers create books for general readers.

Another sector of publishing that’s being transformed by AI is one we don’t discuss much here: academic publishing. Academic publishing is leaping headlong into generative AI, trying to turn it into a new line of business.

This is big news. Scientific, technical, medical and professional publishing accounts for almost half the revenues of the world's largest publishing groups, compared to about a third for trade publishing. It is a different and generally more lucrative business, with a substantial proportion of its revenues derived from institutional subscriptions.

Several AI business models are emerging in the space.

The simplest is licensing scholarship to AI companies and corporate clients. Wiley, a big player in scientific publishing, is licensing its content both to technology companies for training AI models and to corporate clients, including pharmaceutical companies, for use in their own AI-powered research. It is also aggregating and licensing content on behalf of other publishers. Wiley earned $49 million(US) in AI licensing revenue in its last fiscal year. That’s a small percentage of its overall revenue, but it has come out of nowhere in the last few years.

Other academic publishers are developing mechanisms for researchers to access their intellectual property through existing AI platforms. Publishers Weekly reported this month that Oxford University Press has announced a pilot program that will let students and researchers at Harvard Library, the Wharton School, and the University of Oxford interrogate its licensed content through OpenAI’s ChatGPT Edu.

Not to be outdone, Cambridge, Princeton, and Johns Hopkins university presses have launched Meridian, a shared system for providing authorized access to their published works through AI tools, with attribution and usage reporting. These initiatives allow publishers to retain a measure of control over how their intellectual property is accessed and used in AI environments.

A third and more ambitious approach is for publishers to develop AI research platforms of their own. Two of the biggest scientific publishers in the world, Elsevier and Springer Nature, each publishing hundreds of thousands of articles a year across thousands of peer-reviewed journals, are transforming themselves into AI-powered research services.

Elsevier has introduced LeapSpace, an AI research environment that allows scientists to ask questions, compare studies, and evaluate and analyze scientific literature, drawing on a collection of 23 million full-text articles and books.

Springer Nature has developed Nature Research Assistant. It has about 25,000 beta users and is planning a commercial launch next year. Springer Nature is also pursuing licensing opportunities through a system called ARC3, which allows clients to use published scientific content in their own AI environments. Wiley, too, is developing AI research tools of its own—these business models are not mutually exclusive.

You see the pattern. The old business was selling academic books, journal subscriptions, and online access to scientific articles. The new business is licensing scholarship to AI companies and corporations, managing access to scholarship through AI platforms, and selling AI research and analytical services.

The latter seems to be the most competitive frontier. Rather than simply supplying intellectual property to the technology companies, Elsevier and Springer Nature want to own the platforms through which the scientific community does its work. And instead of just giving researchers access to millions of articles, they want to collaborate in answering whatever questions researchers bring to their platforms.

It’s a huge upsell. If these publishers have their way, researchers or their AI agents will have to knock on the doors of these companies’ platforms, pay a toll, and do their work within the publishers’ walled garden, using the publishers’ research and analytic tools. The tools available through Elsevier’s LeapSpace include research synthesis, comparison tables, evidence verification, manuscript assistance, and research-funding discovery. The more tools a publisher supplies, and the more of the research process it can accommodate, the more it can charge its clients.

All of that promises to move publishers beyond the business of providing books and articles into the business of selling AI-enabled research capabilities. They’re competing to become global knowledge platforms, the Amazons or Googles of the academic world.

The divide between trade and academic publishing is not as absolute as I’ve painted it. Academic publishers have their own concerns about AI-generated writing and the unauthorized use of their intellectual property. And there is no obvious reason why trade publishers couldn’t pursue some of the same commercial opportunities as academic publishers.

Imagine what Penguin Random House could do with the accumulated knowledge in its enormous nonfiction backlist.

Imagine an alliance of trade publishers along the lines of the Cambridge group, licensing their nonfiction to AI platforms or developing research services of their own, with some mechanism for compensating authors.

Obviously, there would be rights issues and pushback from authors and agents, but five years from now, if the money were good?

I don’t know where all this leads, but it may be that the most interesting developments in trade publishing’s relationship with AI are, for the moment, occurring outside trade publishing.
