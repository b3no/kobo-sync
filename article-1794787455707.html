Dispatch from the Front Lines: Don’t laugh too hard at the States, friends.

We’re only one weird glitch away from some massive infrastructure problems of our own.

Line Editor
Nov 15
 
READ IN APP
 

Happy weekend, Line readers. We know a lot of you are probably more interested in the Grey Cup than anything we have to say, so we’ll keep it snappy.

Starting with the latest episode of The Line Podcast, with Andrew Potter filling in for Jen Gerson, who’s spending a few weeks reporting from abroad. It was a fun episode (and had probably our funniest title card image to date).

After that, check out last week’s On The Line, where host Matt Gurney is joined by two guests, Philippe Lagassé and Mike Moffatt, to discuss what the federal budget tells us about Canada’s plans for national defence, immigration, housing and the economy.

And now, on with the dispatch.

We open with one more bit of self-promotion. Yesterday, The Line published the first report by our new reporter, Alan Findlay. We had previously announced that Alan was joining us for a one-year fellowship, with a mandate to focus exclusively on how to get Canada building again. What are we doing well? What are the impediments? What needs to change if we are to be the country we all seem to agree we ought to be, yet somehow are not?

This is a theme that we cover all the time in our commentary, but Alan is going tackle it via traditional, straight-up reporting. We really hope you enjoyed his first long-form report. We’ll publish more over the coming year, usually on the weekends, since the pieces will be longer and benefit from a more relaxed reading experience. So sit down, pour a drink and read what he has to say, this time, about Alto, our high-speed-rail program.

Alan’s position was made possible thanks to a generous grant by Build Canada, which provided the funding to establish the Build Canada Fellowship. The project remains entirely under The Line’s editorial control, but we want to offer our thanks to Build Canada for making it possible. REPORT: Fast Trains, Slow Country

Alan Findlay
·
Nov 14

Alto’s high-speed rail project promises to redraw passenger travel between Toronto and Quebec City. The bigger test is whether Canada can rediscover how to build big — without repeating its biggest mistakes.

Read full story

Speaking of infrastructure and national-scale problems, we had just finished recording our podcast on Friday, of course, when word came down that the United States had ordered a nationwide ground stop for all departing flights and was only going to consider international arrivals on a “case-by-case basis.” Many flights did indeed turn around and return to their destination rather than approach the U.S. This highly unusual event immediately set off a frenzy of speculation; it takes something pretty remarkable to see any nation, let alone the United States, effectively shutting down its airspace. A quick check of our sources reassured us that we were not looking at any kind of military emergency, which led us pretty quickly to conclude that something had happened to the U.S. air traffic control systems. And that is, of course, what was ultimately revealed to have been the case, with telecommunication problems impeding the ability of air traffic controllers to communicate with each other and airborne flights.

The incident is as yet fully unexplained; U.S. government sources have said that part of the air traffic control system, reliant on older technologies, crashed because of router difficulties, while more modern elements of the system remained online, but were insufficient to take over all of the necessary work.

U.S. officials have said at this time that they do not believe the outage was a cyberattack or an act by a rogue AI, but this will not be definitively ruled out until an investigation is complete. We confess that given the state of things in the United States today, we don’t take much the U.S. government says for granted.

Obviously, we will be watching it, and we certainly feel vindicated in one of our lifelong little rules: when travelling anywhere that requires a flight of more than an hour or two, we always keep a light change of clothes and minimal toiletries with us in our travel bag, because spending the night in an airport without a toothbrush and a change of underwear is not an experience we care to repeat.

Long story.

But the incident also brought to mind the fact that, though this is an American story, and Canada’s navigation system notably remained fully functional during the crisis, this is something we cannot rule out ever happening here.

Because whatever ultimately ends up being the explanation for Friday’s outage in the United States, the Trump administration officials blaming it on technology could very well be telling the truth. A pretty depressing amount of North American infrastructure is very old. We suspect Line readers would be shocked to discover how much of our modern infrastructure dates back to the mid- or sometimes even early 20th century.

In Canada, unfortunately, we have massively neglected spending on basic upkeep for civic infrastructure. This is a problem that cuts across all orders of government. None of our jurisdictions have done a good job taking care of their toys. It’s hard to put an exact dollar figure on the issue, because, again, the problem is divided up among so many jurisdictions. But there are estimates ranging well into the mid-hundreds of billions in necessary but overdue spending on existing infrastructure.

We want to repeat that for emphasis: existing infrastructure. We are not talking about hundreds of billions of dollars worth of stuff we arguably should have built, but didn’t. The Carney government and the Major Projects Office and the various bits of enabling legislation are all focused on the problem of the things we should have built to be economically competitive but did not, because we became bureaucratically paralyzed. We’re talking about a completely separate problem: the stuff we’ve built that we haven’t taken proper care of.

Deferring maintenance “just one more year” is an easy way to solve a short-term budget problem. But when those budget problems remain unsolved in the long term, deferred maintenance has a habit of becoming more and more serious an issue. Canada’s bridges, roads, ports, rail networks, transit systems, and various essential utilities are old, often due for replacement, and though generally safe, the risks of a sudden and catastrophic failure rise with every decision to defer.

Consider recent stories just from the hometowns of your editors. Calgary’s water system has become notoriously dysfunctional. Toronto’s road and transit networks are something of an omni-disaster. These are longstanding issues that everyone knows about, but we sleepwalked into them because no one wanted to spend the money to take care of their stuff. Politicians get excited showing up to announce new things, with big novelty cheques, bespoke lectern placards and huge ribbons to cut. No one wins elections by posing with guys in hard hats as they scrub out a sewer. But you sure as hell notice, and fast, when the sewer stops working.

We have a hunch that many Canadians were quietly amused to see the U.S. struggling on Friday. We get it. But we are encouraging our readers not to get too cocky. The situation isn’t much better here. And we’re not sure any level of government is prepared to invest the political and financial capital into doing the work that we very much urgently need to do.

On a very much related note, The Line observed (and endured) last week’s major Microsoft outage with only minimal fuss and bother. Some of our systems were indeed taken out, but we had enough redundancy to stay in business. Not everyone was as lucky. One friend of The Line, a lawyer on the verge of starting a trial, had the entire process thrown into chaos by the outage, which locked both parties out of their key files. Hospitals in Toronto (and we’re sure elsewhere) lost connectivity among their various systems. It wasn’t fun.

The Microsoft outage landed at an amusing time. Or perhaps ironic time is the better description. The internet has been abuzz of late with a resurgence in “prepper” content. We aren’t aware of any particular threat that we ought to be preparing for, and yes, we absolutely asked around. Much of the online chatter seemed to stem from a series of allied governments issuing public announcements to their citizens calling for emergency preparedness measures, usually, enough to be independent for as long as 72 hours in the event of some kind of emergency.

As far as we can tell, the various orders were not a secret, coordinated event, just a coincidence that led to a lot of speculation among the Very Online, among which we must sadly count ourselves. We aren’t at all fussed or panicked about the announcements, which are, to our minds, simply good advice. You should be able to go 72 hours with only the supplies you have on hand. That’s just reasonable planning, as far as we’re concerned. We’d urge everyone to plan around a week, frankly. Three days can come and go quickly.

What we would say, with an exasperated sigh, is that though we see no imminent or unusual threats looming, we wish a basic level of preparedness didn’t make so much sense these days. But the world’s geopolitical situation is deteriorating, our critical infrastructure is in rough shape (see above), cyber attacks are increasingly threatening, and now we have the risk of rogue AIs to contend with, too.

Your Line editors aren’t that old, but will admit to some pining for a simpler age. We liked not having to worry about all these things at once! But in the meantime, on top of having your own household emergency kits ready, we would encourage government and industry alike to begin thinking about how to design resiliency into our society from the get-go. Do we really need everything plugged into the internet? Should the entire economy run off the servers of like three companies? Do we maybe wanna rein in the AI expansion briefly, until we can get better at this?

We aren’t paranoid here, but we are paying attention, and it seems like as good a time as any for us all to ask ourselves if there are smart things we ought to be doing, and dumb things we should stop doing. Before it does become something more serious than being locked out of your email for a day. That, frankly, we could use more of.

Tonight is the Grey Cup, as mentioned above. The annual championship game for the CFL will be marked by a flypast by the RCAF’s Snowbirds, in their final flight for years. The Snowbirds are standing down, pending the arrival of new planes, as their existing Tutor jets are ancient, more than 60 years old, and are being retired. Yet another life extension of the Tutors was rejected by the government, in favour of returning the Snowbirds to service once new planes are available.

We’ll have more to say about the legacy of the Snowbirds one day. For now, we’ll simply note how sad it is to see the squadron idled without replacement planes ready. The Snowbirds will receive turboprop CT-157 Siskin IIs sometime early in the next decade, apparently. Contrary to some, we aren’t bothered by the Siskins being turboprops, instead of jets. The Siskins are very modern and advanced aircraft that will serve Canada well as trainers and stunt performance planes. We look forward to seeing them fly some day.

But we’re pissed off, frankly, that we let the Tutor jets get so old that the government couldn’t get new planes for the squadron before the old ones aged out. The age of the Tutors was no secret. The long lead time for any new plane is also no surprise. The gap between tonight’s performance and whenever the Snowbirds next take flight in their new aircraft is entirely a consequence of Canadian government paralysis. Much like with our infrastructure woes mentioned above, no one wanted to make the decision to either retire or replace the Tutors, so we just muddled on for decade after decade, until we simply couldn’t any more.

It’s a little symbol of Canadian governance in miniature, and a sad one. We hope everyone at tonight’s game in Calgary enjoys the show. We hope they all remember why it’ll be the last one for a while, and get mad enough about it that the politicians never do something like this again.

In conclusion, The Line was saddened to read this weekend that Pte. William Douglas Dal Bianco, of the Canadian Army’s Princess Patricia’s Canadian Light Infantry regiment, had died of injuries sustained during the tragic fire at CFB Wainwright. Pte. Dal Bianco, who was being treated for his injuries in Ontario, is the fifth soldier to die of injuries sustained in that disaster. Twenty-four remain in hospital, with many still in critical condition.

Our thoughts are with the private’s family, the families of the other fallen, with the troops still in hospital, and all their loved ones. It’s a nightmare.

And, gosh, it’s hard to look at the photos of these soldiers, isn’t? When did they all get so young?

Thanks, everyone. We’ll talk to you soon.
