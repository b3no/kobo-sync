Democrats should fire bad teachers and bad cops

Standing up for workers doesn't mean letting public sector unions undermine public services

Nicholas Bagley and Robert Gordon

The left has two competing impulses: Expand high-quality government services and embrace the public sector union agenda.

But those two impulses are in tension with one another — and too many Democrats are in denial about that. At its core, the problem is that public sector unions generally fight to minimize differences among employees, including both standouts at the top and weak links at the bottom.

That means governments cannot recruit and retain the best workers or manage up or out the worst performers. That, in turn, badly degrades the quality of government service in ways that damage the Democrats’ own cause.

In California, for example — as Zach Liscow at Yale Law School and two coauthors recently showed — higher-quality engineers saved the state a ton of money on transportation projects. When these engineers retired, project costs rose by six times their wages.

And no wonder those excellent engineers retired. Good engineers can earn much more in the private sector. The same is true for excellent technologists, who can save governments millions on vendors but are often paid far below average market levels. Higher pay for effective teachers has likewise been a part of performance gains for schools in the District of Columbia and Dallas.

But labor serves to compress pay across jobs and reject salary differences based on performance altogether. Excessive job protection for poor performers has an even greater effect on government results.

Although few in number, the worst employees in most jobs are very bad — worse than the bell curve would predict. These “bad apples” can also spoil morale and productivity for others.

First, consider schools. Students with the least effective teachers earn less as adults, save less for retirement, and are less likely to attend college. They even have more kids as teenagers. Raj Chetty and his coauthors found that replacing the worst 5% of teachers with an average teacher would raise the present value of an average classroom’s lifetime earnings by more than $250,000.

Now, consider policing. Max Schanzenbach and Kyle Rozema found that the worst 1% of officers, as measured by misconduct allegations against them, cost their departments four times as much as an average officer in payouts to civil-rights plaintiffs. Other research indicates that bad cops make a disproportionate number of low-quality arrests — which is to say, arrests that don’t lead to charges or convictions.

Under collective bargaining agreements, government dismissal processes are often incredibly onerous. Across multiple rounds of review, the government must extensively document and defend multiple efforts to help employees succeed.

In New York State’s prisons, a Marshall Project report found that the state succeeded in dismissing corrections officers for excessive use of force in fewer than 10% of cases. In the 2011 to 2012 school year, for every 1,000 teachers, only one with tenure was fired or nonrenewed.

Corruption, cronyism, and patronage are all harmful to an effective civil service, and governors and mayors (not to mention presidents) should not be able to sweep out incumbents and install loyalists at will. But government can provide due process and vigorous oversight to prevent abuses without parodies of procedural overload.

The public sector’s allergy to differentiating based on results is especially painful when budgets get tight.

Collective bargaining agreements often mandate that seniority should be the only thing that matters for layoff decisions. This means that an outstanding new hire will lose out to an indifferent veteran waiting for their pension to vest. When schools face layoffs, poorer schools with more junior teachers see the most turnover — exactly where you would hope for more stability.

Some brave leaders have tackled seniority protections in layoffs.

Denver Mayor Mike Johnston recently changed the rules so that merit, not just seniority, has weight in layoff decisions. Johnston took a lot of heat from union members who shouted “shame” at him. But he did the right thing for the city he governs.

Better pay for strong performers, accountability for weak ones, and layoff decisions based on merit: none of this should be radical. It matters to government performance, and it may well matter to voters.

Don’t unions help attract talent?

In recent decades, some red states have cut public spending in ways that devastated public universities and education. Some blue jurisdictions have avoided these deep cuts, and unions are one reason why. Good for them.

Unions also secure higher pay for their members. That’s been shown in work stretching back to Harvard’s Richard Freeman in the 1980s. In principle, the compensation premium secured by unions could be effectively designed to make government work better by attracting and retaining high-quality teachers, cops, and other public sector workers. We would be all for that.

It’s just not what happens in practice.

Consider public pensions. A lot of research suggests that a dollar spent on pensions is less effective at recruiting and retaining staff than a dollar spent on upfront pay. The reason is a familiar one: people care about the present more than the future.

Matthew Yglesias recently noted an incredible fact: “while New York pays 70 percent higher teacher salaries than Louisiana on average, its entry-level salaries are only 7 percent higher.”

This is doubly inefficient. The state struggles more than it should to attract teachers and then commits itself to late-career salary increases all out of proportion to improvements in teacher performance. (While experience is valuable to educator effectiveness, the difference between five years and 15 years of experience is modest.)

Americans deserve to retire with financial security, and as a source of social insurance for all residents, the federal government rightly acts to ensure strong retirements through Social Security and Medicare.

But police departments and school districts do not provide social insurance. They provide policing and schooling. And government retirement plans are not designed to get and keep the people they need.

At times, public sector pensions seem almost deliberately designed to distort the recruitment and retention benefits they do offer. At the front end, young people who haven’t put down roots aren’t much tempted by pensions that don’t even begin to vest for five years or later. At the back end, the promise of full retirement after 30 years of service (sometimes combined with escalating accrual rates) means that excellent workers leave earlier than is ideal, while burned-out workers stick around longer than they should.

How did so many governments end up with retirement plans rarely found in the private sector? Federal law leaves states and cities far more freedom than private employers to underfund pensions. And that means unions fighting for their members can secure promises of future payouts that current leaders need not fulfill. Politicians win praise for the promises, then bill their successors.

This is how America’s public-sector pensions came to have a funding shortfall of more than $1 trillion. Just digging out of the hole will require higher taxes or service cuts — Forget about new services.

Two days after we published an op-ed in The New York Times urging governors and mayors to create more distance from unions (and specifically faulting Gov. JB Pritzker for signing a pension sweetener), two bond agencies downgraded Chicago’s credit. One specifically called out that bill. The city now has to pay more simply to borrow enough money to cover its existing obligations.

In Albany, unions have been pushing hard for guaranteed retirement with a full pension for public employees at age 55. This is an unthinkable arrangement for most Americans outside dangerous or debilitating professions. Many Democrats and some Republicans have backed the unions’ play: They are formidable political allies in state and local elections because they are able to dominate low-turnout elections.

While all of this has unfolded, both Chicago and New York City face tight budgets. The month before Pritzker signed the pension sweetener, Chicago Public Schools cut almost 1,500 staff, including more than 400 teachers. New York Mayor Zohran Mamdani is now in court fighting against a legally mandated expansion in housing vouchers that he supported as a candidate.

Hiking pensions while cutting schools and housing makes no sense. It is possible only because no one outside of state capitols or public sector unions is paying much attention.

Isn’t the problem billionaires?

It’s tempting to say that the answer here isn’t to push back on union demands, but to force the rich to pay higher taxes. This was the perspective of many critical commenters on our piece in The Times: “Bagley and Gordon think there is only so much to go around,” wrote one. “The better argument is to tax the rich and grow the pie.”

We’re all for more progressive taxation. But we also have to live in a real world where tax increases go only so far.

To take the most obvious recent example: Mamdani and Gov. Kathy Hochul engaged in a long dance about whether to raise taxes on the rich. For a long time, Mamdani pushed and and Hochul resisted. Last month, they agreed on a pied-a-terre tax. A budget deal in Albany is imminent.

Wherever your sympathies, here’s the thing: Even the full package of tax increases Mamdani pushed this year would have done little to extend new initiatives like free universal child care. They would have paid mainly “to keep the lights on.” The pied-a-terre tax closes less than 10% of the city’s budget deficit.

Democrats need to figure out how to better spend the money they have. And it is only broken, transactional politics that prioritizes early retirement for government workers over day care slots — or that pays for bad government employees while failing to attract great ones.

A better bargain is possible.

Cities interested in effective government services should adopt pay scales that appeal to talented people early in their careers. Compensation would still grow, but not at the same rate.

Rhode Island traveled some of that path beginning in 2011, when its then-treasurer, Gina Raimondo, raised the alarm about pension costs, shifted workers into a hybrid between traditional pensions and 401(k) plans, and was able to cut the pension pressures that hung over her state’s education spending. For that, she earned the opposition of the state’s public sector unions — and easy election as governor in 2014.

But this is not about opposing unions. It is about ensuring the public sector works for everybody. Here is Vincent Schiraldi, who battled to secure reliable staffing at Rikers Island jail while working for then-New York Mayor Bill de Blasio: “The unions are going to fight for their members … They’re going to fight for the best possible deal. We’ve got to be the grown-ups — and say no. It’s been decades of letting the unions run roughshod.”

The unseen political cost of always backing teachers’ unions

The policy is one thing. The politics of disagreeing with public sector unions is something else altogether.

Many moderates believe Democrats have lost the public because of culture and values issues like transgender rights and immigration. They may also believe the party is captured by the professional-managerial class at the expense of the working class. If they’re right that these issues are the whole story, then public sector union influence is not a problem. Unions represent ordinary people seeking a better life.

There is compelling new evidence that this view is wrong.

David Broockman and Josh Kalla recently tested how Democrats and Republicans changing their views on issues might move voters. Across 29 topics, the authors found that embracing teacher accountability ranked third for winning voters over. It’s a slightly better issue for Democrats, the study found, than moving to the middle on transgender kids in sports or on immigration.

And, in parallel, Republican candidates got hammered especially hard for expressing opposition to the view that bad teachers should be dismissed.

Wow. How many millions more words have Democrats spoken on gender rights than teacher accountability over the last year? That gap exists because getting crosswise with teachers’ unions is more politically costly than fighting with gender-equity groups.

Over the last decade, Democrats have returned to a union-aligned education agenda, breaking with their heterodox approach during the early Obama years. Educational outcomes declined nationally over the same period, eroding Democrats’ one-time political advantage on the issue.

While more polling here would be valuable, we suspect two things are true: First, voters care a lot about public education. And second, they think good teachers should be rewarded and retained and bad teachers should be out of the classroom.

They know that there are poor teachers and that nothing is usually done about them. It’s hard to run a good school like that. They do not buy arguments that making these kinds of judgments is impossible or unfair. Many of them see their own bosses make these kinds of judgments every day.

Defending subpar employees while Chicago lays off teachers and New York struggles to meet its child care commitments isn’t pro-worker. It’s pro-some workers at the expense of the rest.
