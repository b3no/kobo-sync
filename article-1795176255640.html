I was at the YIMBYtown conference for much of this week, and it was a great opportunity to meet, hear from, and talk to tons of people who are doing housing-supply work in state and local government. There are so many different things happening in so many different places at this point that it’s challenging to say anything fully general — the dynamics in the state legislature in Montana are very different than the dynamics in New York, and the local politics in Raleigh are very different from the local politics in Los Angeles.

But something you do see generally is along the lines of one of the points I made in Wednesday’s column, namely that there’s just a big difference between “affordability politics” as a campaign theme and actually shifting policy priorities in a way that makes housing more affordable. It’s hard! It’s not impossible, but it requires a lot of difficult coalition-building and a lot of attention to detail and good judgment.

I saw one panel about single-stair reform where they had a former fire chief on, and she was talking about why most fire departments don’t like this and why they’re wrong to see it as a major fire-safety hazard. I am fully convinced by her (see this Pew brief), but it’s easy to see why a mayor, a state legislator, or a random person might find fire chiefs to be more credible about the safety implications than housing reformers. Actually persuading more fire chiefs is probably going to be important to winning this issue, because even in a huge affordability moment, people (reasonably!) do not want to make what they worry would be large fire-safety sacrifices for the sake of cheaper housing.

And yet there’s nothing you can do that doesn’t have some kind of tradeoff or politically powerful opposition. If there were fruit hanging that low, it would have been picked already.

Chris Granner: What will it take to restore the mandate to the ACA? At the time, it was one of three required legs of the stool; Trump 1 killed it, and within a few years, most of the “bent cost curve” went poof and the extra expense, thanks to the ACA subsidy rules, went straight to the deficit. Switzerland (“Obamacare done right” as I read more than once) has a mandate. As M4A (that’s “MedicARE”, not “MedicAID”) skeptics, don’t we need to restore it?

I keep meaning to write this take, so thank you for asking.

To recap, the way the Affordable Care Act individual marketplace works is that insurance companies (with limited exceptions) are not allowed to engage in normal insurance underwriting that would deny coverage to or demand exorbitant premiums from people with high levels of health risk. This makes sense because the social function of health insurance policy is to make sure that sick people can get care.

The obvious problem with this, though, is that if you make insurance a much better deal for people with serious health problems than for people in good health, you risk the people in good health getting sticker shock at the premiums and just saying “thanks but no thanks.” If those people drop out of the pool, then premiums get even higher and you get more sticker shock and eventually a death spiral.

The A.C.A. has two features that partially mitigate this. One is that for lower-income families, public subsidies cover a large share of the cost of premiums so buying in is a better deal in practice than it looks like on paper. The other is that A.C.A. plans cover a bunch of preventive care that it makes sense to get whether or not you are sick. So you don’t get the immediate death spiral. Still, it’s a problem. So as originally conceived, the A.C.A. included a financial penalty for going without insurance. This is designed to ensure that affluent healthy people are nudged to buy insurance, which reduces the average cost of an unsubsidized plan, which also reduces the average cost of subsidies. In the context of Massachusetts, where you had moderate Republican Mitt Romney as governor, this worked well in securing bipartisan support for a plan. Business interests essentially liked it better than higher taxes and higher explicit public subsidies, and bipartisanship is usually good politics.

But when Barack Obama took the idea to D.C., Republicans weren’t interested. And as a partisan matter, a mandate to buy insurance was politically unpopular.

The Obama administration handled this poorly, I think, by insisting on the mandate as part of the A.C.A. legislation but then implementing the mandate in their rule-writing in a relatively soft way. That meant the A.C.A. didn’t achieve true universality, even leaving aside state-level failures to adopt Medicaid expansion and the ongoing problem of illegal immigrants — who realistically and for fairly obvious reasons are not going to be given health care at public expense. But the weak mandate worked okay, and the program got off the ground. Then Donald Trump killed the individual mandate in his first term, but we only got two years of that until the Biden administration came in during Covid with increased subsidies as an alternative to the mandate — carrots rather than sticks. Now in Trump 2, the Obama-era stick is gone and the Biden-era carrot is gone and things are unraveling.

“People are mad about health insurance” tends to make people want to elect Democrats, and I think if Democrats win in 2028, they will revert to the Biden-era solution for this of stepped-up subsidies, perhaps with or without a public option, to try to reduce costs. I think that if Republicans were interested in thinking more creatively about their political and substantive weaknesses on health care as an issue, they might want to consider coming back around to Romney’s original thinking on this. Fixing the marketplaces by bringing back an individual mandate would be a lot cheaper than doing it in a progressive-coded “all carrots” way. And if anyone on the Republican side is interested in this, I would encourage smart Democrats to work with them on it.

Tejas: I frequent Slow Boring and several other Substacks and writers in the center-left, pro-abundance movement, and I have a good understanding of this space that I largely agree with. However, I want to remain open-minded, battle test my beliefs, break down echo chambers, and be well versed accordingly. Do you have any progressive, or right-wing writers you can recommend? Writers who are substantive, intellectually honest, and effective at challenging beliefs, even if their logic, methods, or conclusions are not always what you’d endorse?

My acid test on this is, “Does this writer bring to my attention accurate factual information that I didn’t already know?” Lyman Stone, Cremieux Recueil, Charles Lehman, Veronique de Rugy, and Chris Pope are all right-wing people who dispense a lot of factual information that does not get widely discussed in left-of-center circles.

The difference between the right and the left in this regard is that the most prominent right-wing media personalities — Fox News hosts and the like — are extremely low quality, so you get better takes from lower-profile Twitter personalities and think tankers. On the left, mainstream media organizations do a good job of elevating quality people. The basic roster of New York Times columnists contains lots of people who are more progressive than I am but who are also good commentators. Chris Hayes is very smart, and so is Bhaskar Sunkara. If you want to know in general “what do smart left-wing people think about Topic X?” you can just look at the academic consensus on Topic X. There’s no particular need to go diving into non-obvious sources of information to find progressive viewpoints.

Ben Bartelmay: Hi I don’t have a specific question but my wife is expecting our first child in November (we don’t know what we’re having) and if there is a question you wish you asked a parent before you had your child that I should be thinking to ask please feel free to answer that. If it helps my wife and I are rich white homeowners in the Detroit Public Schools Community District (and I just read the Nikole Hannah-Jones piece yesterday). Or if you want to just list your favorite “Dad books” (thick history books/audiobooks) to recommend I’d appreciate that as well.

Welcome to the dad lifestyle! Beyond the obvious Robert Caro tomes, let me suggest that one good angle for the contemporary dad is to get steppe-pilled. You could maybe start with Kenneth Harl’s “Empires of the Steppes: A History of the Nomadic Tribes Who Shaped Civilization” or Jack Weatherford’s “Genghis Khan and the Making of the Modern World.” David Anthony’s “The Horse, the Wheel, and Language: How Bronze-Age Riders From the Eurasian Steppes Shaped the Modern World” gets you into the linguistic angles, and David Chaffetz’s “Raiders, Rulers, and Traders: The Horse and the Rise of Empires” explores some more economic history angles.

There’s so much happening out there in the vast Eurasian grasslands!

Mitchel Hochberg: Wave elections far more modest than this week’s build the bench for Democrats in elections to come. Which newly elected Representatives do you think could become stars and win statewide? Which newly-elected Senators and Governors could become party leaders and potential Presidential candidates?

It is hard to know the future. But just based on the results we’ve seen so far, Bobby Pulido should clearly be running for the U.S. Senate in Texas in 2030 and Ben McAdams should run statewide in Utah. I think Pia Dandiya should have strong statewide potential in Florida, though obviously that’s a tough state.

For new presidential aspirants, I think it’s a timeline question. If Democrats have a non-leftist nominee in 2028 and that nominee loses, then Abdul El-Sayed is clearly well-positioned to run in 2032. Conversely, if Democrats nominate a Gavin Newsom type in 2028 and lose, then I think Rob Sand instantly becomes my favorite as a true moderate option for 2032. But if the Democrats win in 2028, then it’ll be a whole new landscape by 2036 or 2040 and all bets are off.

As a strong believer in the political power of do-nothing governors, I think both Gina Hinojosa and Keisha Lance Bottoms are likely to be kind of surprisingly popular as they wrangle with split state legislatures. That could lead either of them to become nationally prominent if they seek that kind of coverage.

Alex Smith: Is there police tech you would like to see adopted at scale that is (mostly?) politically feasible? Also, should agencies drop Flock ALPR and just use Axon ALPR? How much of “Flock” is Flock and how much is ALPR? Any sense for that?

My main interpretation of camera politics is that the dirty secret of American criminal-justice politics is that there’s a block of conservatives out there who don’t really want America to be a low-crime country. They want criminal behavior sort of corralled into low-income Black neighborhoods and to then have personal security provided by a mix of NIMBYism, driving everywhere, and a little light racial profiling, but they’re not interested in the question of why New York and California have systematically lower levels of shooting and murder than Texas. You add those people to the soft-on-crime left and you get an anti-camera horseshoe.

That said, I do think there are some specific concerns people have with the idea of police officers abusing access to the cameras (a valid thing to worry about but I think easily addressed) and with the way that Flock exists as a national system of cameras, which contravenes the American tradition of policing localism. In other words, they’re not just selling equipment to individual police departments; they’re building this large privately owned system in partnership with various police departments.

Would people feel better about departments using different vendors? About the F.B.I. building its own national camera network by buying equipment? I’m not really sure. Personally, I think catching a large share of criminals so that our deterrence improves and ultimately we have fewer people in prison would make life in the United States dramatically better. So I’m broadly out of sympathy with anti-Flock politics, which I think in both its left-wing and right-wing variants is just oddly indifferent to the upside.

Bryan T. Mathew: Matt, I’m curious how you think about a Rahm Emanuel presidential candidacy in 2028.

On substance, Emanuel seems fairly close to your preferred approach to Democratic politics. But you’ve also argued that moderates should worry less about defeating the left in intraparty fights and more about getting competitive candidates, regardless of faction, to adopt moderate positions.

Given Emanuel’s generational positioning, political baggage, and association with an earlier era of Democratic politics, do you see an actual candidacy as helpful or counterproductive to that project? Could he play a constructive role in shifting the party’s policy agenda even if he’s not the right person to lead it?

I like Rahm, but I also find his apparent determination to run for president to be a little bit frustrating. I think it’s incredibly obvious that over and above ideological shifts in the electorate, there is a lot of desire for “new leadership” and “generational change.” There’s also a lot of desire from Democratic Party voters to have party unity and not just refight endless old factional battles. If an Emanuel candidacy were to start to gain traction, it would close off a moderate lane for any fresher faces and then almost immediately turn not into a debate about the future of the Democratic Party but into a re-litigation of controversies from the Clinton and Obama administrations and arguing about Laquan McDonald.

The role that I’d like to see Emanuel play is as a mentor to some of the younger moderate figures in the party. Whether that’s senators exploring presidential runs or the bumper crop of freshman Blue Dogs in the House, there are tons of people who have broadly good instincts but could benefit from the wisdom of someone who has served in White Houses and in congressional leadership and in local government.

Evan Ferguson: Hi, I am a long-time subscriber from a fairly blue state (Oregon) with a very left wing local Democratic Party, of which I am a member. Both of our Senators, to my knowledge, plan to vote against BAAJA. I have never written a letter to a member of Congress before but plan to this week. I doubt I can change their minds, particularly the more left wing of the two, but I want my letters to be as effective as possible. Do you have any advice for me or specific arguments you think might be persuasive? I do not know any politicians personally so it is tough to tailor my arguments to the specific individuals.

I don’t think the content of these kinds of letters makes that much difference relative to their basic existence. The main way a legislator is going to think about this is:

  • Labor likes the bill, but the greens don’t, and the greens’ preference intensity is higher.

  • Voting yes on bipartisan bills is good for my moderate credibility with swing voters, but voting no on a bill Trump likes is good for shoring up my left flank.

  • The smart highbrow pundits like the bill, but the balance of constituent calls is against the bill.

This is a world where a reasonable senator could go either way. And under the circumstances, any pro-BAAJA calls or letters just help shift the balance of considerations toward BAAJA. I think the best argument is probably just, “This is a bill that is good for affordability (not just energy but housing and transportation too) and it reduces emissions and it’s kind of crazy on its face that you’d vote against something like that just because professional environmental lawyers feel sad about filing fewer lawsuits.” But really say whatever’s in your heart.

Chris Nolte: Is now a good time (or a particularly good time) to push the incoming Democratic majority to ditch the filibuster? The fact that the President is of the other party means that Congress won’t be able to run rampant, which might make it more palatable politically. But on the other hand it would allow Dems to cut deals with Trump that would not get 60 votes.

This is not how senators think, but I think the best time to scrap the filibuster is at a time like 2027 when the substantive stakes are low. We can debate if it actually makes sense for 60 votes to be required for a law to pass the Senate. The answer is that it does not. The practical consequence is that some Democratic message bills will make it to Trump’s desk that otherwise wouldn’t and he’ll veto them, but it’s not a huge deal. It also becomes marginally easier for bipartisan deals to pass, though it’s usually not that hard to get bipartisan deals to pass. So just kill it now and clear the decks to legislate in the future.

The problem is that senators really love their precious filibuster because it lets them not vote on stuff.

I was saying to a friend who works on the Hill that senators should practice in the mirror saying, “I hear what you’re saying and I respect you but I’m not going to vote for that because I think it’s a bad idea.” He told me they are nowhere near the headspace of embracing that. They love to be able to sign on to bills that can’t possibly pass and then reassure people who don’t like the bill that it can’t possibly pass. Or else to tell proponents of some bill that they’d love to do X, Y, and Z but it’s just not possible given the filibuster. I think this dynamic of constant lying and ducking of responsibility is bad for democracy, but I’m not a senator.
