Marlon James and the Horrors of Vanishing

In “The Disappearers,” the Booker Prize winner returns to Jamaica with a furious epic about homophobia, violence, and survival.

Ron Charles
Sep 1
 
READ IN APP
 

The Tuesday Review

You’ll have to read Marlon James’s new novel, The Disappearers, wearing asbestos gloves. The pages of this incomparable epic are fueled by desire and torched by rage.

The author, who won a Booker Prize for A Brief History of Seven Killings and then began a trilogy of mind-blowing African fantasies, has returned to Jamaica, his homeland, for what he’s calling his most personal novel.

The Disappearers is a story about living and dying, particularly dying, in the toxic homophobia of the late 20th century, when James was growing up in Kingston begging God to cleanse him. He doesn’t appear in the novel — “I am still lucky,” he quips, “never to have been one of the characters in a Marlon James novel” — but its spirit is informed by his own closeted life before he left the Caribbean. “Whether it was in a plane or a coffin,” he wrote in 2015, “I knew I had to get out of Jamaica.”

Out — and out of Jamaica — for years now, James has produced a book no less ambitious than Beloved, a novel to reckon with the ectoplasm of suffering and restore the presence of vanished people. Just as Toni Morrison’s classic shifted our understanding of the experience and legacy of slavery, so The Disappearers aspires to explode complacency and sear in the minds of readers the horrors of living in a world hostile to homosexuality.

I asked Marlon James, “What could fiction say about your experience of growing up gay in Jamaica that memoir couldn’t?”

This is an omnivorous novel, a book straining against its covers to contain the whole racial, economic, and political construction of sexual prohibitions imported from Britain and fermented in the Jamaican heat. But the length of The Disappearers — some 616 pages — is only its most obvious challenge. Far greater are the difficulties posed by its eclectic structure, its mercurial style, and its explicit content, an abiding issue of too-muchness that similarly freighted James’s previous novel, Moon Witch, Spider King.

If you know anything about The Disappearers before you start it — and you should — you know that it’s about a group of gay men brutally attacked by a mob. But like any Pearl Harbor story, this one delays that crisis as long as possible.

The first quarter of the novel presents a series of diary entries written by 18-year-old Eddy Porteous. Smart, sassy, and noticeably light-skinned, Eddy drops us right into rehearsals for a play, an adaptation of John Herbert’s Fortune and Men’s Eyes, about prison sexual abuse. Their snobby director intends to “blow open the whole fragile myth of Jamaican male sexuality and homophobia,” but so few volunteers risk showing up that auditions are just a matter of handing out the six parts and badgering everyone to be quiet.

Although they’re all Black gay men, these amateur actors hail from diverse worlds, all fascinating to Eddy, who has endured a year of such inventive bullying that he’s decided to start over. Among the cast are a couple of office workers, a wealthy shipping owner, a young Asian-looking man who’s distractingly handsome, and Jordan Brown, who lives in his car and confesses that doing this play is better than spending “all summer being terrified of AIDS.” Each of them has negotiated his own precarious method of passing.

Eddy is the diary writer few are and every nosy reader craves, a genius at scene-setting with a playwright’s sensibility for dialogue. Trapped in a panopticon of relatives and schoolmates on the lookout for a limp wrist, a suspicious lilt, or an incriminating choice in music, he started this journal to unload all his forbidden thoughts, but it mostly looks outward. “This is an exercise,” he writes. “Watch, observe, report,” which is clearly the impulse behind this novel, too. The result is a portrait of a life titillated by erotic pleasures but teetering on the edge of peril.

Kingston is a culture obsessed with homosexuality — with identifying it, condemning it, and rooting it out. Stories in the news about anti-gay violence, even murder, press against Eddy’s lungs. “I don’t think about it a lot,” he claims, unconvincingly, “but people want to kill us. Us, I’m a part of an us that people just want to beat to death. Everybody wants to kill us.”

His involvement with the play provides a rare opportunity to talk frankly with other gay men, to commiserate, to joke, even sometimes to encourage each other — all conveyed in the sharp, snapping lines of Eddy’s journal. And through his entries we get to know these men, too, in their quirky, hopeful endurance.

That’s all shattered one night when men wielding pipes and cricket bats march into the rehearsal room and carve every stage direction onto the stripped bodies of these actors. The beating and torture of Jordan and his fellow volunteers make for a singularly shocking scene. One has his eyes gouged out. Another, raped with a tree branch, dies on the spot. I provide these few details only to make clear that this is one of the most graphic and disturbing novels I’ve ever read.

The men rehearsing Fortune and Men’s Eyes are people we’ve come to know and care about through Eddy’s diary, but his voice falls away just before the assault begins. Instead, we experience this atrocity through a ghastly collage of statements from the “blackjacks” themselves. Their fragmentary testimonies of righteous anger pour down the pages like lava. Brimming with confidence, thrilled by the fear they inspire, they mock, they condemn, they sing, and they quote the Bible while wailing away on their victims. “Kill a man with a high voice,” one of them shouts. “Kill a man who float when he walk. Kill a fish. Kill Boy George. Kill Rock Hudson. Kill a man in short-shorts. Kill a man with no girlfriend.”

It’s a lot — everything smeared with blood and viscera; the air filled with the sounds of breaking bones and unearthly screams. Even a couple of the blackjacks are stunned by their comrades’ savagery. You’ll finish this brief chapter grateful to have survived it.

It’s at this point that James makes one of several daring maneuvers. The Disappearers picks up six years later in the voice of Jordan Brown, the bookish actor Eddy was closest to. The competition is intense, but this section is the best thing James has ever written. It’s here, in Jordan’s narrative, winding hypnotically between exhaustion and outrage, self-loathing and fury, that we experience the corrosive effects of trauma in a country where anti-gay violence isn’t a crime; it’s “a national service.”

Together with Eddy, Jordan sets out to find some of their fellow actors who disappeared after the assault, at which point the book shifts into a kind of mystery novel. What’s fascinating, though, is how firmly the story resists a solution in favor of examining the meaning of this mystery. After all, “people go missing in Jamaica all the time and nobody will ever find them,” Jordan says. “Look at the many ways we vanish without a trace.” To be gay in a homophobic culture is to be regularly disappeared by AIDS or pious parents. To render oneself invisible is a survival tactic.

Across journal entries, letters, and recorded interviews, The Disappearers sometimes feels more committed to its narrators than to its readers. James allows the people who give voice to this long, multivalent story to speak in their own accents and patois, to follow their own erratic obsessions and fears, and even, sometimes, to venture beyond our comprehension. When one character says, “I am not even going to pretend to understand what that means,” I simply nodded in agreement.

That’s particularly true with Jordan. Disgusted with Christian hypocrisy and shattered by the violence rained down upon him, he hovers in a Hamlet-like state, torn between self-slaughter and revenge. How, he wonders, might the moral account be balanced by lashing out at everyone who’s ever hummed a homophobic song, recited Leviticus, thrown a burning tire around a gay boy’s neck, or gang-raped a lesbian? It’s a brilliant, terrifying act of imaginative spelunking into some of the darkest caves of the human psyche.

So, there’s no denying this is a monumental novel — already longlisted for this year’s Booker Prize — but is it a monument you want to climb? James’s commitment to erotic realism, to the mechanics of oral and anal sex, voluntary and violent, is total and relentless. For James, that explicitness is clearly an aesthetic and thematic choice. Inspired by the work of John Rechy, Jordan is exasperated with a world that demands gay men abjure promiscuity in favor of assuming a polite mirror image of straight domesticity. Fuck that, Jordan would say. The fluids are the message, and James won’t let us miss it.

But if there are plenty of sweaty moments of transgressive love, the novel is also an encyclopedia of abuse and degradation. I finished The Disappearers glad to have read it but equally glad to be done with it. To the extent that it’s a great novel, it’s also an ordeal.

What’s most surprising, given the book’s abiding terrors, is how James inches it erratically toward the light. Nothing can feel securely joyous in a novel that hoards so much suffering, that maps the taxonomy of hate with such precision, but the final two movements change both the tempo and the temperature of the story. The survival of a few characters and their eventual thriving arrive, by the end, like a desperate embrace.

Right now, somewhere in Texas, there’s a sweet high school senior being bullied to the brink of suicide. He’s unlikely to find this novel in his school library, but what if it could lead him through the valley of the shadow of death?

The Disappearers, by Marlon James (Riverhead, 616 pp., $32)
