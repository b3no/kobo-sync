Republicans did even worse than we warned

How did The Argument’s analysis hold up?

Lakshya Jain
Nov 4
 
READ IN APP
 

If you went into a coma and just woke up, congratulations. You just missed the tsunami of the century. Not since the Watergate midterms of 1974 has an incumbent party been routed this comprehensively.

There’ll be a lot of time to dissect and analyze the results. We won’t even know the full set of outcomes just yet — in Mississippi, there’s a very real chance that Scott Colom could beat incumbent Senator Cindy Hyde-Smith to give Democrats a 55th seat, which could grow to 56 if Mary Peltola clinches the win in Alaska (as she looks likely to do).

But we do know enough to give you a brief postmortem on how the models and the polls did. In particular, for subscribers to this magazine, it’s probably worth running through our coverage and modeling from this cycle.

I’m pretty happy with the way we nailed the story of the election.

On June 17, when both The Cook Political Report and Sabato’s Crystal Ball still had more House seats leaning Republican than Democratic, we said the experts were wrong. Our model gave Democrats an 86% chance of winning the House, even after mid-decade redistricting. The experts spent the rest of the fall inching towards our conclusions (Cook went from a 9-19 seat gain in early October to 12-25 seats three weeks later). By Election Day, every major expert was saying what we had pointed out all along: The Democrats were nearly certain to win the House.

Then there’s our polling. Since September 2025, we’ve been correctly flagging that it was shaping to be a blue wave — and as the country’s trust in Donald Trump collapsed in the midst of the Iran War, our polling also picked up on the impending blue tsunami.

Back in April, even before we released a likely voter model, our survey found Democrats with a significant persuasion and enthusiasm advantage. We had Democrats up by 6 with registered voters and by 10 with voters who said they were likely to vote, which would have been the biggest midterm blue wave since at least the 1980s.

In fact, our final poll was D+11 with likely voters, which was more than 2 percentage points bluer than the Silver Bulletin’s generic ballot average of D+8.9.

At times, this number made me worried – Republican-friendly polling misses had made many people skeptical of Democratic-friendly forecasts. But our goal and guiding principle has always been to release the data that we get, and to be transparent on everything we do.

As it turns out, there was no Republican-friendly polling miss (which is another feather in the cap for the principle of “let the data do the talking”).

In fact, polls underestimated DemocratsParty by more than they underestimatedRepublicans in 2016 and 2024, and about as much as in2020. At the time of writing this article, (it’s very late) Democrats are on track to win the national popular vote by 13 points. Our poll still undershot that, but it was closer than most and well ahead of the averages. Readers of The Argument would have been early in seeing this blue wave coming.

Our poll will come considerably closer than most did — and notably outperform the aggregates — so I’d like to call our polling a success story for the cycle. In particular, the story that we told to explain the margins, where Democrats cleaned up with Hispanic, working-class, and young voters, was also substantiated by the exit polls and the election results.

So that’s a plus for us.

What about our model?

There are a few ways to evaluate this, but broadly, even though models (and polls! And everyone else!) undershot Democrats, I still think the models did a really good job of telling the “story of the cycle.”

Back when experts called for Democratic gains of 2-10 House seats, we came out swinging and told you that they were likely to pick up far more due to a significantly bluer environment than anyone was pricing in.

Even at the end, our forecast remained quite bullish on Democratic odds to take the House and Senate, giving Democrats an average count of 239 seats in the House and 53 in the Senate, with a 92% chance of flipping the former and a 77% chance of flipping the latter. Some of our more bullish calls included placing the Kansas Senate race at Leans Democratic and Texas Senate at Likely Democratic, both of which were ratings that were far more optimistic for Democrats than it seemed most people wanted to publicly be caught with. (For context, our 81% probability on Talarico’s victory was the most bullish that any model or expert had for him. Even this undershot him — he won by 12.)

As it turns out, even this was an underestimation! We’ll probably end up 18 seats too low on the Democratic Party in the House, while being 2 seats too low in the Senate (which could be 3 if Scott Colom pulls off the upset).

We’ll update you as the results pour in, but at this point, looking at where prediction markets see this race shaping up, I’m confident that we built one of the best — if not the best — congressional models in the country.

Assuming the candidates currently favored by The New York Times and prediction markets win their remaining races¹, our preliminary calculations put The Argument/Split Ticket model first among the five public forecasts compared below, with the lowest combined Brier score² — a standard measure of probabilistic forecast accuracy.

We couldn’t be more grateful to have run through the cycle with you — and I think we did a good job, if I do say so myself.
