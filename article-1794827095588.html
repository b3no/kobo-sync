What I got wrong about the midterms

Complications avoided, a big turnout advantage, and some candidates I underrated

Matthew Yglesias
Nov 16
∙
Paid
 
READ IN APP
 

I got a lot right about the 2026 midterms, and I feel like many Slow Boring positions have been vindicated — most notably the argument that winning a Senate majority is possible if and only if Democrats make some moves to the center on cultural issues.

We also called some small, specific things correctly, such as that nominating Matt Dunlap would cost Democrats ME-2 and that Josh Turek was the right candidate for the Iowa Senate race. There’s been an incredible amount of beefing on the internet about the fact that Abdul El-Sayed won comfortably in Michigan while putting up a mid-sized underperformance, but this is exactly what I said all along would happen.

That said, it’s my job to comment on issues in the news despite uncertainty, and when you do that, you get things wrong.

My view is that the only way to improve your judgment is to try to be honest with yourself about errors. There are prominent members of the media who I see out there every day full of bluster claiming to have been right about everything. To me, people like that are basically Elizabeth Holmes telling Nathan Fielder she’s never lied — it’s a huge red flag that means your heart and mind are completely closed and you’re not learning anything.

Obviously, in a sense, basically everyone was “wrong,” and the final national popular vote was about D+13 rather than D+9 as the polling averages said.

What I would say is that even though this was a decent-sized polling error, it was still a victory for the general concept of trusting the polls. Not because polling is precisely accurate but because errors happen in both directions — they don’t always underestimate Republicans — and it’s not possible to predict in advance which way they will be off. Nobody has come up with a reliable way of doing better than looking at polling averages. What I want to focus on today, though, are more interesting mistakes: things that destabilize my worldview a little and will cause me to adjust my takes going forward.

This focuses mostly on the role of turnout, the success of a particular way of ducking inconvenient issues, and the ways that these two things maybe interacted in putting a few races on the table that didn’t seem winnable to me.

Turnout seems to have mattered a lot

Everyone is aware that turnout matters in politics. But my sense is that after almost every election, many people massively overstate its significance in explaining the outcome. The facts are not currently 100 percent known yet, and we’ll get a better picture in the weeks and months to come, but that seems to have not been the case this year.

As my friend and fellow turnout skeptic Charlotte Swasey says, the big thing the polls missed this year is they were trying so hard not to undersample Republicans that they missed Republicans legitimately just not voting.

Slow Boring readers knew that Democrats had been benefiting from differential turnout in special elections, but my dominant interpretation of that was that the advantage would evaporate in a higher-profile midterm. That does not appear to have been the case. Instead, at least in Texas and Georgia, where we have the most complete data, Democrats essentially got to run in a special election environment. Fewer Kamala Harris voters showed up this year than in 2018, but dramatically fewer Trump 2024 voters showed up.

We’ll have to find out more about the details, but obviously this shaped the battlefield in an important way. Interesting nuance!

Democrats end up talking a lot about turnout any time they lose, which generally strikes me as a kind of cope, and it’s one reason I’m generally so dismissive of people who just want to talk about turnout. For some reason, though, both JD Vance and Ted Cruz wrote long tweets recapping the election in which they specifically disavowed turnout-based explanations, even though turnout really did explain why the blue wave turned into a tsunami. I don’t know what the deal with that is.

The optimistic take for Republicans is that turnout is always higher in a presidential year, so these voters should show back up again. The optimistic take for Democrats, though, is that non-participation is often a halfway house on the road to converting. If Democrats can put up a fresh face in 2028 who people don’t have a years-long relationship with (i.e., not Harris, Newsom, or A.O.C.), then we could see a bunch of these demoralized, disengaged Trump voters actually flip blue.

The politics of evasion

For years — for decades really — Democrats have been dogged by the idea that they can navigate culture war politics not by moving to the center but by just ducking the question or pivoting, arguing that these topics are distractions from the real economic issues of the day. My bias is toward believing this evasion strategy almost always fails and that candidates need to either defend their views in a way that persuades people (very hard but clearly possible in some cases) or else just adopt the more popular view.

But Gina Hinojosa in Texas seems to have really pulled off the evasion strategy in a pretty effective way.

She did her “Pronouns” ad to signal moderation on transgender issues without committing herself to anything specific. She indicated opposition to Texas’s draconian abortion ban without addressing any details about how far she’d go with this — again using a personal story to signal some sympathy with culturally conservative sentiments without making any policy concessions to the right. On the death penalty, she made a Slow Boring move and implied that she has no desire to change Texas’s laws. And she had some praise for Trump’s work to shut down illegal border crossings but stood by her fairly liberal record in the Texas legislature.

Greg Abbott hit her hard as a gun grabber, and she just didn’t say anything about gun control. She was asked at the Texas Tribune Festival specifically about stuff like Texas rules barring pride clubs at public schools and public-school bathroom issues, and she simply refused to answer.

That’s sort of exactly why I think these evasion strategies don’t work. Whether or not Hinojosa is “focused on” culture war issues versus economics, she’s running for governor, and these K-12 school management topics are within her area of responsibility. She should just pick some progressive changes to promise (so L.G.B.T.Q. activists view her as more progressive than Abbott) and some to disavow (so swing voters are reassured).

Instead, she ducked — and it worked really well!

If she’d asked my advice about this (which of course she did not), I would have added that not only was evasion unlikely to work, it was pointless because if she won, the G.O.P. legislature would obviously just jam her up with message bills on these issues. But Democrats flipped one house of the legislature. That means both conservative and progressive bills stay off her desk.

She really will be able to focus on what she wants to focus on — though she’s still going to have to address these school management questions.

Randy Villegas

The situation in CA-22 was, in some ways, structurally similar to ME-2 in that it pitted a moderate candidate backed by the Democratic Congressional Campaign Committee (Jasmeet Bains in California, Joe Baldacci in Maine) against a Progressive Establishment candidate (Randy Villegas in California, Matt Dunlap in Maine).

I like Bains more than Villegas on the merits, and I still wish she won. But I collapsed these two cases in my mind and wrote Villegas off on electability grounds.

He actually did fine-to-great depending on how you want to account for district demographics, making it one of many races where a Latino Democrat in a heavily Latino district rode a huge Latino wave to victory.

I don’t think there was any good reason to doubt that he could pull this off, and I would even go so far as to say that this was an inappropriate D.C.C.C. intervention. The national party should intervene selectively to get moderate nominees in critical races — like Baldacci in ME-2 or Johnny Garcia in TX-35, who they backed against an antisemitic conspiracy theorist. But progressives are always going to worry about dirty pool here, and it makes everyone look bad to intervene against a Villegas and then have him win.

South Carolina and Mississippi

I thought it was silly that people were asking national Democrats to invest money in the South Carolina and Mississippi Senate races. I really liked Scott Colom, but my take was more that Democrats should try to run candidates like that in the Carolinas and in Florida than that Democrats should play in Mississippi.

And Annie Andrews in South Carolina honestly struck me as a borderline grift. Some of the stuff she’s put out on social media over the years is squarely in “not even trying to win” terrain. I looked at this and thought, “There is absolutely no way someone like this is going to win in a state where Trump won 58 percent of the vote.”

In Mississippi, Trump got 61 percent. I thought maybe if you took Colom and his campaign and transposed it to a less red state and also blessed him with a terrible opponent like Darline Graham, he’d be viable. But Colom in Mississippi and Andrews in South Carolina? My view was let’s just spend in Iowa.

But Andrews, it turns out, kind of locked in down the stretch.

She called for an all-of-the-above energy strategy, disavowed socialism, argued that sports teams should be based on biological sex rather than gender identity, and said “Democrats have failed” on border security.

So there was certainly an opportunity here for me to be prescient and say, “Actually, Andrews could win because she did a lot of last-ditch moderation in response to polls showing it was kinda close and got over the top.”

But I did not say that and was not actually prescient, in part because I thought this was probably too little too late, given that South Carolina voters would likely be skeptical and her new positions didn’t really reflect her record. I think the 2026 midterms should cause most of us to update broadly in favor of flip-flopping as a strategy, and I include myself in that, even though I think I’ve always been more favorable to flip-flopping than most.

As for Colom, I am still shocked he won.

He ran a really smart campaign and seems great, but it’s still Mississippi, and I had previously believed the level of racial polarization in that electorate was impossible to overcome absent some huge scandal. I wonder if, when the data is in, this will turn out to be an epic collapse of white turnout.

The mystery of the groups

If you just paid attention to the 2026 campaigns, you’d think that the power of “the groups” over the Democratic Party had been completely obliterated.

Forget about the Slow Boring candidates and just look at El-Sayed, Troy Jackson, Peggy Flanagan, and other progressive winners.

None of these people talked a ton about climate change or promoting electric cars. They were viciously critical of ICE and Trump’s approach to interior enforcement, but none of them called for reopening asylum claims at the southern border. None of them “centered” racial justice in their campaign or used terms like BIPOC. Everyone seems to have agreed that Woke 1 was crazy. On the front lines, things were even bolder in terms of blowing off “groups”-think. Even Hinojosa, who didn’t really moderate on culture, did at least a little bit, and nobody tried to cancel her over it.

On the one hand, I feel semi-vindicated by this. On the other hand, I am genuinely surprised that any of this occurred without an actual fight. Only El-Sayed, in an interview with Ross Douthat, articulated what I think the actual issue is:

Douthat: Why didn’t the Biden administration enforce the border? What was the problem?

El-Sayed: I think the politics of it at the time were complicated.

Douthat: Complicated by what?

El-Sayed: I mean, complicated by a lot of advocacy, right? And I think we’ve learned the lesson. And I also think that it’s important for us to realize that — the idea that politicians don’t learn from what comes before them, because it was done by a different party, is kind of ridiculous. There are things that some Republicans have gotten right. It’s just that they also get a lot of things wrong. So learn from the things that they get right. And fix the things that they get wrong.

This is a much clearer articulation of not just the specifics of border security but of the broader structural dynamic than I heard from any of the candidates with a more moderate positioning.

What surprises me isn’t that El-Sayed can see the issue clearly — he’s probably the smartest of the new class of senators — but that the advocacy dogs just chose not to bark during the 2026 cycle.

I thought, based on my interactions with them, that they were unrepentant and that Democrats would need to learn the lesson that they need to tune these guys out. But because they chose to just shut up, it’s not clear to me whether El-Sayed is right that “We’ve learned the lesson.” The question — what do you do in the face of a lot of advocacy complicating your decision-making? — simply wasn’t posed.

I didn’t believe Democrats would be so lucky as to avoid that complication this cycle. And even after it happened, I don’t really believe it will happen again, given the realities of a large presidential primary field. But I’ve been wrong before, and maybe I’ll be wrong about this.
