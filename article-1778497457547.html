Hantavirus is a reminder we should prepare for the next pandemic

We’re going backward instead of forward

Matthew Yglesias
May 11
∙
Paid
 
READ IN APP
 

When I heard about human-to-human transmission of hantavirus on a cruise ship, I made sure that my household box of emergency supplies was where I thought it was. That box includes a brand of elastomeric respirator that I saw recommended by a biosecurity expert on a podcast in October. I later went online to find the link to the company so I could share it with others, only to discover that it recently went out of business.

And that I think about sums up the problem with pandemics.

I checked with knowledgeable medical professionals and none of them seem particularly worried about this particular hantavirus outbreak, which is great. But when I start to see headlines telling people not to panic, I get worried.

Not because you should panic about hantavirus, but because panicking is — by definition — not the correct response to any situation. If you’re facing an imminent threat, you still shouldn’t panic because panicking is never correct. So by the same token, while it’s good to hear that the objective threat level from this outbreak is by most accounts lower than I initially feared, just issuing instructions not to panic doesn’t add very much.

The implication is that everyone should just continue going about their day without changing anything. And it’s possible that’s correct advice for you and your family.

But my sense is that most American households have in fact under-reacted to the threat of novel virus outbreaks (which is perhaps why companies making well-reviewed respirators go out of business). And it’s definitely the case that the American government has under-reacted.

For any given novel outbreak, the initial read is almost always going to be that the odds of it becoming a deadly pandemic are low. That’s just the nature of things.

But we’re in a structural situation where, due to population growth, prosperity, and increased connectivity, the odds of new pandemic outbreaks are mechanically rising. Separately, technological improvements mean the odds of engineered pandemics are also rising. We ought to be taking aggressive countermeasures to get ahead of these risks, and we largely aren’t.

So, yes, of course don’t panic. This hantavirus outbreak will probably be fine.

But sooner or later an outbreak will not be fine, and we’re not taking appropriate steps to avert or mitigate that.

The hantavirus situation

U.S.-based pandemic-heads probably know hantavirus as an illness that people in the rural West sometimes get because it crosses over from the western deer mouse, typically through aerosols associated with rodent saliva, urine, or feces rather than from another human. The information about human-to-human transmission during the outbreak on the MV Hondius thus sounded very alarming to me.

In reality, though, hantavirus has long come in a number of different strains. The one people get in the U.S., the Sin Nombre virus, is different from the Andes strain that infected people on the ship. The Andes virus has been known to transmit from human to human for at least 30 years.

What’s novel about the recent outbreak is its location, not the virus itself. The outbreak happened on a cruise ship that had been to South America rather than in South America. There’s no terrifying new mutation here.

But while the Andes virus transmits from person to person, it’s not a particularly efficient spreader. And though the long incubation time makes tracing annoying, it’s not known to spread asymptomatically. And last but not least, symptoms are almost always severe, which is of course bad for the patients but also means they’re not likely to be walking around shedding hantavirus, thinking they just have allergies.

Cramming a bunch of people together on a cruise ship creates nearly ideal circumstances for an inefficient virus to spread, and this is an unbelievably shitty situation for the people on board that ship. But nothing so far indicates that we’re poised for massive global spread of this virus. Epidemiologists Katelyn Jetelina and Marisa Donnelly talked through this last week, and their conversation is mostly reassuring.

What’s less reassuring is that every new person who picks up a virus is an opportunity for the virus to evolve and get better at spreading. This ship outbreak is not the result of a big, scary new evolutionary leap, but it could be the circumstances in which such a leap takes place.

This probably won’t happen, because such leaps are always somewhat unlikely. Evolution is an essentially random process; it’s not like the virus sits around “trying” to become more efficient at human-to-human transmission. You just get mutations, most of which are neutral or unhelpful to the virus, but there’s always a chance that a mutation goes in the other direction.

And the structural odds of breakthroughs are going up.

Pandemic risk is rising

The debate over where Covid-19 came from was never 100 percent settled, but it’s either a crossover from an animal disease or something that escaped from a laboratory.

The risk of both kinds of events is in a structural state of increase.

The global population is much higher than it used to be and continues to grow. And more people means more opportunities for crossovers. There’s also more livestock than ever before in order to feed those people, which again means more opportunities for crossovers.

The larger population is also much more mobile on a per capita basis than ever before. The U.S. keeps setting records for air travel, as does Europe. In poorer countries such as China and India where economic growth has been faster, the rate of increase in long-distance travel has been more dramatic. But even in poorer countries with less growth, we’re still seeing more people use cars and more of the people who can’t afford cars using motorcycles or e-bikes.

I’m the last person to be a doomer about growth. It’s just the case that almost everything in life has some downsides, and the growth of the global economy and increasing global connectivity mean many more opportunities for viruses to spread.

Medical technology has certainly improved and, in the aggregate, more people are living longer and healthier lives.

But the hantavirus outbreak is a scary reminder that novel infections to which people have no acquired immunity continue to be a tricky problem, even with access to good medical care.

We need to be investing in technology for prevention, and we largely aren’t.

Meanwhile, on the engineered pathogen side, there is a raging and highly politicized debate about this.

The 2024 election brought to power people who are (basically correctly) enraged about the way the scientific establishment handled this debate in 2020 and also certain (I think much less persuasively) that Covid-19 definitely did, in fact, originate in a lab leak.

But as Alina Chan, herself a major lab-leak proponent, noted back on April 9, the Trump administration embraced the lab-leak hypothesis, but has not actually done anything to tighten the screws on dangerous research.

The administration’s interest in this topic, in other words, more or less begins and ends with wanting to go after Anthony Fauci and his colleagues. It’s not engaged on the substantive public-policy question.

I would not go as far as Lawrence Gostin in saying the origins debate is pointless, but the people who are more hung up on this than they are on the forward-looking policy question are missing the forest for the trees. Dario Amodei, the C.E.O. of Anthropic, says that within the next six to 12 months, frontier A.I. products will pose a major biorisk hazard and “we should be thinking about regulating A.I. the way you regulate cars and airplanes.”

Now say that’s all massively overhyped and we’re actually 10 years away from A.I. being that powerful — that’s not particularly reassuring. We need to deploy technology to harden our defenses.

We’re doing the opposite of what we should be

In narrow terms, an obviously important aspect of the current outbreak is that hantavirus is not new. It is believed to have affected soldiers during World War II and was plausibly the cause of ailments dating back to World War I, the American Civil War, and possibly far beyond that.

But it really got on the medical radar as a result of the Korean War — the name of the virus references the Hantaan River in Korea, where a major outbreak among soldiers occurred. There’s been research into a hantavirus vaccine, but little progress made.

The fundamental issue is that hantavirus infections are very rare, which means there’s not a particularly large or lucrative market for hantavirus vaccines and that conducting clinical trials would be challenging.

There’s a Korean vaccine called Hantavax that doesn’t work very well, plus a few research programs, but no really major effort. Moderna and a Korean university have apparently been working on an mRNA vaccine since 2023, but it seems like a relatively low-priority undertaking.

This basic structural situation is why vaccines are such a good candidate for a public-sector push. When vaccines were first being developed, people made them for diseases that were already endemic in the population.

That made a lot of sense as a question of prioritization. It also basically aligned with market incentives. The most valuable vaccines were for the most widespread and most damaging viruses. Running trials was straightforward.

But it doesn’t really make much sense to wait until a new virus has become a huge problem to start working on a vaccine. What we should do is take viruses that we know exist and do well-funded vaccine research now before they become a problem. Ideally, you want to develop broad-spectrum vaccines that could target an entire genus of viruses so you don’t just have protection against one strain — you have protection against new things that might emerge.

In addition to generously funding this kind of research, we should be creating clear pathways to test these vaccines in human challenge trials when possible so that testing is faster.

The Trump administration has been going in the other direction, halting funding for mRNA vaccine research and spreading distrust of vaccines in general. Trump has taken the U.S. out of the World Health Organization, so we’re not involved in coordinating the response to any outbreaks that happen around the world. And he certainly doesn’t have us contracting to pre-position high-quality personal protective equipment around the country or taking steps to prevent its makers from going broke due to lack of demand.

The odds are quite high that this hantavirus outbreak won’t be the next global pandemic. But the odds that it will be aren’t zero. And the odds that another pandemic will happen are incredibly high. We ought to be prepared.
