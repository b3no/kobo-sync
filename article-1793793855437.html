Eliot Pence on building a new defence industry for Canada

He speaks softly, he’s building big sticks, and he’s just getting started

Paul Wells
Nov 4
 
READ IN APP
 

Meet Ottawa Business Journal’s 2026 Newsmaker of the Year. The man Be Giant says is turning Canada’s defence procurement upside down. The guy who raised more cash in his Series A round than any other defence startup in Canadian history. The guy who left Washington to defend Canada’s Arctic. The guy who actually tries hard not to sound like all of that hype in an interview, but who’s working on stuff that’s inherently high-stakes, big-budget and potentially landscape-changing.

That would be Eliot Pence, the founding CEO of Dominion Dynamics. And you’re forgiven if you haven’t heard of that company, because 18 months ago nobody had.

Pence founded Dominion Dynamics in June 2025, raised $169 million in his first year, and is the subject of many Ottawa conversations these days. What’s Dominion Dynamics? It wants to build the fast-changing, bleeding-edge communications and analysis tech that will be central to the future of Canada’s defence, especially its Arctic and northern defence.

Oops, I accidentally made that sound interesting. Pence, in contrast, was born for the soft sell, and prefers to call the company a “systems integrator” or a “neoprime.” Which means it would be the company future governments call when they want a broad solution to any emerging threat or opportunity. A first-call solution-builder for a global game of Risk in which Canada’s ambitions are bigger than ever, but the problems are as stubborn as ever. Oops, I’m making it sound interesting again. It kind of comes with the territory.

Pence learned the trade in Washington DC at Anduril, the fast-growing US defence tech startup founded by Palmer Luckey, who looks like this and is tight with Pete Hegseth. He says he shares none of his former employer’s geostrategic goals, at least not that he’s got his own firm to run.

Pence’s own goals go well beyond waiting for the feds to send him thorny challenges. He wants Canada to recover the industrial capacity it surrendered decades ago, to get over a long-ingrained habit of buying defence tech off the rack from other countries that build better. “What is very clear from the Ukraine and Iran wars,” he tells me here, “is that national industrial capacity is a fundamental defensive capability.”

In this conversation, Pence explains why he thinks Canada needs its own military-industrial complex, what he learned from Silicon Valley, why Canadian capitalism needs more ambition, and how a company whose only real customer is the government can claim to be a model of entrepreneurial independence.

Over the short term, he says, “We’re not going to wait for a contract. We’re just going to build what we think the future is. And then when the government catches up to us, we’ll be there with a product.”

Over the longer term: "I want to list the company in five years. And in fact, we want to list it in three years. And I want to be the best, biggest defense company in North America. And I think we can do it."

That’s the scale of the ambition that makes Pence a figure of so much interest these days. It’s way too early to know whether he’ll succeed. But not too soon to sit him down and ask what he has in mind. I hope you enjoy this interview.
