Would You Still Be Unafraid of Aging It Involved Looking Old?

It's great to bust stereotypes about aging--but at some point, "accepting aging" has to mean letting go of looking young and hot.

At only thirty-six, I feel a bit silly writing anything about aging at all. I’ve always held the belief that you can’t really know about how you’ll feel about aging until you’re actually old, and despite everyone over thirty reaching certified chopped unc status with the zoomers, we really aren’t that old.

But I see women my age—and younger!—write about “aging tips” as if they had already been through the trenches of menopause, osteoporosis, and season 64 of Criminal Minds. And 99% of the time, these women are not musing on the wisdom and life experiences that come with age, or even ways to stay healthy and live to see great-grandchildren. They’re literally just talking about hotness: currently being “hot for their age,” doing things to remain hot, being hotter than when they were younger (guys, I literally look the same as I did when I was 27, two years ago!) and being unafraid in the face of aging because—you guessed it—they are confident that they will stay hot. And they will stay hot gracefully. Unlike all those ridiculous older women on the red carpet who, for some insane reason, choose to get cosmetic surgery, they will glide into old age looking effortlessly and eternally thirty-eight. Geez, why didn’t I think of that?!

It was Mother’s Day this past weekend, which meant many denizens of the Internet took it upon themselves to post photos of their mothers for all to see, and, you guessed it—several women posted how hot their moms look for their age and how, as a result, they aren’t afraid of aging. INSPIRING: This 28-year-old isn’t scared to get old because her mother still looks forty!

Look, I’m not above vanity. For several years in a row, I kept my waist and hip measurements in a spreadsheet so I could optimize for peak waist-hip ratio. I used to wear a steel-boned corset to the gym, which was…a choice, I suppose. Although I still haven’t opted for any injectables, I have been wearing retinol since I was nineteen. All this to say: I’m not above wanting to look young and hot. But we should be honest with ourselves about that. It’s giving the same cognitive dissonance as, “I don’t pay someone to rip my pubes out for icky men, I do it for my sensory disorder that only applies to pussy hair, so it’s actually feminist and self-actualizing.” I’m aware this makes me the friend who’s too woke and the friend who’s not woke enough, but so be it.

Celebrating the youthful beauty of particularly lucky (or rich) older women is not the same thing as busting any kind of ageism stigma, and it certainly has nothing to do with celebrating aging or being unafraid of it. In order to fully accept aging—something I believe very few young people have actually done—you have to accept eventually not being hot.

I get where some of this impulse comes from. Media about older women often paints them as completely undesirable and devoid of sexuality. And sexuality is important! When I published my findings that married women over 40 were having the best sex, I was disappointed to see how many people were completely incredulous that such a thing could be possible, including older men. And yes, menopause can tank one’s sex drive, but there’s a reason that STIs are running rampant in retirement homes. You may not want to think about the elderly getting their freak on, but it happens, and I for one hope that they’re enjoying themselves. So yes, I completely understand the urge to push back against the idea that women become sexually irrelevant after a certain age. (This is doubly true because if you spend enough time on Twitter, you will be under the impression that “a certain age” is twenty-five, not fifty-five.)

Plus, as a woman who loves fashion and probably always will—yes, I find it inspiring to see older women (not just moderately older than me, but older than my mother) still looking cool, graceful, elegant, and, yes, sexy, in creative and stylish outfits. I think this content is fine and there should be more of it! But there is a huge difference between broadening the idea of what it means to be an older woman—showing us that being sixty doesn’t necessarily mean looking like Strega Nona—and being “unafraid of aging.” Images of svelte, likely surgically-enhanced older women, are aspirational. Posting a photo of a top 1% attractive older lady and saying that she is a reason not to fear aging is like posting a photo of Jeff Bezos as proof that nobody should fear starting their own company. Yes, some companies become wildly successful. Many more are moderately successful. Most aren’t successful at all. Being truly unafraid of starting your own company means accepting outcomes other than becoming Jeff Bezos.

If you are lucky enough to live to ninety-five, you will eventually no longer be hot. You may still be hot to your spouse or partner, but you will not be conventionally attractive to the vast majority of people. It’s normal to be a bit scared of this! But denying that reality is not the same as overcoming fear.

My hope is that most older women eventually just stop caring about hotness in the way they cared when they were twenty-nine. I understand it is hard for women (myself included) to imagine a world where hotness just doesn’t matter to us as much. In a world of “Hot Girl summer” and “Hot Girls for Zohran,” and “Hot Girls have IBS” a woman’s hotness must always be the forefront of her identity, no matter how progressive and feminist she might be. Attractive women are hired more often, for higher salaries. We are constantly reminded that men are more noble because they “only” care about looks, and none of that superfluous stuff like our interests or values. We know that men consistently rate the appearance of women in their early twenties the highest, no matter how old the men get. If a show or movie stars a female protagonist, it’s unlikely she will be anything other than beautiful, although they might put her in glasses to show that she’s just like us. Or she might be “curvy,” which really just means a size six instead of a size two, with an exaggerated hourglass shape that only 0.7% of women naturally have. When men focus on their hotness even half as much as a typical appearance-obsessed woman, they are branded mentally ill “looksmaxxers.” Especially if you are a heterosexual woman, it can be very difficult to fully separate the idea of your worth as a person from your looks and I don’t blame women for being unable to do that.

And yes, I worry about aging. But as I get older, I actually don’t worry about aging as often as I worry about other things. For example, I have Googled just the word “hantavirus” at least twenty-nine times in the past six hours, which at one point was autocorrected to “Santa virus” during a particularly unhinged text to my brother. (This message brought to you by OCD and my Twitter algorithm.) But I do still worry about aging sometimes. When I’m feeling particularly stressed about the jump-scare that is discovering the tired middle-aged woman in the car window reflection is me, and not some hag behind me, I think about the older women I know, none of whom are especially preoccupied with attempting to look thirty. From spending time with older women, I can only glean one thing, which is genuinely reassuring: most of them do not care about aging as much as I would expect them to care, and may care even less than I currently do.

I used to laugh at the idea that I would get older and suddenly stop caring about what people thought of me. Hearing “you will get older and stop giving a fuck” sounded like some stupid Reddit slogan that would be embroidered on the pillow of someone who does roller derby. This phenomenon would be a bit overstated in my case—I still want everyone to like me and think I’m great all the time—but I definitely care less. When I was twenty-four, I would meet a coworker’s friend at a happy hour, and immediately wonder if that person thought I had jowls. I constantly uploaded photos of my face to various scammy face score apps and kept track of all the plastic surgery I wanted to get. I also overanalyzed every social interaction—paranoia which may have been slightly warranted given that a lot of people actually didn’t like me, but the constant post-mortem didn’t help matters.

I still care about how I look and how people think of me, but yeah, it’s gotten better, and I hope that trend continues. Last year, I was roasted on Twitter for having some crepey skin on my stomach since having my two children, but at the time, I hadn’t even noticed this imperfection. If it had upset me as much as people assumed it did, I certainly wouldn’t have posted a picture where it was visible! But in this case, other people zoomed in on the imperfection more than I did. To this day, I don’t really care about it nor do I have plans to fix it. I cared about getting back into shape after having kids, I cared about not having a gaping abyss between my stomach muscles, but when it comes to a couple wrinkles that are only visible in certain positions, I’m sorry, I just have other things on my mind. Like the Santa virus. But I also know that if I had developed this imperfection in my twenties, it would have destroyed me.

Another thing about which I felt passionately in my twenties: going to the club. I used to genuinely worry about how pregnancy would hamper my ability to go to the club. I worried about scenarios where a bouncer wouldn’t let me into a club because I was visibly pregnant. I was convinced that pregnancy would suck specifically because it would prevent me from going to the club. I worried about how quickly after childbirth I’d be able to go to the club. I even stayed up at night panicking about “who would watch my baby when I went to the club.” It never occurred to me that I would get pregnant and no longer want to go to the club. In reality, my husband and I lost interest in clubbing before we had our first baby. Now, after two kids, we barely drink alcohol.

When I worry about whether or not I’ll still have a nice ass when I’m eighty, I have to remind myself of the fact that just ten years ago, I thought my top priority as a mom would be taking down five kamikaze shots at the club in a Nastygal bodycon dress with a variety of mesh cutouts. Perhaps I don’t need to push myself to be unafraid of aging. Maybe that will happen on its own, assuming I’m lucky enough to age.

I cannot expect your average woman—especially your average young woman, who is still expected to fulfill the role of “hot girl”—to stop caring about how she looks. But we do not serve ourselves by burying our heads in the sand and reassuring ourselves that aging is actually fine because we’ll still be hot. We won’t. Maybe some of us will stay hotter longer than others, but most of us will eventually reach a point where “hotness” is very much in the glaucoma-clouded eye of the beholder—and for a young woman, that’s much harder to accept without fear.
