In Which Winnie-the-Pooh Turns 100

A Bear of Very Little Brain still has a surprising amount to teach us

Ron Charles
Oct 9
 
READ IN APP
 

“Once upon a time, a very long time ago now, about last Friday, Winnie-the-Pooh lived in a forest all by himself.”

It has, indeed, been a very long time. In fact, this month marks the 100th anniversary of the publication of Winnie-the-Pooh.

Owl-eyed readers of Punch magazine might have first caught sight of Christopher Robin’s stuffed animal in A.A. Milne’s poem “Teddy Bear” in early 1924. But the story collection appeared more than two years later on Oct. 14, 1926.

Sales rose like a bear disguised as a rain cloud, eventually reaching — in our era of global merchandise and licensing — billions of dollars a year. And believe me, you don’t get a buzzing-noise like that, just buzzing and buzzing, without its meaning something.

The stories in Winnie-the-Pooh and The House at Pooh Corner, published two years later, have stuck to me like honey.

That could be because, like Pooh, my spelling is Wobbly, which is hard for a bear or a writer. “It’s good spelling but it Wobbles, and the letters get in the wrong places.”

But Milne’s influence on my life goes much deeper.

I have the clearest, sweetest memory — from almost 60 years ago — of my dad reading the story “In Which Eeyore Has a Birthday and Gets Two Presents.” When Piglet, running too fast with his red balloon, trips on a rabbit hole, we laughed so hard we could barely breathe:

“At first he thought that the whole world had blown up; and then he thought that perhaps only the Forest part of it had; and then he thought that perhaps only he had, and he was now alone in the moon or somewhere.”

One of the happiest moments of my life as a parent was reading that story to my own daughters and rediscovering the scene in which Piglet hands a small piece of damp rag to Eeyore for his birthday.

To this day, when my wife and I unearth some completely useless object around the house, one of us will quote Pooh calling it “a Useful Pot to put things in.”

Sometimes I think the patter in my house may be laced with more phrases from the Hundred Acre Wood than from Shakespeare or the Bible.

How many dinners end with my wife asking, à la Rabbit, “Sure you won’t have any more?”

To which I ask, with Pooh-like hopefulness, “Is there any more?”

“No.”

“I thought not.”

Confounded by some stranger’s behavior, one of us is bound to mutter, “You never can tell with bees.”

Weirdly enough, even my work as a reviewer is influenced by that silly old bear. In college, I first learned about critical theory from Frederick Crews’s very witty book The Pooh Perplex, which uses Milne’s stories to satirize Marxist, Freudian, and other strains of literary criticism.

For instance, in a chapter called “A Bourgeois Writer’s Proletarian Fables,” Crews writes, “It is hardly fortuitous that all the chief actors are property owners with no apparent necessity to work.”

But Winnie-the-Pooh had more formidable enemies than disgruntled Marxist critics.

Dorothy Parker famously couldn’t stomach Milne. In a short, mocking review of The House at Pooh Corner for The New Yorker, Parker quotes a few lines of dialogue in which Piglet and Pooh are working on a Good Hum. Unable to endure it for long, she breaks in:

“And it is that word ‘hummy,’ my darlings, that marks the first place in The House at Pooh Corner at which Tonstant Weader Fwowed up.”

Surely, Parker was a better poet than Pooh, but I’d take Milne’s comedy over hers. He’s subtler and kinder. Disney may have shifted the antics into primary colors, but in the original stories, the humor dwells in irony as grey as Eeyore’s fur. It’s there in Piglet’s anxiety, Owl’s pomposity, and Rabbit’s brusqueness. Wandering round and round “a small spinney of larch trees,” the characters are always negotiating that tension between what they want and what they know their friends want.

That’s the fundamental challenge of people bound together by affection: how to harmonize others’ desires with our own. It’s the great game of life that sends comedy and pathos drifting along side by side like Poohsticks in the Forest stream.

Shortly before Pooh gets stuck in Rabbit’s front door, he’s “walking along gaily, wondering what everybody else was doing, and what it felt like, being somebody else.”

What nobler function does literature have than to help us understand “what it felt like, being somebody else”?

That, after all, is what we need from “a Sustaining Book, such as would help and comfort a Wedged Bear in Great Tightness.”

We are all that Bear in a tight spot.◆

A Two-Star Rant

Of the 17 fundamental errors in modern history, No. 9 is the substitution of faux numerical precision for critical judgment.

Movies lost that battle long ago, when newspapers and magazines began assigning stars. Rotten Tomatoes and Metacritic suggest there’s something informative about aggregating critical responses into a number like 87.

If I ever want to take 100 of my closest friends to a movie, that would be helpful. Otherwise, what’s 87 to me but a prediction of how common my tastes are?

Books have largely avoided the humiliating numerical ratings that one might confer on breakfast cereals or a vacuum.

But Goodreads, which is owned by stats-obsessed Amazon, has long allowed users to give star ratings, and I have always done it and felt ashamed of it, the same way I did when giving myself a sprinkling of 3s and 4s on my self-evaluation at The Washington Post to affect just the right level of modest pride.

It’s data but rubbish — rank subjectivity preening with precision like a chicken in a tux.

Sweating over a thousand carefully chosen words for a review and then clicking on 4 stars always feels degrading — to me and the book.

On Monday, Goodreads got worse.

“Half stars are here!” the website announced. “And quarter stars, too.”

Starting this week, users can provide fractional stars, as though 3¾ articulates something more precise than the equally meaningless 3 or 4.

This change gives you “more ways to say exactly what you mean” because, after all, nothing captures your experience with a 400-page novel like a really thoughtful fraction.

Now, Goodreads claims, we’ve got “more nuance.”

That’s 92 percent inane.◆

Literary Prizes and Honors

Anne Carson won the Nobel Prize in Literature yesterday. The Swedish Academy gave the Canadian poet 5½ stars “for her bold and inventive oeuvre that, in playful dialogue with the classical tradition, has created new forms for contemporary literature.” Carson, 76, is the author of many award-winning works, including Autobiography of Red, The Beauty of the Husband, and Nox. She will receive the approximately $1.2 million prize in Stockholm on Dec. 10.

Edwidge Danticat’s Dèy is among the finalists for this year’s National Book Award for Fiction. (Surprisingly, Marlon James’s The Disappearers is not.) The winners — in fiction, nonfiction, poetry, translated literature, and young people’s literature — will be announced on Nov. 18 (full list of finalists).

The Kirkus Prize winners are:

  • Fiction: Tayari Jones’s Kin.

  • Nonfiction: Pamela Colloff’s Catch the Devil: A True Story of Murder, Deception, and Injustice on the Gulf Coast.

  • Young Readers’ Literature: Elizabeth Acevedo’s Anger Is Only a Shadow.◆

Picture This

Jessica Goecke’s debut picture book, Billy and the Hole, is something like The Giving Tree cut down by Edward Gorey.

At the start, Billy notices a hole in the ground that “wasn’t there yesterday.” Curious about its depth, he drops in an apple, noting, “His mother would be pleased that he had finished his lunch.”

But instead of a splash, Billy hears an ancient voice: “I prefer red apples.”

Eager to please, Billy rounds up some red apples and throws them in. The hole is delighted. But it wants more — so much more: books, cheese, “a fourteen-layer pineapple guava cake with banana buttercream,” even a string quartet.

It’s lovely to be needed, but this holey friend is insatiable.

There’s a touch of Audrey II here and a Grimm bargain that stretches all the way back through fairy tales to Faust.

You know the standard advice: If you find yourself in a hole, stop digging. But what if you find yourself in thrall to one?

That touch of manipulative danger is unnerving, but with its gothic wit, Billy and the Hole is Stephen King for the Lunchables set.◆

This Week’s Poem

Brenda Hillman’s new collection, Still House in the Desert, offers “an eco-contemplation” anchored in memories of her childhood home.

This is an alluringly designed book, laced with small, evocative photos. On Tuesday, it was named a finalist for the National Book Award for Poetry.

The poem below has become a kind of anthem in our house.

Books You Will Not Get To

A consciousness without borders spills out
   from unread books, a shine in
      the ledgers exacting no tax or a night
sky arranging its spikes around Jupiter.
      The books know of your good will
   as you pass by their bindings,
the commas of dust on gold rims,
      old fonts of Bodoni, Baskerville or
      Didot engraved in the colorful cloth
of the matched sets. Chekhov knows
from the other side the joy or suffering
that can’t speak. Some title pages
were signed in ink. Some people read
      whole books in the 20th century:
      stories, epics, lines, poems from
tricksters, loved prophets, doubters,
mystics taking dictation. Back then
      there were “condensed” books &
bound magazines, though you couldn’t
   read fast enough for your conscience
& you did think you’d get to them,
      they kept half-hopes alive in you,
resting on shelves for decades before
         it’s off to the library donation bin
or a box for the bedside of strangers.
   In the posture of those that remain,
there’s a calm, as in crystals of geodes
formed under pressure, waiting for their time.

From “Still House in the Desert: An eco-contemplation,” by Brenda Hillman. Copyright © 2026 by Brenda Hillman. Published by Wesleyan University Press. Used by permission.◆

This Old Haunted House

Because generations of high school kids were never forced to endure it, The House of the Seven Gables remains far less familiar than Nathaniel Hawthorne’s smoldering masterpiece, The Scarlet Letter. But every year around this time in Salem, that lesser sibling rises like a harvest moon.

After all, The House of the Seven Gables rests on an accusation of witchcraft, and in October, Salem descends into kitschy-witchy madness. Who wouldn’t want to stand on the cursed property that inspired Hawthorne, peer into Hepzibah’s cent shop, and see the very parlor where Judge Pyncheon expired with his eyes open?

Remarkably, you can.

The House of the Seven Gables is a work of gothic imagination, but it’s also an imposing mansion on Salem Harbor. The house, once owned by Hawthorne’s cousin, had only three remaining gables when he visited her, but in the early 20th century a wealthy social reformer named Caroline Emmerton pulled off a complicated feat of restoration and fictionalization.

With an eye to literary tourists, she rebuilt those fabled peaks and, by adding a cent shop, a secret staircase, and other elements, shifted the design of the interior toward the details of Hawthorne’s novel. Today, the story and the building twine around each other in an eerie symbiotic relationship.

The house and its gorgeous gardens are maintained by the association that Emmerton established to support Salem’s immigrant population, which probably spooks the MAGA descendants of Judge Pyncheon even more than witchcraft.

Hawthorne’s novel is essentially about the persistence of guilt and the exposure of past sins, which gives it curious relevance to Trump’s efforts to whitewash American history.

This summer when I visited the House of the Seven Gables, my guide was a graduate student appropriately named Calvin, who spoke softly at auctioneer speed.

I appreciated both his historical knowledge and his stark aesthetic judgments. Guiding us into one bedroom, he huffed, “The previous wallpaper was shockingly anachronistic!”

He also wanted us to understand that this grand house had originally been designed to impress and that its considerable wealth flowed from the exploitation of enslaved Africans.

“These systems of oppression are still deeply embedded in our country today,” he noted, with a clarity that would sound alarms in Washington, where the president has complained that the Smithsonian spends too much time discussing “how bad Slavery was.”

When we finished touring the attic, Calvin opened a door next to the fireplace that exposed a secret spiral staircase. Glancing at the narrow wooden steps inside that dark chamber, one young woman in our group declined and took an alternative route down.

But that was okay. All of us arrived at the same place eventually.◆
