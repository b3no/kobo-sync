The Odyssey proves the online right isn't conservative

The anti-woke warriors came for The Odyssey (and failed)

The Odyssey is a film with a fundamentally conservative message.

Writer and director Christopher Nolan fixates on the ancient Greek concept of xenia, a ritualized form of hospitality between guests and hosts. Nolan takes several liberties with the concept — his characters call it “Zeus’ Law” rather than by its Greek name, and it’s occasionally framed in a Golden Rule-esque way to be more recognizable to modern ears — but violations of this duty are at the very center of the story.

Odysseus and his men, in their quest to return home, are constantly landing on strange shores, asking for hospitality, and finding themselves denied — whether by a hungry cyclops or by the witch Circe, who offers them food only to transform the weary travelers into pigs. Meanwhile, Odysseus’ wife Penelope and son Telemachus are dealing with unwanted suitors who abuse xenia to feast every night at the absent patriarch’s mansion, demanding that Penelope choose one of them to remarry.

Nolan’s narrative innovation is his revelation, at the end of the film, that Odysseus has had to suffer all these violations of xenia because he and his men have broken divine law themselves. During the sack of Troy, they leave the famed Trojan Horse as a symbol of peace and an offering to the goddess Athena. Then, after deceiving the Trojans, Odysseus and his men jump out of the Trojan Horse, murder the priestesses in Athena’s temple and behead her statue. Nolan’s spin on the Homeric tradition is to frame the entire Odyssey as the punishment our hero has earned for violating one of his society’s most sacred duties.

The movie was marketed with the tagline “Defy The Gods,” but it is repeatedly shown that defying the gods is impossible and attempts will only bring ruin. Every prophecy Odysseus is given comes true to the letter. He is unable to save his men, and only by finally surrendering himself to the will of the gods is he able to move past his violation of xenia and make it back home — where he is finally able to exact revenge on those who have violated his household’s xenia.

What lessons does Nolan impart? That you cannot escape punishment for defying the gods. That only by submitting yourself to the divine can you cleanse your mistakes. That the violation of tradition is what causes society to collapse. The Odyssey is a movie about a man returning to his family and a wife who stays faithful to her absent husband for 20 years.

These are deeply conservative ideals, which is no surprise given Nolan’s history of flirting with conservative messaging in films like Dunkirk and The Dark Knight Rises. So why does so much of the American right wing seem to hate it so much? I think it has to do with the very particular kind of right-wingers they have become.

Why the online right hates conservative values

For months, Elon Musk has been throwing an extended hissy fit over the idea that Christopher Nolan engaged in allegedly woke casting, saying that Nolan was “pissing on Homer’s grave,” that he showed “total contempt for the Greek people” and that he is an “anti-White racist.” The rest of the online right hopped on the bandwagon, heaping criticism on the casting of Lupita Nyong’o (who is Black) and Elliot Page (who is trans) to play Helen of Troy and Sinon, respectively.

But it’s not — or not only — that the online right has become a mob of utter killjoys in search of anything they can tar as “woke.” It’s that their actual beliefs are not, at their core, conservative. They’re reactionary.

Classical conservatism asks what institutions, customs, and moral obligations are worth preserving. It assumes that inherited traditions contain wisdom and that even powerful men are constrained by moral laws they did not create.

This was 18th-century Anglo-Irish philosopher Edmund Burke’s central insight: Society does not appear out of thin air but is instead something built incrementally over generations of trial and error. Traditions are important because they stand the test of time. Strength matters, but so do fidelity, honor, and the willingness to submit oneself to societal obligations greater than one’s own desires.

Reaction begins elsewhere. Its worldview is closer to Nietzsche than to Burke, and reactionaries understand politics primarily as a contest between strength and weakness. Especially in their modern, MAGA form, reactionaries are proponents of what Nietzsche called master morality — the idea that it is natural for the strong to dominate the weak, that might makes right and power is its own justification.

It’s not an accident that Elon Musk is leading this reactionary wing of the right. He has previously expressed support for not allowing non-parents to vote and for a “republic of high status males.” Earlier this month, in response to a post that said “universal suffrage leads to universal suffering” and that asked him to “wise up and abandon classical liberalism,” he replied, “I have wised up.”

Musk himself has moved further into reactionary politics, and he’s dragging the online, anti-woke right with him.

Nolan’s Odyssey intervenes on the side of the Burkeans. It preaches the value of sacred tradition. But to reactionaries, tradition is only valuable insofar as it reinforces their place in the hierarchy. They do not care for the conservative view that tradition imposes duties on us all and that even the strong cannot overcome the moral stain of violating those duties.

The blockbuster concludes with Odysseus departing into voluntary exile. He has violated Zeus’ Law too many times to be fit for rule, and he wants to honor his fallen comrades. He chooses to crown his son Telemachus — who throughout the film was far more consistent in respecting Zeus’ Law — to rule in his stead. This kind of humility, which treats power as a responsibility rather than as a prize, is illegible to the reactionaries at the helm of the American right. MAGA politics requires never showing weakness, never admitting fault, never acknowledging obligations.

The Argument columnist Matthew Yglesias has noted that anyone who truly wants to promote Western values should obviously want nonwhite Westerners to buy into those values as well. That’s why a diverse Odyssey can also be a conservative Odyssey.

But if you just want to promote a racial hierarchy, you won’t care if any nonwhite people are invested in the story. The question for many reactionaries isn’t the substance of the movie or its ideas, in other words. It’s which group gets to claim ownership of a classic story.

There is a deeper irony here. Both the original work and Nolan’s adaptation repeatedly emphasize that appearances are unreliable. In the original poem, Athena disguises herself as a beggar to test whether Telemachus will honor the sacred duties owed to a stranger. Nolan repeatedly has characters warn that any traveler asking for shelter could be a god in disguise. The entire point of xenia is that you owe obligations to strangers and to the weak in particular, and you cannot judge who deserves hospitality by looking at them.

And yet, much of the online reactionary right could not see past the skin color of one actor or the trans identity of another. The Odyssey practically slaps you in the face with the idea that sacred duties matter more than appearances. The online right became fixated on appearances anyway, because any message that involves caring for the weak is anathema to their worldview. And this may be why their attempts to cancel The Odyssey fell so flat with most of America.

The great anti-woke overreaction

The movement that’s producing fury about The Odyssey is essentially the same movement that got angry about the Cracker Barrel redesign, that boycotted Bud Light for featuring a transgender influencer, and that gets mad virtually any time a video game features a less-than-maximally-attractive woman. And while that movement had a moment, there are signs that it’s fizzling out as a cultural force.

The campaign to cancel The Odyssey is just the latest in a string of failures over the past year. As cultural commentator Ryan Broderick summarized:

“The online right couldn’t stop Charlie Kirk from becoming a giant punchline after he was murdered. And the people who lost their jobs for social posts about Kirk are all getting big payouts now. They couldn’t run proper defense for Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE) during the occupation of the Twin Cities in January. They couldn’t get people to stop watching The Olympics, the NBA Finals, or the World Cup. They couldn’t make people care about Trump’s UFC birthday party. They couldn’t make Armie Hammer’s racist action movie number one at the box office, as we reported on Friday. And they certainly could not influence the box office of Christopher Nolan’s The Odyssey. And that last one seems like it might be the straw that finally breaks the camel’s back.”

The parallels to The Great Awokening are striking. The original era of wokeness took legitimate cultural concerns and then wildly overextended in ways that caused backlash among normal, apolitical people. Woke politics overreached in areas like DEI policy, trans women in sports, and defunding the police.

Anti-woke culture warriors took advantage of their missteps. But, just like their opponents, the anti-woke right vastly overinterpreted their mandate. They’re now trying to purge every element of woke from society in exactly the same way that the most annoying proponents of woke tried to purge unwoke ideas.

In the end, the people who insisted they were defending The Odyssey weren’t interested in the story’s value system at all. They wanted an excuse to fight liberals for cultural control, but they wanted to fight without actually asking what specific cultural ideals they were fighting for. In doing so, they’ve positioned themselves against the time-honored conservative values of tradition, piety, and honor — all because they can’t see past their own anti-woke identity politics.
