The end of purple Colorado?

Colorado Democrats won supermajorities in the state legislature — is it a blue state now or just a blue wave?

Halina Bennet
Nov 7
∙
Paid
 
READ IN APP
 

The votes are still being tallied, but Colorado Democrats appear to have had one of their best midterms in the state’s history. Their governor and senators, to no one’s surprise, are Democrats. They are leading or have won seven of eight congressional seats — only four of which they held prior to Tuesday — including seats in El Paso County and on the Eastern Plains. Moreover, the Democrats won supermajorities in both the state House and Senate.

At the same time, two conservative ballot initiatives on transgender issues passed. In Colorado, as anywhere, there are limits to how progressive public opinion really is. But in partisan terms, my home state is now a definite Democratic stronghold.

Sitting Democrats all held their seats comfortably. Representatives Joe Neguse, Jason Crow, and Brittany Pettersen were re-elected. The 29-year-old Democratic Socialist Melat Kiros, who unseated longtime Representative Diana DeGette in the primary, won her Denver-based district in a landslide. Her Republican opponent has yet to crack 15 percent of the vote.

None of that was a surprise. And in a year of Democratic enthusiasm, Democrat Manny Rutinel’s 15-point victory over the Republican incumbent Representative Gabe Evans in the state’s only true swing district was hardly a shock. More surprising is the victory of Jessica Killin, whose race was called Thursday, and the apparent success of Eileen Laubacher, who, on Friday, led Representative Lauren Boebert by nearly a point. On the state’s Western Slope, incumbent Jeff Hurd beat his Democratic challenger by less than two points in a part of Colorado that Donald Trump won by 10 points and Hurd won by five in 2024.

Is this the end of Colorado’s purple-state status and the official start of a true blue Rocky Mountain state?

A changing tide from red to blue

Colorado’s population more than doubled between 1980 and 2020, and much of that growth came from younger, college-educated newcomers and a growing Hispanic population, both concentrated in metro areas and suburbs. This growth had a dramatic political impact, felt most strongly in the suburban ring around Denver, which used to anchor Republican majorities and now leans reliably the other way.

Party registration changed with the population. In 2016, unaffiliated voters were about 35 percent of the electorate, and Democrats and Republicans were about 32 percent each. By the early 2020s, unaffiliated voters made up nearly half of the electorate, with Democrats ahead of Republicans. Among unaffiliated voters who express a preference for either party, about two-thirds prefer Democrats. The state’s primary system lets unaffiliated voters pick a party’s primary to vote in, which gives them sway over nominees.

In retrospect, the political breakthrough moment for Democrats came in 2018 when they won a trifecta under then-Governor-elect Jared Polis, sweeping every statewide executive office, every competitive statehouse race, and every legislative seat that changed parties.

By 2020, Biden carried the state by 13 points, and former Governor John Hickenlooper comfortably beat the Republican incumbent Cory Gardner in the race for U.S. Senate. Hickenlooper was declared the winner of his re-election bid on Tuesday before a single vote was counted. It will be surprising if a Republican wins statewide elected office any time soon.

Even with a blue tide rising steadily, driven by majority-left metros like Denver and Boulder, major parts of the state have remained red, resulting in a 50/50 U.S. House delegation — until, of course, this year.

A tsunami that climbed the Rockies

Midterm elections reliably produce a favorable result for the party that is not the president’s, and that was true this year more than any other since the 1974 election, when voters punished Republicans for the Watergate scandal. Colorado Republican Chair Craig Steiner said he believes this year’s result was a response to Trump rather than an embrace of Democratic ideas, and he pointed to the war in Iran and elevated gas prices as policies driving dissatisfaction in the party.

Colorado has a precedent of waves that recede. After 1974, when Democrat Gary Hart defeated a two-term Republican incumbent for Senate and Democrat Richard Lamm unseated the Republican governor, voters bounced back to the right. In 1976, Colorado voted for Republican Gerald Ford, and in 1978 the other incumbent Democratic senator was voted out of office. In 1980, the state chose Republican Ronald Reagan for president.

But this year appears to have brought more than a normal swing as Democrats won or are winning seats Trump carried by double digits.

Colorado Springs has remained a Republican stronghold in a state sliding left, due in large part to its military and defense presence. The Springs is home to Fort Carson, Peterson Space Force Base, Schriever Space Force Base, and the United States Air Force Academy. The Fifth Congressional District, which encompasses the city and its suburbs, has not elected a Democrat to the U.S. House since it was established in 1973. That is, until Jessica Killin, an Army veteran who served as chief of staff to former Second Gentleman of the United States Doug Emhoff, won this week.

Once described by the Almanac of American Politics as “one of America’s most Republican metropolitan areas,” Colorado Springs was ranked by the Cook Political Report first as “Likely Red” and then “Lean Red” heading into this year’s midterms, signaling a shift that has been slow but steady amid the state’s population boom. Trump won El Paso County — most of which is in CO-5 — by more than 20 points in 2016. Since then, Colorado Springs has undergone the second-largest political shift among the top 30 metro areas in the country, according to Vivid Maps. Trump won by 11 points in 2020 and 10 points in 2024. That year, Republican Jeff Crank won election to the U.S. House by 14 points. This year, Killin bested him by five.

For the congressional district encompassing most of Colorado’s most populous county (more people live in El Paso than even Denver County) to swing 19 points in its House election in just two years is a clear sign that if a blue tsunami has hit the nation, Colorado has embraced the wave.

Even more damning, perhaps, is Representative Lauren Boebert’s struggle in the Fourth Congressional District, which covers the eastern third of the state. As of Friday, she trails Eileen Laubacher and is running out of ways to make up the difference, the Denver Post reports. CO-4 is known to be the state’s most conservative and wealthiest district, and Boebert, a member of the Freedom Caucus, is by far the most right-wing member of Colorado’s delegation. The district has not elected a Democrat since 2008.

Like Killin, Laubacher is a veteran of both the military and D.C. She is a retired two-star Navy rear admiral and a former member of the National Security Council under President Joe Biden.

Whereas those two candidates have or may pull off stunning upsets, Democrat Dwayne Romero, a combat veteran, came up short against Jeff Hurd in the western part of the state. Also unlike the other two’s incumbent opponents, Hurd has a more fraught relationship with Trump, who pulled his support from the Republican in February after he broke with the president on his tariffs.

Hurd was one of six Republicans to join the majority of House Democrats in voting to lift the levies. After the vote, Trump wrote on social media that Hurd would “seriously suffer the consequences come Election time, and that includes Primaries.” Trump re-endorsed the incumbent in March, and Hurd won his primary, but he has spent the general election distancing himself from Trump and highlighting his history of disagreement with the president. So, unlike Boebert, who is known as one of the most conservative voices in the nation and has never faltered in her MAGA identity, Hurd has not been nearly as hurt by a midterm that was so clearly driven by anti-Trump sentiment.

Colorado’s Third Congressional District is a diverse part of the state that is mixed both economically and socially, and it is less conservative than CO-4 or CO-5. In re-electing Hurd, voters there signaled that the election was not an outright rejection of Republicans but, as with the rest of the country, a referendum on Trump.

The red-leaning district covers nearly half of Colorado’s land mass, starting with the Western Slope and stretching east across the San Luis Valley and the Arkansas River Valley to include southern Colorado and parts of the Eastern Plains. The district encompasses all or part of 27 of the state’s 64 counties. It has wealthy ski towns like Aspen, massive agricultural areas and public lands, and cities like Republican-leaning Grand Junction and Democrat-leaning Pueblo, which is home to a large Latino population. It’s urban and rural; blue, purple, and red; wealthy and poor. It’s huge and complex and prone to fires and floods, like the ones that are currently washing away parts of the state’s southwest.

A Hurd win, however close, shows, then, that right-leaning parts of Colorado still sway that way, even if their representative is not an obvious Trump ally.

But the closeness does, indeed, indicate that the district isn’t out of reach in future cycles.

The test for Republicans is whether the new Democratic seats hold in 2028 when Trump is about to leave office and whether the party can rebuild with suburban and unaffiliated voters.

The ballot measures

Further complicating the blue wave read is the outcome of the most regressive ballot measures put forth this time around.

Proposition 134 would bar students from school sports teams that do not align with their sex as defined by their “biological reproductive system,” banning transgender students from participating on sports teams that match their gender identity. Proposition 135 would bar providers from performing gender-affirming surgeries on minors. Opponents of the measures conceded Thursday, as both propositions continued to lead.

The campaign for both measures was led by Protect Kids Colorado, which has been described as a dark-money group. Kyle Clark, who anchors 9NEWS, reports that the group’s co-founder Kevin Lundberg said the measures were a way “to stop the transgender plague in Colorado.”

The measures revive a familiar fight about L.G.B.T.Q. rights in the state. In 1992, voters passed Amendment 2, an anti-gay-rights measure that gave Colorado the nickname “hate state” before the U.S. Supreme Court struck down the legislation in 1996. Activists cited by Westword said the moniker wouldn’t return in the wake of these new measures.

A state that just elected so many Democrats, where nearly every corner and every demographic shifted left, was still conservative on these progressive issues. This could be because Colorado Republican Chair Craig Steiner was right and the voters who moved toward Democrats did so because of Trump. It could also be because despite leaning left, the unaffiliated bloc is not tied to party positions. Candidates who flipped seats also campaigned more on the cost of living and less on social issues.

These measures can be amended or limited by the Democratic-controlled legislature, so their fates are not sealed by the votes. But the mismatch between candidate and measure success is evidence that for some Colorado voters, supporting moderate Democrats is different from supporting progressive policies.

Blue state or blue wave?

While Colorado’s tilt toward Democrats has been building for decades, this election’s results, especially in districts four and five, suggest that this year marked a decisive turn to blue.

But more than a complete abandonment of purple Colorado, the state’s election results highlighted Colorado’s status as a microcosm of the country as a whole. The midterm brought about success for a democratic socialist, for moderate Democrats, and for a G.O.P. congressman who in 2024 campaigned on being “as exciting as a bread sandwich” after Boebert’s Trump-style eccentricities made the seat vulnerable. (Hurd won CO-3 after Boebert moved to CO-4.)

Its representatives run the gamut on policy positions, and so do its voters.

Opposition to the erraticism of the president or Boebert is different from an acceptance of progressivism, and if the Democrats want to prove that they have flipped the state from red to blue in less than 30 years, they will have to do it when Trump is no longer on the ballot, even indirectly. They will have to hold Colorado Springs and the Eastern Plains without a foil, persuading voters who sent them to Washington that their votes were for something, not just against someone.
