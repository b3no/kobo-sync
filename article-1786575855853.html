New York is building. But where?

Plus San Diego’s YIMBY mayor and recovery from wildfire displacement

Halina Bennet
Aug 12
∙

Mayor Zohran Mamdani’s plan to tax homeowners who have high-value secondary residences in New York City hit a hurdle Monday when Staten Island Judge Wayne Ozzi ordered a pause on the pied-à-terre tax rollout. The decision came just days after a group of three homeowners brought a lawsuit against the city to delay the implementation because of frustration with how it was handled to that point, the Wall Street Journal reports.

Ozzi ordered the city to remove a public list it had published in July containing the names, addresses, and property values associated with more than 900,000 properties that the city said might be subject to the new tax. The tax applies to non-primary residences valued at $5 million or more and to co-ops and condos worth $1 million or more. He also barred further enforcement against the roughly 17,000 owners who had already been sent notices that they would be taxed. The ruling stated that the city’s process did not constitute proper notice under tax law and unlawfully put the burden of proving they should not be subject to the tax on property owners.

The tax was proposed by Governor Kathy Hochul and then backed by Mamdani as a way to generate about $500 million annually for the city’s budget, which faces a gap, the New York Times reports.

Late Monday, shortly after Ozzi’s ruling, the Mamdani administration submitted an appeal. The next hearing is scheduled for August 31.

Another element of Mamdani’s housing plan was paused this week — this time, by himself. After Mamdani campaigned in support of an effort to demolish Elizabeth Street Garden in NoLIta to make way for 123 units of affordable housing for seniors (as well as green space) on the one-acre site, his administration is no longer pursuing the plan, the New York Times reports.

Former Mayor Eric Adams had been a fervent supporter of the project until he abruptly changed course last summer, abandoning the plan and saying he would build housing somewhere else. After Mamdani was elected but before he assumed office, Adams’s first deputy mayor had the property designated as “parkland,” which blocked the development plans that both politicians had previously championed in a way that is difficult for the new administration to override. The project has caused tremendous friction among downtown New Yorkers fighting about preservation versus development for more than a decade.

According to court documents first reported by Gothamist, the city and the development team are now on the verge of an “amicable resolution” that will allow the developers to build more than 180 units on a nearby city-owned lot that was previously floated by the Adams administration as an alternative parcel. If all goes according to plan, the city will still produce a comparable amount of housing to what was originally envisioned and end a standoff that had resulted in zero housing in more than 10 years.

This kind of action on housing has become a hallmark of Mamdani’s tenure in office.

The Mamdani administration has reportedly financed more than 12,000 below-market-rate homes during the mayor’s first six months in office. That total includes more than 2,000 homes for formerly homeless New Yorkers, with more than half serving low-income residents earning less than 50 percent of area median income (about $68,000 for a two-person household).

The development is backed in large part by a $22 billion five-year investment that is part of the Block by Block plan the administration released in May to build 200,000 new affordable homes and preserve 200,000 units over the course of a decade.

But even with great development motivation comes great pushback. One plan to build 200 units of housing (a mix of supportive and affordable-lottery units) in Harlem is facing significant resistance from residents who are concerned that the development would demolish and replace a health care center. The Heritage Health Care Center has not received the kind of investment or attention that it needs to be functional, Gothamist reports, with a security guard recently having had to turn patients away amid issues with the building’s air conditioning.

Still, a group of residents has sued to block a city-approved plan to tear down the clinic because they say it has been a vital resource, providing care to uninsured and low-income patients since it was founded in the 1960s to fill a gap in local health services. The suit’s plaintiffs say they are not opposed to housing. Rather, they are concerned by the apparent choice between housing and health care and have voiced their support for any other plan that would include space for the clinic and housing, Gothamist reports.

“ We do need affordable housing, I kid you not,” Amin Shabazz, a resident, said to Gothamist. “But I think in the sequence of priorities, I think our health care is much more concerning.”

HUD’s latest attempt at dismantling permanent housing programs thwarted

A federal judge on Friday once again blocked the U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development’s effort to overhaul the Continuum of Care initiative by reprogramming much of the $4 billion in federal funding previously dedicated to housing assistance for people experiencing homelessness.

The ruling, which was made by U.S. District Judge Mary S. McElroy — a 2019 Trump appointee — stops HUD from making changes to CoC’s 2026 budget on the grounds that the department failed to follow procedure before shifting resources away from permanent housing programs, violating the Administrative Procedure Act’s notice and comment requirement. It is the third time a federal judge has ruled against HUD on issues related to CoC funding during Trump’s second term, Politico reports.

Mary Frances Kenion, chief equity officer for the National Alliance to End Homelessness, told Politico that the changes could have put around 97,000 people at risk of losing housing access.

Nearly everywhere in California is underbuilding. San Diego’s mayor is trying to fix it.

A report by CalMatters found that most of California is falling far short of reaching the state’s housing goals, which call for adding nearly 2.5 million units over eight years. It found fewer than a third of cities and counties are on track to permit enough market-rate housing and only five jurisdictions are permitting quickly enough to hit all four income targets. Those five jurisdictions are mostly rural, while most California cities are far behind. Irvine, for example, has permitted only 9 percent of the very low-income housing it needs to reach its target by the end of the decade and 3 percent of the next income category — households earning up to 80 percent of area median income.

For many California cities, that lag is not for lack of trying.

I spoke this week with San Diego Mayor Todd Gloria, whose advocacy for supply-side housing development and reform extends as far back as 2002, when he served on the loan committee of the city’s housing commission.

“In my career, housing’s always been a concern,” Gloria told me. “Earlier in my time in public service, it was maybe issue number seven or eight. I find that it’s issue number one now.”

Gloria said he wanted to be mayor because of his commitment to fixing the affordability crisis that 50 years of underbuilding in San Diego helped create. He’s determined to pull every lever at his disposal to reduce barriers to housing. As the state has begun to focus more on development and has implemented its own mandates, the mayor said that he has taken those statewide directives and built on them. Perhaps most effective, Gloria said, has been San Diego’s approach to statewide laws that were signed by Governor Gavin Newsom in 2024 to make accessory dwelling units more permissible. San Diego offers financial and density incentives, including construction loans of up to $250,000 for low-income residents in addition to free assistance navigating design, permitting, and building.

“I think those laws have made an impact across the state, but the city of San Diego chose to see that really as a floor,” he said. “What we found is a very responsive market.”

San Diego, which is one of the most expensive cities in the country, saw rents flatten and decline more quickly over the last year than nearly all other top markets after a surge in its housing supply.

Gloria said that there is no single change that has had the greatest impact on affordability but that several policies, such as incentivizing development through accessory dwelling units, pursuing transit-oriented development, and making cuts to permitting timelines, as well as three San Diego programs — the Bridge to Home Program, which supports the development and preservation of affordable homes, the Affordable Housing Permit Now expediting program, and the Complete Communities planning initiative — have all worked together to reduce the friction that causes projects to bottleneck before construction. Above all, Gloria identified certainty as the single biggest driver of impact.

“We are saying to builders that we’re going to de-risk these projects as much as possible, we’re going to give you a pathway to success to occupancy,” Gloria said. “That’s what I’ve been able to do on a local level. Generally, what I aspire to and I’m interested in is getting similar certainty from our state and federal partners to complement that and further de-risk the projects so that people are willing to make these investments.”

This summer, Gloria was elected to serve as president of the U.S. Conference of Mayors — the nonpartisan organization of mayors whose cities have populations of 30,000 or more. In his position as president, he said, he’s had the chance to work with other municipal leaders on housing, which he said has become far more salient to everyone, calling it a “uniting issue.”

“I want my fellow mayors to know their housing permit numbers the way that they know their crime stats,” he said.

He said that the passage of the 21st Century ROAD to Housing Act last month was celebrated by the mayors, many of whom helped shape the bill’s provisions.

But, for Gloria, the work is far from over. He said that it is time for “housing 2.0.”

“We’ve got to get moving faster and implement more and pass more changes in order to try and speed this up as best we can,” he said.

“The six years I served as mayor is not going to undo 50 years of underproduction. We need to stay committed to our vision, which is to build more homes that everyday San Diegans can afford.”

Private-sector investment meets a legislative push in Illinois

Illinois Realtors — the trade organization that supports real estate professionals — this week launched a partnership with local officials to accelerate housing production statewide. The Illinois Housing Supply Accelerator Program aims to identify and break down community-level constraints to homebuilding such as zoning restrictions, permitting delays, fees and costs, and financing gaps.

“We’ve seen momentum building at the federal level, and we’ve seen state leaders recognize the severity of this supply crisis,” Illinois Realtors President Jeff Kolbus said in a statement to HousingWire. “Now, the battleground is local. Mayors are asking for help, developers are ready to build, and this program delivers the exact blueprint and leverage needed to turn policy intent into actual homes.”

This program is a private-sector-led complement to Governor JB Pritzker’s BUILD legislative package, which aims to boost development through supply-side reforms like legalizing duplexes, triplexes, fourplexes, and accessory dwelling units, cutting down processing times and fees, and reviewing building codes.

Wildfires shock an already tight housing market

The Pacific Northwest has long been an example of a housing market plagued by shortages and high costs driven by the tech industry. The ongoing crisis became more acute in one area of the region as wildfires ripped through Spokane, Wash., at the end of July and beginning of August, destroying at least 800 structures and forcing around 65,000 people to evacuate. It was the most destructive collection of fires in Washington history, and the Seattle Times reports that the destruction will further strain an already tight housing stock, especially for rentals.

It may take years for supply to get back to pre-fire levels if other instances of wildfire displacement are any indication. A report published this week by the University of Hawai’i Economic Research Organization found that three years after the August 2023 wildfires on Maui, the rate at which displaced residents are returning to West Maui is rising modestly, with the homeownership rate among fire-affected households in the survey having recovered to about 42 percent.

Rental costs for studio and one-bedroom apartments have fallen to their pre-fire levels, while rents for larger units are unprecedentedly high. With rental assistance, about half of fire-affected renters are paying less than before the fires, while poverty remains above pre-fire levels and most households are still poorer than they were before the fires, according to the report.
