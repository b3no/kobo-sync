For NFL Analysts, Memory Is A Drawback, Amnesia An Advantage

modern football analysis is defined by wildly overreacting to the last game

Freddie deBoer
Nov 16
 
READ IN APP
 

Flash back to the 2023 NFL season with me. It’s later November and the Philadelphia Eagles are the talk of the league. After losing to the Kansas City Chiefs in the Super Bowl that February, the Eagles appeared to be a team on a mission. They started the season with a 10-1 record, and had put their stamp down as the league’s best team with wins over Dallas, Kansas City and Buffalo in November. They were +400 to win the Super Bowl at the time, odds you very rarely see during an NFL season. Conor Orr of Sports Illustrated wrote that the overtime period needed to beat the Bills was “merely a formality” because “there was nothing they could do to stop the inevitable.” He went on to declare that the team’s performance had smashed the myth of the Super Bowl loser’s hangover. This is rather extreme language, but there’s no reason for Orr to be singled out unfairly. Nate Davis of Yahoo Sports said that we could “go ahead and pencil them in” for another consecutive Super Bowl appearance. ESPN’s Dan Graziano suggested that QB Jalen Hurts had to be the MVP to that point.

And then the Eagles got absolutely waxed by the San Francisco 49ers, 42-19, they lost six of their last seven games, and went on to be blown out in the wild card round of the playoffs by a 9-8 Tampa Bay Buccaneers team. The trendy MVP candidate Hurts went 110 of 180 (61%) for 1,161 yards, five touchdowns, five interceptions, and a 77.6 passer rating, adding only 195 rushing yards despite a reputation as one of the game’s great dual threats. Coach Nick Sirianni suddenly found himself on the hot seat. The juggernaut had been revealed to be a fraud; the team that seemed poised for a championship run was proven to be fatally flawed.

I remember all of that! And, in some vague, dim way, I imagine that the average NFL analyst remembers it too, the collapse of the 2023 Philadelphia Eagles. I mean I’m sure many of them could be prompted to remember that that team had looked really good before looking really, really bad. But in the broader sense? I’m not confident many of them learned anything. The trouble is that NFL analysts - from independent YouTubers to ESPN celebrities, “you gotta run the football” ex-player traditionalists and protractor-wielding analytics nerds alike - frequently don’t appear to remember anything. The whole business is dominated by recency bias; whatever just happened in the previous slate of games is the truth of the league, the teams, and the players in it. That they felt equally certain the week before that and the week before that, only to be proven wrong again? Not rejected, not disputed, but forgotten. And the biggest, most important lesson - that good seasons become bad at the drop of a hat, that players that are dominant one moment can become mortal the next - never, ever gets absorbed. To be an NFL analyst is to spend most of your time shouting, if you’re shouting you have to have something to say, and in a cutthroat media environment, well, that something better be dramatic, forceful, and expressed with total certainty.

The Eagles did go on to win the Super Bowl the following year, which you could take in several contradictory directions - you could suggest that the collapse didn’t matter because they ended up winning a championship, even though no one was predicting that they would fall off a cliff and return to glory the next year. Or you could say that the 2023 history was forgotten because of what happened the following season. But what you can’t say is that NFL analysts writ large learned the lesson of never getting too caught up in the moment and committed to not overreacting to periods of sustained superior play. That didn’t happen. No, every Monday the slate gets wiped clean, whatever happened Sunday becomes the full and final and certain truth about teams, quarterbacks, coaches, the season… but only for a few days. Last Monday’s full truth, delivered with exactly the same confidence, is gone. Nobody is going back to check everybody else’s work after the fact. Nobody asks what it means that the facts we were all just certain of a week ago turned out to not be facts at all. We just press on to the next acts of absurd overconfidence. And this isn’t just a matter of annoyance but a real, tangible problem with how football is analyzed and understood and debated -and thus with who wins awards and how people bet their money. The 2026 Los Angeles Rams are about as good a case study as the league has given us in years.

Remember a few months ago, when the Rams were anointed as an unconquerable team of destiny? Last season the Rams lost the NFC Championship Game to division rival Seattle, after an MVP season from Matt Stafford, their greying gunslinger QB. It was clear that Stafford still had what it took to bring the team back to the promised land, and it was equally clear that he didn’t have much time left. The team’s response was to go on a shopping spree. They acquired Super Bowl-winning cornerback Trent McDuffie for their 2026 first-round pick, pairing him with his former Kansas City running mate Jaylen Watson in free agency. Then came the move of the offseason, trading for reigning defensive player of the year Myles Garrett, by wide acclamation the single best player in the league. This was to be the first time a reigning MVP and Defensive Player of the Year played for the same team. This prompted future Hall of Famer Aaron Donald, the dominant defensive tackle of his era, to come out of retirement. These new additions were added to already talent-rich team that also enjoyed the services of star WRs Puka Nacua and Davante Adams, under the direction of offensive wunderkind head coach Sean McVay. It was very fair to believe that the Rams were one of the clear favorites to go to the Super Bowl… so long as you also remembered teams like the 2023 Philadelphia Eagles, or the 2019 Baltimore Ravens, or the 2007 New England Patriots, or the 1998 Minnesota Vikings, all of whom looked unbeatable until they were unexpectedly beaten.

If the roster looked crazy, the coverage was even crazier. Journalists searched for words powerful enough to express how dominant this team would be. Segment after segment on ESPN debated whether this would prove to be one of the greatest NFL teams of all time. Newsletters and podcasts and YouTube shows devoted endless hours to projecting their greatness - was 15-2 their floor??? Major sportsbooks had the Rams at +550 to win the Super Bowl at their home stadium; nobody else had odds better than +1000. The prediction models were on board too. ESPN’s Football Power Index listed the Rams as Super Bowl favorites, with a 14.9% chance to win the title - more than 4 percentage points ahead of the next-best team. One outlet’s simulation went a lot further:

At the top, and it is not remotely close, sit the Los Angeles Rams.

They did not merely grade out as the best team in football. They graded first in the league on offense and first on defense at the same time, which almost never happens. Matthew Stafford projects for a monster line: 5,045 yards and 39 touchdowns from a clean pocket. Puka Nacua is set to lead the NFL in receiving at 124 catches for over 1,600 yards, with Davante Adams lining up across from him to pull the other cornerback while posting 90 grabs, 1,300 yards and 10 scores of his own.

Then the front office went and landed Myles Garrett, a double-digit-sack wrecker off the edge, and a defense that already had teeth jumped to first in the league alongside the offense. When we ran the season, Los Angeles pushed toward fifteen wins and blew through its schedule with no weak link anywhere on the depth chart to attack. Everybody else in the NFL is playing for second.

If this was an extreme, it wasn’t much of one. ESPN’s betting preview put it simply: Super Bowl or bust. ESPN’s Mike Greenberg, who might be the poster child for the ultra-reactive, zero-chill nature of NFL coverage, speculated that they might be the first NFL team to go 20-0.

Then the games started. Week 1 was a 27-7 loss to the 49ers in Melbourne, which was the largest Week 1 margin of defeat for a preseason Super Bowl favorite in the past 50 seasons. Garrett, their ultra-pricey, ultra-talented addition, got hurt in that game and went on injured reserve after arthroscopic knee surgery following the opener. In Week 3 the Rams led Denver 16-0 at halftime only for Stafford to throw two interceptions in the fourth quarter, as the defense gave up 30 second-half points, and they fell to 1-2. A 37-34 loss to Buffalo followed, with Josh Allen running wild and the McVey special teams kryptonite dooming them again, and the 49ers became the new Super Bowl favorite after only five weeks. Week 6 was the low point. The lowly Cardinals didn’t need a touchdown to beat the Rams; all 18 of their points came on field goals, including the 43 yard game-winner in the final seconds. The Rams were 2-4 and in last place. It sure looked like a wrap. Social media started to light up with arguments that the team should consider making a switch to their first round rookie QB Ty Simpson. Things got loud enough that Yahoo ran a piece arguing that the Rams shouldn’t bench Matthew Stafford yet. The reigning MVP! “Yet”! Greenberg, for his part, responded by asking whether the Rams might miss the playoffs, from 20-0 to home for January in a few weeks. I’ll give the man this: he’s consistent.

Then the Rams won four in a row, beating the Raiders, the Chargers, and the Commanders before winning a rematch against the hobbled Cardinals this weekend. This is, I will happily concede, not a murderer’s row of opponents. But they’re now 6-4, level with the reigning champs in Seattle, ten games in. They’re very unlikely to catch the 8-1 49ers, but the playoffs are a realistic goal. (Although losing the all-world Nacua for a month-plus is a big blow.) The very useful New York Times playoff predictor has them at a 43% chance of making it to the postseason, with an absolutely crucial game against the Green Bay Packers coming up on Sunday. But by now the national conversation has mostly moved on. The press crowned this team, then buried it, and now that it’s doing roughly what a talented but thin and old team does once its best defender is healthy, the football media has seemingly forgotten that the Rams exist. No memory of the months-long coronation they themselves held, no lasting impression from the funeral. Just the current meme, today’s news, the fleeting conventional wisdom, which right now is fixated on sexier teams.

You could read this as a story about the media being too high on the Rams and then becoming too low. I think that misses the larger point, though. Both reactions stem from the same failure: overconfidence, wild overconfidence that the evidence couldn’t support. The NFL season is short, increasing the role of random variation, injuries are constant and highly significant, and with twenty-two players on the field at any given time there’s so many ways that the slightest slip or mistake or bad decision can change outcomes. No one should have been anointing the Rams with the confidence that everyone was anointing them. I mean, the history was no secret; it was sitting right there. Over the past 30 years, the preseason Super Bowl favorite has entered the season at +500 or better 20 times; those teams went 0-for-20 when it came to actually winning the thing. The the last team with +500 or better odds to win the Super Bowl to actually do so was the 1994 49ers, before I entered high school. Hell, last year, the Super Bowl was played between two teams that entered the season at 60-1, the Seahawks and the Patriots. How many times do we need to learn the same lessons?

Apparently, over and over again. In late October, the Chicago Bears traded for Jeffrey Simmons, a four-time Pro Bowl defensive tackle, and the hype train arrived right on schedule. Pundits fell all over themselves to declare that, with this defensive improvement, the Bears were an unstoppable juggernaut. Never mind that Chicago’s overrated offense has already shown a ton of inconsistency of the type that can stop a playoff run in its tracks or that the secondary still stinks. It has been decided - the Bears are Super Bowl bound. An anonymous NFC general manager told Jordan Schultz that the Bears now had “a Super Bowl roster for this year and for years to come without a doubt.” Without a doubt! The Rams were Super Bowl favorites, without a doubt, a few months ago! What do you mean, without a doubt? How many times do we have to watch this same movie? Eight weeks ago the Rams added Myles Garrett and Aaron Donald to the reigning MVP and the league’s best coach-quarterback pairing, got crowned a superteam, and then lost four of their first six games. One star defensive lineman doesn’t make a team unbeatable. Obviously. Football is just too random, too injury-filled, and too dependent on the other 52 guys on the roster for that. The Bears may well be very good. But anyone saying “without a doubt” in November has learned nothing since September.

Why are NFL pundits incapable of remembering the recent past and calming down? Why do they chase after every new result like a cat hunting a laser? As usual, the answers are structural and based on incentives.

The biggest thing, I think, is that the weekly nature of football (where most games are played on the same day, one week since the previous set of games) creates a ton of time to fill, while football’s immense popularity creates a voracious public appetite for more #content, and the result is a set of perverse incentives. Football is a weekly sport with daily coverage - each game is about six percent of the regular season but gets an entire week of analysis, and a week of recaps and hot takes and podcasts and panels can’t be adequately filled with the most sensible things to say. (Things like “we learned a little and should update our priors, but not very much, who knows?”) If you’re looking to fill time and you want what you say to have some viral potential, it’s much more useful to see every new development as earth-shaking. Nobody at ESPN is looking to book segments called Modest Bayesian Updating of NFL Microtrends That Are Unlikely to Continue, after all. There’s just so much football analysis, and the format requires us to believe that each game means something that’s a) interpretable and b) consequential. So each game gets treated as if it offers up tons of league-changing information.

We also can’t underestimate the role of analytics and its veneer of scientific rigor, either. I say this as someone who (mostly, generally, usually) agrees with the analytics nerds about football questions. Numbers can dress overconfidence up as rigor, regardless of the domain, and there’s an awful lot of numbers floating around NFL football these days. “41.4 percent playoff odds” sounds more scientific than “they look kind of mediocre, trending towards bad,” but if the data that’s determining that percentage is based on six games, then that decimal precision is decoration and the “mediocre” label might be more true. Models that update every week are built to overreact to new data, and that’s fine when people understand how they work and how to interpret them. Unfortunately in the social media era few people bother with restraint; most are racing to say who sucks and who’s great and how the teams they like are underrated…. Somewhere along the way, “we have a model” started being taken to mean “we know exactly what’s going to happen,” which for the record the smarter analytics people are always arguing against. The reality is that football really is noisy, really is subject to major swings from minor events. For that reason I understand the urge to see one bad play and say “Oh this guy stinks.” In a sport where so much is random, some people are always going to grab hard at anything that feels like a pattern. But that’s like visiting Los Angeles on a day when it’s raining and concluding that the weather generally sucks; it’s extrapolating from too little information.

Also, you know… there’s no consequences for being wrong. This is the Memento part, or maybe the Groundhog Day part: there’s no accountability for overreacting so people just keep overreacting, again and again. Preseason predictions fall into the void of memory by Labor Day and nobody brings them back up. Wild claims are made in week four; nobody has the clipboard out in week eight to ask why they were so sure. Nobody holds up the next wild-ass Stephen A. Smith prediction to the many, many past wrong ones. Without accountability for wrong things said with great confidence in the past, there’s no cost to saying new wrong things with great confidence. Analysts might keep score if being right is what determines how much money they make, but it isn’t. Analysts make their money by getting attention and they get attention by saying wild shit, not by being sober and judicious. This is the overarching problem of contemporary media and it’s particularly obvious (and particularly obnoxious) with NFL football.

There’s a lot I could say about how to understand football better. There’s also a lot to be said about the bad incentives of media and how broken our attention systems are, how we’ve been conditioned to only respond to overconfidence and wild predictions and massive claims. But the most important piece of advice is the simplest: learn to say “I don’t know.” It’s just three words! Saying them won’t kill you; in fact, it might make people trust you more. We don’t know anything until the final whistle of the Super Bowl, really, and then we look ahead to the next season about which we know nothing. Please, if you can, as you hear ESPN pundits declare the Bears an unstoppable killing machine or claim that Joe Burrow is having the greatest season in the history of quarterbacking, remember the 2026 Los Angeles Rams, the unstoppable superteam that now must fight to scrape its way to a 7 seed. And when you remember them, remember that there’s nothing particularly unusual about that outcome - the preseason favorite losing is normal. It’s happened more than twenty straight times. Because nobody knows anything. Because the future is hard to predict. Especially about football.

The Minnesota Vikings boast the league’s best defense but have struggled terribly on offense, thanks to a series of poorly-performing quarterbacks. Now they’ve traded for Kirk Cousins, who looked great on Sunday, and the entire football world thinks that the Vikings are now sudden Super Bowl contenders who will be terribly hard to beat in the playoffs. Is that true? I have no idea. And neither do you. It’s fine to throw predictions out there. But maybe do so with humility.
