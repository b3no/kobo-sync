What, exactly, are environmentalists protecting?

You need a permit to shut down a pipeline

On Easter Sunday in 2018, the Erie Trader, a barge loaded with iron ore and bound for an Indiana port, left Sault Ste. Marie in northern Michigan. Ice covered the narrow waterway ahead. Farther south were the Straits of Mackinac, the narrow strip of water that divides lower Michigan from the Upper Peninsula and Lake Michigan from Lake Huron.

All but a skeleton crew was off duty and below decks. Temperatures were below freezing and a gale wind blew from the west. The barge shuddered against the ice as it moved through the water. “It sounded like thousands of freight trains hitting the hull,” said one sailor.

In tricky conditions in tight waterways, standard practice is to ready a barge’s anchors for possible emergency deployment. But the starboard anchor on the Erie Trader had recently been repaired, and the few men on deck didn’t trust it. They agreed to ready only the port anchor.

That was the plan, anyhow. At some point that day, according to a subsequent Coast Guard investigation, one of the sailors, probably out of habit, released the two main restraints on both anchors. No one noticed when an improperly repaired brake failed and the starboard anchor, juddering as the barge crunched into ice, dropped to the lake floor.

It was such a small mistake. But it nearly spelled disaster.

In 1953, state officials had given permission for two pipelines to be run side-by-side across 4.5 miles of the bottomlands of the Straits of Mackinac. These “dual pipelines” became part of what is now known as Line 5, which connects the tar sands in western Canada to refineries in Michigan and Ontario. Today, Line 5 is owned by the energy giant Enbridge and carries up to a half-million barrels a day of oil, much of which is refined into propane and used to heat homes across Michigan.

Over the decades, concerns about a possible rupture of Line 5 have grown. A spill could release as much as 58,000 barrels of oil into the Great Lakes, befouling more than 400 miles of shoreline, destroying fisheries, and costing $1.9 billion in damages. The state’s Indian tribes see it “as an existential threat to our treaty-protected rights, resources, and fundamental way of life.”

If a ship’s anchor dragged across the bottomlands, it could hook a pipeline and snap it, much as a child snaps a twig.

That is very nearly what happened that Easter Sunday. As the Erie Trader passed through the Straits of Mackinac, Enbridge received a warning from an acoustic monitoring device that Line 5 had been hit. But Providence smiled on Michigan. The dual pipelines were dented in three places, but they held. The anchor also damaged three underwater power cables, releasing about 800 gallons of insulating oil.

The sound of that anchor strike reverberated through Michigan’s gubernatorial campaign. Gretchen Whitmer, a Democrat, ran on blocking Enbridge from using the bottomlands for Line 5.¹ The sitting Republican governor, Rick Snyder, was also concerned. On his way out the door, he struck a deal with Enbridge to build a tunnel under the Straits of Mackinac to house the dual pipelines. Construction was estimated to take seven to 10 years; Enbridge would pay for the whole thing. In the meantime, oil would continue to flow.

Whitmer, who handily won the governor’s race, approved several permits to get the tunnel built. But she didn’t want to wait a decade to address an immediate threat. She wanted Line 5 shut down. In 2020, Whitmer canceled the 1953 easement that allowed Enbridge to run its oil over the bottomlands of the Mackinac Straits. Enbridge was now a trespasser on Michigan land.

Shut off the dual pipelines or build a tunnel to protect them: Either would be safer than the status quo. Gov. Whitmer has pursued both. Yet, more than eight years after the Erie Trader dragged its anchor across the bottomlands, and as Whitmer’s two terms in office draw to a close, neither has happened. The oil continues to flow.

Last week, the Senate passed the Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act, a permitting bill that would make it easier to site new energy projects and string interstate transmission lines. Most Senate Democrats voted against the bill, worried that faster permitting would mean more fossil fuel infrastructure and that the clean energy built along the way isn’t worth the trade.

But the environmental costs run in both directions. Every year Michigan spends fighting over Line 5 is another year oil flows through exposed pipes on the lakebed.

Conservation requires permits, too

It’s a cliché at this point to say that America can’t build new things. The debacle of California’s $126 billion high-speed rail project has made the state a laughingstock. New York spent $11.1 billion just to connect the Long Island Rail Road to Grand Central Station, at a cost of around $3.5 billion per mile of track. In the past 50 years, we have built just one major airport (Denver).² We’re not even good at building roads. It costs three times as much to build a mile of road today as it did in the 1960s, even after adjusting for inflation.

But many of the nation’s most essential infrastructure projects don’t involve building something new. They aim to fix what we already have — to protect against a new threat, to reroute around an obstacle, or to restore a natural area. These projects are the infrastructure equivalent of replacing your roof or waterproofing your basement: not sexy and not cheap.

They’re also desperately needed. American infrastructure, much of which is a product of the go-go years after World War II, is showing its age. One in four water mains is more than half a century old; many of the 2.2 million miles of the nation’s pipes are nearing or past the end of their useful life. Old pipes mean more breaks. (In 2023, a break in a 127-year-old pipe flooded Times Square in New York City.) In the coming years, the Environmental Protection Agency estimates that local water systems will need a staggering $625 billion for maintenance and improvements.

Or take bridges. Over 4 out of 10 are more than 50 years old, and fully 6.8% are in poor or structurally deficient condition. In 2007, the collapse of a Minneapolis bridge killed 13 and injured 145. In 2022, hours before President Joe Biden visited Pittsburgh to tout his big infrastructure bill, a bridge fell in the city, hospitalizing three people.

Dams are a particular risk. The average age of the country’s 92,000 dams is 64 years. Nearly 17,000 of those dams have “high hazard potential,” which means that a breakcould kill downstream residents. Failures are not uncommon. The states reported 270 of them from 2000 to 2020, including a dam break in Hawaii in 2006 that killed seven. Two dams in Michigan failed in 2020, flooding the town of Midland and causing at least $200 million in damages. Loss of life was avoided only because the local emergency manager ordered a gutsy midnight evacuation.

How to undrain a swamp

It hasn’t always been this hard to get things done. Until about 1970, the courts took a hands-off approach to government projects. As the U.S. Supreme Court said in 1954, “It is not for the courts to oversee the choice of the boundary line, nor to sit in review on the size of a particular project area.” That hands-off approach helped the United States build the Interstate Highway System, construct dozens of nuclear power plants, channel millions of gallons of water to Los Angeles and the Central Valley, and dig the St. Lawrence Seaway.

Many of those projects became the indispensable foundations of American prosperity. Some went awry.

The trouble is that American law changed dramatically from the time we made those mistakes to the time we chose to fix them. Conservationists are relaxed about that: They don’t tend to worry about the costs and delays associated with permitting. If we get less development, including clean energy development, they think that’s a fine price to pay. But that calculation ignores how much building it can take to undo the damage we’ve already done. Once you’ve drained a wetland, for instance, it takes permits to put the water back.

Consider one of the largest infrastructure projects in American history — the $30 billion, multidecade effort to restore the Florida Everglades.

At the crown of the Everglades is Lake Okeechobee, an enormous freshwater lake in South Florida. Historically, the lake would rise and spill over its southern lip, much like water running out of a too-full glass. The spilled water would move ever so slowly across the bottom tip of the state into Florida Bay. That broad, slow-moving sheet of water — the River of Grass, as the conservationist Marjory Stoneman Douglas dubbed it — is the Everglades.

As Florida’s population swelled in the late 19th and early 20th centuries, state officials worked to transform much of the Everglades into dry land fit for development and agriculture. But their drainage efforts proved unable to protect against recurrent, disastrous floods. In 1948, Congress directed the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers to conduct a massive infrastructure project to finally control South Florida’s unruly water.

Over the next two decades, the Corps dug hundreds of miles of canals, installed massive pumps, and built huge floodgates. The project mostly succeeded at controlling floods. But it was environmentally disastrous. By bottling up water that used to flow freely, the new levees and floodgates destroyed the Everglades’ delicate ecology. As the journalist Michael Grunwald wrote in his 2006 book The Swamp, the Everglades became “an ecological mess — sometimes too wet, sometimes too dry, always obstructed and convoluted by highways, levees, and canals. More than 90 percent of its wading birds and alligators had vanished.”

As the damage became impossible to ignore, saving the Everglades became a subject of bipartisan concern. In 2000, in one of his last acts as president, Bill Clinton signed a law committing to an Everglades restoration plan of almost unimaginable scale and scope. Estimated at the time to cost $8.2 billion and covering an area the size of West Virginia, the Comprehensive Everglades Restoration Plan, or CERP, was expected to take 30 years to complete.

But the law had changed in the interim. In court cases in the 1960s and 1970s, rules governing who could sue were relaxed. Almost anyone with an objection, however gossamer-thin, could now get into court. Judges also subjected government decisions to much closer scrutiny. Where courts had once been deferential, a seminal 1971 decision from the U.S. Supreme Court instructed judges to conduct a “thorough, probing, in-depth review” of agency decisions. New laws, including the National Environmental Policy Act and the National Historic Preservation Act, were read to impose heavy analytical burdens on agencies before they acted.

The upshot was to make the law significantly more hospitable to opponents of government action. No surprise, then, that Everglades restoration has not gone smoothly. Ongoing disputes over water quality, Indigenous rights, a flood-prone 8.5-square-mile settlement, endangered species, and mining concessions have mired the project in legal controversy and red tape. Even Buddhists have sued, claiming that the restoration project would “disrupt the serenity and harmony with nature central to their religious practices.”

Of the 68 projects that make up the plan, only a handful have been completed. Most are not yet underway. Costs have more than doubled and the completion date has been pushed back two decades, to 2050. Construction on a huge reservoir that’s needed to restore water flow won’t be completed until 2029, if then.

Conditions are slowly improving. Water quality in the Everglades is better than it once was. But progress is partial and uneven. A 2024 scientific assessment rated the ecological health of Lake Okeechobee and the northern estuaries as “poor,” with the Everglades themselves only “fair.” Several measures of water flow and ecological health have stagnated or are even deteriorating.

BAAJA would remove some of the procedural snares that have made it so hard to restore the Everglades. Fewer people would be allowed to sue. Some cases would be channeled immediately into appellate courts, speeding the litigation process. And, most importantly, judges would no longer be allowed to halt projects over perceived deficiencies in environmental review documents.

Why would environmental groups defend the status quo?

Yet environmental groups, with few exceptions, are implacably opposed to permitting reform. They’re not wrong to think that BAAJA would make it easier to build, or to worry that some of what we build might damage the environment. But they overlook that BAAJA would also make it easier to repair the damage that we’ve done.

The Everglades may be an extreme case, but the problem is everywhere. North of San Diego, the train tracks that run to Los Angeles are built along eroding sandstone bluffs. Those bluffs periodically collapse and interrupt rail service. It’s been clear for two decades that the tracks will have to be rerouted. But fierce disputes over a possible tunnel — led by the residents of Del Mar, a wealthy beach enclave — have stalled the project. A route still hasn’t been chosen. Even when it is, good luck getting it built. The good people of Del Mar know how to hire lawyers.

In Louisiana, a $3 billion project was intended to restore thousands of acres of coastal wetlands at the mouth of the Mississippi River. Upriver levees had cut off much of the sediment that historically replenished the wetlands, leading to coastal erosion and ecological catastrophe. The Mid-Barataria Sediment Diversion Project was meant to right that wrong. But oystermen fought the project in court and local officials issued stop-work orders that stalled construction. In 2025, Louisiana killed the project, which it said was “no longer viable due to multiple factors, including costs, permitting concerns, and ongoing litigation.”

For more than two decades, Washington and Oregon have been trying to build a new bridge to replace two decaying ones that span the Columbia River. A first push collapsed amid a fight over whether to extend Oregon’s light rail, after nearly $200 million had been spent. Planning began again in 2019. With the environmental review taking years longer than expected, the projected completion date has slipped from 2034 (already nuts) to 2045 (completely ridiculous). Costs for the project have swelled to $14.4 billion. Last month, the owner of a heavy-manufacturing facility filed a lawsuit, arguing that the U.S. Coast Guard should never have approved a bridge too low for the facility’s boats.

And on and on. It shouldn’t be this hard to fix what’s broken or restore what we once destroyed. But that’s what you get when you give the loudest objectors the legal right to insert themselves into the process. And many groups — private businesses, environmental litigators, elderly homeowners, you name it — prefer that public projects be done just as they want them done, or not at all.

The oil is still flowing

For environmentalists frustrated that they can’t just block fossil fuel infrastructure, it’s instructive to look at what happened when one Democratic governor tried.

Not long after taking office, Gov. Whitmer and the Michigan attorney general both filed suit against Enbridge to demand the shutdown of the dual pipelines. Enbridge filed its own lawsuit, with the staunch support of the Canadian government. But those lawsuits and countersuits over shutting the pipeline down have been mired in procedural battles, especially over whether the cases belong in state or federal court. The governor and the attorney general want home-field advantage. Enbridge thinks it will be treated better in federal court.

Deciding which court should hear the cases should have been resolved swiftly at the outset. But court cases are slow by nature, and these have moved even more slowly than usual. It took until earlier this year for the U.S. Supreme Court to finally resolve that one of the lawsuits — not all of them, just one of them — should proceed in state court.

In the meantime, Enbridge’s separate lawsuit was parallel-tracking in federal court, where a 75-year-old judge allowed it to languish before handing it off to a colleague in 2024. The new judge sided with Enbridge late last year, but that decision has been appealed. In time, it too could end up before the U.S. Supreme Court.

The result of all this litigation has been delay and frustration. More than seven years after the first case was filed, only one trial judge has even reached the merits of whether Gov. Whitmer was within her rights to withdraw permission for Enbridge to keep using the pipelines.

With Line 5 still pumping oil across the bottomlands, the tunnel is stuck in permitting hell. The state’s environmental agency has devoted thousands of staff hours and several years to issuing permits. Those proceedings included “four public information sessions, four public hearings and four tribal consultations,” and review of “more than 2,600 comments from the public.” Then came a separate round of permitting from Michigan’s utility regulators, which reviewed the tunnel for safety concerns, in particular the risk of fire or explosion. The record swelled to 1,300 pages of testimony, 8,000 pages of exhibits, and thousands of public comments — after which regulators told Enbridge to submit still more studies about fire and explosion risks.

Environmental groups and Indian tribes have fought the tunnel every step of the way. They want to see Line 5 shut down, not new construction to preserve an old oil pipeline. Understandable as that position is, it depends on winning a shutdown in court, and after seven years, no court has ordered one. In the meantime, every permit that gets struck down leaves the dual pipelines exactly where they were in 2018: exposed on the lakebed, under a shipping lane.

Nonetheless, the tunnel’s opponents found a receptive audience at the Democratic-controlled Michigan Supreme Court, which ruled, eight years after the tunnel was initially announced, that the state had to go back to the drawing board. State permits will now take years to reissue. That’s assuming the incoming governor supports the tunnel, which is by no means certain.

The federal permit, at least, is finally in hand. The Army Corps of Engineers signed off on the tunnel in August, after taking six years to issue a 1,500-page report. Now that it’s done, environmentalists and Indian tribes are certain to sue. But without the state permit, it hardly matters.

Environmentalists who oppose permitting reform are not just taking a stance against fossil fuel development. They are also defending a status quo that is full of its own environmental risks. The rules that slow a gas pipeline are the same rules that slow a reservoir, sediment diversion, and tunnel.

BAAJA would help us fix what’s broken, but it is only a first step. American law, especially at the state level, would remain full of levers for disgruntled opponents to pull, and courts are still much too quick to take their concerns seriously.

Do we just wait for an anchor to rip a hole in Line 5? Just accept that the Everglades will remain an ecological basket case? Just watch our bridges and rail lines collapse, the victims of age and erosion?

We made some mistakes in the past. Of course we did. But we made an even bigger one when we disabled ourselves from moving past them.
