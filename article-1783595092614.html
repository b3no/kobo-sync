Mark Carney’s national liberalism

The center-left exception to the rule

Matthew Yglesias
Jul 9
∙
Paid
 
READ IN APP
 

About a month ago, I found myself asked to speak on a panel about the crisis of the global center-left and struggling a little to think of something new to say. What occurred to me is that in the spirit of “the exception that proves the rule,” it might be useful to approach the question from the opposite direction and think about Canadian Prime Minister Mark Carney, who is running what seems to be clearly the most successful center-left political party in the world right now.

The idea of the exception that proves the rule is not that the existence of an exception demonstrates that a rule is true. It’s that if you lay down a rule (“the center-left is in crisis globally”) and then find an apparent exception (“the Liberal Party of Canada is doing great”), this tests what the rule actually is and lets you rephrase it more precisely.

In other words, if we understand what’s distinctive about Carney — or about the Liberal Party or about Canada — we can better understand what’s happening everywhere else.

Note that what is impressive about Carney’s political achievements isn’t just that he won an election. Joe Biden and Keir Starmer also won elections. I know a lot of people on Bluesky think Starmer’s collapse debunks popularism and all the rest. But I think it mostly just goes to show that the popularism debate is over and boring because popularism is obviously correct and that the most interesting and difficult question is how to actually govern a country. That’s where Biden and Starmer both struggled.

Unlike them, Carney didn’t just win. He has maintained a net positive approval rating and put the Liberals on track to win a new election. He also persuaded several members of Parliament to switch parties and join the Liberals, helping take his government from a minority to a majority. And what makes this even more impressive is that the Liberals have been in power, mostly under former Prime Minister Justin Trudeau, since November of 2015.

My read of this situation is that some of the successes are tactical points that more or less everyone could learn from relatively easily and apply elsewhere. But a structural factor that’s harder to copy is that the Liberal Party has a different kind of relationship to Canada than you see from most center-left parties around the world.

Canada is a country whose existence has been repeatedly contested throughout history, whose statehood could have evaporated at various points in the past, and whose place on the map might vanish someday. The Liberals, more than any other force in Canadian politics, are the national party of Canada — they’re the party that says it’s good that Canada is up there, the true north strong and free. The particulars of Donald Trump’s assault on Canadian sovereignty re-engaged that mentality. That’s not just an electoral shot in the arm; it gives the Liberals a mission and purpose and a way of thinking about policy choices and relating to voters that most center-left parties around the world really struggle with.

Justin Trudeau’s success story

To get this story right, I think you need to start with the point that even though Trudeau left office unpopular, he is probably the most successful politician of our time.

In a democracy, nobody stays on top forever. But Trudeau took over as leader of a once-great political party that had fallen into third place and was plausibly going to go the way of the old Liberal Party of the United Kingdom. Instead, he led them to three electoral victories. He governed for nine and a half years, and when he stepped aside he did so in favor of a fellow Liberal, Carney, who proceeded to win an election on his own and then improve the Liberals’ standing through the aforementioned floor-crossings. This kind of successful transfer of power is extremely hard to pull off. It’s what made Ronald Reagan and F.D.R. such titans of 20th-century American politics, and it’s why Barack Obama, though he mostly got things right, ultimately left a shallower legacy than he should have.

It’s true, of course, that Carney’s political success has come in part from breaking with Trudeau on key issues. He rescinded the unpopular Trudeau-era consumer carbon tax and has moved to sharply cut immigration after it surged under his predecessor.

In the very short term, this can make Trudeau look like a failure whose successor has done well purely by repudiating him. Zoom out, though, and you see that Trudeau’s cash benefit for low-income kids is still in place. So are the expansions of Canadian Medicare to include dental benefits and prescription drugs. The repealed and unpopular consumer carbon tax was not having a large impact on emissions. The bulk of the work of Trudeau’s climate plan was done by the industrial carbon tax, which was (and is) less salient to consumers and remains in place. On immigration, the Stephen Harper-to-Trudeau-to-Carney seesaw is going to leave what was already one of the most open countries on Earth more open to immigration than it was before the Liberal win in 2015 — hopefully in a sustainable, controlled, and selective way.

Getting squeezed out of power must have hurt Trudeau’s feelings on a number of levels. And unlike Kamala Harris, Carney really did vocally break with him in several ways and credibly position himself as an agent of change. But in doing so, he secured Trudeau’s legacy and set him off to travel the world with Katy Perry.

Tactical lessons from Canada

A big part of what happened here, I think, is that Canadian Liberals learned some valuable tactical lessons from the failures of American Democrats.

In Trudeau’s case there was no question of incapacity or coverup or scandal; it was just that he was unpopular and clearly going to lose the election, so he got dumped as leader. The challenge to him came in the first instance from Chrystia Freeland, a senior member of his government who had served in various cabinet roles over the years. Freeland used to be a D.C.-based journalist, is well-known globally, and I think was broadly positioned as “next in line” to lead the Liberals.

But, again learning a lesson from the United States, the Liberals ditched not only the unpopular leader but also the natural successor.

The problem with Freeland, after all, is that if Trudeau’s unpopularity was the problem then replacing him with a senior member of his cabinet doesn’t really solve anything. My intuition, trained on the mechanics of the Democratic Party, was that there was no way a well-qualified woman who clearly deserved the leadership based on service and history was going to get shoved aside in favor of a less-qualified man. And the Liberals I’ve spoken to about this really did feel bad about Freeland losing her opportunity. But they felt that they had to be tough-minded rather than sentimental. Pierre Poilievre and the Conservatives had real weaknesses, but to exploit those weaknesses the Liberals needed to position themselves as offering a form of change. That was easier to do with Carney, so they picked Carney.

Their choice was a contrast with recent Democratic Party decision-making on another level.

Carney — like John F. Kennedy, Jimmy Carter, Bill Clinton, and Barack Obama — was an outsider to Ottawa and Canadian party politics but not a radical. This is the magic formula for progressive political parties: fresh faces and good government and reform without hard-left ideology. Democrats keep acting as if the only options are the most tedious time-serving establishment warriors and radical leftists. But Canada reminds us that party leaders can be tough-minded and look beyond their own insular cliques to find talent and win. But what’s most impressive about Carney isn’t his electoral win; it’s the staying power of his popularity. And for that you need to look beyond tactics to strategy.

The Canadian party

Canada is a bit of a weird country. When it was a British colony, the United States tried to conquer it repeatedly. Granted, there’s a kind of obvious logic to the large English-speaking former British colony absorbing the adjacent English-speaking British colony. Of course, not all of Canada is English-speaking. But that’s odd, too. For Canada to today be neither a British colony nor annexed by the United States nor divided along linguistic lines into separate countries is a bit like paddling upstream.

And a big part of the story the Liberal Party tells about itself is that it is less ideological than most modern political parties around the world, in part precisely because it is a party that is dedicated to that difficult paddling mission.

It’s not that only the Liberal Party has supported Canada qua Canada throughout Canadian history. But the Liberals have been most distinctively dedicated to it. In the 19th century they were the more anti-imperialist party. They became the party that was more interested in conciliating Francophone concerns. They were the party that crafted the Constitution Act of 1982 and formally secured total Canadian autonomy from Britain. By contrast, the Progressive Conservative Party under Brian Mulroney won big majorities along the lines of Ronald Reagan and Margaret Thatcher, but (unlike other Anglophone center-right parties) regional differences of opinion tore it apart and it no longer exists. A Canadian center-right was eventually reconstituted, but that happened by embracing a destiny as a party of western grievance politics. The left-wing New Democratic Party reached its high-water mark in 2011 by dominating Francophone seats.

The Liberals are the party that is focused on keeping the country from flying apart and keeping it independent. That meant the crisis induced by Donald Trump trolling about turning Canada into the 51st state played right into their core competency of doing what it takes to win and save Canada.

Patriots and pragmatists

One part of this is just that the Liberals do a better job than contemporary center-left parties in most countries of embracing patriotism and positivity about their country.

Canadian patriotism can seem a little funny to Americans at times because it’s a kind of very non-nationalistic patriotism grounded in the “mosaic, not melting pot” vision of multiculturalism. It’s hard to imagine an American political ad thoroughly rooted in patriotism also being as ironic and jokey as this Carney ad. But it’s also hard to imagine an American Democrat running an ad that is so comprehensively situated in patriotism and national identity and celebration of the country’s quirks.

Patriotism is smart politics, but jokes about the Tragically Hip do not win elections alone.

What I think is really critical here is that sincere belief in the Liberals’ mission of national salvation has allowed Carney to be a bit cynical. He pivoted hard away from some unpopular Trudeau commitments on climate and immigration. He’s brokered new energy deals with Alberta. He’s done the kind of stuff that people like me are always saying Democrats should do. But notably he’s also not getting a ton of blowback the way a Democrat who moved this decisively would. He’s also not dogged by allegations of being “inauthentic” or excessively calculating and poll-tested even as he does a bunch of poll-tested stuff that’s calculated to win. That’s because there’s a kind of meta-earnestness behind the cynicism — just as, for all the jokiness of the Mike Myers ad, they’re actually deadly serious about the ongoing existence of Canada being on the line.

Democrats have done a lot of existential threat rhetoric during the Trump era, but it’s always rung pretty hollow.

Not because all the Orange Man Bad stuff is insincere, per se, but because it’s just clearly always taken a bit of a back seat to other considerations, including to dozens of different interest-group advocacy campaigns and also this long factional struggle playing out between “the establishment” and Bernie Sanders and his followers. This happens because the Democrats are, historically, an interest-group coalition party that slowly over the past two generations has been transforming into an ideological progressive party. It’s not an un-American party or an anti-patriotic one or whatever slanders the right might direct at it, but it’s certainly not an aggressively patriotic party. If you see a person flying a flag on his lawn, your guess is going to be that he’s a Republican. This is an electoral handicap on its own terms but also a bit of an impediment to certain kinds of solidaristic projects — including calls for electoral pragmatism.

The nation and the state

Back to where we started with the problems of the global center-left: I think an issue here beyond any specific election is that parties of the center-left are committed to being parties of the state and specifically parties of the government’s taxing function.

It’s not a big mystery how that came to be the case — the existing social safety net was largely created by the center-left. Where it was created by the right, that was normally an effort to preempt more progressive forces.

But those welfare states were also largely created by earlier, more patriotic and nationalistic iterations of center-left political parties. After all, part of the idea of redistribution and solidarity is that we all owe something to each other as members of the same national community. This is a totally reasonable idea and it’s not a coincidence that defending the existing safety net is Democrats’ most popular version of themselves. But the idea of a state whose function is to fulfill our mutual obligations to each other doesn’t make a lot of sense unless you also value the national community that the state governs. In a European context, maybe you can imagine a post-national progressive politics coming out on the other side as a forceful advocate for a European super-state full of Euro-patriot followers who wave the E.U. flag — though I would say witnessing everyone’s fervent attachments to their national teams during the World Cup makes me doubt how plausible that is. Here in America, meanwhile, there’s just no viable post-national option at all.

That doesn’t mean you have to become a frothing-at-the-mouth demagogue who’s talking about conquering Canada or Greenland. And it also doesn’t mean you need to become a paranoid xenophobe who thinks immigrants are destroying the country or has a zero-sum view of world trade. But you do need a real sense of being a national party that believes in your country qua country as something worth fighting for.
