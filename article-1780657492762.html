The age of snark

Punching in every direction

Jeremiah Johnson

It’s impossible to address the subject of internet snark without first revisiting the classic Tom Scocca essay “On Smarm.” Published on Gawker almost 13 years ago, the essay’s influence continued well past the death of the site.

Scocca described snark as a useful and natural reaction to smarm. Smarm is snark’s opposite, the yin to its yang. Where snark is disrespectful, skeptical, glib, and unconcerned with tone or social niceties, smarm is smug, sanctimonious, condescending, and obsessed with superficial politeness.

According to Scocca, smarm is “a kind of performance—an assumption of the forms of seriousness, of virtue, of constructiveness, without the substance. Smarm is concerned with appropriateness and with tone. Smarm disapproves.”

Snark is The Onion reprinting its classic article “‘No Way To Prevent This,’ Says Only Nation Where This Regularly Happens” word for word in response to the Parkland shooting. Smarm is David Brooks writing a column in response to the same shooting titled “Respect First, Then Gun Control,” where he argued the real problem was that liberals weren’t nice enough to conservatives.

Scocca also distinguished between snark and smarm by pointing out power differentials — a typical example of smarm would be a smug insider with enormous power and influence asking “Why can’t we all just get along and respect each other?” to an outsider critic, all the while abusing said power and deflecting all criticism into complaints about tone.

Smarm was arguably the dominant conversational tone during the 2000s and 2010s when Scocca was developing as a writer, especially for elites in political, cultural, and journalistic circles. Smarm was the reaction of a besieged elite confronted with people who refused to follow the rules of engagement. Smarm is Dave Eggers proclaiming you should not dismiss a book until you have written one or dismiss a movie until you have made one.

The internet and social media tore down walls and made criticism easier and more direct than ever before. Gawker, an irreverent outlet best known for tearing down elite tastemakers, was the foremost battleground between snarky outsider new media and smarmy traditional institutions.

Writing in Gawker, Scocca defended snark as the appropriate response to smarm, the “fuck you” to sanctimonious bullshitters who would rather tone police their critics and demand credentials than defend their choices on the merits to people they considered beneath them.

And in context, Scocca was right. In 2003, as liberals questioned the government’s case for war in Iraq, the president smarmily resorted frequently to the idea that “now is not the time for politics,” while his administration’s officials fired back by appealing to tone:

“I call on all the vociferous Democrat critics, from Kerry to Dean and from Daschle to Pelosi, to have the courage to tell their hero Ted Kennedy that he went too far,” DeLay said in a statement responding to Kennedy calling the Iraq War a fraud. “Are they leaders or are they just liberal pundits?”

In an environment like that, snark was a useful corrective.

But today’s media environment is different. We live in an age absolutely drenched in snark, especially online. Snark oozes out of the pores of every single platform. It’s almost impossible to have an object-level conversation on any major social media site that doesn’t devolve into snarky asides, cheap dunks, and insults rather than arguments.

Snark was a useful weapon against smarmy, insulated, credential-obsessed elites. But now that the outsiders who wielded it have themselves become the elite, snark has lost its target and moral force, curdling into a cheap and corrosive default register. Snark was a weapon of democratization, meant to pull the self-important off their high horses. But when it’s lobbed freely at any and all people you disagree with, it eradicates the practice of reasonable disagreement.

The age of the quote tweet

It’s difficult to overstate how omnipresent the cynical, ironic, snarky tone has become online. It’s the default conversational register for nearly every social media site.

We communicate in the language of snark now: We celebrate “clapbacks” and “dunks” over our opponents. We “read them for filth” while “throwing shade.” We “mogg.”

Our algorithms are built to encourage this kind of behavior, amplifying conflict because it juices engagement metrics. They are structurally biased toward cheap snark.

The development of “quote tweeting,” where instead of responding to the person you were disagreeing with, you presented your disagreement to your followers, changed the dynamics of online discussion. It’s no accident that so many of the internet’s worst and most internecine battles happened on Tumblr, a platform where reblogging was the defining feature.

Beyond the general tone of snark that permeates the internet, specific “snark communities” have sprung up like weeds. Typically organized on Reddit, snark subreddits like r/FundieSnarkUncensored, r/Blogsnark, and r/NYCinfluencersnark were created to allow users to hate-consume and snark on specific types of content.

These communities are numerous and popular: Here’s a list of 184 snark subreddits. This list only contains snark subreddits specifically dedicated to TikTok influencers (and was created more than three years ago).

Beyond our posting behavior, snark is now the default tone in nearly everything.

Arguments about sports on ESPN are now framed around snarky panel shows. Our superheroes are almost all sarcastic quippers, too cool and disaffected to be serious.

Literary critic Lauren Oyler became famous for how harsh and cutting her reviews were (until, in the great circle of media criticism, the snarkers came for Oyler herself).

Politicians now speak in the tone of snark, always sarcastic, searching for a snide remark to insult the other side. The vice president of the United States posts on Twitter with the maturity of a teenage troll:

@JDVance
The president told me he did this on Indigenous Peoples Day in honor of you.
Elizabeth Warren @SenWarren
For two excruciating years, I have called for the return of the hostages brutally kidnapped on October 7th and held in Gaza. Today is a good day. Surviving Israeli hostages are finally home and reuniting with loved ones. I'm thinking of them and their families on this joyful day

This sort of behavior is everywhere.

Certainly, our algorithmic systems select for the most outrageous, trollish behavior because that behavior typically leads to the highest engagement. But algorithms can’t tell the entire story here; the rise of snark dates back well before algorithmic feeds dominated culture.

In 2022, Andrey Mir wrote about the explosion of authorship in Discourse Magazine. Mir’s basic point was that for most of human history, the idea of authorship — that is, the idea that you are writing down words, and those words are worthy of being read and respected by others — was limited to a tiny, tiny minority of the population. Most people throughout history weren’t even literate, and even post-printing press and post-literacy revolution, “authorship” was mostly reserved to a privileged or professional class.

Normal, everyday people did not have the automatic assumption that their opinions or thoughts needed to be written down and shared.

But today, everyone thinks that. We live in an age where we all believe by default that we have something to say. Generations of primary school teachers have taught us that our voice is important and that we should share our thoughts with the world.

Just as importantly, everyone has an audience now. Anything you say online might end up getting broadcast to millions of people, and it’s now cheaper and easier to share your thoughts than at any time in human history.

The world now consists of billions of authors, and while I’m sorry to be the bearer of bad news, most of them don’t have anything new or interesting to say. But they still feel a deep-seated need to say something, an instinct that tells them to keep posting. So we have a lot of people who want to comment on the state of things, who don’t have anything insightful to contribute… and who default to snark.

Snark is easy. It’s simple to imitate. It’s gratifying in the way that being a part of a mob is gratifying.

See a topic trending? Find out what others are saying, who’s getting dogpiled, or what idea is currently in or out of favor, and jump in with your own snark. Quote tweet that person with “Imagine being this much of an idiot,” “Read the room,” or the always infuriating “Um, don’t know if you realize, but that’s bad, actually. Hope this helps.”

None of these responses have much argumentative content, and they rarely explain the nature of the disagreement, leaving the reader to fill in the blank at their leisure.

Snark is a cheap signal. It’s costly to make original insights or to communicate in careful language. It takes effort and time, and it requires a level of openness and vulnerability to make an earnest argument.

It’s much easier to snark. Snark is a great tool for those who treat arguments as soldiers and who view online conversations not as exercises in truth seeking but as battles to be won at all costs. And it’s slowly poisoning us all.

Elite snark is killing us

Snark as a reaction to smarm served a practical purpose. It was especially useful in the hands of intelligent writers like Scocca, whose anger penetrated the smarmy bubble of self-satisfied elites. Scocca wrote that “anger is upsetting to smarm—real anger, not umbrage.” But while our elites will sometimes still dabble in smarm, they’ve adopted snark as the dominant tone.

When everyone, everywhere is snarking, snark loses its moral high ground.

The nature of snark has changed as well. Scocca imagined a platonic ideal of snark, a righteously angry outsider speaking truth to power in a tone that hypocritical, contemptuous elites can’t stand.

But today’s snark isn’t just aimed at snobbish elites. It’s aimed at everyone. And any honest analysis would reveal that, in almost every way that matters, the outsiders have already won.

The beleaguered little guys stormed the gates of our media, our culture, and our politics. Outsiders are the elites now. YouTubers are making smash Hollywood hits. Rebel Substackers now control major media institutions. Our current president is the definition of a glib, sarcastic political outsider. Who exactly are the elites we’re left to rage at?

If Scocca’s idealized snark was earnest, righteous anger, modern snark is more of a casual, thoughtless dismissal. The typical example of snark today is not a well-constructed, blistering takedown of a powerful blowhard. It’s a snide reply in a comment section saying “typical dumb lib 🙄.”

The internet’s self-described snark communities

Modern snark replaces curiosity with contempt. It reduces human interaction to a series of insults and trains us to think the worst of one another. We’re overdosing on snark, and it’s corrosive to the soul. Nowhere is this clearer than in the dedicated snark communities.

While some may just be innocent places to joke about a particular pop star or influencer, most are incredibly toxic places. They frequently body shame, bully, and harass celebrities they don’t like.

Members of a snark community encouraged users to harass influencer Trisha Paytas’ doctors, snarkers called Child Protective Services on YouTuber Ethan Klein, they reported another YouTuber to ICE, and they were cruel enough to allegedly drive one influencer to kill herself. Snark communities often reach the level of obsessive hatred, with some creating paranoid, Pepe Silvia-style diagrams of influencers they despise, and others creating a 28-page document attempting to prove a TikTok influenceer didn’t actually have cancer (she did).

Sometimes the people attacked by these communities are called “elites.” But that term is so capacious as to have almost lost all meaning. Is an influencer with a few hundred thousand Instagram followers an “elite” that needs to be torn down with the power of the mob’s derision?

There’s a role for righteous anger when it’s wielded by discerning commentators against truly hypocritical elites. But when that snark is turned on society as a whole, you end up with the hateful groupthink seen in the communities above. Does anyone really believe that what the world needs right now is for everyone to be even angrier at everyone else? Or does that level of omnipresent anger and snark cause us to retreat into cynicism and irony?

Today, irony and detachment are attractive because they’re safe — if you wrap everything you say and do in multiple layers of protective irony, you’re immune from criticism. You can’t be snarked if you never believe in anything, if you’re too cool to care. But believing in things is good! And half the time, it feels like we’ve collectively abandoned the idea of earnest belief in favor of a postmodern, nothing-is-real approach to life.

David Foster Wallace, in his 1993 essay “E Unibus Pluram,” imagined a better way forward:

“The next real literary ‘rebels’ in this country might well emerge as some weird bunch of ‘anti-rebels,’ born oglers who dare to back away from ironic watching, who have the childish gall actually to endorse single-entendre values. Who treat old untrendy human troubles and emotions in U.S. life with reverence and conviction. Who eschew self-consciousness and fatigue. These anti-rebels would be outdated, of course, before they even started. Too sincere. Clearly repressed. Backward, quaint, naive, anachronistic. Maybe that’ll be the point, why they’ll be the next real rebels.”

There are likely some limited instances where snark is still useful — perhaps in spaces where smarm still dominates, such as critiquing insincere religious leaders. But for the most part, modern snark is toxic. And Wallace’s call for a return to sincerity would do us far more good than yet another snarky takedown.

The world would be a better place if more people spoke up for what they believed without pretense or sarcasm, if more people respected the norms of good-faith public debate, and if more people practiced baseline kindness.

I know that I frequently fall short of that standard, but hopefully you’ll consider the idea before the messenger. None of us can change the discourse overnight. But even in a poisoned landscape, we can tend a garden and create a healthy space for a better community to grow.

Even though Gawker’s snark didn’t change the media environment immediately, it did eventually change things. It is possible for a small publication, or a small group of dedicated individuals, to redirect culture. And moving to earnestness would be better — better for our own souls, better for the people around us, and better for society in general.
