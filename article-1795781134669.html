Who cares if bisexuality is a choice?

Besides, it’s probably a good one.

Maibritt Henkel
Nov 27
 
READ IN APP
 

As of 2024, nearly 1 in 4 Americans aged 18 to 27 identified as LGBTQ+ — a rise from just 1 in 16 millennials a decade earlier.

It is no mystery what has driven this increase: Bisexuality is far and away the identity category that has grown the most in the last 10 years. Members of Gen Z are around twice as likely as millennials to identify with the bisexual label — and around six times as likely as Gen X.¹

What accounts for the bisexual boom?

On the right, it’s common to claim that Gen Z is just faking it. They don’t actually experience same-sex attraction at higher rates than earlier generations; they just think belonging to a marginalized identity sounds cool and bisexuality is the easiest one to claim. (There’s a version of this same explanation floating around in queer and progressive spaces as well, encapsulated in the observation that a lot of bi women seem to only date men.)

The intuitive liberal response is to insist that sexual orientation is not a choice. The reason rates of LGBTQ+ identification are so high among young people is that earlier generations are repressed and closeted. Increased acceptance has allowed an underlying, immutable truth to reveal itself. Boomers would be bisexual too, if only they libbed out!

Share

Both the conservative position and its liberal rejoinder assume that attraction is relatively fixed: that there is a “true” rate of non-straightness that holds across generations and was only undershot by past surveys due to social stigma.

But while the proportion of gays and lesbians has remained relatively stable for decades, bisexual numbers are undeniably in flux, driven largely by young women. In the General Social Survey, the gay and lesbian share of the population stayed flat from 2008 (1.6%) to 2018 (1.7%) while the bisexual share climbed from 1.1% to 3.3%.

Imagine a woman who is attracted to lots of men she meets but only occasionally other women. Fifty years ago, that woman would happily have called herself straight, dated and married a man, and called it a day. Today, she might call herself bisexual and be interested in pursuing a relationship with a woman. Neither version is necessarily suppressing her desires or misrepresenting them.

To insist that some natural rate of bisexuality is only now revealing itself would be to miss an opportunity to make a more radical case for accepting same-sex desire: that as gender norms change, more options will present themselves when it comes to sex, dating, and family, and people should be free to consider these with a relative degree of agency.

In other words, maybe there are a substantial number of young people jumping on the bisexual bandwagon who otherwise would have made do with heterosexuality — and maybe, contra the right, it is perfectly fine to do so.

The bisexual wager

Sexuality is complicated because it involves both external behavior and internal experience, and the two don’t always align. Who you have had sex with in the past doesn’t necessarily have any bearing on who you would like to have sex with in the future. As an identity category, sexuality exists always somewhat in the realm of possibility.

Research suggests that the possibilities for internal experience are particularly expansive when it comes to female sexuality. Women have repeatedly been found to have more capacity for same-sex attraction than men, even those women who identify as straight. In studies that measure responses to erotic stimuli, women are generally more likely to get aroused by images of both sexes.

Arousal is by no means the same as desire or identification. Most of us probably have the capacity to get turned on by things we do not actually want. That being said, polling suggests that women do in fact act on their relatively fluid sexual attraction, with 17.4% of adult women aged 18 to 44 reporting same-sex contact in their lifetime compared to 6.2% of men in the same cohort. (These numbers are from the 2011-2013 National Survey of Family Growth so do not even account for the bisexual boom).

Baseline empirics predict that women would pursue same-sex relationships more than men — as would the social reality that men face more stigma for same-sex attraction than women. But the meteoric rise in bisexual identification among women — roughly quadrupling between 2008 and 2018, according to the General Social Survey — suggests that there is something deliberate about their hedge against heterosexuality that goes beyond casual attraction.

According to Gallup, 31% of Gen Z women now identify as something other than straight (mostly bisexual) versus 12% of Gen Z men (mostly gay). For millennials, that gap is 18% versus 9%.

The heteropessimist horseshoe

Right now, traditional relationships between men and women are facing serious structural stressors. Women in particular seem unconvinced that a romantic relationship has anything to offer them. Single women are far less likely than single men to be looking for a partner, and the share of 12th-grade girls who say they want to get married has fallen from 83% to 61% since 1993, while boys’ expectations have held steady.

There appear to be no quick fixes to this crisis. Men’s relative earnings are falling, which complicates the once straightforward decision for women to get married. Likewise, women increasingly expect to share housework and childcare equally, and yet most straight couples struggle to pull this off. And all this against the backdrop of the widest political gender gap in decades among Americans under 30.

If women are seizing this moment to reimagine their romantic and sexual horizons, that would not be altogether surprising. They have much to gain from entering into relationships free of traditional gender norms and expectations. Same-sex couples are, for instance, more likely to split chores and responsibilities based on preference and convenience, rather than conventionally “male” and “female” tasks.

Even prior to the stage of sharing a domestic life together, there are plenty of conceivable advantages to same-sex dating for women: sexual encounters might feel safer and emotional communication easier. The sex itself may be better: the orgasm gap favors lesbian women 86% to heterosexual women’s 65%. Female friendships already involve a great deal of intimacy; converting that into romance can feel intuitive.

Ironically, it is often conservatives who make the most passionate case for single-sex spaces, insisting that there is something unique about being surrounded only by other women. Trans-exclusive policies typically rest on the premise that cis males necessarily introduce the risk of physical violence. Why wouldn’t this logic about the comfort and safety of female homosociality extend to female homosexuality?

It doesn’t take being a hardened heteropessimist to see why young women today would express at least some curiosity about same-sex romance. Until recently, publicly communicating that curiosity would have been madness. Even among Democrats, perceived morality of gay and lesbian relationships was only around 50% in 2005. If you could swing it, then settling for whatever socially sanctioned heterosexual desire you were lucky enough to experience was definitely your safest bet. But given how much public acceptance has improved, many women may feel that the benefits of bisexuality outweigh the costs.

Anyone on the right making the argument that the incentive structure around LGBTQ+ identification has changed is absolutely correct.

Just try it

During his race for California governor this fall, Republican Steve Hilton released a campaign ad titled “Just Try It, California.” The video mimicked an intimate encounter between two young women. One has blue hair, the other a septum piercing. Behind them hangs a pride flag. “I’ve just never done anything like this before,” one says to the other. “What if my dad finds out?” “Just try it,” insists the other, taking her hand. The camera pans to an election ballot lying between them and the nervous girl filling in the bubble for Hilton.

The video plays on the idea of social contagion, the idea that a behavior or attitude can spread through a group like a physical illness. Hilton wanted support for his candidacy to catch on in the way queer identification is shown to catch on in this extremely creepy scene.

In reality, it is not clear how much same-sex action young people are actually getting. Although Gen Z seems eager to label their sexuality, they are having notoriously little sex with partners of either gender. In the U.S., the share of 18- to 29-year-olds who report not having had sex in the past year doubled from 12% in 2010 to 24% in 2024.

Daniel Cox, senior fellow in polling and public opinion at the American Enterprise Institute, makes this point in his recent book Generation Uncoupled: “For many young people, coming into their sexual identities is, in a way, theoretical. That does not mean that they are wrong or confused, but this key part of who they are has not been tested or tried out by engaging with real people.”

Cox sees it as no coincidence that the largest annual increase in LGBTQ+ identity occurred during the pandemic, between 2020 and 2023. He argues that because Gen Z was socially isolated and glued to their phones, they were particularly susceptible to the “sense of belonging” that LGBTQ+ content creators offered them. Identifying as a sexual minority granted them access to a community without having to leave the house. It also gave them a label by which to understand themselves when other defining credentials, like volunteering in your community or being the captain of your high-school soccer team, were no longer meaningful.

I am not convinced it is a problem for people per se to identify with or express interest in a sexual experience before having it. But I agree that online exposure to different identities is not, as he puts it, “the most reliable method of learning about ourselves.”

I would even go as far as to say what Cox does not: that young people should be encouraged to put their sexualities to the test. First, because navigating actual intimacy tends to be formative and enjoyable in a way that abstractly imagining it is not. And second, because if something other than heterosexuality turns out to work for Gen Z, then that is an arrangement with genuine advantages.

Maybe some of the young bisexual women today will date women and discover they prefer men; maybe some will discover they prefer women. I would count either outcome as preferable to a generation of young people who have become so nervous to kiss someone that they barely date at all.

Heterosexuality may adapt to the economic and cultural shifts of the 21st century, but, in the meantime, if Gen Z is giving homosexuality a try and likes it, that seems a whole lot better than remaining withdrawn and disconnected.

Is the boom turning to bust?

Earlier this fall, a graph was making the rounds on Twitter. The accompanying article showed that rates of bisexual identification were significantly down among ninth-grade girls: from 26% in 2021 to 13% in 2025.

This is just an early data point, and the fall appears to be concentrated among young high schoolers rather than their older peers. But it has ignited rumors about a bisexual bust.

Many on the right have gleefully seized on the decline. President of the American Principles Project Terry Schilling tweeted: “Amazing what happens when you cut the Department of Education and USAID.” Erik Kaufmann, author of The Third Awokening: A 12-Point Plan for Rolling Back Progressive Extremism, declared that “American teens are becoming straight again.”

I wouldn’t hold my breath. Perhaps the surge of a few years ago will calm down, especially as the peak excitement of TikTok bisexuality wears off among 14-year-olds. But I suspect a new bisexual equilibrium will form and that it will be higher than many conservatives would like.

Gender is changing for good. Masculinity and femininity no longer map onto the categories “man” and “woman” in the way they once did. As binary gender norms become less prescriptive, you are bound to come across the traits you find attractive in a broader range of individuals, be that how they wear their hair, what they do for work, or whether they pick up the check.

This is going to complicate who people will fall in love with. Even so, most people will probably stick to heterosexuality, which, after all, has plenty of its own perks. Plus, the fact that the rate of lesbian and gay adults hasn’t changed much, even as the bisexual rate has skyrocketed, suggests that plenty of people really do feel unambiguously attracted to one gender.

And yet, as long as tolerance for personal expression continues to expand and heterosexuality continues to fall short of what many young people want for themselves, then attraction is sure to grow only more unpredictable and romance more idiosyncratic. Who can blame Gen Z bisexuals for opening themselves up to all the possibilities this new gender order might hold?
