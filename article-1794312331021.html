Should Texas Dems throw out the Bible with the bathwater?

School choice and rich curricula are good, even if conservatives like them

Nov 10
 
READ IN APP
 

You may have heard that Texas went blue last week.

While some of my Texan friends are delighted, the Texans I follow through my work as an education reporter are miserable.

“They’re overthrowing school choice in Texas,” mourned National Review’s Caroline Downey. “The ONE policy that is the best shot we have at reversing abysmal education outcomes. It’s literally a civil rights issue.”

Downey was responding to Democratic Gov.-elect Gina Hinojosa’s victory speech, in which she promised to fire the Republican-appointed commissioner of the Texas Education Agency, Mike Morath, on her first day in office. She also suggested she would end a $1 billion voucher program to help Texans pay for private school.

“On Day One, I will fire Mike Morath and we will work tirelessly to repair the damage of the Texas vouchers scam and end an era in which our schools are sold for parts,” Hinojosa said.

Now, I don’t think that Downey is right that school choice is the one policy that improves educational outcomes — and I don’t think Texas’ voucher program was a particularly good way of providing school choice. But I agree that poor educational outcomes are a civil rights issue, and that some kind of school choice program can offer a remedy.

Share

So while I am not very enthusiastic about what Texas has done to date, I think it’s incredibly important that the Democratic Party have an alternative positive vision, rather than just an opposition to programs that were supported by Texas Republicans.

Democrats and Republicans are both guilty of frequently putting ideology above results for children: Democrats by opposing any way to check whether kids are learning, and Republicans by mixing their good ideas (such as knowledge-rich curricula) with illiberal ones (such as insisting on putting the Ten Commandments on the walls of classrooms and finding reasons for all Islamic schools to be ineligible for the voucher program).¹

Hinojosa and the (likely) new Democratic majority on the Texas State Board of Education have the opportunity to do this right. They should build a pluralistic and liberal school system, rather than just rolling back the ways that Republicans built an illiberal one. And while the voucher program itself might be too expensive and unpopular to maintain, they should keep in mind that it’s a real injustice if rich families have educational options and poor families don’t.

Public schools can still offer choice

The hottest topic in Texas education for the last year has been vouchers. In 2025, Gov. Greg Abbott got a $1 billion state voucher program passed by the state Legislature; it gives low- and moderate-income Texas families around $10,000 per child to spend on private school.

Critics have pointed out that most of the families that took it were already sending their children to private schools, and only 17% had transferred from public schools; Hinojosa campaigned on ending the voucher program and said she would block its proposed expansion. She could also veto voucher funding in the next state budget.

I’ve always had mixed feelings about vouchers. Private schools are not, on average, better than public schools at educating kids.² Some liberals treat that as a knockdown argument against school choice, but I’m less convinced — after all, parents don’t care about average educational outcomes; they care about the outcomes of nearby schools they could plausibly send their kids to. If they have the choice of several schools, they will want to choose the best one available to them.

In this sense, public schools are always competing with homeschooling and private schools. The task of liberals is to make sure public schools are actually an appealing choice rather than a last resort.

I’ll go further:

It’s good when families of modest means have options, and liberals should consider it unacceptable if only rich families can opt out of poor-performing schools. Sometimes a school is unsafe or failing in its duty. No system is adequate if poor families are stuck in these schools.

Even if the new Texas government does not continue the voucher program, which seems likely, there are other ways to achieve school choice. In the Colorado school district I grew up in, it was straightforward to enroll in any school in the district. A group of organized parents could start a charter school. The district could then see, through parent decisions, which of its schools were failing to provide a compelling offering to parents — and the district would then descend on the weaker performers to address that, usually by adding a magnet program of some kind. (There is some research to the effect that these sorts of “public-school choice” setups improve student performance.)

That, to my mind, is school choice at its best: Plentiful options for families create a wealth of information for school districts, which can then offer incentives for all schools within the public-school system to improve.

In 2025, Texas considered expanding public-school choice with Senate Bill 686, which would have allowed Texas students to transfer to any public school with open seats. Texas currently has some of the weakest public-school open enrollment laws in the country, and some districts charge out-of-district students tuition.

Hinojosa should draw a contrast between Abbott’s private-school-centric vision of school choice and a liberal one: choice among public schools, while the state works to make all of them excellent.

A liberal vision for religious education?

The Texas State Board of Education recently approved a core knowledge curriculum for use in Texas schools called Bluebonnet Learning. The curriculum has many good elements: lots of history and science material starting in early elementary school, which builds the broad knowledge context that upper-middle-class families take for granted. Texas should preserve most of this curriculum, because there is strong evidence that these elements strengthen student reading instruction.

But Hinojosa and the new board members may be looking to reconsider one recent controversial provision: the requirement to teach specific passages and stories from the Bible in public schools. Even if you’re a Christian, as most Texans are, you might be worried about how this would be implemented justly in a public-school setting. And you would be right to be worried: Texas has previously had issues with kids bullying classmates over religious differences.

I am not, in principle, opposed to all possible versions of religious instruction in schools: In fact, my kids’ own microschool includes a religious studies class that covers foundational texts of Judaism, Christianity, Islam, Hinduism, and Buddhism. I’m told that last week they read the Sermon on the Mount.

My kids’ instructors come from a wide range of religious backgrounds and are committed to not teaching their personal beliefs. Instead, they offer a fair and serious depiction of each tradition and its core beliefs and sacred texts.

Here’s why I think some kind of religious instruction is important: The world history class the students will take in the spring will make a lot more sense to them if they have some familiarity with different religions. (Imagine trying to understand why the Protestant Reformation was a big deal if you’ve never heard of Jesus or Paul!)

But the U.S. Constitution is very clear: The state should make no establishment of religion. This was one of the most important cultural breaks between America and Europe, and it has led to the flourishing of free thought and conscience.

Texas’ current religious studies curriculum feels, to me, like it tries to go right up to the line that any honest judge would call unconstitutional. Texas teaches the Bible alone, rather than trying to teach about religions and traditions more generally.

Texas decided to impose this requirement on teachers without the moral seriousness it demands and without providing guidance on how teachers can explain a tradition without proselytizing for it or demanding that the students adhere to it. “I am a non-Christian being forced to give sermons in class,” one teacher wrote in a complaint about the program. “No consideration was given to the rights of teachers and students of various backgrounds with this curriculum.”

Teaching a religious studies course is tough. Kids will respond disrespectfully and jokingly to supernatural beliefs that are unfamiliar to them. They will also express real and serious moral disagreements with worldviews that are alien to their own and their parents’. They will say things that other traditions consider blasphemous. It is an injustice to those students and those teachers — and a betrayal of our founding principles — to try to shoehorn the Bible into schools to fight the culture wars.

There’s a path forward that better respects what America is. If you choose to include religious studies in your curriculum, have students study multiple religions — and give teachers training on how to respectfully present a tradition without asking their class to participate in it, agree with it, or believe a word of it. If Texas cannot do that, it needs to drop its focus on bringing the Bible to public schools.

“I’d suggest they figure out a way to either make Bluebonnet inclusive of more religions or take religion out of it,” Andy Rotherham, an education policy analyst at the think tank Bellwether, told me in an email. “The basic idea is that you could have a blue-state show that’s serious about knowledge-rich curricula and content and also serious about pluralism. Texas only got the first part of that right.”

But Texas now has the opportunity ahead to get both parts right. That would be a vastly better legacy than an endless seesawing between liberal and conservative visions in America’s newest swing state.

Don’t neglect the basics

Texas policymakers seem unusually committed to education: Well before the high-profile skirmishes of the past decade, education was a central focus of George W. Bush as governor (and then, of course, as president).

The state is also a fairly high performer: If you look at the Urban Institute’s demographically adjusted National Assessment of Educational Progress rankings for 2024 (which control for factors like English-as-a-second-language, family background, and income), Texas ranks third in fourth-grade math and ninth in fourth-grade reading, as well as sixth in eighth-grade math and 10th in eighth-grade reading.

This is all the more impressive because the state is 42nd out of 50 in per-student spending.

So Texans should be proud of their system. That’s not to say they’re satisfied with it — Hinojosa’s promise to take “our children’s education back from a government that has failed them” in her victory speech was a rebuke to the state’s takeover of local schools. That move has been met with controversy in cities such as Houston, which has lost 21,000 students and faced increased teacher turnover, even as state officials point to improving test scores.

But while she’s likely to target both Abbott’s voucher program and the state’s biblical instruction — and she’s right to do so — she should make sure that her replacement pick for Texas education commissioner preserves and builds on the state’s strong track record.

Most of the work of improving schools isn’t ideological. You need to select high-quality curricula and offer excellent books for kids to read. You need to drill core skills in math. You need to do teacher training that helps teachers leverage the curricula to the best effect, and you need to measure how students are doing so you can intervene in underperforming classrooms. You need students to show up, and you need to be able to remove students whose behavior has large negative impacts on the rest of the class.

Does anything we do in schools actually matter?

It would be a mistake for the incoming Texas government to zero in on only the big, bitter ideological conflicts. The most important things it can do will be consistent execution on the basics.

If you go to any school and check what’s happening in the classroom, it will be at most 20% the result of state-level directives; the rest will depend on individual districts, principals, teachers, and families. While it’s impossible to take the politics out of education policy, those taking over education roles should keep in mind that they are not, primarily, stepping into political roles.

Follow Mississippi’s lead: Teach the kids to read first.
