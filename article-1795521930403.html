NEPA is not an environmental protection law

Pure process and status quo bias does not reduce pollution.

Matthew Yglesias
Nov 24
∙
Paid
 
READ IN APP
 

Stepping back from the specifics of the Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act (BAAJA) debate, I want to make a larger point about the National Environmental Policy Act (NEPA) and its role in American public policy:

This law has “environmental” in its title, but in a critical respect it’s just not an environmental law at all.

Which is to say it does not set any kind of standards related to environmental quality. The Clean Air Act makes rules about air pollution. The Clean Water Act makes rules about water pollution. We could make stricter rules about microplastics or forever chemicals or something else. We also have parks and other kinds of wildlife and landscape conservation programs.

None of this is NEPA.

NEPA is a law that makes it harder to do certain classes of things by imposing various procedural requirements, and it creates a litigation attack surface for people who do not like these classes of things.

NEPA is entirely indifferent as to whether the proposed thing is good for the environment or bad for the environment. What’s more, unlike real environmental laws, NEPA does not govern private-sector economic activity. The key idea of NEPA is the “major federal action.” It burdens government activities relative to private-sector activities and it burdens “action” (doing something new) relative to the status quo.

If you want to cut taxes on business investment to encourage more data center construction, that’s not a federal action for NEPA purposes, even though common sense says that tax law is a very important facet of federal policy. Maintaining the regulatory status quo is not a federal action even if the status quo is bad for the environment. If you want to build a light rail line that’s partially financed with a Department of Transportation grant, that’s a federal action and you need to do your NEPA homework.

If requiring federal (but not private) actions (but not inactions) to meet a set of burdensome procedural requirements leads to better environmental outcomes than not doing this, that would have to be essentially by coincidence.

I think a lot of progressive analytical errors about the post-1970 history of the United States are downstream of not understanding this.

It’s not just that progressives overrate NEPA but that they have an essentially false narrative of why contemporary government is the way it is. There’s this view that something called “neoliberalism” happened and that now because of “neoliberalism,” all the Democrats do is hand out tax credits rather than being cool New Dealers who support a robust state. But guess what — a tax credit to buy electric cars is not a major federal action but a mass transit line is.

NEPA is not the only reason for the rise of what Suzanne Mettler calls the “submerged state” or what Mike Konczal calls government by coupon, but it’s striking that progressives tend to neglect its existence while workaday bureaucrats are painfully aware of its relevance.

The bias toward inaction

When New York City was thinking about launching its congestion pricing program to reduce traffic in parts of Manhattan and raise revenue for mass transit, it was granted the grace of being allowed to comply with NEPA via an environmental assessment rather than the more burdensome environmental impact statement. That less burdensome document runs 868 pages with a two-volume appendix. The first volume of the appendix has 1,966 pages and the second volume is 1,173 pages. Yes, that is 4,000 pages worth of environmental review under the less burdensome of the two NEPA tracks for a program that charges people money to drive cars and uses the revenue to fund mass transit.

The reason the report has to be so long is not because the question of whether this is bad for the environment is hard to answer.

The report has to be long because creating the congestion charge zone was controversial. On net, it’s clearly a good policy. But like most policies, it’s not strictly Pareto optimal. Some people really would be better off if it had never been implemented. Beyond that, most people are a little bit change averse, so faced with a policy like this, there was a tendency of people (especially suburban drivers) to overrate the odds that they’d be made worse off.

When you enact a policy change that’s controversial, the people who don’t like the change try to find ways to stop it.

One way that you can stop a project in America is to sue, arguing that a major federal action has been given an official “finding of no significant impact” determination improperly. To get a court to rule in your favor on this, you do not need to prove that the federal action is in fact damaging to the environment. You simply need to establish that the environmental assessment leading to the finding of no significant impact was in some respect derelict, that something was overlooked or not considered.

Notably, in America’s adversarial legal system, there is absolutely no requirement that your participation in environmental litigation have any kind of good faith basis. You can be the Society of New Jersey Cheapskates Who Don’t Mind Getting Stuck in Traffic and sue with the argument that something went wrong in the finding of no significant impact. The agency being sued isn’t allowed to say, “Your honor, I think the plaintiffs are just cheapskates who live in New Jersey and don’t mind getting stuck in traffic and that’s what they’re actually mad about.” Or, rather, the agency can say that, but the fact that the claim is brought in bad faith is not legally relevant.

The coalition that fights against offshore wind projects is a complicated melange of groups that includes right-wing think tanks and fossil fuel interests but also whale conservationists. When it comes time to litigate, there is no legally relevant distinction as to whether you actually care about whales or just hate competition from alternative energy sources.

The transformation of progressive politics

Reasonable people can disagree as to whether making it arbitrarily harder for the federal government to take action is a good idea.

But I think it’s an important part of intellectual hygiene to get clear on who stands where and for what reason.

Contemporary progressives often like to claim the brand of things such as Franklin Roosevelt, New Deal public works, and the Tennessee Valley Authority. Yet it’s not just that you couldn’t build something like the Hoover Dam under today’s environmental rules — the origin of the Sierra Club was specifically in organizing to oppose building the Hetch Hetchy dam. The Natural Resources Defense Council originated as a project to oppose the proposed Storm King Mountain hydroelectric project.

It’s fine to think that the opponents of these kinds of projects were correct on the merits and that it’s good that the government can no longer execute major civil-engineering projects in a timely or cost-effective manner. But if that’s your view, I think you need to own the view.

What I tend to hear a lot instead are takes vaguely gesturing at political timidity or somehow blaming the ghost of Ronald Reagan.

The truth is that there are actual legal obstacles to getting big things done that simply did not exist in the heyday of the New Deal. Those obstacles are not “democracy”; they are an enormous increase in the ability of plaintiffs to engage in post hoc litigation against projects undertaken by democratically elected officials.

That litigation sometimes directly blocks projects, sometimes simply deters them, and oftentimes delays them and burdens them with compliance costs. NEPA is not the only such rule — there are similar procedural burdens related to historic preservation, and lots of states have adopted state-level NEPA-like bills that impose additional requirements — but it’s a prominent and widely misunderstood example. And we’re never going to get back to an active, competent state unless we get the intellectual history right.

An active state will sometimes do bad things

One issue that came up in the BAAJA debate was the idea that since the A.C.L.U. successfully threw some NEPA claims into their litigation against the Alligator Alcatraz immigrant-detention facility, this proved that BAAJA restraining pretextual environmental litigation is a bad idea.

I don’t think you can reasonably blame the A.C.L.U. or anyone else for finding every law they could to throw at Donald Trump. That’s just good lawyering. But by the same token, NEPA litigation against immigrant-detention facilities really is good lawyering, not good environmental protection. Nobody is seriously saying that the problem with Alligator Alcatraz is that they didn’t do a sufficiently thorough environmental impact statement. I sympathize with the goal, but again, that’s precisely because the goal is not actually trying to get more thorough environmental review.

By the same token, if we’d had NEPA in F.D.R.’s day, then maybe internment camps could have been blocked on pretextual grounds and that would have been good because the internment policy was really evil and bad. But only a very stupid person would think the lack of environmental review was the actual problem with F.D.R.’s internment policy.

An active and capable democratic state will sometimes do bad things that could have been prevented by crippling state capacity. But make no mistake, the crippling is completely general. As Richard Day reminds us, an environmental impact statement for a 5.6-mile extension of the Red Line in Chicago took 13 years to prepare and was more than 18,000 pages long. California High-Speed Rail, which has been underway for nearly 20 years at this point, just managed to complete its environmental reviews this month — which unfortunately is several years after the project was de facto abandoned due to cost overruns and delays. The Purple Line in Maryland went through a 12-year environmental-review process, after which a court ruling against it prompted additional litigation before the project moved forward.

I know a lot of transit examples because I’m very interested in transit and it’s also a core example of how NEPA just is not an environmental law at all, since dramatically slowing down all rail projects is obviously bad for the environment.

But the point, again, is that it’s general. If you want to stop some cruel new prison, a broader litigation attack surface helps. On the other hand, if existing prisons are cruel and you have an idea for a federal initiative to build kinder, gentler Nordic-style prisons, a broader litigation attack surface is bad.

The question really is whether you think it’s good, on net, for it to be extremely time-consuming and expensive for anything federally funded or touching federal land to get done. I guess “don’t ever build anything” counts as environmental protection if your dominant lens for understanding the environment is that of a hiking enthusiast who cares a lot about Western vistas. But if you’re worried about the impacts of air and water quality on human health — and certainly if you’re worried about global carbon-dioxide emissions — this is not helping. Even from a public lands perspective, though, note that oil and gas drilling have had a categorical exclusion from NEPA since 2005.

The rule of lawyers

I think that part of what’s happened here is that the tail is wagging the dog inside most legacy environmental organizations.

Conditional on the current litigation situation existing, it is rational for environmental groups to employ large legal teams to try to achieve their goals via pretextual litigation. This is much more labor-intensive work than doing policy analysis or funneling money to support good politicians. So you have these big litigation teams, and because everyone wants to be the hero of their own story, the people on the teams convince themselves that they are the thin green line standing between the status quo and catastrophe. It may be true that the same statutes they use are also used by other people to stymie progress, but that’s someone else’s problem.

The plain truth, though, is that not only is there a ton of bad litigation (and time wasted avoiding getting sued), but the litigation strategy is just not that effective.

If we want to restrict where data centers can be built, the best way to do that is for states to pass laws. And passing those laws won’t be that hard, because data centers are unpopular. My main actual concern with data centers is that they are a big new source of unpriced air and climate pollution, but since they’re unpopular, it should be feasible to actually make them pay a carbon price.

Unfortunately, an economy-wide carbon price is not politically feasible, because voters do not care as much about this issue as they, morally speaking, ought to. That’s a big problem, but litigation whack-a-mole is not a solution. You’re just not going to dramatically increase fossil fuel prices via lawsuit and get away with hoping nobody notices. All that’s really being achieved is making it harder to deploy newer, better infrastructure, whether for energy or transportation or anything else.

This is a democracy, and the United States is a large, diverse, and pluralistic country, so even though you might want the rule to be “no legal harassment of projects I like, but plenty of legal harassment of projects I don’t like,” there’s no real way to make a rule like that.

For the past 50 or so years we’ve been living in the world with high levels of legal harassment, and I think progressives more than anyone else recognize it as unsatisfactory. But there’s no Green New Deal without the basic concept that if you win the election and pass laws appropriating funds to build something, you can actually build it.
