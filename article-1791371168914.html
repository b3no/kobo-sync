There aren’t 100 good TV shows

The original slop medium doesn’t need a canon.

Matthew Yglesias
Oct 7
∙

My father is a novelist and screenwriter who’s made several films and also worked on a couple of TV shows, plus some projects that never got off the ground. And while I’m not a film critic or a pop culture writer, the behind-the-scenes glimpses I’ve gotten over the years have always left me a little unsatisfied with the way these things are conventionally covered.

A lot of cultural commentary basically seems to me like what you’d get if you did political journalism without covering the ugly “how the sausage gets made” side of the legislative process, just naively taking what everyone says and does at face value.

The people who write about this stuff know better, obviously, but it’s sort of convention to pretend that they don’t.

One thing writers don’t like about writing novels is that it’s hard to get paid a lot of money, because selling novels to the public is not very lucrative. On the other hand, one thing writers do like about writing novels is that your imagination and creative desires aren’t limited by budgetary concerns.

After I saw the movie “American Fiction,” I picked up Percival Everett’s “Erasure” (the novel it’s based on) and was surprised to learn that unlike the movie, the book is largely set in Washington, D.C., where I live. The book includes some very realistic descriptions of the city, which is something you almost never see in film or television. D.C. is an expensive location to film. It’s common for projects like the first season of “The Night Agent” to shoot in Canada and just say it’s D.C. Ninety percent of the audience probably doesn’t care, but I live here, and I care!

The Jack Reacher books themselves are sloppy with settings and geography, so I sort of don’t mind that the adaptations are like this, too. “Night Agent” author Matt Quirk is meticulous about his verisimilitude, though, and I think it’s kind of annoying that doesn’t carry through into the adaptation.

But filming location is hardly the end of it. Every creative choice in film or television is also a business decision — and that’s especially true in television. With movies, the big business question is which projects get made at all. But television is kind of a grind-it-out medium, where tons of projects are produced and cost control matters a lot.

I was thinking about this when I was reading the New York Times “100 Best TV Shows of the 21st Century” list because while lists like these are quite literally made to be argued about, I just kind of dispute the premise that there are 100 good TV shows.

The medium does not always live down to Newton Minow’s infamous characterization of it as a “vast wasteland.” But what’s clear from the vantage point of 2026 is that while “The Sopranos” (ineligible for inclusion on the New York Times list because it launched in 1999) showed the world that television could be a forum for really great, really ambitious storytelling, it mostly hasn’t been. The success of that show created a brief flowering of efforts to push the medium, but so-called “prestige” television retreated relatively quickly into the same tropes and economic constraints that have always made television a land of mediocrity. And the exercise of list-building really drives that home.

What are lists for?

Again, viewed literally, the only real point of a list like this is to drive arguments and engagement, and on that level, mission accomplished by the New York Times.

But conceptually, I think the point of these exercises is to build a canon.

While I obviously do not agree with every inclusion on the “100 Best Movies of the 21st Century” list, it is a plausible canon-building exercise.

If you’re someone who’s into movies and enjoys having opinions about movies, you should watch the movies on this list. Even if you never come around to my point of view that lots of otherwise sensible cinephiles are grossly overrating “The Social Network,” it’s still worth watching the movie and thinking about it. If you’re a young person getting into movies, you should browse the American Film Institute’s list of the 100 best American movies of all time and the Sight and Sound poll of the greatest movies and check out the selections.

Of course, any given viewer won’t like everything on either list. But the batting average will be high, and you’ll learn something. Notably, though, the main quibble I would make with these lists is that by including films I would leave out, they’re squeezing out other films that I think are worthy. There’s just been a lot of good movies over the years!

The same is true for best novels lists and best albums lists. I was looking at Rolling Stone’s 500 Greatest Albums of All Time and wondering how often the staff is actually throwing on their number one pick, Marvin Gaye’s “What’s Going On.” That being said, Gaye is clearly a great musician! There’s tons of stuff on this list that I would not consider a personal favorite but is still very good, and if someone else wants to play it, I’m not going to complain.

With the TV list, by comparison, it’s not so much that I want to quibble with the specific choices as just … this is a lot of television.

Consider “House,” 95th on their list. I like “House.” I watched “House” pretty religiously when it was on. I’ve seen every episode. When I had the flu last December and was holed up in bed all day trying not to infect my family, I think I rewatched some episodes of “House.” But am I going to ask a 24-year-old I meet at the office, “Oh, you like watching TV shows? Have you checked out ‘House’?”

No, that’s absurd.

There’s like 130 hours of “House,” and it’s a formulaic procedural. I’m not saying don’t watch “House.” Sometimes in life you need large quantities of soft watch programming to fill time, and well-made formulaic procedural television is a great time-filler. But this whole thing just kind of underscores the extent to which it’s almost a category error to contemplate the vast majority of TV shows as major works of art that deserve a canon. They’re comfort food, not fine dining.

The false dawn of prestige television

“The Sopranos” looms so large over the whole “good television” discourse that the New York Times had to explicitly note that it was ineligible for inclusion because it debuted in 1999.

That show did something that had really never been done before and leveraged the long runtime of a TV series to tell a sprawling, novelistic story that couldn’t have been told in a movie format. Critics loved it, and it was a big hit with audiences, too. And it left us all with the sense that television had perhaps not been living up to its potential. You could do things in a TV show that were unique and astounding, and naturally, the success of “The Sopranos” inspired some similarly ambitious works — notably “The Wire” and “Mad Men,” which both came out in the 21st century and rank high on the New York Times list.

What I think is really striking after “The Sopranos” is that despite our eyes being opened to new storytelling possibilities, the TV revolution didn’t really pan out.

Television did not, after all, change forever. “The Wire” is a critical darling but was never a big commercial success. HBO cut short two well-loved shows, “Deadwood” and “Rome,” that were attempting to work on similar canvasses because the R.O.I. didn’t make sense. We now have this whole genre of “prestige” television, but that mostly signifies casting famous stars (which notably the “Sopranos” / “Wire” / “Mad Men” big three didn’t really do) and deploying more cinematic photography.

“Breaking Bad” and its lesser imitators look great in a way that no traditional TV show ever did. But it and other shows like “The Shield” and “The Americans,” which I enjoy, stick with the traditional business-driven TV convention of small casts with unrealistically narrow spheres of social interaction. When Jim and Pam get married on “The Office” and the whole wedding is full of coworkers who they don’t even like, it doesn’t make any sense as a story, but it’s completely forgivable — it’s a sitcom, and one of the funniest sitcoms, so what do you want?

But the fact that you have to react this way when evaluating even the shows that everyone agrees are good is just a sign that the whole medium has to be graded on a curve.

It’s not that television is unique in having constraints. Just like television, the nature of live theater obviously means that your story needs to work on a limited number of sets with a limited cast. But playwrights are expected to write stories that make sense within these constraints. On “Breaking Bad,” Hank is somehow a charismatic extrovert who has no friends and no social life, and everyone agrees this is one of the best shows ever.

“Game of Thrones” was a fascinating effort to leverage the power of valuable intellectual property to go back to really big TV storytelling. And the first few seasons are excellent, but as the show goes on, it gets bad. This is a real “only on television” problem. People don’t normally recommend a book or a movie to you until they’ve finished reading it, because endings are important! The end determines what the story was all along, how the themes land, and otherwise what the work actually is.

Television and our lives

I think it’s indicative of the genuine paucity of high-quality TV shows that to round out their list the New York Times felt compelled to include stories like “Ted Lasso” and “The Diplomat” that aren’t finished. If “Shogun” were over, I’d feel comfortable proclaiming it an excellent limited-series adaptation of James Clavell’s novel. But it was successful and didn’t involve any big stars, so the commercial incentive to churn out new seasons even though there’s no literary text to adapt is overwhelming. We haven’t seen what’s coming next. If it sucks, then “Shogun” sucks!

Speaking of which, there’s also a bunch of works on the list that aren’t TV shows at all in a traditional sense. “Chernobyl,” “Watchmen,” “The Queen’s Gambit,” and others are limited series. “RuPaul’s Drag Race” and “Anthony Bourdain: No Reservations” are reality shows.

Yes, this is all “on television.” But lots of stuff is on television! Was live coverage of the September 11 terrorist attacks the greatest TV show of the 21st century? It was certainly compelling. Everyone I know was watching. Nothing on “Survivor” (also on the list) matches the real-life drama of Chris Christie’s 2016 debate stage takedown of Marco Rubio or the high stakes of Joe Biden shitting the bed in his 2024 debate with Donald Trump.

The most popular show, reliably, is the Super Bowl, followed by other pro football games. Of course, popular is not the same as good — there’s no “NCIS” on the list — but football isn’t hacky in the way that network police procedurals are.

Lots of the things that are on the list as proper shows palpably fell off in their later seasons. If we’re talking about a sitcom, that’s forgivable; by the end of the run, we’re sentimental about the characters. But if you’re trying to judge shows as works of art, then it’s a big problem that “House of Cards” and “Ozark” went off the rails.

Which isn’t to say I have tons of other shows I would include above them. I think it’s just genuinely very rare for a TV show to tell a coherent story at a sustained high level of quality from beginning to end across a multi-season arc. The role of television in our lives is just that we forgive our shows for things that would not be forgiven in a book or a film or a play that’s supposed to hold together consistently.

None of this is to say that I’m some huge snob who doesn’t watch TV shows.

I’ve been having a blast firing up “Lanterns” every week and listening to recap podcasts and speculating about what’s happening next. I enjoy watching “House of the Dragon,” even though I don’t even think it’s particularly well-made. The whole family likes “Great British Bake Off.” For a stretch, my kid was really into “Jeopardy!,” which was fun. I like to put Amazon dadcore shows on as background while I answer emails. If I’m sick or I’m on a plane or I’m trying to chill out before going to bed, I tend to prefer the soft comforts of television.

But those soft comforts are the whole reason this kind of list-building and canon-making feels like a category error to me. Are you going to tell somebody that they need to stretch their horizons and raise their ambitions by watching more difficult TV shows and getting out of their comfort zone? Are you going to tell me that my cultural literacy is fundamentally deficient because I only watched three seasons of “Gilmore Girls” rather than all seven? I don’t think that makes sense.

It’s definitely possible to create works of real artistic merit in this medium, especially in the limited-series format if that counts (but does it?). It’s just extremely hard.

The way shows get made does not lend itself to outlining coherent stories, and the role television plays in our lives doesn’t generally align with artistic aspirations. People read “Middlemarch” generations after its original publication because it’s a great work. People watch old “Law & Order” episodes because “Law & Order” was built from the ground up to live forever in syndication because the episodes don’t need to be watched in order. It’s an entirely different path to classic status because it’s a medium that’s fundamentally dominated by low aspirations and bad commercial incentives.
