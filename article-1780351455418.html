The primaries are heating up

Crypto, AIPAC, and Republican maps are stoking the midterms.

The generic ballot continues to slowly creep in Democrats’ direction. As of May 30, polling aggregators have Democrats up about seven percentage points, with Democrats leading Republicans 49 percent to 42 percent among likely voters.

A Data for Progress survey that was conducted from May 15 to 18 found Democrats were ahead against Republicans by eight points among likely voters and were trusted more on 12 out of 15 issues, including the economy and jobs. Polymarket shows a Democratic sweep of both chambers as bettors’ most likely outcome, though the math shows the Senate as much more competitive than the House. Democrats would need a net gain of four seats to flip the chamber.

Of course, take the prediction with a grain of salt, as you should for most oracles.

Last Tuesday in Texas

Paxton wins, Cornyn’s out: The week’s biggest actual election news, which Matt weighed in on this morning, was in Texas. Trump-endorsed Attorney General Ken Paxton beat incumbent Senator John Cornyn in the Republican primary runoff on May 26, taking 63.8 percent of more than 1.38 million votes cast.

“Tonight we just sent a Texas-sized message to Washington,” Paxton said in his victory speech.

The result came a week after Trump officially backed Paxton, making Cornyn the latest in a string of Republican incumbents the president has worked to oust. Paxton will now face Democratic State Representative James Talarico in November in what’s expected to be one of the most expensive Senate races in the country. Republicans spent more than $100 million in the primary fight, which became a proxy battle over the future of the party. Paxton’s supporters faulted Cornyn for working with Democrats on gun legislation after the Uvalde school shooting and for not helping eliminate the Senate filibuster to pass the SAVE Act, NPR reports.

Menefee ends Green’s 20-year run: Overshadowed by the Paxton-Cornyn showdown was another notable ending that same night in Texas. U.S. Representative Christian Menefee beat U.S. Representative Al Green in a rare Democratic runoff between two incumbents of the same party, taking 69.4 percent of nearly 49,000 votes cast.

Green had represented the state’s Ninth Congressional District for two decades before being forced into the 18th after Republicans redrew his old seat to favor the G.O.P.

Menefee had already won a special election earlier this year to fill the seat left vacant following former Representative Sylvester Turner’s death, but had to run again to hold it for the full term starting in 2027.

Green’s steadfast opposition to both cryptocurrency and Israel made him a target. Fairshake, a pro-crypto super PAC, spent $6.5 million in the runoff to unseat him, The Hill reports. According to the Texas Observer, AIPAC was quick to congratulate Menefee on defeating “one of the most outspoken anti-Israel voices in Congress.” Even though AIPAC didn’t directly back Menefee in the race, the Observer reports that the group was thrilled to punish Green. From a different ideological angle, David Hogg, the Democratic National Committee’s former vice-chair, also took credit, attributing his Leaders We Deserve PAC with helping “elect and re-elect” this “next-generation leader.”

The Observer also noted that the race foreshadows fights to come across the South, as Republicans — emboldened by the Supreme Court’s recent decision weakening the Voting Rights Act — gerrymander Black districts like Green’s into oblivion. As many as one-third of Congressional Black Caucus members are vulnerable, and the C.B.C. is reportedly working on a strategy for safeguards.

The seat in question is safely Democratic, so Menefee is all but certain to win the general election in November.

The quicksand of a slow-motion map war

The real action of the past two weeks has been the continued redistricting war that is increasingly breaking Republicans’ way after a string of G.O.P. wins and a brutal loss for Democrats in Virginia.

Louisiana enacted a new congressional map last Friday, May 29, that eliminates one of the state’s two majority-Black House districts and replaces it with an additional Republican-leaning district. The map is likely the last one passed by a state legislature ahead of the 2026 election, NBC News reports. Louisiana had already delayed its House primaries, throwing out some 40,000 votes that had already been cast, in order to enact its new map.

According to CNN, it looks as though Republicans are positioned to win this year’s redistricting war roughly 10 seats ahead of Democrats. But still, some Republicans are unwilling to engage. South Carolina’s Senate rejected Trump’s call to redraw congressional maps and target Representative Jim Clyburn’s seat.

Tomorrow’s primaries to watch

Primary season is well on its way. On Tuesday, California, Iowa, Montana, New Jersey, New Mexico, and South Dakota will each hold theirs. Here is where the most closely watched races for Democrats stand:

Iowa

Iowa votes in its primaries tomorrow. Both a U.S. Senate seat and the governor’s mansion are open for the first time in years after Senator Joni Ernst declined to seek a third term and Governor Kim Reynolds also stepped aside.

In the Republican primary for the U.S. Senate, a JMC Analytics poll conducted on May 27 and 28 found U.S. Representative Ashley Hinson leading former State Senator Jim Carlin 58 to 19 percent, with Hinson’s lead widening to 61 to 22 percent when undecided leaners are included. Hinson is the heavy favorite and will almost certainly be the nominee.

The Democratic primary is more competitive. A Public Policy Polling survey of 672 likely Democratic primary voters conducted May 20 and 21 found State Representative Josh Turek leading State Senator Zach Wahls 52 to 31 percent. Unlike many of the closely watched Democratic primaries, this race is not defined by a traditional ideological divide, as both candidates support progressive economic policies. Rather, the consideration is whether Democrats are better served by a candidate with a proven record of winning in conservative territory, or one who believes that an unapologetically progressive message can break through, Newsweek reports.

In general election polling, both Turek and Wahls have led Hinson by about two percentage points in recent surveys — 46 to 44 percent — with around 10 percent undecided.

In the governor’s race, prediction markets give Democrats a 64 percent chance of winning the seat, which is striking in a state Trump carried by about 13 points in 2024. Democrat Rob Sand, Iowa’s state auditor, is running unopposed in the Democratic primary and leads Republican Randy Feenstra 51 to 39 percent in polling from April. But it’s unclear if Feenstra will be Sand’s opponent in the general. A JMC Analytics poll of the Republican primary found entrepreneur Zach Lahn leading Feenstra 24 to 22 percent, with Lahn’s lead widening to 27 to 24 percent when undecided voters who indicated a preference were included. The poll found Feenstra with weaker favorability ratings.

Iowa’s economy shrank at the start of 2025, and tariffs have hit the state particularly hard. Economic anxieties tied to those hurdles could conceivably move the state away from the president’s party.

California

California’s top-two primary is on Tuesday. There are 61 candidates on the ballot to replace term-limited Governor Gavin Newsom. The top-two finishers will advance to the November election regardless of party.

Earlier this year, then-Representative Eric Swalwell was the front-runner, but he ended his campaign and resigned from Congress in April following sexual assault allegations, reshuffling the field.

The final Emerson poll before the primary, which was conducted from May 27 to 28, provides some clarity on where the truly uncertain race stands in its final days: Democrat Xavier Becerra leads at 28 percent, followed by billionaire Democrat Tom Steyer at 22 percent and Republican Steve Hilton, the Trump-endorsed former conservative commentator, at 21 percent.

Becerra is effectively locked into the general election, leaving the race for second place as the real contest. If Steyer advances, California will be guaranteed a Democratic governor because both candidates are Democrats. If Hilton advances, Republicans will have a chance to win a statewide office for the first time since 2006. My advice? Watch the Steyer-Hilton margin — that’s the whole story.

Even when undecided voters are pushed to choose, the race for the number two spot remains deadlocked, with Hilton and Steyer each at 23 percent while Becerra stays at 28 percent. Further back are Sheriff Chad Bianco at 12 percent and Katie Porter and Matt Mahan at 5 percent each.

The crosstabs suggest why the battle for second place remains difficult to call. Becerra’s support is broad: He leads among Democrats (44 percent support), Hispanics (36 percent), and women (36 percent). Hilton has consolidated most Republican voters (59 percent), though Bianco still retains a sizable share of that bloc (29 percent). Steyer’s strongest support comes from voters under 30 (36 percent of that group). At the same time, 88 percent of Hilton and Bianco voters say they will definitely support them — the highest of any of the candidates — compared to 76 percent of Becerra voters and 74 percent of Steyer voters. Around 52 percent of Porter voters say there is still a chance they could change their minds. If any of the swayable voters decide to put their weight behind one of the three front-runners, the race could really go in any direction.
