Historical accuracy is a surprisingly modern idea

“The Odyssey” itself is deeply anachronistic.

Matthew Yglesias
Jul 27
∙
Paid
 
READ IN APP
 

Christopher Nolan’s adaptation of “The Odyssey” is dominating the box office, scoring excellent reviews, and spiking sales of the epic poem.

But rather than taking the win for Western civilization, right-wing culture warriors are crying into their soup and Elon Musk is promising to harness the power of artificial intelligence to create a “historically accurate” version of “The Odyssey,” which in his mind seems to mean a specific Old Hollywood aesthetic.

Of course, in that era of epic filmmaking, cross-racial casting generally went in the other direction. “Lawrence of Arabia” is an amazing watch, but to the modern eye it’s funny to see Alec Guinness playing Prince Faisal. As recently as 2014’s “Exodus: Gods and Kings,” Ridley Scott claimed that if he’d cast “Mohammad so-and-so from such-and-such” to play lead Egyptian characters, it would have been impossible to get the movie financed, which is how we got Joel Edgerton as Ramses II.

From where I sit, ultimately the difference is that “Exodus” is incredibly boring, even though it looks cool, while “Lawrence” holds up even though it’s a million years old.

Arguably the more interesting casting choice from Scott’s movie is Christian Bale as Moses. Bale fits very well within a cinematic tradition that looks back to Charlton Heston’s iconic performance as Moses, but he’s not Jewish or Middle Eastern-looking. Unlike Guinness, he’s not doing any kind of exotic accent, and unlike Edgerton, he’s not made up to look “ethnic.” There’s just English Moses shooting the shit with Pharaoh.

And yet, this seems fine to me. It’s a boring movie, but this specific scene isn’t boring, and in terms of “realism,” obviously these characters should be speaking Ancient Egyptian, but that wouldn’t be very fun to watch.

But I think what’s most interesting about the rightists’ opportunistic complaints about “The Odyssey” is that the whole idea of “historical accuracy” is a lot more modern than most people seem to realize.

The Victoria and Albert Museum in London, for example, has a War of Troy tapestry series. This is how it depicts the arming of Pyrrhus:

It looks like medieval Europe!

I am not an art historian, and I can’t speak to exactly how much of this was genuine confusion or ignorance about the classical Mediterranean versus stylistic choices. But obviously people in the 15th century were not deeply invested in the question of what Bronze Age Anatolia looked like. Making a tapestry is hard work, not the sort of task undertaken carelessly. Historical authenticity just wasn’t something artists were aiming for.

“The Odyssey” itself is ahistorical

One might note that “The Odyssey” features one-eyed giants and all manner of other supernatural creatures, so the whole thing defies realism from the start.

I don’t think that truly answers the objection, though — there is such a thing as a grounded story that nonetheless has fantastical elements.

But the text of “The Odyssey” itself was written centuries after the time period in which it’s set. For modern audiences, the distinctions between Homer’s Iron Age authorship and his Bronze Age setting are easy to ignore. But a lot changed between the two, and the text itself takes an extremely casual stance toward realism.

Perhaps the easiest way to see this is through the many references to iron in the poem. I draw attention to this not because these passages are of particular literary or thematic significance but because they reflect an author who (like the tapestry-maker) clearly does not care that much about historical authenticity:¹

  • Odysseus tells Telemachus “for iron of itself draws a man on” (αὐτὸς γὰρ ἐφέλκεται ἄνδρα σίδηρος), a proverb about iron weapons that a Bronze Age warrior would obviously not use.

  • When Odysseus stabs the cyclops in the eye, Homer compares the hissing noise it makes to the sound made when a smith dips an axe in cold water to temper it “for from this comes the strength of iron” (τὸ γὰρ αὖτε σιδήρου γε κράτος ἐστίν). The narrator is making an analogy to an iron-working technique that didn’t exist in Odysseus’s time.

  • Athena, in disguise as Mentes, says she’s “sailing over the wine-dark sea to men of strange speech, on my way to Temese for copper; and I am bringing with me shining iron” (πλέων ἐπὶ οἴνοπα πόντον ἐπ᾽ ἀλλοθρόους ἀνθρώπους, ἐς Τεμέσην μετὰ χαλκόν, ἄγω δ᾽ αἴθωνα σίδηρον). Pretending to be an iron trader is not a cover story you’d use in the Bronze Age!

“The Iliad” also contains dozens of references to iron. Notably, that text is attentive to the fact that the warriors should be using bronze weapons, but there are lots of casual references to iron tools and iron-based metaphors and analogies.

None of this is essential to the story, and it would be the easiest thing in the world for a filmmaker to “improve” on Homer’s work by purging these casual references and doing a fully authentic Bronze Age story. But the point is that trying to do a fully accurate historical rendering would be an adaptational choice that is at odds with the text.

What’s more telling, though, is the story itself.

Critics and viewers seem to agree that beyond questions of casting or boat design, Nolan has given us a modern or Christianized Odysseus who is haunted by remorse in a way that doesn’t really reflect the ethos of the poem.

This isn’t a question of inaccuracy exactly, but it’s a notable departure. Part of the experience of immersing yourself in old texts is immersing yourself in older attitudes. But which attitudes? Moses Finley’s argument in the classic book “The World of Odysseus” is that Homer is very much doing what Nolan does: telling a story that is set in the Bronze Age but reflects the ethos of a later time, which he locates in the ninth or 10th century B.C.E. Ian Morris argues for the eighth century, which is also roughly when, as best we can tell, the poem was actually written down in canonical form. Anthony Snodgrass makes the point that the society described in Homer’s poems is a pastiche that has historical elements but doesn’t reflect anything real.

An analogy not available to Snodgrass might be “Game of Thrones.”

These books have a broadly “medieval Europe” setting and are clearly based on some real aspects of history. But not only do they incorporate magical elements, there are also sociological concepts that are made up (“any knight can make a knight”) and bits of technology (moon tea as a highly effective abortifacient, boiling wine to disinfect wounds) that are too advanced for the period.

And while plenty of characters have deliberately retrograde attitudes, many other characters express contemporary concerns about gender roles and society’s treatment of the disabled. These would be crippling flaws in a history book, but the “Game of Thrones” books aren’t history; they’re literature, and the genre of historical pastiche with fantastical elements has deep roots in the Western tradition dating back to “The Odyssey.”

Medieval depictions of the classical world were weird

And just to be clear, that tapestry I showed earlier wasn’t cherry-picked. The “Histoire ancienne jusqu’à César” is the oldest French prose book dealing with the classical world that we have. It’s full of illustrations like this depiction of the Greeks attacking Troy by sea while wearing medieval French armor and using medieval galleys:

There’s an illuminated manuscript created in France circa 1500 of a French translation of an older Latin “History of the Trojan War” that depicts the invading Greeks as possessing big ships from the Age of Sail:

This tendency wasn’t unique to Homeric stories. Here’s a 16th-century German work titled “Three Worthy Pagans: Hector, Alexander the Great, and Julius Caesar” in which all three of them look like 16th-century Germans.

To some extent this reflects the fact that people back then had less information about the classical world than we do. But 16th-century Germans were aware that Alexander the Great and Julius Caesar lived hundreds of years apart. The idea of faithfully reproducing the past just wasn’t seen as important. Hector was broadly seen as an ideal warrior, so he was depicted as an ideal warrior according to the standards of the time and place of his depiction. Placed alongside other worthy ancients, they are all shown as worthy contemporaries.

This is part of a larger tradition of the Nine Worthies (or Nine Valiants) defined as three sets of three: Hector, Alexander, and Caesar as three worthy pagans; Joshua, David, and Judas Maccabaeus as three worthy Jews; King Arthur, Charlemagne, and someone named Godfrey of Bouillon (his reputation has clearly not held up as well as the others) as three worthy Christians.

There are many depictions of these nine, and they’re almost always shown as looking roughly the same as each other rather than in period-appropriate costumes.

Life before progress

Personally, I like the more recent trend toward valuing historical accuracy.

“The Odyssey” itself is a weird focal point for this conversation because the source text is itself both deeply fantastical and profoundly ahistorical.

But I enjoy Robert Eggers’s exercises in historical recreation and am fascinated by the idea that his forthcoming werewolf movie will not only be set in 13th-century England but also feature Middle English dialogue.

One reason that I think we as a society have moved in the direction of historical realism is that more so than our medieval ancestors, we have a sense of progress. There in fact was a lot of forward technological progress between the Roman era and the Middle Ages (stirrups, windmills), but pre-modern history did not show any directional tendency toward rising standards of living, and, though modern historians have complicated the notion of “dark ages,” there very clearly was a retrogression in terms of cultural output and the size and sophistication of cities.

Detailed depictions of the past are part of a modern sense of stepwise progress in which the fact that they didn’t have iron tools in the Bronze Age is meaningful.

I think we moderns have, by and large, improved on our predecessors by coming to understand the significance of such matters. But let’s return to the A.I.-generated “Odyssey” clip that Musk praised and note how Hollywood it is — everyone looks fantastic!

The real past would have been full of dirty people with bad teeth and gross hair, and there definitely would have been no sunscreen or skin care regimen. The clip is not “accurate” or “realistic” at all. It’s operating in a romantic tradition of idealized depictions of the past. It’s just white people (speaking English, of course, with vaguely “old-timey” accents) thus exorcising a particular bugaboo these right-wingers have about Lupita Nyong’o.

Now that it looks like Troy was actually a real city, I would love to see someone attempt a historically accurate Trojan War film — perhaps one stripped of most of its supernatural elements, such that the gods are real to the characters but not per se real, a film that really tries to show us something about the Bronze Age world.

But that’s a totally different exercise than making a Trojan War film that simply appeals to the sensibilities of contemporary conservatives.
