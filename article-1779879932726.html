The strange rush to declare the end of inequality

The "we solved inequality" crowd is delusional

Matt Bruenig
May 27
∙
Paid
 
READ IN APP
 

Income inequality is one of the dominant economic narratives of our time. However, between 2012 and 2022, the United States underwent a significant wage compression — wages at the bottom grew at a faster rate than wages at the top. From roughly 1980 to 2012, the top 10% had pulled away from the rest, and then from 2020 to 2022, between a quarter and a third of that was reversed.

So, have we solved economic inequality? Should we consign that narrative to the dustbin of history? Some certainly think so: “The world changes faster than the stories we tell ourselves,” wrote economist Cameron Murray about this development.

Prominent pollster David Shor wrote that he thinks “it’s pretty interesting that we saw massive wage compression from 2014 to 2024 and outside of a handful of economists, [approximately] zero people who claimed to care about inequality noticed or cared.” Economics pundit Noah Smith echoed Shor’s opinion, adding that the inequality discourse in America is really about “downwardly mobile educated people resentful about not being millionaires” and “petty millionaires resentful of the social status of billionaires.”

The implicit charge here is that those of us concerned about income inequality should be celebrating — or at least openly recognizing — this reversal of fortunes. And our silence is evidence that our animating concern is not egalitarian politics but hatred of high-income people.

Share

Yet another argument emerging as a response to the recent wage compression is that it may have been the cause of all the “bad vibes.” From 2021 to 2024, economists noted that consumer sentiment — as measured by the University of Michigan’s Index of Consumer Sentiment — should have been 13 points higher. That is, given where inflation, unemployment, stock returns, and consumption were, one would have expected people to feel better than they told pollsters they did.

Matt Yglesias and others, like New York City Council policy analyst Alex Yablon, posited that perhaps people were actually mad that the goods and services provided by low-wage labor became more expensive because of rising wages for lower-income workers.

I think basically all of this analysis is confused and wrong.

We have not seen very much wage compression. What compression we did see was almost entirely driven by one-off COVID effects. And, post-COVID, wage inequality has started going up again.

The fake wage compression

Let’s start with dissecting this supposedly massive wage compression. As we can see in the below graph, in 1973, the 90th-percentile wage-earner in the United States received 3.66 times the pay of the 10th-percentile wage-earner. That number rose to 5.22 times in 2012, dropped to 4.63 times in 2022, and has come back up to 4.79 times in 2024.

For comparison, I have also included the same figures for the four Nordic nations and the OECD Average over this same period.

If you were to just look at this graph, you might say that wage inequality peaked around 2012 and then underwent a substantial wage compression since then. But this is a mistaken interpretation.

The 2012 spike that we see in this graph is illusory. In 2013, the top marginal tax rate increased from 35% to 39.6%. According to the CBO, in anticipation of that tax hike, some rich people likely “accelerated the payment of wages, salaries, and dividends, along with capital gains realizations, into calendar year 2012 so that individuals could avoid the higher tax rates that took effect in 2013.” If we ignore this tax-planning spike, we see that the ratio doesn’t really change at all between 2011 and 2019.

Starting in 2020, we do see some real wage compression, as the ratio falls from five in 2019 to 4.63 in 2022. But 2020, 2021, and 2022 are the peak COVID years.

In his new book, The Wage Standard, Arindrajit Dube persuasively argued that this wage compression was caused by the unique circumstances of the COVID economy. During COVID, tens of millions of people were moved from low-wage employment to generous unemployment benefits and then engaged in job-searching within an economy juiced by fiscal policy. This made looking for a job easier and also allowed workers to reject bad offers since they had a more robust social safety net to fall back on. So, employers were forced to raise wages to get more people into jobs.

This COVID experience provided some insights about how wage-setting in the economy works, but it did not permanently alter the economic situation in the United States.

By 2024, nearly half of the COVID-era wage compression had already been unwound.

The idea that egalitarians should find comfort in the fact that American wage inequality, which is sky-high even in good times, fell a little bit for a few years during a once-a-century exogenous shock is obviously preposterous. If we are going to psychoanalyze the reactions to this development, the more curious one is not the very reasonable left indifference to it, but rather the performative centrist exuberance about it, which seems motivated more by the desire to conclude that the left does not care about equality than by any sincere excitement about having one year of a 4.63 P90/P10 ratio.

Low wage is not low income

Like a lot of economic discourse in America, the wage compression discourse generally operates on the assumption that low-wage workers are all living in low-income households. But this is not really true. Lots of low-wage workers live in middle- and high-income households, so wage compression doesn’t reduce income inequality by as much as one might expect.

In the below graph, we see how the bottom 10% of workers — individuals earning less than $11.76 per hour — are distributed across households. Only 18.5% of the lowest-paid workers live in the poorest tenth of households. Around 29% of the lowest-paid workers live in the middle 30% of households, while nearly 5% live in the richest tenth of households.

Distribution of bottom-decile wage-earners across the household income distribution

Certainly, low-wage workers disproportionately gravitate toward the bottom of the household income distribution, but they are far more fanned out across the household income distribution than most people seem to realize. Just think, lots of young adults earn a low wage but live with parents who earn far higher wages, and lots of people have partners who far out-earn them. We even have seniors who are living with their children and working part-time.

Indeed, when we look at the ratio of 90th-percentile household income to 10th-percentile household income, we don’t see any compression at all over this period, except of course for the exceptional COVID years. Even in the COVID years, the income compression was overwhelmingly driven by temporary welfare policies, not temporary wage compression.

Once again, the idea that leftists and other sorts of egalitarian-minded people should be thrilled by the recent trajectory of the American economy looks quite ridiculous.

Sources of inflation

Given that we haven’t really seen much wage compression and what compression we did see has already begun reversing itself, the argument that current dissatisfaction with the economy is mostly or partially rooted in aversion to the consumer price consequences of wage compression is hard to believe. But this argument is confused in a couple of other ways as well.

Low-wage labor is embedded in larger value chains and often only makes up a small fraction of the cost of a given product. For example, in fast food establishments and similar restaurants, the wages of the workers typically make up only 30% of the cost of the product. Even if employers fully passed the costs of higher wages of these workers on to consumers, this would mean that a 10% increase in wages would only generate a 3% increase in prices.

In reality, the pass-through tends to be much lower than that for a variety of reasons, with some research putting the number as low as 0.36% for a 10% increase in the minimum wage.

It is easy to see how someone might quickly conclude that if lower wages are rising at a faster rate than higher wages, then lower wages are more responsible for inflation and price rises than higher wages are. But this is not how the math actually works.

Someone whose annual nominal wage goes from $20,000 to $22,000 has seen their wage increase by 10%. Someone who goes from $100,000 to $105,000 has only seen their wage increase by 5%. Yet if the wage increases of both individuals are passed through into prices, it is the 5% hike of the high-earner, not the 10% hike of the low-earner, that contributes more to the rise in the overall price level. In other words, it is the rise in wage levels, not rates, that determines how much each wage group contributes to the increase in the overall price level.

As tempting as it is to think big thoughts about any economic change, sometimes these thoughts can lead us astray and much smaller ones serve us better.

The United States has not actually undergone some kind of stealth move toward egalitarianism that nobody is talking about. It remains a very unequal society, and, if you care about that kind of thing, nothing much should change about how you regard the country and its economic system.

The COVID wage compression, though relatively meaningless in the grand scheme of American egalitarian politics, provides some inspiration that our current level of wage dispersion is not set in stone and that it can be changed by different labor market and welfare state policies. But we have to actually implement those policies, not just pretend like we’ve already solved the problem.
