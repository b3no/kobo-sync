Welcome to The Weekly Scroll!

The Weekly Scroll is a once-a-week roundup of the most interesting, strangest, and funniest stories from around the social internet.

This week, we’re talking about whether or not Russian bots disappeared from the internet, a surprising win from a crypto site, the flame war between Chinese and Brazilian leftists, and more.

Did blowing up Russian data centers make the internet good again?

Earlier this month, Ukraine conducted strikes to take out major data centers owned by Russian technology giant Yandex. Almost immediately, social media users started noticing a difference in their feeds.

“Ever since Ukraine bombed that bot farm, internet’s been a lot more peaceful…” proclaimed influencer dannyphantom.exe on TikTok. Other users noted that, after the strikes, online engagement for Elon Musk and other right-wing influencers plummeted, mentions of the name “Fauci” declined along with health-related disinformation, nationalist European influencer accounts appeared to malfunction, and subreddits focused on European politics got less activity.

While deeply online sickos were the first to notice, news sites began to pick up on this trend as well. “Kyiv destroys Russian data centers, pro-Trump and pro-AfD [Alternative for Germany] bot activity collapses on social media,” proclaimed Le Parisien (translated from French). A gleeful Futurism headline declared “Elon Musk’s Twitter Engagement Shrivels After Destruction of Russian Data Centers.” Even Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy had a theory about the effect of his military strikes: “It suddenly became a little easier to breathe on many social-media platforms… because there were fewer bots.”

Zelenskyy wasn’t just speculating; Cloudflare data did seem to support the idea that Russian bot activity had fallen off a cliff:

This would be an incredible story about how Ukraine’s military success cleaned up our social media platforms. The only problem is that it’s probably not true.

Share

The Cloudflare data is legitimate, but it’s measuring “bots” in the mundane sense of web crawlers and automated tools — meaning a large part of the traffic it’s measuring is not accounts with names like PatriotEagleUSA, exhorting Texas Oblast to secede from the U.S. to take advantage of its warm-water ports. Instead, the real impact is that Russian banking and food delivery apps crashed because their internal systems went down.

Most of the evidence that there was a specific drop in social media bot activity is flimsy. A viral claim that the right-wing German political party AfD saw a decline in activity seems to be pure misinformation with no credible source. And many of the statistics about engagement are misleading.

One analysis showed that a reported 54% drop in traffic to the /r/irishpolitics subreddit is technically accurate — but if you adjust the examined 12-hour time period by a few hours, it instead turns into a rise of up to 109%. That indicates the traffic swings might just be random noise. There were observable traffic declines in Russian-specific social media like the “Ask A Russian” subreddit and Russian Telegram channels, but even those were fairly limited.

Cloudflare itself says that malicious Russian bots simply migrated to other hosting providers. Disinformation trackers say that they see no discernible changes in the networks they’re observing. Valdemar Osted from Common Consultancy, a firm that tracks social media conversations, also saw no real disruptions other than “day-to-day noise.”

It’s hard to prove a negative, and the situation is still evolving, but for now it seems safe to say that the data center strikes didn’t actually have any real effect on social media bot activity.

So why did people think that it did?

Because Russians really are meddling with foreign elections and because many people hold on to the pipe dream that without foreign interference our politics would become civil.

I don’t blame people for speculating that Russia is meddling in Western politics, because Russia has a long history of doing exactly that:

  • Russia did, in fact, hack the DCCC, the DNC, and Hillary Clinton’s presidential campaign. The U.S. even indicted a dozen Russian intelligence officers over the incident. And Russia did attempt to influence the 2016 election through social media, though it’s questionable whether any of those efforts had much of an impact on the results.

  • Russia set up dozens of fake news sites in an effort to influence the 2024 presidential election, and it has set up at least one fake Twitter account impersonating official Republican organizations.

  • It’s not just America — Russia has attempted to meddle in British and French elections as well.

It is almost certainly the case that Russia is still running similar disinformation campaigns and that those campaigns include fake social media accounts. We know that the Russian government spent around $10 million paying conservative influencers like Tim Pool, Benny Johnson, Lauren Southern, and Dave Rubin — and if you think that’s the entire budget Russia has for its influence operations, you’re a fool.

But this particular story on data centers never added up. Sadly, most of the egregious right-wing content you see online is authentic. The folks eager to draw a direct link between Russian data centers and right-wing commentary are correct in the general sense that Russia does try to influence our politics. But they’re reaching well beyond the facts in this case to blame everything they don’t like on suspicious foreign actors.

It’s important to keep track of the social media manipulation that Russia is clearly attempting to do! But not all that’s bad about the internet can be blamed on Russia. And if we’re being honest, it will take more than a few well-placed drones to actually put a lid on Russian disinformation online.

Did someone open a portal back to 2016?

I am sincerely curious: Who is the audience for this? From both directions? Are we still dying to know Clinton’s hot takes in 2026? Does Kara Swisher somehow have a devoted constituency? Does Vox still exist, or is it a hallucination in our collective imaginations, a half-remembered dream of a time when everyone thought “card stacks” were the hot new thing in media? DM me your theories on why this podcast exists, because I can’t figure it out.

I regretfully inform you that a crypto gambling company did something awesome

I want you to picture the most annoying influencer possible. Let’s say it’s a young man. He would have a dead-eyed Gen Z stare, an incredibly punchable face, and that distinctive broccoli haircut that must be mandatory at the Influencer Factory where they make all these people. Odds are pretty good you’re imagining someone like Jack Doherty:

Doherty is one of the most irritating people on the internet, an influencer whose entire shtick consists of actively violating social boundaries, antagonizing strangers, and then hiding behind his much larger bodyguards when people confront him. He has turned being insufferable into a business model.

Last month, in response to a Doherty Twitter post about trading memecoins, the crypto gambling website Shuffle.com replied, “at this point we will pay you to shut the fuck up.” Two weeks later, Doherty posted this text on both Twitter and Instagram:

Hey guys, I think I actually fucked up

I recently signed a contract with a casino called shuffle.com but I didn’t read the contract fully... One of the terms is that I’m not allowed to post online anymore, which I thought was a joke but now they’re enforcing it

Working with my lawyer to try and fix this

This might be my last post for a while, I’ll try and be back soon

That was on Oct. 9, and in the week since, Doherty has not posted on Instagram or Twitter. He also has not streamed on his main platform, Kick. Shuffle’s CEO has posted that the contract is real, that Doherty is prohibited from posting for six months, and that it is “money well spent to get this mf off the internet.”

Now, look. Because everyone involved here is, to some degree, an online supervillain, there’s a strong chance this was all coordinated and staged in some way. And even if it is real, it’s still a marketing stunt.

But if it’s a marketing stunt that can shut up one of the most annoying people on the planet for six months, then I’ll admit you might have to hand it to the shady crypto gambling site. It accomplished what the rest of society couldn’t.

The leftists are fighting (international edition)

A fascinating spat between Chinese and Brazilian leftists began innocuously, with a post from a Brazilian user complimenting Chinese urban planning and infrastructure. The resulting reactions were initially friendly: Brazilians posted pro-China messages, and Chinese accounts expressed pride that people on the other side of the world were noticing their nice stuff.

But some Brazilian leftists soon chimed in to point out that China discriminates against LGBTQ people, executes drug traffickers, and is generally a repressive totalitarian nightmare state.

This kicked off a firestorm of Chinese and Brazilian accounts yelling at each other. The general contention from the Chinese side was that Brazilian leftism was weak, fake, and gay, while the Brazilians thought the Chinese were acting like Nazis. There were arguments about whether or not Chinese leftists are homophobic, whether or not Brazilians should summarily execute drug dealers, and more. So, you know, a normal day on Twitter.

Subscribed

The whole episode exposes one of the problems that China-friendly leftists like Hasan Piker rarely address, which is that Chinese “communism” is in many ways quite right-wing. The Chinese Communist Party is intensely patriarchal, homophobic, skeptical of welfare spending, and harshly intolerant of vices like drug use. There’s an important split between the reality of single-party communist states and the socially progressive Western view of leftist ideology.

It’s also interesting to see open ideological conflict occurring between different national groups. You may remember that when Twitter added automatic post translation back in March, there was an explosion of good feelings between the Japanese and English-language Twitter worlds, with users celebrating their shared love of barbecue, anime, and other forms of culture.

I’ve suspected for a while that it was a matter of when, not if, the opposite would happen, and users from different countries would engage in large-scale flame wars on contentious political issues. It seems like that is the purpose of most social media, and the wonders of technology mean that now we can all shout slurs at each other online, regardless of nationality.
