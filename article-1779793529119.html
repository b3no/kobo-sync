I’m sick of “affordability”

How about economic growth and rising incomes?

Matthew Yglesias
May 26
∙
Paid
 
READ IN APP
 

For as long as I can remember, everyone has agreed that voters care at least somewhat about questions of material prosperity. They care about other things too, of course. But most voters prefer to have a job to being unemployed, they feel better when businesses are opening rather than closing, and they like to have more stuff and higher incomes rather than less stuff and lower incomes.

More recently, though, I’ve had two updates to my view of this.

The first is that back during the Obama years, older people warned me that voters get angry about inflation per se. That even if a wonk might say that 4 percent income growth and 2 percent inflation is the same as 10 percent income growth and 8 percent inflation, most people have a strong preference for the former. This has been vindicated by events, and I think we have a couple of pretty good psychological explanations for it.¹

The second is that we’ve always known that people’s understanding of the world is mediated by news consumption. But the internet-era shift in the news environment has made the tone of coverage more negative, which I think has generated more negative assessments of economic conditions. Lakshya Jain pretty successfully debunks the idea that social media platforms mechanically induce negative economic assessment, but I think he’s underrating the extent to which the entire media climate has been reshaped by having to compete on the internet.

Regardless, these are both relatively minor updates. The second is entirely a messaging point. As an opposition party, your job is now easier. As a governing party, it is now harder. The inflation thing is relevant mostly because there used to be a lot of policy wonks who thought raising the official inflation target to 4 percent was a good idea. I don’t think I ever met a politician or campaign operative who was willing to touch that with a 10-foot pole, and I now think they were right all along.

But instead of just taking advantage of the negativity to beat Trump and win the midterms, everyone in left-of-center politics has become obsessed with “affordability.”

Not just in the sense that politicians use the word “affordability” (it’s as good a word as any), but that every advocacy organization on the planet is spinning up policy ideas on “affordability” to address an “affordability crisis.”

And the problem is this term doesn’t really mean anything.

The ability to afford stuff is certainly not meaningless. But when your income is higher, you can afford more stuff; an affordability agenda is just an agenda for more economic growth and higher incomes.

Which is great! I am all for refocusing policy on those things — it’s literally the first item on my Common Sense Democrat Manifesto. But I don’t think most of the people talking about “affordability” are actually admitting that this really just means we should care a lot about economic growth and rising incomes.

Growth is controversial, affordability is slop

Again, I don’t want to get too hung up on the terminology here. If people want to say “affordability,” that’s fine.

But in terms of factional controversies, “economic growth” — the thing that makes stuff more affordable — has a meaning that is specific and contentious.

The explicit thesis of the Hewlett Foundation’s Economy and Society grant program, for example, was that pre-2020 American public policy was excessively focused on economic growth. Their goal, pursued with grants to institutions ranging from the American Prospect and the Revolving Door Project to American Compass, was to refocus American policy debates toward caring less about growth and more about other stuff. If Hewlett personnel or Hewlett grantees were now saying, “The entire conceptual premise of this grant program was wrong and we’re changing our approach,” that would be great.

But are they?

Similarly, during the first Trump administration, there was a big push associated with Lina Khan (but supported by a much broader movement) to rethink antitrust policy.

The core idea of this movement was that defining the goal of antitrust policy in terms of output and consumer prices was a mistake.

That’s not a crazy idea by any means. When Robert Bork originally put his ideas forward in the 1970s, they were extremely controversial. There’s more to life than output and consumer prices, and antitrust regulation could take a much wider range of factors into consideration. A merger that boosts output and lowers prices could be bad for small businesses or for labor. It could have negative consequences for suppliers. The argument over whether we should use a wider lens is worth having.

But clearly the public’s reaction to inflation and the mass obsession with affordability seems like evidence that voters actually like the Bork program, that prices and output — how much stuff people can afford — are really important.

When you hear about Lina Khan’s enduring political influence, though, you don’t hear that she’s urging people to stay the course on the idea that prices and output are overrated. Instead, the vague rhetoric of “affordability” is invoked to obscure the fact that she’s on the side of the debate that says we should care less about output and prices.

We see something similar with environmentalists. The point of the environmental movement has always been that preserving some aspects of nature is worth sacrificing economic output and abundant consumer goods. And I think almost everyone agrees with that to some extent. But the goal of the movement is to make the political system care less about material prosperity and more about conservation and harmony with nature. Yet again, under the banner of “affordability” we have a degrowth utility-regulation agenda that is the opposite of focusing on broadly rising real incomes.

And finally, Thomas Piketty’s book “Capital in the 21st Century” and the Occupy Wall Street movement centered inequality as a core concern. Piketty’s thesis was that we should care a bit less about whether the median is going up, and a bit more about whether or not the top 1 percent is pulling away from the median. This is, again, a political idea with some fairly deep roots and real thinking behind it. But a return to focus on the core question of how much stuff can middle-class families afford suggests the opposite: that you should take note of distributional issues, but care more about growth than about the Gini coefficient. Yet here, too, under the banner of “affordability” we are explicitly not having this debate. Bernie Sanders and friends are touring the country talking about how they are fighting oligarchy, invoking the idea that the political power of the super-rich is a problem that needs to be addressed over and above any specifically economic concerns.

Picture a big-ass truck

I continue to think the economic pitch Ruben Gallego offered Lulu Garcia-Navarro is quietly profound, precisely because he was so casual in his framing:

It was a joke, but I said a lot when I was talking to Latino men: “I’m going to make sure you get out of your mom’s house, get your troquita.” For English speakers, that means your truck. Every Latino man wants a big-ass truck, which, nothing wrong with that. “And you’re gonna go start your own job, and you’re gonna become rich, right?” These are the conversations that we should be having. We’re afraid of saying, like, “Hey, let’s help you get a job so you can become rich.” We use terms like “bring more economic stability.” These guys don’t want that. They don’t want “economic stability.” They want to really live the American dream.

He wonders why Democrats use wan language like “economic stability,” and I think the reason they do it is the same as the reason they are now pivoting to “affordability” — by retreating into abstraction, they can avoid specificity.

Caring about whether people can buy a nice new truck is meaningfully different from caring about what share of wealth is held by the top 0.1 percent. It is meaningfully different from caring about achieving global net zero. It is meaningfully different from promoting the interests of small businesses over large businesses.

It is absolutely compatible with caring about progressive taxation and the welfare state.

There’s a clear sense in which cutting taxes for the rich, offsetting part of the cost by cutting Medicaid, and raising the budget deficit so that interest rates go up makes it harder for people to buy a big-ass truck.

Which is just to say that “I’m going to get you your big-ass truck” identifies specific ideas as worthy of focus and asks for other ideas to be set aside.

Of course, caring just about trucks would be a little narrow. I want people to be able to afford a nice new luxury rental next to a train station if that’s what they want. I want everyone to get a properly fitted shirt that’s comfortable to wear with a tie. The big-ass truck is a potent synecdoche because most educated liberals (myself included) do not personally aspire to big-ass-truck ownership, so it’s a reminder that an agenda of broad material prosperity means everyone gets to get what they want, not just what the Yale Law School faculty wants them to want.

Long story short, if everyone talking about “affordability” means that they now all agree that the central goal of economic policy should be economic growth and rising incomes so that everyone can afford more stuff, that’s amazing.

But we had this years-long push to get Democrats to believe that the Clinton-Obama era of economic policy featured too much emphasis on growth and the safety net, and we should cast off the shackles of neoliberal dogma and focus on all this other stuff instead. Did the protagonists of that, the people who forged the Biden-Warren synthesis, change their minds and now want a world of material abundance? Or are we getting a big new push for price controls that will ultimately leave people with less stuff?
