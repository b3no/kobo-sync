The Post-Cosmo Era Of Women’s Dating Advice

There is no better place to understand the evolution of women’s dating advice than the former and current state of Cosmo magazine.

I’ve heard a particularly common complaint from many young men in my mentions: any kind of dating advice geared towards men emphasizes personal responsibility and self-improvement, while dating advice aimed at women consists of, “you’re amazing, don’t settle” with a side order of, “if any man doesn’t think you’re amazing, he’s a jerk.”

I have a feeling these guys aren’t really deep into women’s relationship spaces, because this isn’t even a very accurate depiction of what it’s like now, but for argument’s sake, I will concede this particular type of advice exists among the myriad of disparate modern dating advice for women—for example, dismissing any man who isn’t interested in you as avoidant-attached. And if you’re a man, not a woman, you will only see the parts of women’s dating advice that incidentally appears in front of you.

But what we are seeing today (and what is now receiving unsurprising backlash) was simply a different form of backlash to dating narratives millennial and Gen X women received in their teen years. From the 2000s to today, women’s dating advice experienced the same transformation that body positivity did—mainstream sources overcorrected toward a supposedly empowering, milquetoast feminist message, while the less politically correct content thrived in disperse and unregulated social media environments.

There is no better place to understand this transformation than Cosmo magazine.

First of all, we need to define what we mean by “dating advice.” Nobody is currently reading magazine articles about “how to drive him wild,” unless they are the conservative somewhat-sex-positive-but-traditional audience that reads Evie. I checked out the Cosmopolitan website just to see what they’ve been up to since the days of “101 ways to touch his member” and it…basically doesn’t even look like a women’s magazine at all.

On the front page (note that I initially drafted this in February) I see Olympics coverage, various pop culture topics, a couple of articles about beauty and skincare, but a complete dearth of member-stroking advice. Nary a tip about a feather, whipped cream, or “slather” of honey was to be found. I had to navigate to the “Love” section to see anything even member-adjacent, and instead, I saw this:

Hot!

Rest assured, I did find some sex stuff on the Sex and Intimacy tab, but it’s simply not the Cosmo of yore, which was quite explicitly dick-centric from the covers alone. It hollered, I am woman, hear me suck a dick. Yes, there are sex tips on the 2026 site, but when I was growing up in the 2000s, Cosmo was quite literally all about how to attract, keep and pleasure men, with the occasional tips for “self love,” which usually translated into “bean flicking while your man watches,” complete with snippets from Robert, 23, who actually likes a bit of a landing strip.

Yes, Cosmo was a magazine for confident, bold working ladies who wanted to channel Sex in the City energy, and yes, Cosmo repeatedly reassured us that confidence and charisma went farther than looks could ever take us (men find a bit of cellulite hot, actually, they insisted) but at the end of the day, women read Cosmo to get tips on how to snag (and pleasure) Mr. Big. Today, Cosmo offers mostly gender-neutral sex tips with basically zero emphasis on man-snagging, and an entire section on the main page about ICE. (When I told my husband about this, he thought I meant that was an article encouraging women to “bag a man who works for ICE” and he was a little surprised they’d encourage that. I had to inform him that, no, it’s just CNN-style news coverage.) Today, looking at the Cosmo main page, it just looks like a generic news and pop culture website, which may be slightly skewed toward women, with zero indications that single women scouting for a man are the main demographic.

I read Cosmo as a teenager because I wanted to learn how to be hot and get a boyfriend, and occasionally because I was horny and bored at the supermarket while my mom took hours to find adequately plump garlic. I did not open Cosmo to read about the nonbinary indigenous spoken word poets taking on the technocratic AI apocalypse, although good for them. For the better or worse, it’s just a very different magazine.

Just look at the transition from 2003 to 2025. At first glance, it seems like not much as changed, but so much has changed:

It’s not that Cosmo has fully “gone woke.” There’s still an attractive, young white woman on the cover. Earlier this year, Sydney Sweeney graced the cover of Cosmo while pushing her boobs together (look, I get it girl, when they’re that tiny, sometimes you just need a little help!) And, yes, 2026 Cosmo references sex a little bit—I’m being informed people are gooning on the first date, and yet, that 69% people are a little sick of some extraneous thing in sex scenes. Also, apparently, SNL Stefon voice the latest sex trend is not sleeping over after sex.

But look at how different this is from 2003! 2003 wants you to “get him hot.” You need to know his four secret sexual needs. You need to focus on looking hot the morning after because we know you’ve been sleeping over at his apartment after uncovering his four secret sexual needs and getting him hot. Not to mention: there’s apparently a test guys are using to “size you up!” 2003 Cosmo talks about a mystical, omnipotent “He” more than any youth pastor ever could.

It’s not that 2026 Cosmo is sexless, but rather, there is no unrelenting focus on “HIM.” He could be a woman, even. Or nobody. You might read 2026 Cosmo because you’re asexual, but curious about what people are doing sexually these days solely for the sake of understanding the zeitgeist. Early 2000s Cosmo was about getting a man, 2026 Cosmo is not about that, even a little bit.

This is not by accident. First of all, today’s women aren’t taking dating advice from Cosmopolitan, or really any magazine. Dating advice is now loosely all over social media, and comes from everyone, including total weirdos who are just trying to get attention by saying bizarre, attention-grabbing things. For example, a mom went viral for months over her advice on how to obtain the “princess treatment,” (where her husband does everything for her, including tying her shoes and speaking to wait staff on her behalf.) I will die on the hill that this was just some kind of kink (although I can’t really figure out which one of them is the sub—it could go either way depending on context) but the princess treatment videos influenced swaths of young single women into believing this was the bar they should set, lest a man be inadequately worshipful. Some of the popular female influencers giving dating advice aren’t even in relationships themselves (not unlike their male counterparts). As a result, we transitioned from singularly man-focused, member-touching advice, curated by a relatively small group of writers, to a huge, bubbling ecosystem of random people just saying stuff. Some of this stuff is very pleasing-man-focused, and some of it is all about how to make up as many arbitrary rules as possible to ward off most men, ideally leaving you with the cream of the crop.

Young men who see the difference between “self-improvement” stuff aimed at men, and “you’re perfect the way you are” stuff aimed at women are probably not absorbing that much female dating content. While mainstream sources (which nobody is reading) have dethroned men as the ultimate prize—big enough prizes that you must worry about being sexy every minute of the day should you encounter one—this kind of rhetoric still exists online. There is a plethora of videos across Instagram and TikTok warning young women about how innocuous changes in their posture and movement will appear “trashy” and inadequately elegant. (It feels worth noting that these are small differences that no straight man would ever notice.) While people make a big fuss over the recent trend of men looksmaxxing, this behavior is still the norm for a lot of young women! Perhaps most of them don’t overtly say they’re doing it to be attractive to men (this is still verboten for some reason) but after a certain threshold of effort invested in cosmetic enhancements and onerous beauty rituals, it seems silly to go through all that solely for the purpose of “self care.” Meanwhile, the beauty standard for women has gone from “be thin” to “have an inhumanly shaped ribcage, a twenty-inch waist, and enormous, cosmetically enhanced breasts and butt.” Basically, I understand why men, just looking at women’s magazines at kiosks, might feel that there is an imbalance in terms of the advice given out, and that women are only told to love themselves, but when was the last time they were told to buy a $90 pillow because their regular pillow could make them look old?

We have some dating coaches and experts writing advice—take, for example Lana Li (not a dating coach per se, but clearly very analytical about dating) and Evan Marc Katz. But as successful as they may be on Substack, a lot of women’s dating advice is literally just coming from TikTok. There is no cohesive strategy or voice, as in Cosmo. And obviously, men’s dating advice isn’t any more cohesive and also includes things like “Don’t take women out on dinner dates because you should be using that time to conduct business.”

But even with how disparate today’s women’s dating advice is, we have unambiguously moved away from “drive him wild” tier advice in the mainstream. Dating advice for women today is a bit more defensive, a bit more guarded, and more focused on self-preservation than finding a partner. It doesn’t assume best intent, doesn’t encourage flexibility, and it absolutely doesn’t assume the man is the prize—even the insane looksmaxxing doesn’t mention men, even though I think we all know that’s the point. It’s too embarrassing to admit it!

Some of it is what I’ve referred to before as highfivesexuality. Basically, the goal isn’t to snag a man at all—it’s to make fun of men and performatively denounce them as a form of female bonding (and yes, there is a more prominent male equivalent, which was covered.) These women will do things like create ridiculous covert contracts such as standing a guy up if he doesn’t text you the day of the date by 2 PM. I think a lot of this impulse is driven by anxiety and fear of embarrassment, not actual misandry. (I believe the male equivalent is also primarily anxiety-driven.)

There’s also the fact that 2003 Cosmo—and the culture from which it came—existed and made a big impact on many women around my age. When I was eleven, I was terrified of never getting married because I was too ugly. When I was twelve, I asked my parents for breast implants and a nose job. I worried that every boy who liked me would be the only one who ever did, which led me to date a guy who clearly had a crush on my mom because I didn’t think I could do better. By seventeen, I had already resigned myself to settling, I just had to figure out which traits were ones I could deprioritize. I want to say that growing up in this toxic era had no long-term impact on me, but it absolutely did. And even with liberal feminist parents, I was surrounded by messaging I couldn’t escape: that there was no worse thing to be as a woman than undesirable to men, and that every decision I made should center men. I probably still lean toward this tendency more than most women of my generation—there is a reason I am frequently maligned as a “pick me”—but I did eventually recalibrate a little.

With that in mind, women’s mainstream dating advice today (and the deprecation of member-oriented Cosmo) is really just an overcorrection from a culture that sent the message to girls and young women that the most important thing we could be was boner-worthy. It’s obviously not true that your average single woman doesn’t need to change anything about herself. Most single women could probably improve something, and they know it! But most single women (especially ones who remember 2003) are also painfully aware of this fact, and aren’t interested in absorbing more content telling them how to “drive him wild.” Why would they put so much effort into driving a hypothetical man wild when men aren’t driving them wild? It’s a valid question!

There’s also the fact that your average single woman is likely trying harder than your average single man, especially when it comes to looks. When I wrote Why Aren’t Men the Pretty Ones? I covered the fact that even if you disregard clearly-gendered things like nail polish and makeup, most single men aside from Clavicular are putting the bare minimum into their physical appearance, while single women are working overtime to looksmaxx in a way that doesn’t even seem notable because we expect it of them. Many women, including ones who aren’t “into fashion” will have some intentionality behind the clothes they buy, as they generally try to flatter their body types and look put-together on dates. Their male counterparts will say, “I need a shirt to cover my body. Luckily, I already own a shirt” and go on with their day. Obviously, that’s a generalization, so if I must…#notallmen.

Today’s dating advice for women is an overcorrection from the 2000s, but the reason it’s not the same as the dating advice for men is because men and women are different, and if anyone needs to be told to try harder, it’s men.

But again—dating advice is going to be different for every person. There are probably men who over-index on their looks and don’t put effort into their social skills. There are men who, for no particular reason, just haven’t found the right person. There are women who fall into both of these categories too. And with the Internet at our hands, anyone can find dating advice that confirms whatever they want. But in general, a good rule is that if the dating advice is confirming your preexisting beliefs, and ensuring that you are perfect (or that the only thing you need to do differently is something you already wanted to do, like “expect better”) it’s probably worth taking with a grain of salt. Whether you’re a man or a woman.

And I’m sorry, but there’s no way there are over 100 ways to touch a member. There are like, eight ways.
