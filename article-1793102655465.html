The Great Trippification is Ruining Normal Parties

The expectation that milestones be celebrated with expensive getaways is making it harder to build community.

∙
Paid
 
READ IN APP
 

You’re brave enough to tell people to get off the couch and socialize, but are you brave enough to tell people to stay in their hometowns for their bachelor parties?

I’ve long had a pet peeve with what I’m now calling the Great Trippification (partly inspired by the comments of Molly Fleck on Twitter.) While people are throwing fewer parties overall, it seems that those who can afford it are more likely to celebrate birthdays, bach parties and weddings with big, splashy, multi-day trips with a smaller number of guests, which are often cumbersome and expensive for those invited. These events are nearly uniformly childfree, meaning they exclude moms of infants and force other parents to find (and pay for) safe, reliable overnight childcare if they want to come. Which, even if you are open to it, you may not be able to secure!

I don’t blame individuals for hosting these events, but I do have an issue with the fact that, if you are upper middle class, they have become the standard, often replacing more accessible events. If someone is turning forty, there will be a trip, not a house party. If someone is getting married, you will have to secure multi-night childcare because your ass is going to be on a plane, a ferry, a shuttle, and then stuck on a remote island for three days straight. Of course, you can just not come…but what happens when this is the main type of event that people in your community host?

Before I get the: “nobody is doing that,” rest assured that I’m not going to claim that your median American is doing this, let alone that “everyone” is doing this. This is obviously unique to the affluent. But thinking back to the weddings and adult birthday parties to which I’ve been invited over the past ten years, a significant majority required air travel (often involving remote destinations that required car rentals, ferries or shuttles) and all were childfree. I am, quite literally, the only person I know who had a bachelorette party in her own city.

Of course, on an individual level, people are free to celebrate however they like! But on a macro level, I can’t help but wonder if the expectation that milestones be celebrated not with a party but with a vacation is having unintended negative effects on socialization at large. Many people are less likely to be able to attend these baroque excursions, and by comparison, they may wonder if it’s even worth throwing a humble house party if “everyone else” is booking their friends a flight to Mallorca. And while you can always politely decline, if the cost of being a close friend involves elaborate weekends away, you may very well be dropped for not being able to keep up.

I was reminded of. the Great Trippification after I saw this tweet, where a woman was being pretty aggressively dragged for not wanting to attend a multi-day trip for her friend’s birthday:

This tweet sparked drama around whether or not parents are too neurotic and glued to their kids. Frankly, I believe this was the wrong takeaway. In fact, the OP later clarified she didn’t want to go at all, even if her kid was included, so anxious attachment to her child wasn’t even the culprit. Sure, you could argue it’s neurotic not to want to travel away from your kids (and yes, I am guilty of this myself, so there’s a conflict of interest, I admit it!) but what about all the other reasons someone might not want to, or be able to, sacrifice an entire weekend and pitch in for an AirBnB rental for a friend’s birthday party, which requires overnight childcare and probably leaving work early on Friday and being unable to prepare for work and kids’ school on Sunday night? Even if someone “can” afford that, it’s quite the ask!

And yet, it’s not being treated by the Twitter denizens as “quite the ask.” Most of the QTs on this woman’s tweet were accusing her of being a bad friend for not wanting to partake in the road trip and house rental. We can all acknowledge that refusing to celebrate your friend’s birthday is bad, but that’s because up until recently, “celebrating your friend’s birthday” meant going to their party and bringing a bottle of wine, not leaving work early and driving three hours to a rented mansion for the weekend.

You may have noticed, if you are a longtime reader, that my relatively negative attitude about attending destination events doesn’t match my degree of anger over people who don’t attend my parties. But I maintain I am not being a hypocrite—yes, I get mad if people repeatedly don’t RSVP to my house parties or are magically “out of town” (cough, Great Trippifcation, cough) every time I invite them to one. But attending my house party is a much smaller ask than a weekend away! Walk ten minutes, have a few free drinks and guacamole, bring your kids, maybe listen to a Trump impression here and there, spend nothing but a couple hours of your time. In fact, per the earlier controversial tweet, a few people said that it was hypocritical for OP to decline the weekend getaway and then expect the friend to attend her child’s birthday party. Um..not really! One thing costs days, money and childcare, and the other thing costs a couple hours at worst.

Unfortunately now I’m imagining a world where children’s birthday parties are also destination events. I will try my hardest not to get mad at this thing I just invented in my head.

The Great Trippification is mostly downstream of people spreading out across the country and trips being necessary for the people who live farther away. I’m aware that this is also a mass trend and nobody’s individual fault. (It’s not lost on me that this disparate sprinkling of people is also making it harder for people to form solid communities which has them rely more on long-distance friends.) I have hosted a few events that could be described as “destination” events, not because I was in love with an especially exciting locale but because most of the invitees happened to live in different places. My wedding was at my mom’s house, so hardly a “destination” wedding, but plenty of the guests did not live near my mom. When we celebrated my husband’s birthday, it was in our hometown, but his closest friends did not live there, so I invited them with the knowledge they would have to fly. But—and I stress, this is very important!—I have always told people I wouldn’t be insulted or angry if they couldn’t make it, and they did not owe me a reason. The invite was an offer, not a demand, and I’ve never demoted someone to a lower tier of friendship for being unable or unwilling to sit through a four hour Spirit Airlines flight to see me. This was how I felt before anyone had kids, and now that kids are in the picture, I wouldn’t dream of judging someone for refusing to leave them behind. Come to think of it, I cannot imagine asking any parent to travel without giving them the option to bring their kids.

The same cannot be said of our friends who hosted a destination wedding in another country, in a remote, rural village only accessible by a three-hour drive of a rental car, with a mandatory three-day stay at the resort. They wouldn’t let us bring our kids, and then got mad that I wouldn’t attend at four months postpartum. (We are still friends, and they got over it eventually, but I maintain this was ridiculous.)

Even though the Great Trippification is likely just a second order effect of people having friends in different locations, it has created a degree of unhealthy expectations for what “counts” as an adequately memorable celebration, creating an all or nothing mentality. Bachelor and bachelorette parties used to be a singular night out, often in the same city as the wedding (which was often also the bride’s and groom’s hometown.) Now, they are multi-day (often extending beyond a weekend) affairs to exotic, expensive locations around the world—at least for those who can afford it. Then, everyone else sees this content across Instagram or wherever else they’re getting their daily dose of comparison-based toxicity, and decides they need to do the same thing, even if it’s not financially feasible for them or for their guests. I’ve seen countless anxiety-filled posts on Reddit from women who were terrified of pushing back against a rapidly increasing bill for the privilege of being a bridesmaid. For those who can’t justify the cost at all, they may feel demoralized about their inadequately luxurious events—even though that was how everyone did things up until twenty years ago. This is more or less what I already wrote about via excessive consumerism around children’s sleepovers, prom-posals, and other minor milestones, making other people believe that the “bare minimum” for these things is far more expensive and onerous than it actually is.

See: the drama a while back on Twitter about how you “can’t” throw a kids’ birthday party for less than $1,000, and one of the cost items was a rented pavilion. You actually don’t need to rent a pavilion! You can have people over at your home, and it’s fine if it’s small or you don’t have a backyard. I’ve never once gone to someone’s apartment and thought, “What a drag, this place is too small.” And I lived in San Francisco for seven years.

As a result of these ridiculous expectations, fewer people throw regular-ass parties! When I’ve asked people why they don’t throw parties, many of them cite that their houses are “too small” or that it would be embarrassing to have people over because they don’t have an ideal space for hosting. Their concept of the bare minimum for a party is based on much more affluent people who are actually doing far more than the bare minimum. Now, what could possibly give these people the idea that living in a small apartment would be social kryptonite among friends who probably already have a good idea of where they stand socioeconomically and geographically? Oh no! A small apartment! In Brooklyn! The horror! Probably because their Instagram feeds are full of people celebrating turning thirty-two and a half, with a five-day trip to Nashville.

Regular parties, given how much more frequently they can be thrown and attended, are much more conducive to building a good social life and community than fabulous multi-day trips, which just can’t happen as easily or as frequently. You can throw a party every month or two if you want, and if you have a group of friends who do the same thing, you’ll be attending multiple low-stakes parties a month. You’ll have a much fuller social life and community while attending seven ordinary house parties than one big trip-oriented bash. If you have long-distance friends, I can see the case for doing both of these things, so again, I am not saying you are a bad person if you have a girls’ weekend, but it’s unfortunate when weekends away are the expectation or the default for large swaths of people.

Not to mention, travel takes people away from their communities and makes them less available for ordinary socializing. And it’s not just the destination events, which probably aren’t happening every weekend. It’s the destination events plus the millions of other travel obligations for a typical affluent family. If your kid really wants to play sports, guess what? They (and you) now have to dedicate somewhere between eight and twenty weekends a year to travel sports.

To some extent, this is also related to the “no new people” epidemic. People prefer to stick with what they know (their very close friends) over what they don’t know (new people.) By virtue of the way destination events work, you are guaranteed to be around your close friends at such events, without much risk of being stuck in a situation where you don’t know anyone. Ordinary parties, while significantly lower-cost for both time and money, carry the risk of being around unfamiliar people. For many people, being less available to meet new friends is not really a drawback, so they will gladly spend half of their weekends traveling with people they already know. And individually, I get it! But zooming out to society at large, it becomes a problem when half the neighborhood has decided to opt out of the community every other weekend, and when people’s only social efforts involve a small circle of disparate people.

Destination events are sometimes the best option for everyone involved. Often, they are inevitable if your friends and family are located across the country or the world. A local event for you may be a destination event for a friend who lives farther away. But elaborate trips for every milestone should not be the standard or the expectation. There is nothing wrong—and in fact, quite a lot right!—about celebrating your birthday at your house or at a restaurant. Just don’t make me share food.
