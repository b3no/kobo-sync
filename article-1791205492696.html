Two Grandfathers

privilege, celebrity, and the end of up from below

Freddie deBoer
Oct 5
 
READ IN APP
 

I never met my paternal grandfather, John James deBoer. He died twelve years before I was born. Drank himself to death, or so I understand. I’m afraid there’s no one left to ask for the details.

I concede that these things are complicated, and alcohol has been a family curse, on both sides, going back generations. But there’s no question, really, that his later-life dissolution stemmed from having been a victim of the Red Scare. A prominent professor of education in the University of Illinois system, and a communist, he was a specific target of the Broyles Bills, a set of proposed redbaiting state laws. (I say he was a specific target of those laws because, in some of the drafts, he was explicitly named.) The bills never passed, but then that’s the whole thing about reputational warfare: the bad guys don’t have to win for the good guys to lose. My grandfather’s career and life were destroyed. He had tenure and so kept his job. But undergrads avoided his classes, grad students refused to work under him, colleagues would not collaborate on papers, he lost access to all number of resources and opportunities for commendation, friends deserted the family. He hung on and survived, but the work of his life was finished, and he was a proud man. His dear friend and fellow commie Norman Cazden, a brilliant composer and music professor, was not so lucky; he did not have tenure and so was fired. He spent the next dozen years of his life scratching out a living teaching piano lessons to children. You can read about it in this moving testimonial from Cazden’s widow, Courtney Cazden. It’s bracing stuff.

This is all to say that redbaiting and McCarthyism had victims, real victims. Thousands of people lost their jobs, and some of them lost their friends and families and occasionally their lives, to addiction or suicide. The federal government became a Kafkaesque nightmare to work in, a site of relentless mutual surveillance. Gay people and their lives were collateral damage, as many were outed during anti-communist investigations, their careers ruined because of the supposed security threat they represented. Government agencies were forced to fire essential workers; cultural institutions like museums and art schools and literary magazines became more politically cautious; organizations of all kinds collapsed after being targeted, even if there had been no official punishment; the kangaroo-court congressional investigations normalized treating accusations as equivalent to proof. And the allegation, for the record, was that the accused were guilty of doing what the constitution explicitly and unambiguously defends: the right of every American to hold the political beliefs consonant with their values. Joe McCarthy and his many enablers and co-conspirators purported to be defending America by acting in the least American way imaginable.

Of course, there are those who lament only the collateral damage. Some have said, and still say, that the old problem with the Red Scare is that some people who weren’t actually communists fell into its maw - that the only injustice was false accusations. Well, if they’re right, you’ll find no denials here. Because like his son and his wife, my grandfather was a communist, explicitly and unapologetically. My father was a classic red diaper baby. This is just to say that he loved justice, hated poverty, sought out equality, and opposed war. I won’t get into the tired Cold War squabbling here; it’s pointless, as everyone made up their minds a long time ago, and anyway it’s ancient history, much like the history of my family. It’s enough to say that socialists and communists and Marxists, prior to the social upheavals of the late 20th century, were some of the only voices in American society calling for an end to the oppression and injustice that permeated our country and manifested itself in all manner of bigotries and social ills.

You can hate us reds all you like (trust me, I’m used to is) and still lament the Red Scare as a terrible imposition on the basic freedoms that America is so often celebrated for. My grandfather was a communist because he had values he thought were worth fighting for, this country’s ideals failed him, and he paid for it with his reputation, his career, and his life. His wife paid for it, my father paid for it, and in our own way my siblings and I are paying for it still.

And then there’s Zoe Kazan - wealthy, famous writer and director, wife of a movie star, and subject of this glossy New York magazine profile by Allison P. Davis. Kazan, as prototypical of a daughter of extreme privilege as I can imagine - born rich, into a famous name and into Hollywood - is behind the new Netflix adaptation of John Steinbeck’s East of Eden that just premiered. As her name suggests, she’s the granddaughter of Elia Kazan, famed filmmaker who directed of a movie version of the same novel, as well as On the Waterfront and A Streetcar Named Desire and several other classics. Kazan famously named names in the McCarthy hearings, selling out Hollywood undesirables to protect his own neck. Kazan never really apologized, and at the time notoriously defended himself by saying “I’d rather hurt them a little than hurt myself a lot.” Truly, a profile in courage. Davis’s piece is ostensibly about Zoe Kazan wrestling with that legacy. To me, someone for whom the legacy of the Red Scare has never been hypothetical, it all reads like a whitewash. You can say that the profile isn’t entirely sympathetic, but it’s a profile of Zoe Kazan, and the profile is an inherently sympathetic genre, and anyway every chance she has to press, Davis declines. If nothing else, the piece affords Kazan the opportunity to defend her family and herself, an affordance that the victims of McCarthyism never received. Momentary consideration of the sins of the past don’t outweigh giving a person who already enjoys outsized attention yet another platform.

John fucking Steinbeck. Christ.

“[Elia Kazan’s] actions became a family burden, his damaged reputation metastasized into his granddaughter’s burden,” writes Davis. Really? What’s the burden? Where? What definition of “burden” does Davis know that I don’t? “The government decided to crush people for having unpopular politics so now I can’t feed my family” sounds like a burden. “I’m occasionally in a position where I have to remember my connection to a director 90% of my peers have never heard of” is not a fucking burden. McCarthyism was a horrific historical sin. Philip Loeb killed himself over his blacklisting! Canada Lee was maybe America’s greatest Black actor until the State department labeled him a communist and he was unpersoned. Chandler Davis was a brilliant mathematician whose principled stand against McCarthyism not only ruined his career but got him sent to actual prison. Those are burdens, Ms. Davis, actual burden. “Some people are kind of weird for me when I get another great creative opportunity in Hollywood” isn’t a burden. Kazan, I’m afraid, certainly seems to think that she’s paid a very high price. Here’s a charming exchange:

I wonder, out loud, if maybe we’re in a post-nepo-baby world or at least have reached an era when having a famous last name no longer means something negative.

“It’s news to me if that’s true,” Kazan replies with a snort.

“I feel like it’s true,” I say encouragingly but beginning to sweat under her skepticism.

“Okay, great. Um, I’ll take your word for it,” she says with just the slightest sharpness.

The idea hangs between us for a beat longer than is comfortable.

“I don’t think it was helpful for me to be burdened by my family’s connection when adapting this. I had to put it aside.”

Thousands of victims of McCarthyism did not have the opportunity to put their burdens aside, Ms. Kazan. I would think the absurdity and shamelessness of her attitude would be obvious to anyone. Davis, though, says nothing to indicate that she recognizes that absurdity, that shamelessness, and her associated commentary suggests that she feels that she has something to prove to Kazan. But you are the journalist, Ms. Davis! You are the one who is supposed to push back! You are the one who is supposed to speak for the rest of us. But, no. This wealthy, cossetted person who has never been terrified of having to come up with the rent money is allowed to use New York magazine to work through her feelings about the terrible, terrible burden of [checks notes] having gotten the fast track to Hollywood success. Davis seems like a pretty standard-issue social justice progressive and as such is surely familiar with the concept of privilege. But Kazan’s privilege is hardly considered, and everywhere that it is, it’s in service to the idea that it has been very, very hard to be Zoe Kazan. I’d be shocked if there’s a single Republican on staff at New York, but then there also doesn’t appear to be a single person there willing and able to say “Hey, maybe if we’re at all consistent with our institutional politics, we should point out that dynastic privilege is bad?”

But like I said, Davis seems scared, afraid to really confront someone with far more social power than she has. And, I’m sure, she’s starstruck and envious too. I know I would be. If this was a personal failing of Davis’s, oh well, but I’m afraid the problem is structural. This has happened throughout the entertainment media and media writ large, this total surrender to deference to the rich and the famous. Recently Tom Cruise gave his first real interview in many years, to GQ. Incredibly, he was not asked any questions about Scientology. The magazine’s editor absurdly argued that it was because the Scientology story was “old news,” an absurd, pathetic statement given that Cruise is still a Scientologist, Shelly Miscavige is still unaccounted for, and Going Clear came out after the last time Cruise ever talked about any of it. But he and GQ and everyone else know the score: he couldn’t ask that question and maintain the access that enabled him to interview Tom Cruise. And if Davis pressed Kazan to answer the only question that matters - do you really think it’s harder to be you than to be someone born without your money and family and opportunity? - well, maybe she’d lose access too. New York is, like all media, increasingly in the celebrity-laundering business.

In a crumbling media industry, the very idea of an adversarial attitude towards the wealthy and famous has evaporated. “Afflict the comfortable” and “speak truth to power” don’t get you the shiny celebrity faces that drive the clicks. Look at the Ringer, a publication that I have repeatedly nominated as a bright spot in a bleak digital media landscape. I like so much of the work that goes down there. But it’s clear that their institutional mandate is to be conciliatory with the connected, the rich, and the famous as a way to get the access that drives engagement. Look at movie business-centered Ringer podcast The Big Picture, a juggernaut of attention and audience and influence. I don’t have anything against that show; I like the hosts and mostly enjoy it on the rare occasion that I listen. But it’s absolutely clear that the industry loves the podcast because they see it as a soft landing, as a safe place to promote, and it’s for that reason that they get to play host to Steven Spielberg and other luminaries who typically don’t do media. Which would be fine if there was another approach, if there was some other entity that still felt free to ask hard questions of famous people. But if GQ isn’t going to do it, who is? Who’s left to do that essential service? I’m not being dramatic when I say that that kind of inquiry is why journalism exists.

Or look at my very favorite podcast, the Ringer’s The Watch. The hosts are as thoughtful as they come, ordinarily. But every time the concept of the nepo baby has been discussed on their show, it’s been dealt with through literal chuckles, the kind of dismissive hand-waving that signals a refusal to think carefully; it’s a defensiveness expressed through lols that I find increasingly common. Both of those hosts are progressive people, and nepotism is bad for reasons that are at the very heart of the progressive project, and I would love a little more thoughtfulness even if they can’t regularly be tough interviewers. “People who are born to wealth and privilege getting opportunities that those born to poverty and neglect don’t get is injustice” isn’t some crazy Freddie idea. It’s quite literally the core belief that produced the philosophical ideals that undergird the modern left. And yet increasingly, I find it I have to fight tooth and nail to get the average left-leaning person to care about whether ordinary people have the ability to succeed in desirable fields. Many liberals seem to be resigned to the idea that who your parents are should determine your opportunities for success; others actively prefer it that way, especially the ones who enjoy a privileged place of birth themselves. And those are the ones who have an audience. They’re the ones who staff fancy media, entertainment companies, the entire American elite.

I’m reading Shaun Gude’s excellent new biography of Eugene Debs. It’s a worthy addition in the legacy of Ray Ginger’s The Bending Cross, one of my most beloved books of all time. Debs was a family hero; he’s why my father’s middle name was Eugene, actually. And the core of Debs’s legacy can be found in this quote, about the way the politicians of Debs’s time claimed to have “risen up from the ranks”:

you will find that almost all of them claim, in glowing terms, that they have risen from the ranks to places of eminence and distinction. I am very glad I cannot make that claim for myself. I would be ashamed to admit that I had risen from the ranks. When I rise it will be with the ranks, and not from the ranks.

This is it. This is progressivism, this is liberation, this is the left. This is the heart of all true social justice: up from below. Up from below. That’s the whole thing. That is the core: to be progressive, to be liberal, to be a socialist, to be a leftist, means that your politics are based on the principle of up from below, on the idea that we raise up everyone rather than build an elite, that we fight for the people on the bottom and not on the top. That we stand with the powerless and not the powerful, with the poor and not the rich, with the downtrodden and not the comfortable. We speak for the many and not the few. We don’t celebrate rising up from the ranks, becoming an elite and leaving the masses further behind, we champion rising up with the ranks, advancing the interests of all people. That was the core of the moral project of my grandparents and my parents, and I’d like to believe it’s the core of my project too. And what I’ve come to realize, lately, is that this project is dead. People who call themselves socialists evince no particular orientation towards power at all, no fundamental support for the eggs that crack against the high hard wall. They don’t even think in those terms.

Conservatives, at least older school conservatives, love to be romantic defeatists, lament that the whole culture is liberal now. But what difference does it make, if liberal does not imply that you are aligned with the interests of the least of these? If you’re of the reactionary mind, you’d take this deal anytime - trading a bunch of cultural affectations that don’t help the worst off in exchange for total surrender in the fight to rise up from below.

Debs is also a hero to movie star Mark Ruffalo. Here’s Ruffalo reading Debs’s legendary Canton Ohio speech. That’s nice. But it happens that Ruffalo is also a guy who helps get his undeserving son shots in movies and TV shows, shots which therefore do not go to people who were more deserving. (It really is zero sum, you guys, especially in a rapidly-shrinking Hollywood and creative economy.) I wonder how Debs might feel about that kind of patronage, that nepotism, that business of the elite perpetuating themselves to the detriment to the rest of us. His kid, Keen Ruffalo, got an opportunity to launder his nepo baby reputation in (of course) New York magazine, in this relaxed handjob of a piece where he brags that “I needed money, I have no other worldly skills, so yeah, I’d obviously be like, ‘Hey, Dad, can you please set me up for the job?’” No follow ups are asked, including the obvious - hey, Keen, do you realize that a lot of people who have no money in this country have to sell drugs or steal food or have sex with their awful landlord to survive? No follow up, naturally, because New York cannot afford to lose access. It doesn’t help, of course, that as media circles the drain people without rich parents can’t afford to try to break into the field. I would be shocked if even 5% of the staff of New York has ever been poor.

(Perhaps if you need money, Keen, you could ask your Marvel movie regular father for some? I wish I could!)

When I’ve written about nepo babies before, I’ve been quietly amazed to hear people respond by saying “Well, I don’t think people should have to suffer because of who their parents are!” - and, incredibly, they’re not talking about the many millions of people who will never have the chance to make television shows or go to fancy colleges or just live lives of dignity and stability. No, they’re expressing sympathy for the nepo babies who are definitionally rich and connected. It’s that remarkable, to hang your sympathies there? The people who say those things aren’t bad people, aren’t even necessarily conservatives. They just cannot include the poor and powerless in their moral imagination. They weep for the disrespected nepo babies; if challenged, they’ll express sympathy for the poor, but there’s no passion in it, it’s not real to them. And I suspect a big part is that every American now believes that they are a temporarily-embarrassed celebrity who will no doubt be recognized soon. That’s why I have gradually become the doomer I never wanted to be. I was raise to believe that political nihilism was a sin. Even as my family slowly disintegrated, we always believed that the river would open for the righteous. And I don’t believe that anymore. How could I? How often does the New York Times even bother to write about the poor?

Zoe Kazan went to Yale. I live in New Haven county and I grew up on another fancy college campus. Some of the students are cool. A lot of them, I’m afraid, are the kind to spend their humanities seminar inveighing against the evils of privilege and then after class chew out a waiter who dared to bring them mayonnaise when they wanted ranch. I’ve seen it my whole life. And what has me in a state of general political despair is that, increasingly, it seems like no one cares about that sort of thing, because they can’t exercise their moral imagination and put themselves in the place of the waiter. Our elite media, fuck - these days, how many of them have ever waited tables, compared to how many of them went to schools like Yale? Davis includes this tidbit: “At Yale, during her freshman year, she auditioned for several plays, including The Crucible. She expected to get a role as Pitchfork Carrier No. 4 but wound up with two lead offers.” I appreciate the implication from Davis, a hint towards a reality that I guess is still something of a secret: no institutions are more inveterately starfucking than elite universities. But this nod, this implication that perhaps Kazan benefitted from her family name rather than suffered from it, is only that, a nod; Davis does nothing to spell it out, presumably for fear of giving offense. If she had really gone after it, would New York’s editors have let it stay in? I don’t know. Doesn’t matter now.

A few years back, an editor reached out and asked if I maybe wanted to do a piece about celebrity activism, particularly pointed me in the direction of Kaia Gerber, Cindy Crawford’s kid. She was (and still is, I assume) a prominent face in the world of Instagram activism, raising awareness of various injustices through that medium. The piece never went anywhere; it was pretty clear to me that the editor wanted me to mock Gerber and that world and that seemed boring and pointless to me. But I did look at a lot of her posts. And what struck me is what strikes me about Gen Z activism broadly. I would look at her posts and just shake my head and think “This is a very passionate young woman who sincerely wants to do good in the world, and she has absolutely no idea that she’s supposed to care about poor people.” As poor people, I mean, not as an intensifier for some -ism, concern and support and advocacy for poor people because they are poor. I can’t really judge Gerber because she’s lived her whole life in a political culture in which caring about poor people has disappeared from our intellectual life. But there’s also no one who’s willing to challenge someone like that, anymore.

(And if you’re encouraged by the fact that there was an editor who was looking for me to make fun of Gerber’s activism, you should know that he was laid off from that position last year and, I believe, has not yet found another.)

I am not entirely unsympathetic. People don’t know what they don’t know. The same basic moral logic that compels us to understand the actions of disadvantage people through the lens of their disadvantage urges us to be understanding of the privileged. A left that does not include clueless but compassionate rich people is a left that cannot possibly win. You have to judge privileged people, and I do judge them, by their desire to understand what it’s like for people who weren’t born rich. It’s the only compassionate way. To judge a rich kid for not truly understanding what it’s like to not be born rich would be like pulling aside a random person and harshly condemning them for not being able to run a four-minute mile. The problem is that having that kind of perspective and compassion often means that you’re having it for people who do not extend such grace in the opposite direction. And ultimately, if you want to be a force for equality and justice you have to do the work to understand how lucky you are. I certainly fail in that regard myself sometimes. But I do try. You have to try.

Some people are born with cancer. Some people are born addicted to fentanyl. Some people are born with Tay Sachs. Some people are born into sex slavery. Some people are born to parents who abuse and neglect them. Some people are born into good old fashioned poverty, grinding, enervating, all-consuming poverty. And some of us are born into families that leave us having to kindof, sortof reckon with a long-dead relative with a somewhat controversial past that the vast majority of people know nothing about, in a way that they sometimes find momentarily uncomfortable while they cruise through Hollywood. Of course Zoe Kazan would say, if asked, that the burden of her nepo baby status is nothing like those burdens. But she has not been asked, has she? They’re too scared to ask. And fundamentally she does not really understand that difference, between a real problem and a fake one, none of them do, and if she did, she would know better than to say anything that even resembles a complaint about accidents of birth in the media, would simply count her blessings. That’s the thing about a nepo baby accusation: if you react to it like it’s an injustice, you’re proving the accusation. The core of the allegation, after all, is that a nepo baby is someone who has no perspective, who does not know what it’s like to have real problems. And if you think being called a nepo baby is some terrible obstacle to overcome then you don’t have perspective and don’t know what it means to have real problems.

But there’s no one left who will tell them. I can’t, certainly. I don’t have the, what do you call it?, the access. I can’t reach a Zoe Kazan or a Mark Ruffalo or any of them. I never will. And the people who do have the access, who do have the reach, have the institutional blessing and the industry clout to either ignore people like me or mock us in a parenthetical. The decent ones are so terrified of losing access that they refuse to challenge the masters of the universe. It’s not like I don’t understand. It must be cool to interview celebrities. It must be cool to have your heroes come on your podcast. It must be cool to rub shoulders with the beautiful sons and daughters of privilege. But who’s left to ask anything challenging at all? And what is left of a liberalism, of a progressivism, of a left, that is too afraid to make rich and famous people uncomfortable?

Zoe Kazan has had every opportunity to say some version of “I know that I’m the beneficiary of an immense number of advantages that come from the accident of my birth, I know I’m lucky, and I’m grateful. I also think I’m talented, hardworking, and deserving of the opportunities I’ve gotten. But I do understand that many people are talented and hardworking and never get the chances I’ve gotten. As for my grandfather, while I’ll always love and respect him, I know that what he did was wrong and had terrible consequences for a lot of good people.” I don’t know why that’s so hard. I don’t know why it’s so hard for people like Ben Platt or Jason Reitman or Lily Rose-Depp to just be gracious in this way, why they so often fall into publicly expressing resentment over being forced to confront the legacy of their parentage in a way the rest of us have to confront every day when we worry about paying the bills. Why can’t you just say that you understand that many talented people deserve a chance and never get one, and you’re grateful for the opportunities you’ve received, and then shut the fuck up?

It’s up to people like Davis to challenge the powerful in that way, but if she doesn’t feel like her publication or her industry has her back, I get why she didn’t.

That’s Zoe and Elia Kazan. It’s a sweet picture, isn’t it? But you’ll forgive me if I point out that I have no similar photo of my own to share. I never had the opportunity to get a photo like that with my grandfather, and no fancy magazine staffed with Ivy League liberals is going to write a piece about that burden. Such little graces are not afforded to people like us. And I have no ability to enter the kind of rarefied air that permits one to point this stuff out. In the closing of her remarks about organizing against the McCarthyite attacks on her husband and my grandfather, Courtney Cazden writes “Despite the negative impact on individuals, the larger successful movement against repression is your legacy. I hope that you feel rightfully proud. Implications of this legacy, I leave to you to consider.” And I leave all of them - Zoe Kazan, Davis, the editors at New York magazine, the people who reflexively take to their steeds to defend the poor beleaguered nepo babies, the masters of our universe who decide who gets to flourish and who has to fail - I hope all of them will consider the implications of their own legacies. It’s all any of us can do.

As for Elia Kazan, well. I would have liked to have met my grandfather. As for Zoe Kazan, well. Davis writes “Being on set wasn’t without stress. Kazan describes it as ‘the hardest thing’ she’s ever done.” To which I would say, honey, if that’s the hardest thing you’ve ever done, you’ve never done a hard thing at all.
