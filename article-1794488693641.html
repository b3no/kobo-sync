Why Are All the Parents Around Me Banning TV?

No, I don’t mean iPads. No, I don’t mean YouTube. I mean TV shows and movies, on the TV.

Cartoons Hate Her
Nov 12
 
READ IN APP
 

I noticed something recently, while socializing with parents at my son’s new school: the way I was raised in the ‘90s (and how I’m raising my own kids) now seems insanely permissive, if not downright neglectful, as far as TV and movies go.

Let me back up for a moment. How do I even know this? Well, I will sometimes ask parents what type of shows and movies their kids are into. I ask because my kids (who get a little bit of age-appropriate TV every day) have their favorite shows and movies, and it seems like a fun conversation starter to see if they like the same things. But most of the time, the parents stare at me like I’ve just referenced some obscure 4chan meme, then shrug and say they “don’t really do that.” Sometimes, they concede that they let their children watch twenty minute increments of a movie every Saturday.

Of course, I don’t dislike them for saying this. I will gladly pursue friendships with people who say this. But it does make me feel a bit weird. First of all, just to get the obvious out of the way: it makes me feel bad. Finding out that TV is this forbidden in every other home but mine makes me feel like I’m one step away from having a rabid pit bull babysit my kids so I can go score some meth. Is it that unusual to allow a cartoon episode after school and part of a movie after dinner?

Of course, it’s not actually unusual. Your average American child between the ages of 5-8 (my son’s range) has 3-4 hours of screen time per day, more than he has, and my three-year-old daughter doesn’t get any TV until her brother gets home from school, which limits her TV time to “below average.” More than half of American children have their own tablet by age four (I don’t consider my kids to have their own tablets, although we do use Amazon Fire tablets with downloaded movies for plane rides, so maybe I’m pulling a Bill Clinton “I didn’t inhale” here.) But either way, I don’t think my kids’ degree of screen time is actually unusually high! And yet, almost every parent I meet does not just restrict screen time (in the form of old-fashioned TV) or control what their kids watch, but almost completely forbids it.

To a great extent, it’s a socioeconomic thing. Most parents I meet are highly educated and affluent, so I’m living in a bubble. But my parents were affluent and educated too, and so were all their friends, and in the nineties we all watched TV and movies, not to excess but often without a specific time allotment, with the only rule being “nothing inappropriate” and “finish your homework first.” I loved watching TV so much that I feigned interest in The Beverly Hillbillies because all the oldies came on at night after my favorite TV shows ended. (I should add that over time this led to genuine interest in The Beverly Hillbillies.)

My parents weren’t even on the permissive end for their social circle. If anything they were on the stricter side. I was frequently one of the only kids not allowed to watch a particular show or movie because it was inappropriate. In eighth grade (circa 2003) our teacher gave us a lecture about the perils of the Internet and asked, by show of hands, which of us had an AOL Instant Messenger account. Every child but me raised their hands—fantastic for my whole “bullying” predicament, by the way. But at least I watched enough TV to be able to get the other kids’ Kenan and Kel references.

I’m not saying everyone needs to do things exactly my way, but it is a little weird that the way my parents regarded TV—considered strict at the time!—is now unfathomably laissez faire among the same echelon of parent. In fact, often the only kids whose screen time was more restricted than mine was growing up were the ones with very, very bizarre parents. You know, the Christian fundamentalist mom who thinks Spongebob turns your kids gay or whatever. Those kids were more restricted than I was, but those kids were very unusual! Or, at the very least, their parents were kooks. I don’t say this to admonish those kids for how they were raised, and I’m sure they turned out just fine because there are worse things in the world than never watching Rugrats. I only mention that they were unusual because the default way to raise your kids in my milieu is basically the same as what a weird outlier would have been thirty years ago. And to be extra clear, I’m sure those kids are fine too, it’s just a notable shift.

I made a note about this recently, and people offered up their reasons for treating TV as a forbidden Satanic vice, but most did not actually address the kind of TV I let my kids watch. (To be fair, I was vague about what “limiting TV” meant so maybe we were all talking past each other.) But still, none of the objections were really arguments against TV, and almost all of those things could be overcome while still letting your kids watch TV. You don’t like the low-quality children’s media of 2026? Great—never before have we been able to censor and curate our kids’ media to such a specific degree. You can show your kids exclusively content from your own childhood if you want! Hate all the YouTube and all that AI slop? Okay, again: you can simply let them watch Curious George on Hulu. Hate that streaming makes it easier for kids to watch nonstop? Set limits! Turn off the TV when they’re done! It’s kind of like saying, “I don’t know why parents refuse to let their kids drink juice,” and then all the responses are defending the banning of Four Loko. Like, yes, we agree on that, but I am actually not talking about that. I am talking about juice.

The irony is not lost on me that these parents probably also ban juice, which I don’t. I am not opposed to the occasional cup of apple cider, and I’m aware this is basically fentanyl.

I would understand the straight-up TVphobia if it was based on something real. For example, there is plenty of evidence against letting children use social media when they’re too young (and arguably, nobody is able to prove that social media is awfully great for adults either.) But TV and movies, especially when curated and selected by the parents? There just isn’t compelling evidence to suggest age-appropriate content in reasonable amounts is that big of a deal. It’s a bigger deal if it replaces more fulfilling activities, and I guess you could always argue that a child who is watching TV could always be doing something more important, but if the occasional episode or movie was that problematic, we’d see more evidence of it that doesn’t fall apart after other variables are controlled. Of of course, this doesn’t mean that you’re doing anything wrong by avoiding television, just that it probably doesn’t make that big of a difference.

For example: studies that show a correlation between educational outcomes and hours spent watching TV often fall apart when controlled for IQ and socioeconomic status. A longitudinal study on the mental health outcomes for children who grew up watching more or less TV showed no correlation with depression, but a slight correlation with anxiety which weakened when controlling for socioeconomic status and signs of early childhood pre-existing anxiety. When TV was first rolled out in the United States from the 1940s to 1950s, it became available in some locations before others. Economists were able to use this rollout as an experiment with a control group. Exposure to TV during preschool years (via the gradual TV rollout) did not make any difference in children’s middle and high school test scores years later.

If a child is watching TV because his parents want to watch a movie as a family or need a few minutes to take care of something around the house, versus a child who is watching TV all day because their parents are dealing with too many external stressors to do anything else with them (external stressors which probably also affect the children in other ways) the outcomes will be different! And surely, kids in the latter scenario will probably have more hours of TV, creating an obvious “causation but not correlation” with negative after-effects.

Maybe you still think parents who allow a little TV are doing something horrible. Okay, fine. I obviously can’t convince you. But I’m still going to let my kids watch TV and movies, not only because it’s the practical choice when I’m cooking dinner and can’t be rushing in and out of the room to deal with sibling fights, but also because I really like watching TV and I love introducing my kids to my favorite TV shows and movies! Seriously, like, am I the only person who enjoys this? Half the time we watch a movie, I’m more into it than they are. (In fact, I was recently ordered, by my three-year-old, to turn off Fantasia and never put it on again.)

I have also considered that perhaps some parents are just lying. This doesn’t feel super likely to me, because if you were going to lie about your kids’ screen time you certainly wouldn’t do it to me, someone who clearly thinks TV is fine. But perhaps they’re lying to themselves. Maybe they’re allowing more TV in small allotments, or maybe allowing TV but not counting it if it’s “educational.” In fact, I have met parents who said they don’t do screen time “except for Ms. Rachel” and I don’t know how to break it to them that unless they are hiring Ms. Rachel for private events, she does in fact count as screen time. I have met other parents who say they are “anti-iPad” except for regular, daily coding or math games, which are, checks notes, performed on the iPad.

Or, maybe everyone is just doing way better than I am. Many such cases. But unless a total bombshell study comes out linking TV to the likelihood of becoming a groyper, it’s going to be hard to convince me I shouldn’t watch Toy Store 5 with my kids after dinner.
