Against the “Positive Good” theory of Platner

An absurd cope to avoid wrestling with reality

Matthew Yglesias
Jun 15
∙
Paid
 
READ IN APP
 

Most of the people I know are highly partisan Democrats who really hate Donald Trump.

And the vast majority of those people — some of whom are actual registered voters in the state of Maine — are happy to support Graham Platner over Susan Collins. Some of them genuinely like the left-factionalist aspects of his message (Israel is doing genocide, we need to fight the oligarchy) and others really just want to get rid of the woman who provided the decisive vote for Trump’s first tax bill and Brett Kavanaugh’s confirmation, among other things. I also know several people who say that if you could somehow guarantee Democratic wins in the Senate races in North Carolina, Alaska, Texas, and Ohio (thus giving the party a majority even without Maine), they’d be happy to see Platner fall on his face — but that cannot be guaranteed.

These all strike me as broadly reasonable takes, the merits of which hinge not on your assessment of the details of Platner’s life but actually on how you see various policy issues.

Several people I respect reached out and strongly disagreed with me when I said that evaluations of the tattoo issue are really just about how much weight people put on disagreeing with Platner about Israel. Of course, those people are all more pro-Israel than I am.

But there’s also a rogue doctrine out there that I’m going to call the Positive Good theory of Platnerism. This holds not that Platner’s questionable ink and extramarital sexting and apparent womanizing are forgivable or unimportant, but that these qualities actually make him an above-average Democrat.

Ken Klippenstein says that Platner heralds the end of “smoothgroin politicians,” while Matt Stoller hailed him as “a rejection of Dem H.R. lady politics.”

Stoller, ironically, followed that quip with a lengthy quasi-apology for the sexist joke paired with a detailed sociological theory of the rise and nature of American H.R. practices that bears no relationship whatsoever to any policy analysis that Platner has ever offered.

I think this Positive Good Platnerism is preposterous, but it’s in the neighborhood of something correct and insightful.

It’s not just that Democrats in general ought to be more moderate on cultural issues.

It’s that in particular, people who want Democrats to advance a more left-wing economic agenda need even more moderation on culture than those of us willing to advocate for across-the-board moderation.

The problem is that guys like Stoller and Klippenstein don’t want to call for moderation on concrete cultural issues. So they’re left instead with the preposterous idea that instead of nominees who just have more chill stances on policy questions, what Democrats really need are nominees whose personal conduct is inconsistent with their stated values.

The needed disclaimer about Maine

I feel like I’ve said this a half dozen times already, but the cursed thing about all Platner Discourse is that the broad center-left community desperately wants to have an argument about what kind of candidate can win back working-class Obama-Trump crossover voters in red states, but instead we’re stuck arguing about a guy who’s running in Maine.

Maine is a state that Kamala Harris won 52.4 to 45.5 in a bad political climate for Democrats nationally. In the Silver Bulletin’s generic ballot model, Maine is currently a D+14 state. Lakshya Jain’s model for The Argument is even more bullish and sees a D+16 edge. I’ve been really mad at progressives for running Jared Golden out of Congress because I was pretty sure they were giving away his fairly red House seat to the Republican Party. But if you take those numbers seriously, a pretty generic Dem should be able to win the Second Congressional District this year.

Platner has a challenging race on his hands, but that’s just because Susan Collins is a very strong electoral performer who has won races even when the national environment favored Democrats.

This is an interesting political challenge, but it’s not one that has any broader implications for political strategy because there are literally no other Republicans holding down Senate seats in states that Harris won. The whole task here is just to get people who voted for Harris to vote for Platner. Obviously if some disgruntled Trump voters want to vote for him too, that would be great. But there’s no evidence he’s going to pull that off and also no need for him to pull it off.

It’s true that the part of Maine that Platner is from (the part of the state where I spend my summers) is very rural, but it’s also quite liberal. Harris got 55 percent of the vote in Hancock County. Platner held a big rally with Ro Khanna in Bar Harbor, which is an upscale resort town. His victory rally was at the Y.M.C.A. in Blue Hill, which is the kind of small town that has a food co-op and two yoga studios and an economy based primarily on summer residents and cute B&Bs.

To be clear, there is nothing wrong with any of this. He is not running in Alaska like Mary Peltola or Texas like James Talarico or Ohio like Sherrod Brown or even North Carolina like Roy Cooper. He doesn’t need to reach out to Trump voters, he doesn’t need to expand the tent, and I think he’s probably going to win because he’s a pretty charismatic guy who has the macro winds of politics at his back. But for the purposes of understanding the Platner meta-discourse, you have to ignore reality and pretend that this race is a good proxy for rival theories about how to expand the Democratic Party.

Democrats’ actual problem

The Democratic Party is seen by many people as weird, extreme, and hostile to their basic moral values and interests.

Many believe that Democrats practice discrimination against white people, that they prioritize the interests of environmentalists and labor unions over economic growth, that they’ve embraced bizarre ideas about gender identity, that they care more about criminals and illegal immigrants than about law-abiding Americans.

Republicans, fortunately for Democrats, have their own set of vulnerabilities.

But the people who are voting repeatedly for Donald Trump are clearly people who have a big problem with Democrats. Trump himself has never been particularly popular. For all the attention lavished on Trump’s base who’ll forgive him anything, it’s not like he puts up overwhelming numbers in his election wins. Instead, there are plenty of marginal voters out there who see very real flaws in Trump but just think Democrats are worse.

The problem, in other words, is that too many people disagree with some of Democrats’ high-profile issue positions.

Peltola and Talarico are trying to address this by having different issue positions, especially on their home states’ natural resource economies.

Platner, to his credit, also has one issue like this — guns — where he argues that national Democrats’ enthusiasm for banning assault weapons is unresponsive to real crime problems and disrespectful of law-abiding gun enthusiasts’ hobbies. That’s not the issue that I would pick, but it does seem to be authentic to his life and interests, so good for him. I support a big tent.

But the idea that you could take a baseline Democrat and have him overperform in North Carolina not by being more moderate on policy issues, but by cheating on his wife and therefore proving he’s not a “smoothgroin” is ridiculous. We literally ran this experiment with Cal Cunningham and it went as you’d expect.

If you want to reject “H.R. lady politics” by rejecting D.E.I. initiatives or the way the Biden administration handled sexual assault allegations on campus or by proposing child care initiatives that are attentive to the interests of stay-at-home moms, that’s great (though, personally, I would try to come up with a non-misogynistic way to characterize these ideas).

But Platner hasn’t done any of those things. He’s just made some off-color remarks in Reddit posts and maybe fibbed about a tattoo while adhering to orthodox progressive views. That doesn’t make any sense as a theory of change.

And the vast majority of Democrats can see, I think, that it doesn’t make sense.

The problem is that while 80 percent of the party disagrees with Stoller and Klippenstein, the party itself is trafficking in a low-effort version of the same politics.

This is the idea that if you put up a white man with good hair like Gavin Newsom or find the perfect tattooed veteran with a gruff voice, that will somehow pull the wool over voters’ eyes and they’ll forget about their substantive disagreements with progressive politics.

Platner needs to normalize his campaign

Again, though, Platner doesn’t actually need to win over anyone who is profoundly alienated from the Democratic Party.

The thing he needs to do is to distinguish more clearly between the interests of his campaign and the interests of factionalists who want to leverage his campaign to advance their own careers and agendas.

The best part of his primary victory speech was when he thanked Janet Mills for a lifetime of service to the people of Maine. Not because it was a brilliant piece of rhetoric or anything, but just because it was classy and normal and conveyed to people who believed in good faith that she would have been the better nominee that he likes and respects them and wants them to vote for him.

Platner and other leftists backed Troy Jackson for governor. Maine’s ranked-choice counting is very slow, but Jackson seems extremely unlikely to prevail.

In a way, I think that’s good for Platner. Democrats will have a gubernatorial nominee who Platner didn’t endorse and the two of them can appear together at a unity rally and invite Chellie Pingree and whoever else to speak and all talk about how it’s important to stop the MAGA movement. Interesting intra-party factionalism is what brought Platner this far, and it might be good enough to win in November.

But clearly his optimal strategy is to be Mr. Normie Dem who everyone’s going to vote for because he won’t confirm MAGA judges and will re-instate the health-insurance tax credits that Republicans allowed to lapse.

You often see people committing what I’ve called “the pundit’s fallacy,” where they insist that the way to win swing voters is to just say things the writer agrees with.

I often get accused of this, but it’s sincerely not what I think. I want to see Democrats run pro-life candidates in the South not because I’m pro-life, but because voters in the South are. I’ve been a huge proponent of Josh Turek’s campaign, but I would never in a million years suggest that he agree with me about biofuels. It’s politics.

But in the specific context of the Maine Senate race, the relevant swing voters really are people like me: Democrats who do not particularly welcome the ascendancy of the Bernie factionalists but who think Donald Trump is worse.
