Steady on

The US swing vote swung the way most Canadians hoped on Tuesday. But tomorrow’s another day

Paul Wells
Nov 4
 
READ IN APP
 

I’m wondering who’s going to break the news to Pete Hoekstra and Peter Navarro that even the Canadians can’t be blamed for this. This time the calls came from inside the house. In the last weeks of her political career, Susan Collins’ message to Donald Trump was the same as Doug Ford’s and Ronald Reagan's: tariffs hurt American homes and businesses. Donald Trump and everyone around him refused to listen. Now they will get to repent at leisure.

Rarely has a big event been more devoutly wished for or more cautiously greeted. As I write this, neither Mark Carney nor Pierre Poilievre has written a word of comment about the Republicans’ rout in the US midterm elections on X. The silence extends to the accounts of Emmanuel Macron, Friedrich Merz, and Mark Rutte. This will change, but the initial caution is unsurprising: Legislative elections that don’t result in a change of government are usually no outsider’s business. Tuesday’s blue wave won’t break in the House and Senate until the new class of legislators is sworn in, ten weeks from now. And when the reconfigured Congress sits, everyone in it will be a winner, Republican or Democrat. I wouldn’t know what to tell the leader of a NATO country what to say about all of that.

I’m not at all immune to the gigantic catharsis that comes from watching an idiot and his lackeys take an almighty shitknocking, but as a rule governing shouldn’t be a venue for catharsis, so I don’t mind the lack of gloating from other world leaders.

What we do know is that US Presidents have broad power over tariffs and warmaking, and aren’t usually inconvenienced by Congress when they overreach. Democrats have been artfully vague about what they’d do on tariffs if they had all the votes, and they don’t. Impressive as it is, the Democratic majority in either house isn’t veto-proof.

I like what Dominic LeBlanc said when he came out to comment: the midterm returns “are interesting, but they will not have any significant impact in terms of our government’s focus and work.” We’ll see. The pressure on the Carney government to change something in response to an event as big as the midterm rout will be intense. There’s a robust constituency in Canada for the notion that Canada is flouting laws of nature when it does anything except lob raw materials over the Canada-US border so the Americans can turn them into something useful. This is the “get a deal” crowd, and they’ll be emboldened not only by the midterms but by the fact that Carney is actually trying quite hard to get a deal. The prime minister demonstrated in August that he has a bottom line, but that doesn’t mean he’s immune to pressure.

There’s also the question of whether a weakened Trump robs Carney of his power. The PM is a crisis capitalist, in this reading, who’s been pushing an illiberal agenda under the guise of saving liberalism. Liberals are really good at responding to critics by saying “Not now — the stakes are too high.” Now that the stakes are measurably lower, does Carney become more vulnerable?

I have more questions for you today than answers. I’m reluctant to short the Carney lucky streak, which, for instance, has lasted at least long enough to send him a separatist Quebec premier who seems not to have the faintest idea what Quebecers want their governments to work on. Try as I might, I can’t really make a Democratic wave in US politics into a short-term problem for Carney.

So maybe it’s best to resist the Ottawa temptation to game everything out. What governing choices would you and I make, if we were trying to govern well?

I can only speak for myself. First, I’d be reluctant to count on the Democrats to make Canada’s economy function well. I’m old enough to remember Joe Biden. A swing vote can swing back, indeed seems lately in the American case to be riding loose hinges, and the Biden-era global subsidy race wasn’t a lot more pleasant than the Trump tariffs while it lasted. I think the neighbours have made themselves hard to rely on, so Canada should rely on them less. That means making Canada into a place that can build things again — the subject of my latest podcast episode, and of several to come — and being less of a stranger to the rest of the world. So Tuesday changed everything, and also not much.
