The Moms Who Homeschool So They Won't Be Lonely

A growing number of moms are homeschooling to avoid being alone all day

To curb my phone addiction, I deleted Twitter off my phone, figuring that if I was only able to access it on desktop, I would think twice before opening it. I mean, if you have a funny thought, is it really funny enough to warrant going all the way downstairs to get your laptop? On second thought…

Anyway, an unexpected (and by that I mean, extremely predictable, actually) side effect of no longer being addicted to Twitter is that now, I sometimes absent-mindedly open my phone and go on Facebook. Yes, the app full of people who don’t exist, trying to scam each other.

But because of the way the Meta algorithm works, most of the content I see is at least somewhat tailored to my interests. My personal Facebook is basically inactive, but on the occasion that I post or search anything there, it’s usually related to parenting. As a result, almost all the Facebook Reels I see are parenting-related. And since I’ve been watching more of them, I’ve come across one troubling trend: moms who are homeschooling specifically to avoid being lonely after their kids reach school age, and encouraging other moms to do the same.

I first noticed this when I saw a video from the perspective of a SAHM with two children in public school. She mentioned all the changes she had to confront since her kids started school, such as “not hearing ‘Mom’ a million times a day.” She reflected on the bizarre combination of tranquility, peace, freedom and bittersweet feelings about her children growing up.

Honestly, I could relate! I dread the day I am an empty nester, and I’m a bit scared of my kids both going to school full-time. I think about it…..hourly, maybe even in fifteen minute intervals. Even though my kids are still very little, I feel sad about the possibility that I didn’t soak up their infant stages enough and regularly go through OCD rituals where I look back at their old photos and try to retain as many memories as possible. A big driver for this Substack was my desire to finally stop working full time and be with my kids more often (and even when I was working full-time, I frequently skipped out on my work to rock my baby to sleep.)

So anyway, yeah, I’m not coming at this from a place of “who the hell misses their kids all day?” I COMPLETELY UNDERSTAND why someone would miss their kids, or want to spent lots of time with them.

But what I thought was strange was that most of the comments on the original video were presenting OP with what they considered an easy fix for her loneliness: just homeschool.

I began seeing more and more of this content evangelizing for homeschooling, usually in comments sections of videos where moms share bittersweet or challenging moments of mom life. Interestingly, none of these comments were focused on the academic benefits of homeschooling, tailoring a curriculum to special needs kids, religious studies—or really, any studies at all. Instead, they focused on the parent’s need to spend more time with their children, limit their children’s exposure to other people, and advertised homeschooling as an easy way to prevent getting lonely when your kids would otherwise be at school. Homeschooling was also offered up as a remedy for other parental anxieties, with one video reminding me that if I don’t homeschool, my child may start to like his friends more than me by age seven, and I will be able to retain my position as the “most important person in his life.”

Even as a major softie who refuses to go away for a weekend with my husband because I’ll miss my kids too much, and who feels guilty when my in-laws spend more than an hour with them at a time so I can get stuff done, I couldn’t help but have one reaction: what the fuck?

I don’t have strong opinions on homeschooling when done well. It’s my understanding that historically, homeschooled children have outperformed kids in public school. But most of those studies not only have some confounding variables but were done in the 2010s or earlier, before the recent explosion of homeschooling.

Therefore, it’s not unfathomable to consider that the Facebook Reels moms homeschooling post-2022 are not the same people who would have been represented in studies in 2015. And any conclusions you would draw about homeschooling in the ‘90s or even 2000s are likely very far removed from anything you could assume in today’s very different climate. I don’t have the data to prove it, but I would venture that your average homeschooling parent in 2010 had much stronger educational qualifications than your average homeschooling parent in 2026, and likely wasn’t homeschooling for the express purpose of easing their own loneliness or anxiety.

I’m not going to blanket-condemn homeschooling, because I do think it can work. But—and I don’t think this is too controversial—it probably mostly works when parents’ primary motivation is education. I’ve heard of homeschooling moms who used to be public or private school teachers, or moms who homeschool because the public schools in their area are poor quality. I’ve heard of moms who believe they can give their special needs child a more accommodating education at home. I’ve heard of moms who homeschool because they want their children to get a very specific religious curriculum (this is more controversial, but was the main stereotype of homeschoolers when I was growing up.) But being lonely when kids go to school, or wanting to stop your child from making too many friends other than you, wasn’t part of the equation for these parents. Maybe they enjoyed having a bit of extra time with their kids, or maybe that was more of an occupational hazard. I can tell you with certainty that it was weird for any parent in the ‘90s to desperately miss their kids when they were at school, let alone pull them from school over it. My parents loved me very much (or so they claim) but having me home full-time for a whole summer was unfathomable for them. When I finally went to theater camp around age eleven, my parents were practically forcing me to go.

As I’ve written about before, the loneliness epidemic extends to parents, even married ones. Parents have fewer friends and spend more time with their children than ever. Even working moms today spend more time actively parenting than SAHMs in decades past:

This isn’t just a mom thing—dads are more involved than ever too, despite protestations that modern women are dealing with uniquely incapable and lazy spouses. As Matthew Yglesias said on my podcast, the overall standards for “good parenting” ratcheted up as men became more involved, so instead of dads shouldering some of the burden for their wives, both parents just do more overall, and the mom still does more than the dad (usually.)

“That what’s happened is that we’ve seen an upward ratcheting of parental involvement, right? But the nature of the inequality isn’t really that we’re doing like …subsistence levels of parenting and then it’s not divided 50/50. It’s just as effort increases, there’s more effort going into it.”- Matt Yglesias

With parents spending more time than ever with their kids, obviously something else has to be on the chopping block because we only have twenty-four hours in a day. Despite how unmanageable modern work seems, we actually work the same or fewer hours than in decades past. What’s pretty obviously being sacrificed for increased time with children is time doing any kind of adult socialization.

According to a Guardian study, your average parents spend only fifteen minutes together per day. Most of the time parents do spend together is spent on various screens (I know my husband and I are guilty of this! We put the kids to bed and immediately watch Tim Robinson content.) Many parents are “alone together,” where they’re engaging in entirely separate forms of media consumption.

Socialization with friends and people outside the home is also on a decline. Derek Thompson decried the end of partying in the US, while one of my first viral essays was about the fact that anecdotally, parents do not seem interested in making new friends, but in staying home or if they’re affluent, going out of town, with their own nuclear families. And a Pew Research study indicated that I wasn’t imagining this—even since covid restrictions lifted, people did not return to their baseline levels of socialization outside their homes. As I covered in my own study, this impacts not only parents but singles—almost half of today’s heterosexual singles have withdrawn from the dating pool entirely for a considerable chunk of time, and the majority spend most weekends at home alone.

This is the real loneliness epidemic—not one about men who can’t get laid, or women who can’t find a man who is emotionally intelligent enough, but everyone, of basically every demographic, spending less and less time with other people outside the home. And while parents are impacted by our changing society that prioritizes the path of least resistance and an aversion to embarrassment or undue effort, they have one thing that other people don’t have, which they can use to escape the loneliness—their kids.

Parents of very young children often find themselves with no time to hang out with other adults. Even now, only caring for a two-year-old full time, I find it hard to schedule outings with friends, let alone outings that don’t also involve the children. If you are a SAHM of a baby or toddler, you might go to the park or baby classes to meet other moms, only to become friends with seven separate nannies who you only see in these settings. It’s not effortless to become friends with other moms—you really have to work at it. And if you’re already dealing with the stress of caring for a child all day, you might not feel like you have any time or energy for that work. And even if you do, it’s likely that other moms just aren’t up for it.

But when your young child goes to school, you’ll have more time. When I was growing up, it was always assumed that SAHMs whose kids were in school finally had time for themselves and their own social circles. They volunteered, they met for lunches, they got involved with their churches or temples, they had mani-pedi dates with other women. I’m sure this still happens, but from the looks of these Facebook Reels, a lot of these women are just home alone waiting for their kids to come home and, well, scrolling Facebook Reels.

This is hard for me to write about because I can so easily see myself falling into this trap. Obviously, having kids isn’t something we do altruistically and I think all of us envision “enjoying time with them” as one reason to have them in the first place. But there’s also the fact that even your biggest OG proponents of attachment parenting would say that the endgame of such intensive bonding from ages 0-3 is that eventually kids will develop a healthy degree of independence and enjoy a wider net of social connections, including other relatives, teachers, friends, and other members of their community. The idea that it’s essential to keep your kids home with you as much as possible, for as long as possible, isn’t backed up by any data about childhood outcomes. It’s all about the parents and the fact that they (we) just have nothing else going on.

When I posted about this to Twitter, followers backed me up that I wasn’t just imagining this phenomenon based on some Facebook videos. One woman shared that her teacher friend reports mothers taking their daughters out of school for hours at a time to go shopping or get lunch—not as a one-time surprise or even occasional surprise, but monthly or more often. I also discovered that parents are going to the school cafeterias to eat lunch with their kids, with one parent reporting feeling “punched in the stomach” when schools cracked down on the behavior. One mother who doesn’t join her child for lunch mentioned that her daughter felt jealous of the kids whose parents were able to join them for lunch and began begging her to join in. I’ll be honest—I did not know this was even an option. I’m pretty sure if I tried to join my older child for lunch the school would just…make me leave?

And who could forget the phone bans? Despite ample evidence that smartphone use is screwing over children academically and turning our kids barely-literate, parents overwhelmingly rejected phone bans because they expected to be able to text their kids throughout the day. Teachers have referred to this phenomenon as the “digital umbilical cord” and noted that most of these texts were not urgent or related to emergencies, but rather “stream of consciousness questions.” Parents were/are also reportedly acting as their children’s personal Instacarts, showing up at school unannounced with desired drinks or snacks that were mentioned over text.

Some people will assert it’s not natural for children to be in school anyway, because children weren’t in school “in the past.” (Compulsory public school in the US actually began in the 1920s, so it’s hardly ultra-modern, but point taken.) It’s true that children of yesteryear did not go to school for eight hours a day. I’ve been skeptical of the fact that school hours seem inordinately long and focused primarily on accommodating parents’ work schedules (and they don’t even successfully do that). But the reality is that the working world of 2026 is different from the working world of 1725. As silly as some of it seems in historical context, kids need a modern education to obtain and hold modern jobs (unless AI just does everything for them, but that’s a separate issue entirely.) Hand-waving this issue away by saying that most of what kids learn in school isn’t necessary, or insisting the entire school day can be accomplished in under one hour of free play doesn’t really address the point, and actually makes me even more skeptical of what today’s batch of new homeschool moms are really doing all day.

Troublingly, I’ve also seen countless moms advocate for homeschooling while also working a full-time remote job, or even homeschooling will working outside the home full-time. I cannot imagine that parents who are out of the home from 9-5 were represented in those studies that showed homeschooling outperformed public school.

Parenting can be hard for people in different ways, but for me, I find it tremendously hard to want my kids to be independent. At the same time, I know it must happen! Even the attachment parenting softies who advocate for constant breastfeeding and babywearing would admit there is a point where kids should just…have friends and go play outside. That’s supposed to be the whole point. None of the attachment parenting ethos was meant to be about assuaging a parent’s anxiety and loneliness. But that’s unfortunately what it’s becoming for a lot of parents.

This advice is probably easier for me to give than to take, since I am the mom who refreshed the signup page for my child’s school party volunteer signups until it opened, like I was waiting in line for the latest iPhone—if your main motivation for homeschooling is to spend more time with your kids because you would be too lonely without them in the house, you probably shouldn’t do it.
