Does it suck to have a job?

Evaluating three viral narratives about the American economy.

I’ve been thinking a lot about the nature of work; in part because of the potential for AI to remake the modern economy and in part because running The Argument over the last 8-ish months has given me a very different vantage point on what “having a job” actually involves.

I keep noticing a pattern: Every few years, a totalizing, negative narrative goes viral.

In the 2010s it felt like all anyone could talk about was how Big Tech was ushering in a new era of gig work.

Then, of course in COVID-19 years, there was the quiet quitting narrative and the “nobody wants to work anymore” meme, which are two sides of the same ‘workers are actually lazy’ coin.

And, of course, who could forget the “bullshit jobs” narrative, anthropologist David Graeber’s idea that endemic to capitalism is the proliferation of useless, makework jobs.

Each of these narratives is based on something real—usually qualitative evidence or concerning anecdotes about worker exploitation and harm. But each of them is wrong in ways that matter. Here are a few things that I’ve found as I’ve looked through the data:

Gig workers are happier than employees.

Earlier this week, Gallup released new polling results on the state of work in America. The survey includes responses from more than 18,000 employed U.S. adults and was fielded over a year ago between January 13-February 25, 2025.

Gallup’s headline finding is that self-employed workers are more likely to report having “quality jobs” than employees.

Quality jobs are defined by five metrics: Financial well-being, workplace culture and safety, growth and development opportunities, agency and voice, and work structure and autonomy.

Gallup broke down self-employed workers and employees on each of these metrics.

Self-employed workers have higher quality jobs than employees

What jumps out at me first is that 66% of self-employed workers reported high levels of “agency and voice” compared with just 50% of employees. One of the core arguments against gig work has been that it strips employees of their power in the workplace by classifying them as independent contractors.

Now that we’re arguing over whether to save rideshare workers’ jobs from Waymo, it can be easy to forget how totalizing this narrative was, but just a few years ago, gig work was nearly synonymous with exploitation.

This perspective was well encapsulated by a 2017 editorial in the New York Times titled “The Gig Economy’s False Promise.” Contra Big Tech’s claims about how the gig economy could provide flexibility to workers, the opinionated voice of our paper of record argued that “there is no utopia at companies like Uber, Lyft, Instacart, and Handy.”

Yes, quite famously there is no heaven on earth. (Ironically, such arguments are simultaneously unobjectionable and self-defeating. Are digital column inches really so worthless?)

The Times wasn’t alone in its repudiation of the gig economy, prominent elected officials, other legacy media, and new media fed into this broader narrative.

In reality, many tech companies were treating their gig workers quite badly, and many non-tech companies were treating their gig workers quite badly and companies of all types were treating their workers quite badly. At no point did anyone actually convincingly show that this new type of work was specifically worse or that reclassification would actually fix the broader problems of worker exploitation.

Gallup even breaks the “self-employed worker” category into owner-operators and independent contractors and while the former is more likely to report having a higher quality job (50%), independent contractors (45%) are more likely to do so than employees (39%).

Workers aren’t lazy

Kim Kardashian’s now-famous interview with Variety that “the best advice for women in business” was “get your fucking ass up and work. It seems like nobody wants to work these days,” will, I’m sure, one day be a Document Based Question on an AP U.S. History exam.

The quote was a perfect distillation of the growing public sentiment from bosses that the American workforce had gone soft during the COVID-19 expansion of remote work and government benefits.

The left-coded mirror image of this narrative arrived under the label “quiet quitting.” While more sympathetic to workers, the concept referred to workers who didn’t actually quit but began doing the bare minimum.

I don’t want to spend too much time on this because it’s been pretty thoroughly debunked already, but I do think it’s worth pointing out that the entire narrative seems to have been based on a slight dip in the number of “engaged employees” in 2022.

As Derek Thompson put it, what people were calling “quiet quitting” was, in previous decades, simply known as “having a job.”

Meanwhile, the actual data showed that the prime-age labor force participation was climbing toward historic highs, and unemployment to historic lows.

Turns out managing stakeholders and sending emails isn’t all bullshit

In 2013, Graeber argued that “huge swaths of people, in Europe and North America in particular, spend their entire working lives performing tasks they secretly believe do not really need to be performed.”

Graeber deemed these Bullshit Jobs, the title of his popular book unpacking the question of whether society would be “any worse off (or even notice) if such jobs disappeared entirely overnight.”

Jobs like financial services, telemarketing, human resources, and public administration all are subjects of Graeber’s ire. Notably, mostly jobs that are largely performed by women. Other than lawyers and financial analysts, every single job Graeber criticizes is between ⅔ and over 90% female dominated according to 2025 BLS data.

Graeber may have anticipated this criticism, writing that “the assumption that it’s mainly women who end up in bullshit jobs is not only sexist but also represents, to my mind, a profound ignorance of how most offices actually work. It’s far more likely that the (female) administrative assistant for a (male) vice dean or ‘Strategic Network Manager’ is the only person doing any real work in that office.”

I’m not exactly sure why that would be the case, but regardless, the jobs he is excoriating are overwhelmingly made up of women.

This isn’t really an argument for why they’re not bullshit, I just want to point out that it’s a bit weird that one of the most influential theories of what’s wrong with the modern economy is a wordcel version of the international freakout over a trio of Australian skincare employees dancing on TikTok.

Others have pointed out that the number of workers self-reporting as working in bullshit jobs was both tiny and shrinking. From 2005 to 2015, self reports dropped from 7.8% to just 4.8%, “exactly the opposite of Graeber’s prediction of a rapid rise… Graeber may have been referring to just the Anglo-Saxon countries but here the drop in the percentage of BS jobs has been even greater – from 8.7% in 2005 down monotonically to 5.5% in 2015.”

Personally, I think the fact that chatbots and LLMs can cheaply do so many tasks and yet employers are unable to eliminate these so-called “make-work” jobs is proof positive that the complicated coordination and logistical tasks are actually very important and not at all bullshit.

In each of these narratives, critics identified a real qualitative experience—many gig workers are exploited, some employees do feel like their work is worthless—and inflated it to a structural diagnosis of the modern economy.

It is an unfortunate tendency of modern journalism that every harm has to be structural and every solution comprehensive. The result is that people who seek to elevate the subjects of their attention end up flattening their experiences instead.

More importantly, I think there remains serious conceptual confusion about what the nature of work is meant to be.

For instance, critics of gig work largely focus on the fact that independent contractors aren’t granted the same types of benefits that employees are–namely health care and retirement benefits. But, do we want employers to be responsible for providing benefits? The country as a whole has moved left on the importance of government-provided health care, for instance.

Why expend so much energy for a tiny fraction of people to be classified into a suboptimal system? It’s odd.
