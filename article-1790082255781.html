Holy Scandal

The Tuesday Review

The sins of a book critic are mostly sins of omission. Sure, I get things wrong — the sentimental drama I swooned over, the future classic I panned. But my biggest mistakes take place right at the point of selection from a tower of books. There are, after all, so many new titles to choose from. Stepping obliviously over a vein of gold is almost inevitable.

In 2020, an unknown fiction writer named Deesha Philyaw published her debut short story collection, The Secret Lives of Church Ladies, with West Virginia University Press.

Honestly, I didn’t even consider it.

But when The Secret Lives of Church Ladies was named a finalist for the National Book Award for Fiction, I became curious. When it won the Story Prize, I was concerned. When it won the Los Angeles Times Book Prize, I began to sweat. And when Philyaw accepted the PEN/Faulkner Fiction Award — at a ceremony where I served as emcee — I felt downright mortified.

I vowed right then and there that I would not miss Deesha Philyaw’s next book, and here it is, and it’s a smart and saucy delight.

The church ladies still have secrets in The True Confessions of First Lady Freeman — secrets wedded mostly to sex — but they’re much wealthier now and the comedy has risen to the heavens.

Our outrageous narrator, Scharisse Freeman, opens by declaring that she wouldn’t sign an NDA. “I’m telling it just like it happened,” she swears, “protecting no one, least of all my damn self.”

Philyaw sweeps us into the rarefied world of First Ladies, the queens of America’s Black megachurches. At 40, Scharisse is the wife of Rev. David Freeman, 15 years her senior and the pastor of one of the largest and richest congregations. The couple’s empire includes a mansion with a 30-seat theater, a fleet of luxury cars, a branded set of dishware “sold exclusively at Dillard’s,” a popular salad dressing, a bestselling book that Obama loves, and, if Scharisse had her way, a line of sex toys.

Yes, Scharisse has a voice — and a libido — that just won’t quit, and she’s not ashamed of either. When the page isn’t big enough for her, she jumps down to the footnotes. And indeed, the sexual explicitness of The True Confessions of First Lady Freeman would inspire the church ladies I once knew to mutter, “Get thee behind me, Satan!”

The challenge, as Scharisse lets us know on the first page, is that becoming a First Lady at 22 was not the salvation she’d hoped. Marrying a prominent pastor was supposed to wash away the impurities of her impoverished, fatherless youth, but the other First Ladies have never accepted her, never even hidden their disdain. These are women who can hurl chapter and verse at each other just as the good Lord intended.

Alas, Scharisse’s favorite passage is Revelation 22:12: “Behold, I come quickly!”

“Vulgar and no-class to the very end,” one of the First Ladies scolds.

But Scharisse has a plan (Scharisse always has a plan): Against all odds, she will win the 2011 First Lady USA pageant. “Becoming First Lady USA would be my life’s pièce de résistance,” she hums, “the great equalizer between me and the other women.”

In the words of Proverbs, “Pride goeth before destruction,” which we might eventually see, but another passage from Proverbs sits at the center of The True Confessions of First Lady Freeman.

Philyaw is particularly interested in interrogating Proverbs 31, which serves as the novel’s preface. (Scharisse uses the New International Version, explaining that evangelicals shifted toward it and away from the archaic King James Version, “like when Quaker Oats took off Aunt Jemima’s headscarf and gave her a relaxer on the pancake box.”)

Proverbs 31, if it’s been a minute since you went to Sunday School, is an extended description of the ideal woman — her virtues, her domestic industry, and especially her devotion to her husband. “A Proverbs 31 woman is the Beyoncé of evangelicals,” Scharisse explains. And she became “the ultimate Proverbs 31 woman” when she married Rev. Freeman.

Trouble is that role doesn’t fit Scharisse any better than her cheap high school jeans. She’s not even sure she believes in God. “I’m definitely not on speaking terms with Him,” she admits. And two decades of pretending have produced serious stress fractures. How long can she disdain the other pastors’ wives and crave their approval?

The year-long preparation for the First Lady USA pageant provides the novel’s comic structure — Scharisse can’t help but call out a tacky wig when she sees one — while a series of shocking family secrets serves as the story’s emotional core. Philyaw lets the wheat and the tares grow together till the harvest, but that diversity of subjects and tones can feel disorienting. The very funny antics of bitchy First Ladies sometimes feel incongruous next to revelations of pastoral crimes, rape, and even child abuse. But tell this narrator to pick a lane and she’s likely to flip you the bird and shout back, “Have a blessed day!”

God and Philyaw work in mysterious ways. Both also tend to go on a little too long — par for Him, perhaps, but a surprise from such a deft short story writer.

Despite its ribald humor and its sassy satire of the world of evangelical glitz, this is essentially a novel informed by Philyaw’s intimate knowledge of the church and her profound disappointment with its hypocrisy. Bringing that incisive critique into such a zany novel is the real miracle of The True Confessions of First Lady Freeman, which should have HBO producers praying for the rights day and night. (An adaptation of The Secret Lives of Church Ladies is already in development.)

Scharisse’s respected husband and the sharp-tongued First Ladies insist that she must act in a particular way to protect the church at all costs, while her teenage daughter reminds her it’s no use gaining the whole world if she loses her own soul.

How, Philyaw asks, did the church begin to see its own survival as more important than the truth?

Broad is the way that leadeth to destruction, and it’s not clear which way Scharisse will choose until the pageant’s final contest. Can the Proverbs 31 woman ever cast off her raiment and run free?

Gird up thy loins.◆

The True Confessions of First Lady Freeman, by Deesha Philyaw (Mariner, 432 pp., $32). Forthcoming Sept. 29, 2026.
