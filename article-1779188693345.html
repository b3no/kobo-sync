A diverse “Odyssey” is the triumph of Western civilization

We should want a canon, and that means an inclusive canon.

Matthew Yglesias
May 19
∙
Paid
 
READ IN APP
 

My ninth-grade history teacher was a bit old-fashioned. She’d taught the class for many years and always referred to it as Western Civilization I rather than its official title World History I, presumably because that was the name when she began teaching it.

Her framing always stuck with me, so much so that I’ve recently been considering a post making the case for bringing back the notion of “Western civilization.” I think a certain old-fashioned and somewhat Whiggish version of history that emphasized the idea that contemporary Americans are the heirs to a kind of specific historical legacy is a useful and constructive idea.¹

There’s something of course both arbitrary and false about this kind of historical conceit. But there’s no one true way for nations to narrativize themselves, and this traditional understanding of “the West” has a lot of real value. I was envisioning this as a fairly conservative-inflected take that would, among other things, endorse the Trump administration’s push to get civic buildings to use neo-classical architecture. I’m not a hater of modernist buildings per se, but that design language has a particular meaning that pays homage to the Founders’ conceit that they were acting in the tradition of Athens and early Rome and it’s good to respect that.

Instead, though, I get to give a more libbed-out version of this take because Elon Musk spent much of last week being mad online about the casting of Christopher Nolan’s forthcoming movie, “The Odyssey,” arguing in particular that the decision to cast Lupita Nyong’o as Helen of Troy reflects the nefarious influence of Academy Award diversity rules.

Musk is factually wrong about the Oscars,² and I genuinely think the underlying pattern here is one of his worst qualities as a public figure. He likes to mouth off on Twitter (I sympathize), which means he sometimes gets stuff wrong (I also sympathize), but he absolutely never acknowledges error.

Whatever is going on with this movie, it’s not about the Academy Awards. And more broadly, I think we should take a more positive and optimistic view of a racially inclusive “Odyssey.”

Part of the point of “the West” is that ancient Greek literature is part of “our” cultural patrimony, even though very few of “us” are of any kind of Greek ancestry. And part of the misguided pressure to dismantle things like the traditional literary canon came from the sense that said canon was insufficiently diverse. But it’s good to have a canon. And realistically, the canon we have is going to be one composed primarily of old European stories.

But that just means that telling these stories in a more inclusive way is part of reaffirming that they are core texts of our common cultural heritage.

A larger argument about casting

Musk followed up his complaints about Nyong’o by retweeting an observation from another right-wing account that there are a bunch of iconic film roles that it would be inconceivable to imagine a white actor in.

The obvious difference here is that he is talking about historical biopics and stories that are largely about race.

In the 2009 film “Invictus,” Morgan Freeman plays Nelson Mandela, and it would be very odd to cast Anthony Hopkins in that role instead. But it’s a movie about Mandela’s relationship with South Africa’s white-dominated rugby team, whose captain is played by Matt Damon, and it would be equally bizarre to cast a Black actor in the Damon role — the story wouldn’t make sense. In “Glory,” Matthew Broderick plays the lead role of Robert Gould Shaw, and again you absolutely cannot cast a Black actor in that role because the story would not make sense.

And to be clear, this isn’t a narrow point about historical accuracy; it’s about themes and story. “Hamilton” is deliberately staged with historically inaccurate ethnic casting, but that’s part of the conceit of the show.

In terms of alleged double-standards, I do think there is a real one here. Prominent Black actors spend a much larger share of their career doing stories where racial identity is central to the character and you couldn’t reasonably swap them out for a white one. There are, of course, plenty of instances of Black movie stars in race-neutral roles (Will Smith in “Men in Black,” Denzel Washington in “Man on Fire”), but the Movies About Race loom larger in the Black actors’ work.

You can feel grumpy about this from a right-wing perspective if you want, but, conversely, a common complaint among African American creatives is precisely that it’s often quite difficult to mount projects that feature Black characters without being Movies About Race.

It’s also true that if you set aside the historical biopic example, there is a kind of directionality to casting.

As Denzel Washington became an increasingly celebrated movie star, he ended up playing a lot of characters who were originally conceived of as white. (John Creasy from the aforementioned “Man on Fire,” Lincoln Rhyme in “The Bone Collector,” and Robert McCall in the “Equalizer” movies.) There are plenty of cases where you could remake an old property that had a Black lead (“Passenger 57,” “Crimson Tide”) with a white actor in the role instead, but there aren’t many specific examples of this happening.

I don’t think you need to interpret this as a nefarious plot.

Profit-seeking movie studios like to do adaptations and reboots, but also like to find roles for popular actors. When Chris Hemsworth took over from Will Smith as the lead actor in the “Men in Black” franchise (not recasting the part but introducing a new character as the main star), there was no huge meltdown. But the stock of legacy intellectual property is whiter than the current audience demographics, so there is a commercial incentive to update in the direction of more diversity.

So to return to where we started, the whole question here is whether one should view the whiteness of “Odyssey” characters as essential to the story.

Myths aren’t history

Robert Eggers’s 2022 movie “The Northman” is a fascinating exercise in historical filmmaking.

The plot is based on the Scandinavian legendary figure Amleth, whose tale is also the basis of “Hamlet.” But while “Hamlet” uses Denmark as a historical setting, “The Northman” tries really quite hard to convey a great deal of historical specificity about pre-Christian Nordic culture and society. Eggers apparently did an enormous amount of historical research on how things should look. He replicates cutting-edge historical scholarship about Bronze Age dog-themed warrior initiation rituals. And even though the movie is in English, he has the Swedish Alexander Skarsgård in the lead role. The screenplay was co-written by an Icelandic guy. Many Nordic actors (Claes Bang, Gustav Lindh, Björk) are cast and the well-known American and English actors (Nicole Kidman, Anya Taylor-Joy, Ethan Hawke, Willem Dafoe) who round out the cast all look plausibly Northern European.

A project that someone absolutely could mount would be an effort to faithfully render Bronze Age Greece to the best of our understanding, in much the way that Eggers did with “The Northman.”

But it’s worth being clear that it’s Eggers who is the eccentric here.

Doing the Amleth story in an authentic way is interesting in part because “Hamlet” is such an iconic literary work, but Shakespeare is totally indifferent to questions about what Denmark is actually like. “The Merchant of Venice” and “Romeo and Juliet,” similarly, are not aspiring to sociological realism about Italy. This kind of insouciance about what the past was actually like is by far the more mainstream element of the Western dramatic tradition. Homer’s texts themselves are anachronistic, incorporating elements of Iron Age Greece into a Bronze Age setting. The notion that things should be historically accurate is a relatively modern³ development.

Nolan obviously knows how to mount an “accurate” historical drama. The costume designer for “Dunkirk” manufactured fabric from scratch because the fabric that was used in the British military uniforms of the period isn’t made anymore and he wanted their clothing to have the correct texture. “Oppenheimer” is almost shockingly good at recreating a specific midcentury Jewish leftist intellectual milieu.

From the very first photographs of “The Odyssey” that were released, the production has been dogged by criticism that the armor isn’t correct. It’s entirely possible that the movie will be bad (though I think that’s unlikely). But when you see the attention to detail that went into “Dunkirk,” you have to understand that ahistorical armor isn’t a mistake but a creative choice. This is not a historical drama about Bronze Age Greece. It’s bringing a classic myth to the big screen — a project closer to the “Batman” movies than “Oppenheimer” and “Dunkirk.”

And so, yes, Helen of Troy is going to be Black for the same reason that there’s no particular effort to cast olive-skinned Mediterranean-looking white people in the Greek roles.

A canon for everyone

Some people just love to be mad on the internet and gin up conflict and paranoia. There are people claiming to have been “lied to” about Rosa Parks and revealing “hidden” truths about her that are actually available on her Wikipedia page (she was a veteran N.A.A.C.P. activist deliberately chosen to be the face of a push for bus reform in Montgomery).

But these hucksters succeed by tapping into sincerely felt emotions. So the thing I really do want to try to say to conservatives about all of this is that it’s worth opening your heart to the more optimistic interpretation of what’s going on.

A few years ago, Denzel Washington played Macbeth. Why? Well, again, because he’s a popular movie star and I’m sure producers hoped the choice would help market a Shakespeare adaptation. But also because he’s a major American actor, and Shakespeare is a critical part of America’s cultural heritage, so major figures in American drama like to take on Shakespeare. The play as a whole is set in Scotland, but it’s not “about Scotland” in any thematic sense and the character has almost no relationship to the historical Macbeth.

It’s good for America that Denzel wants to play Macbeth, that he wants to lay claim to this important part of our cultural heritage. And if that gets a slightly wider audience interested in Shakespeare (or in the German Expressionist cinematic tradition that the movie’s very unusual set design references), that’s fantastic.

Contemporary American society is ethnically diverse.

And we can also decide to be an ethnically diverse society with some unifying cultural values and precepts grounded in a common set of canonical works and traditions.

That canon must and should include diversity in terms of its authors, but the only realistically available canon is composed overwhelmingly of European authors telling European stories. And if you insist on limiting those stories to people of European ancestry, then the demand to tear the canon down will be overwhelming.

But while once upon a time Homer’s epic poems were a unique set of ethno-religious myths for Greek people, they have for centuries now been the common cultural heritage of “the West,” whatever that means exactly. Riffing on the stories in new and more inclusive ways is part of re-inscribing them into the canon. That’s progress!

The key conceit of “Western civilization” was that while you’re telling the origins of the contemporary United States, you’re not starting with events in the Western Hemisphere because the American tradition derives from the English tradition. And the English political tradition in the most relevant sense has its origins in France, and also in the Protestant Reformation, which happened in Germany and was in turn heavily influenced by the Italian Renaissance. And all that refers back to the Roman Empire and the Roman Republic, but the Roman tradition is heavily influenced by Classical Greece. So even though nobody cares very much about the most recent 2,000 or so years of Greek history, it’s very important to learn about the Peloponnesian War.
