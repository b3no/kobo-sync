Are we kind of being pricks?

Everybody has a crafty way of getting around development incentives without doing any development.

Halina Bennet
May 6
∙
Paid
 
READ IN APP
 

This week proved that even with legal momentum, housing gets built only at the will of NIMBYs. As cities test the limitations of development incentives, the Trump administration proposes stripping HUD of billions of dollars of community- and housing-development tools amid a six-year high in foreclosure rates. Outside of Washington, one resident of Marblehead, Mass., asks the $64,000 question.

The HUD budget request

On April 3, the Trump administration released its FY2027 budget request for the Department of Housing and Urban Development, proposing to cut funding by $10.7 billion. After the roughly 13 percent cut, total HUD appropriations would be down to $73.5 billion.

The cuts are concentrated, eliminating several of the federal government’s longest-running community- and housing-development tools. On the chopping block are:

  • the Community Development Block Grant ($3.3 billion annually, distributed by formula to more than 1,200 cities and counties)

  • the HOME Investment Partnerships Program ($1.3 billion)

  • the Housing Opportunities for Persons with AIDS ($529 million)

  • the Choice Neighborhoods Grants ($25 million)

Those programs are part of a group that accounts for about $9.1 billion in spending and currently underwrites everything from housing rehabilitation to small-business loans to lead-paint removal.

Homelessness policy would also be reshaped. The Continuum of Care program, which funds the bulk of permanent supportive housing nationwide, would be scrapped. In its place, the administration proposes consolidating resources into the Emergency Solutions Grant program, which would be expanded and oriented more toward short-term shelter and transitional housing. Overall Homeless Assistance Grants would be reduced by $393 million.

The fiscal shift is also a philosophical pivot away from long-term housing stabilization and toward more temporary interventions.

Other proposals include a freeze on issuing new rental vouchers, a five-year cap on assistance for most non-elderly, non-disabled households, and new work requirements to receive aid.

Still, history cautions against treating the proposal as inevitable. Congress has rejected previous attempts to eliminate programs like the Community Development Block Grant program, including in prior Trump budgets. Mayors and county officials in red and purple districts rely on them, and members of Congress would have a difficult time justifying the cuts.

But even partial cuts, the most likely scenario this go-around, would reshape local budgets and development pipelines.

Foreclosure pressures build as costs rise and aid fades

Foreclosure filings rose sharply in the first quarter of 2026, increasing 26 percent year over year to nearly 119,000 properties, the Wall Street Journal reports. Foreclosures are currently at their highest level since early 2020.

The numbers reflect a return to normalcy after foreclosures were artificially suppressed during the pandemic, according to the Journal.

For several years, federal relief programs, forbearance options, and emergency funding kept distressed homeowners out of the system. That infrastructure is now winding down, and the pathways that allowed borrowers to delay or restructure payments have narrowed. The housing market today is no longer defined by these pandemic-era protections, but by mounting cost pressures and tightening safety nets.

A key accelerant in foreclosures is the rollback of federal relief programs that had kept many distressed homeowners afloat. Federal Housing Administration rules limit access to loan modifications, generally allowing borrowers to apply for them only once every 24 months. That constraint is now binding in ways it wasn’t during the pandemic, the Journal reports, meaning that fewer homeowners can access help once they fall behind. As a result, foreclosures are expected to rise further, with some estimates suggesting as many as 250,000 households could lose their homes over the next year to year and a half.

The composition of housing costs is also changing.

Some homeowners still hold low fixed-rate mortgages and significant equity, so their monthly payments have remained stable. But the costs surrounding those payments have changed. Insurance premiums rose 12 percent last year, reaching an average of $2,948 annually. Average property taxes climbed 3 percent, to $4,427. These expenses don’t respond to interest rates, and, according to the Journal, their rise is outpacing incomes.

Taken together, these trends reflect a shift from an artificially suppressed foreclosure environment to a more normal, but still fragile, market. While overall levels remain below the peaks seen after the 2008 crisis, the combination of elevated costs and reduced government backstops is exposing financial strain — especially among recent buyers and lower-income homeowners — suggesting that distress in housing may broaden even without a full-scale downturn.

Trump complicates the path to a bipartisan housing law

Just as legislators inch closer to a rare bipartisan housing package, the 21st Century ROAD to Housing Act, new political uncertainty is emerging from the White House. According to Politico, President Donald Trump has privately raised objections to a Senate-passed housing bill that his administration had previously supported, creating fresh doubts about whether the legislation can ultimately become law.

He is reportedly opposed to “language in the bill that would require mega-landlords to sell single-family homes built as long-term rentals after seven years.” A.K.A. that pesky Section 901 that was put in at the last minute to appease the president and entice him to sign the bill.

California’s transit housing law meets reality

California’s SB 79, which takes effect July 1, represents one of the state’s most aggressive attempts to link housing production to transit access. The law allows higher-density residential development near major transit stops, overriding local zoning restrictions.

On paper, the policy is straightforward: If you want more housing in high-opportunity areas, allow it where infrastructure already exists. In practice, implementation has been complicated.

Cities have significant leeway in how they comply, and many are using that flexibility to shape outcomes. Los Angeles and San Francisco are both developing alternative plans that meet the letter of the law while attempting to manage where density occurs by drafting their own workarounds for local implementation. State law requires zoning capacity, not construction, and local governments have incentives to appear compliant while minimizing disruption.

A recent CalMatters article finds that the implementation fight is exposing fractures within the Democratic supermajority, with pro-housing members framing SB 79 as essential and others uncomfortable with how far state preemption has gone.

The San Francisco Chronicle reports that local YIMBYs are pushing back on Mayor Daniel Lurie’s own upzoning proposal, arguing it uses SB 79 compliance as a pretext to limit density in some neighborhoods while concentrating it in others. The mayor’s Family Zoning plan has generally been framed as ambitious, but there is tension between local discretion and actual housing production.

Meanwhile, Governor Gavin Newsom has been publicly criticizing Los Angeles and San Diego for their plans to phase in implementation in ways that shield portions of their cities from the law’s requirements.

Colorado’s housing push hits resistance

Two Colorado bills aimed at encouraging denser development failed last month.

One of the bills would have stopped local governments from requiring lot sizes that exceed 2,000 square feet, while the other would have allowed homeowners to split lots to build additional units on land zoned for one, the Colorado Sun reports.

Both proposals ran into opposition from local officials, who argued that they needed flexibility to manage growth, traffic, and infrastructure. Water availability, in particular, emerged as a central concern.

Colorado has enacted a series of supply-side reforms in recent years, but, unlike previous changes, these bills threatened to change the structure of single-family neighborhoods rather than amend less prominent parts of the housing system. The failure of these bills suggests that the next phase of reform, at least in Colorado, will be more contested.

A viral moment in Massachusetts

At a town meeting in Marblehead, Mass., a resident stood up and asked a simple question: “Are we kind of being pricks?” The comment brought a long debate about zoning compliance down to earth, and the clip went viral.

The town had been under pressure to comply with a state law requiring communities served by the Massachusetts Bay Transportation Authority to allow multifamily housing by right, the Marblehead Independent reports. After initial resistance and legal action from the state, voters approved a plan that technically meets the requirement.

Whether it will produce housing is another matter, and David Modica, the vocal community member, is skeptical. He pointed out that the argument was about nothing — that designated areas include locations where development is unlikely, like on a golf course, allowing the town to satisfy the law without actually building any housing.

“We’re trying to make sure we build no houses?” he asked. “Like, I don’t get it. People live in houses.”

The state’s enforcement efforts are ongoing, and the Marblehead episode illustrates both the challenges and the stakes of development proposals across the country. Mandates can change rules, but they don’t automatically change incentives.
