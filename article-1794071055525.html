How I dropped out of pre-med to join an orchestra

An excerpt from violinist Winston Webber’s charming new memoir, Best Seat in the House

Paul Wells
Nov 7
 
READ IN APP
 

On Nov. 3 Sutherland House published Best Seat in the House: Love Letters to a Profession by Winston Webber. The profession is that of a violinist in a symphony orchestra. Webber retired last year from his post in the National Arts Centre Orchestra in Ottawa. The book is a memoir of his more than 40 years in music. It began as a series of generously nostalgic Facebook posts he started writing in the autumn of 2020, during COVID. When he was up to 80,000 words of the stuff, Webber asked me what else he could do with all that prose. I sent him to Ken Whyte at Sutherland House. Now, substantially rewritten into one long narrative, it’s a book.

An orchestra can be a deceptive beast. Dozens of musicians who dress in black, never speak, stand or sit in unison, follow the conductor’s lead. They might seem interchangeable. But each lives the life of a disciplined, emotional artist. That’s the story Webber tells in his beautiful book. But as he reveals in this excerpt, he almost gave up music early for a very different profession.

Upgrade to paid

MY LOVE-FROM-AFAR FOR the National Arts Centre Orchestra stayed from afar for a long time. Just out of school I had landed a dream job in the Toronto Symphony—a fine orchestra — but after seven years (classic) the dream had become like working in a musical factory, though admittedly a very good one. It wasn’t just that I was turning thirty. Musicians in big orchestras, even in great orchestras, can soon feel like a small cog in a very big set of gears.

For years I had thought about going back to school for pre-med courses and making a try for that unique, professional gold ring. Yes, the mid-life side of this is banal, but the ambition was long-standing and genuine. Music had never been the only thing I’d wanted to do, but it was by far the best thing. I just didn’t know it yet. My age said it was now or never, so I made a break for it and was accepted at the University of Toronto as a mature student, not an easy get. I sold my instrument to pay the tuition and kept my conducting jobs to pay the rent. Yes, it was totally crazy.

At the University of Toronto I signed up for the standard pre-med curriculum — calculus, physics, biology, general chemistry, physiology (doing terrible things to cockroaches), and organic chemistry (the washout course). And I learned that the old joke was actually true: if it’s green it’s biology, if it stinks it’s chemistry, if it doesn’t work it’s physics. The courses were well-designed and well-taught and the students (almost all pre-meds) were desperately attentive. In those pre-internet days, exam results were posted on the walls outside the lecture halls — reams of dot-matrix printer paper filled with tiny numbers that held the key to your future.

One of the perks as a student in the chemistry department was sitting on the floor outside the office of Nobel Prize winner Prof. John Polanyi while waiting for your lecture to start. It was a very special floor. As soon as exam results were posted the pre-med students descended on them like a school of sharks. I have to say it felt good to be one of them, even being a decade older. And, back then, I could sit on the special Nobel Prize floor and actually get up again. The lectures and labs were quiet, advanced, and intense. I loved it.

I did the usual pre-med hospital volunteering at Toronto General, in my case in peritoneal dialysis and in the ICU waiting room. That was real. And I was accepted as a summer research fellow at the Ludwig Institute, then connected to Princess Margaret Hospital, to assist a well-known cancer epidemiologist. That work resulted in an actual published paper in the Journal of the National Cancer Institute (JNCI 1986; 77: 397–404). If you actually look it up, I’m listed as first author but my supervisor, Dr. Norman Boyd, was the real first author. I just did the legwork in the library stacks, but he listed me first as a leg up on my med school applications, which was kind.

The professional side of the hospital cafeteria was full of white coats talking non-stop about their research projects. I sat with them, just surfing on their enthusiasm. This was the thing, at least as I saw it then. The best of music was in the past. Medicine — all of science — was the future.

Following the first-year biology finals, a good chunk of it genetics, the whole class of a hundred or so met in a big lecture hall to get the answers to the thirty exam questions. Each answer was met with groans and cheers, it was fun. But there were also five bonus questions. Did anybody get any of them? Silence. The answer was, if anybody had actually got a correct answer, there’s a Nobel Prize waiting for you. Because nobody knows the answers. The lesson was, the bonus questions looked exactly like all the rest. There was the future. I loved that.

But my friends and family, who had been with me through all my years studying music, did not love my change of course. Even worse for them, I think, was how much I was enjoying it. In the middle of the second year of pre-med courses, I accepted an invitation to conduct the Calgary Philharmonic. I did well, but the only friend who came to hear me was an airline pilot who happened to have a layover in Calgary that night. My musical friends felt I had betrayed myself and there was some uncomfortable truth in that. And it would catch up with me.

After the Christmas holidays, I was in the Royal Conservatory of Music building on Bloor Street waiting to meet a friend when I spotted a notice of audition on the bulletin board.

VACANCY

National Arts Centre Orchestra

VIOLIN

Damn. Damn! Now I was in trouble. My courses were going well, I had volunteered in scary parts of the hospital, I had published research, I’d even received an encouraging letter from the medical school at Queen’s University, inviting me to apply. They found my background interesting. But here was an advertisement for a rare opening in this wonderful orchestra I had loved for years. What to do?

It was a party full of doctors that did me in. It was at a friend’s house who was a senior researcher at the Banting Institute, a pathologist working with state-of-the-art electron microscopes. In his early days he had made the first electron microscope photograph of the centriole, a vital part of the cellular machinery responsible for cell division. In other words, life. His photograph is in every cellular biology textbook, he even gave me a signed copy. He invited me to look through one of his multi-million-dollar electron microscopes at the Institute. I wore a suit and tie for that, like he did, none of this sitting on the floor student stuff. I was in his world now.

Well, not so fast. A doctor at the party (he was a dermatologist, which makes no difference at all) asked me what I was doing. I proudly told him about my pre-med courses and the research paper and how I was going to apply to med school. He squinted at me over his Scotch. “You look a little old for a pre-med student. What did you do before?” When I told him I’d been a member of the Toronto Symphony he almost threw his drink at me. “What? You have to have talent to be a musician! Any idiot can be a doctor! How the hell can you throw that away?” I knew he’d had a few and that not any idiot could be a doctor, but I didn’t have a good answer for him. How could I throw that away? And suddenly the whole thing felt shaky. Maybe my family and friends were right. I’d worked incredibly hard to win that position in the Toronto Symphony. You actually did need talent to do that. And here was an ad for a position in the National Arts Centre Orchestra, my true love. And that’s not just literary license.

Via viatores quaerit. I had been sought. So I applied for the position. Turning my back on two years of hard science courses and a possible, perhaps even likely career in medicine was insane. I didn’t even have an instrument. All the time I’d been taking courses I hadn’t played at all. So I borrowed a violin from the fine instrument dealer across the street from the conservatory, Michael Remenyi, who said with a generosity that still astounds me, “When you get the job, you can pay for it.” As I held a violin in my hands for the first time in two years, I replied, “When? You mean if!”

University-level science courses move fast. Miss a week and you’re lost. I started practicing for the audition, missed some classes, went to my physics lecture section and suddenly didn’t understand a thing. It was over.

The Toronto Symphony hired me for some concerts, whew, which was nice and some income. My first week back playing was an all-Strauss program with Andrew Davis, plus a recording. Talk about in the deep end. I practiced like mad, did an application tape in a conservatory classroom right next to The Bulletin Board and marked the April NACO audition date in my now-blank calendar. I arrived in Ottawa a day early, holed up in a hotel room and kept practicing, now just down the street from the promised land. I wasn’t going to let a delayed train mess this up. I got so frustrated with my playing that I threw my little windup metronome out the window. It landed on the roof of the building next door. It’s probably still there.

Share

The NAC Orchestra’s concertmaster, Walter Prystawski, ran the audition. I didn’t know him at all then except I was in awe of him, since he had basically founded the orchestra. He gave me a tempo for a Brahms excerpt and, before I could think, I said, “That’s too slow.” I honestly don’t remember anything after that, I knew I’d blown it. After the audition, for lack of anything else to do, I went across the street to the National Gallery and walked around the French Impressionists in a daze. Maybe I could drive a taxi.

A week later, the National Arts Centre Orchestra personnel manager called me and said a contract for the following season would be arriving in the mail. Was I still available? Was I still available? It was four in the afternoon. After the hyperventilation and dizziness passed, I took a cab straight to the airport and caught the next flight to Ottawa. I can remember like yesterday standing in line at the NAC box office, getting a seat for that evening and hearing the forty-six musicians of the NAC Orchestra play an all-Beethoven program: the Overture to Coriolan, the Piano Concerto No. 5 (“Emperor”) with André Watts, and the Seventh Symphony. That small orchestra on that huge stage . . . how in the world do they sound so big? So perfect, so musical, just like on the radio, only better. And next fall, I’ll be on that stage with them.

What can I say? When you’re young, stupid, and very lucky, these things can happen. It’s not until years later that you realize how close you came to the abyss. I had dropped the dream of a medical career in favour of a one-in-a-thousand chance of winning an audition on a borrowed instrument. And yet here I was. In the years following I would play Beethoven’s Seventh many times with the NAC Orchestra. For years it was NACO’s party piece, the way we play the last movement can break things. It’s what George Balanchine called an applause machine, it always brought down the house and we deserved it. And later, we played Metamorphosen, just like on the record.

The life it brings.
