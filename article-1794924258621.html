The Circus Is Back in Town

Nick Harkaway — John le Carré’s son — brings the gang back for a treacherous new adventure.

Nov 17

The Tuesday Review

I’m not a spy, but if I told you anything different, I’d have to kill you.

My cover, after all, is perfect: stooped middle-aged man with a made-up job and a face as common as a Honda Civic. That’s the CIA’s ideal candidate here in Washington, which is reportedly home to some 10,000 spies — about 12 out of every 10 people who say they work at the State Department.

David Cornwell, who served in British intelligence in the late 1950s and early ’60s, knew the key to invisibility was schlumpy, not hunky. When he started writing spy novels under the nom de plume John le Carré, his hero was more shaken than stirred. Bond — James Bond — might assassinate a terrorist while having sex in a helicopter, but George Smiley will expose a Soviet agent by taking off his loafers. He is, he humbly concedes, “unremarkable to the eye.”

When le Carré died in 2020, he left behind 26 novels and legions of fans around the world. As often happens with a writer so bankable, there are millions of reasons to keep the franchise going. (I’ll give you one clue, Agatha.) But le Carré’s literary estate had a secret weapon: The spymaster’s fourth son, Nicholas, is also a talented novelist. Under the name Nick Harkaway, he’s published several fantastically inventive thrillers. In 2024, he picked up his dad’s pen — watch out, it’s also a single-shot .22 — and published Karla’s Choice, his own novel about George Smiley.

I don’t usually review spy stories because I’m always that guy in the movie theater whispering to his wife, “What’s happening?” but Harkaway’s new Smiley novel, The Taper Man, has brought me in from the cold.

This time around the crisis begins with a Telex shattering a quiet night: “HELSINKI TO LONDON, URGENT,” it screams. “SHOOTING.”

A British agent was involved in an exchange of gunfire at a Helsinki marketplace. He survived, but a Soviet agent and a woman didn’t, and the Finnish officials are supremely kyrpiintynyt. Caught in the hinge of the Cold War, Finland crackles with the threat of nuclear annihilation; the country has no intention of letting England and Russia turn its capital into “a bloody Wild West saloon.”

In the wreckage of this mess, a document comes to light that suggests far more alarming news: Moscow is closing in on a high-ranking Soviet officer spying for Britain. But Smiley has a crafty idea to exploit the crisis. “We turn their counter back on them,” he tells his boss, a cantankerous cipher known as Control. “Their triangulation becomes our obfuscation. We preserve the Russian networks and Moscow Centre carries the cost.”

In short, Smiley and his team will find the person about to burn their operative, and through methods persuasive or coercive, they’ll get him to feed Moscow bad intel, preserving their agent while sending some other, falsely besmirched Russian officer to the gulag.

The Circus, le Carré’s nickname for MI6, has been in the doldrums for a year. “The whisper was categorical,” Harkaway writes. “Britain was in decline, and her spies were not immune. The team wasn’t winning.... In Smiley’s judgement it was not just time, but long past time, to make the other fellow taste the canvas.”

Central to this complicated scheme is locating the Russian agent who’s about to expose Britain’s prized source in Moscow. Smiley begins to suspect he’s dealing with a “taper man,” a lone spy left behind to trim the wick and keep the candle of espionage lit. Could that figure be hiding in California some 10 years after a whole nest of Soviet agents was cleared out of Los Angeles? The codebreakers get to work “with the proud, assiduous care of a pig farmer raising his bacon.”

I asked Nick Harkaway, “What have you discovered about George Smiley that your father never knew?”

It’s terrific fun to see Smiley wincing through the heat and ugliness of L.A. “The British Consulate,” he notes, “was an inconvenient building with a pale yellow frontage and arches which evoked the refuge of a mid-sized and not influential order of nuns.” His counterparts in American intelligence — the Cousins — treat him with a mixture of deference and mockery. One obnoxiously fit officer insists on calling him Sir George.

But Harkaway also exposes the deep-seated prejudices that infected America’s intelligence establishment and thwarted its effectiveness. Smiley’s discovery of a brilliant African American woman sidelined for her skin color provides a rich exploration of the strategic costs of racism. The dismissive treatment she endured in America after her valiant service in Berlin offers another sobering chapter in the story of those hidden figures whose contributions were erased.

Meanwhile, in alternating chapters we follow the antics of a British academic who’s spent years flattering his way into the Soviet academy. When the Circus sends word that his services are desperately needed to complete the Taper Man mission, the professor loyally springs into action. But can he do so without getting himself tenured in a coffin?

I was captivated by this tense story even when I felt abandoned under a bridge waiting for the next set of instructions. It was reassuring to hear one of Smiley’s colleagues admit, “No bloody idea what we’re doing here, any of us.” I read on, hungrily, picking up bits of the craft. “There was,” Harkaway writes, “a rat that gnawed at the innards of every good spy. What it really liked was secrets, and when it saw one, you could feel the teeth and claws as it scrabbled to be allowed to feed.”

Harkaway, like his dad, plots with the kind of density that I suspect is close to real spycraft: more paper chase than car chase. Nobody’s sipping martinis and skinny-dipping on Santorini. There are a thousand carefully calculated chess moves just to tell an agent in the field that a message will be coming.

But as much as The Taper Man has John le Carré’s nose, it’s got Harkaway’s face. His plotting and style offer an homage without imitating Dad. That’s most evident in the novel’s singular tone. Hold this book to a flame, and you can see a line of wit written across these pages in invisible ink.

Having grown up with his father reading to him about the perils of George Smiley, Harkaway knows this quiet spy as he would a grieving uncle, “lost, frightened and confused.” Smiley’s desire to make things right with his wife, to atone for the errors he’s made in the field — these yearnings throb beneath his rumpled exterior with a rare mixture of “empathy and ruthlessness.”

“Sometimes we think something is true,” he muses. “Sometimes we just carry on as if it was, because the alternative is desolation.” Only Smiley’s closest colleagues know — and the enemies he snuffs out learn too late — that he’s “the most dangerous man in the world.”

The Taper Man: A Novel of John le Carré’s Circus, by Nick Harkaway (Viking, 400 pp., $32).
