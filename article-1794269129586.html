Voters went for YIMBY candidates — but not their ballot initiatives

The electorate supported YIMBY- and abundance-pilled candidates but rejected the kind of measure that would make their promises possible.

Halina Bennet
Nov 9
∙
Paid
 
READ IN APP
 

Abundance was on the ballot last week, and now that the votes are in, the results are mixed.

Midterm candidates across the country ran on an abundance-adjacent message, leaning into affordability as an animating issue for voters. And the electorate overwhelmingly chose leaders who ran on abundance themes. But supply-side ballot measures — even ones with strong elite support — often fell short.

Abundant wins for abundance candidates

After a campaign cycle awash with messaging about the high cost of living, the candidates who succeeded were, far and away, the ones who most directly addressed voters’ concerns about affordability. Seats in the U.S. House will be filled by abundance-pilled representatives.

Perhaps most notably, State Senator Scott Wiener will succeed U.S. Representative Nancy Pelosi in CA-11 after defeating Connie Chan, who ran against him as an old-fashioned growth-skeptical leftist. Wiener is one of the original and most visible housing-supply champions in California. Chan made her own campaign promises to expand housing affordability, but she was far more cautious about private development, as progressives with gentrification concerns tend to be. Wiener was the YIMBY candidate of the cycle in any race, with an urbanist legislative record that has put housing affordability first at every turn. His bullishness on how he has delivered — and will continue to do so — on the issue made him the standout abundance legislator, and his victory indicates an embrace, at least in the Bay Area, of a supply-side approach to housing.

In Los Angeles, Mayor Karen Bass conceded to City Councilmember Nithya Raman, who will become the city’s 44th mayor. Raman was positioned clearly to Bass’s left but also ran as a YIMBY change agent in the mold of Zohran Mamdani. She is a member of the Democratic Socialists of America and ran a true underdog campaign with a focus on engaging and centering renters. As a left-YIMBY, Raman’s relationship with developers is more complicated than Wiener’s. She is a strong advocate for tenant protections, but she also supports State Senate Bill 79 (Wiener’s legislation), faster permitting, and an exemption for new apartment buildings from L.A.’s so-called mansion tax. Raman’s coalition may not be the usual abundance one, but many of her policies align with the movement, and her emphasis on construction and delivery made her the pro-housing, pro-abundance candidate in the race.

In the upper chamber, the incoming slate of Democratic senators includes Abdul El-Sayed, who supports streamlining regulations to unlock new housing, and Sherrod Brown, who has a history of supporting affordability legislation.

Post-election, the argument isn’t going away. Newly elected officials are already talking about plans to lean into supply-side housing policies and reforms.

The clearest abundance signal came from Texas. Less than 24 hours after he beat Ken Paxton, Senator-elect James Talarico was already chatting supply with Fox News.

“I also want to work with the president on housing,” he said. “He’s a builder, and we need to cut the red tape, the regulation that sometimes my party has been guilty of so that we can build millions of new homes in this country.”

Talarico often spoke in these terms during his campaign.

Housing became a ballot staple

Voters in at least five states voted on measures related to housing affordability, the highest number of housing ballot measures in at least 20 years. As recently as 2024, housing hardly registered as a ballot issue. Now it is one litigated regularly in red and blue states, in places that are development-friendly and not.

Every measure offering financial assistance or tax relief passed.

  • California Proposition 1, an $11.25 billion bond to fund affordable housing and address homelessness, passed with more than 60 percent of the vote.

  • California Proposition 37, which authorizes the California Housing Finance Agency to issue up to $25 billion in bonds to finance loans for buyers of new homes, passed with more than 62 percent support. Since the program mainly applies to new construction, it has a supply angle.

  • Rhode Island Question 2, a $120 million bond aimed at developing affordable housing, passed with more than 61 percent of the vote.

  • Florida Constitutional Amendment 3, which expanded a property tax exemption for homeowners, passed with 62 percent support.

  • Hawaii Question 2, which deals with infrastructure bonds for new housing construction, passed with nearly 62 percent of the vote. Of the five successful measures, Hawaii’s is the closest to abundance-adjacent supply-side assistance since it lowers the infrastructure costs that make new home projects more expensive.

Bonds and subsidies are wins for immediate affordability, but the true abundance-aligned measures — the ones that dealt most directly with deregulation and supply-side politics — came up short.

Massachusetts Question 7 would have allowed single-family homes on smaller lots statewide (excluding Boston). It proposed capping minimum lot sizes at 5,000 square feet for single-family homes on residential lots with public water, sewer, and at least 50 feet of frontage. Many towns in the state require at least one acre (more than 40,000 square feet) per lot. The measure lost by slightly more than a single point. It was the most direct test of political interest in statewide upzoning, and though it didn’t pass, it came close.

In California, Proposition 45 would have shortened environmental review times for select essential projects. It was rejected by more than 10 points. The measure had more to do with infrastructure projects than housing ones, leading California YIMBY to decline to take a position on the matter.

Taken together, the success of candidates with abundance vibes and the failure of Question 7, the single true abundance measure in America, suggest that voters like the results abundance promises — more homes and lower costs — but they continue to be cautious about the mechanisms that deliver the results when those mechanisms threaten to take power from local government or limit environmental review.

The next test will be whether the abundance-minded can make their case well enough to prove to voters that the rules themselves are the thing standing in the way of what they want. Based on Talarico’s post-election comments, it looks like some advocates have already started.
