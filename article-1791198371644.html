Not ready to make nice: the Canada-US relationship

If you’re waiting for “normal,” you’ll be waiting a while.

Kyla Ronellenfitsch
Oct 5
 
READ IN APP
 

This time last year, I wrote an article that, in perhaps painful detail, explained how Canadians perceive the US. Not just how do they feel about Donald Trump (it’s fairly self-evident), but what do they think is happening in America?

I wrote that the general population consensus was further ahead of the overt elite consensus. That is, Canadians clearly saw not only an administration that was problematic and disruptive to Canada, but one that was fundamentally anti-democratic. At that time, 69% of Canadians felt that the word “dictatorship” applied to what was happening in the US. 68% would use the word “authoritarian,” and 65% said “fascist” applies. This was 2 in 3 Canadians’ truth – one I didn’t often see reflected by our politicians, journalists, business community, or other individuals responsible for explaining this moment in time.

And then came Davos. The magic of the Prime Minister’s speech was the melding of realities. He reflected back, in slightly different terms (ahem, hegemony), how Canadians perceive the US, and has been significantly rewarded for it politically. This was compounded by the PM’s withdrawal from trade negotiations with the US this summer, another moment hallmarked by truth-telling (“that its signature was written in pencil.”)

Today’s article tracks some of the measures from last year’s poll. I’ve also looked at what comes next. I often hear commentators say things might look better after the midterms, or Donald Trump will be gone in 2 years and then the relationship can return to normal. But, as always, what do Canadians think? Spoiler: not that.

Let’s dig into it.

Donald Trump is as unpopular as he has ever been.

I know I promised (implied?) a progression of our understanding. Do we really need to talk about Trump? Well, we have seen change over the past couple of years! It just happens to fall somewhere between “a majority” and “nearly everyone” showing disdain for Trump.

Leading up to last year’s federal election ~6 in 10 Canadians had an unfavourable view of Trump, half of whom had a “very unfavourable” view. Toss in a couple “51st states” and we saw Trump’s “very unfavourables” rise to 66% of Canadians on the eve of the election. Then there was a bit of a brand rally. Trump didn’t become popular, but “very unfavorable” views fell by 10 points. But over this year, Trump has reached a new peak of unpopularity (68% “very unfavourable”). The more you know.

Favourability of Donald Trump
Q: How favourable are your feelings about Donald Trump?

Few Canadians see Donald Trump as a blip. Pessimism about the future of the US is widespread.

If you understand anything about how Canadians perceive this moment in America, let it be this: the roots of their displeasure run deep. Half of Canadians think “America has been fundamentally changed by Donald Trump and will never be what it once was.” This rises to 64% of NDP voters, and 59% of Liberals. A plurality of Conservative voters also side with this argument.

Conversely, 29% of Canadians think “Donald Trump is just a blip. In two years he’ll no longer be president, and the US will return to normal.” Conservatives are no more likely to think Trump is a blip. Rather, more are simply uncertain.

Trump: Blip or Permanent Change Agent
Q: Which perspective better describes your point of view?

So then what is that change? What’s happening under Trump’s leadership? We see an understanding very similar to last year. Basically, 2 in 3 Canadians say “all of the above” to the non-democratic terms tested.

68% of Canadians think the term “oligarchy” applies to what’s happening in the US. Belief that this term “strongly applies” has increased since last year (+4). Similar proportions think “authoritarian” (67%) and “fascist” (67%) apply. Belief that “democracy” applies to the US under Trump’s leadership has declined over the past year, from 53% in 2025 to 48% today. Notably, just 19% of Canadians think “democracy” “completely applies” to the United States. The biggest of oofs.

Defining What's Happening in the US
Q: Some people use different terms to describe what’s happening in the United States under Donald Trump. To what extent do you think the following terms apply?

When I tested this question last year, I’m not going to lie… I was feeling a little bold. But just as I think it’s important to say the thing in politics, it’s critical to test the thing in polling. If you flatten the conversation, you miss it.

So I took something I often hear, whether it’s on social media, from family, or in a focus group, and tested it. My mom’s “he’s acting like a dictator!” became the question above. But even for this, what does it mean? How far will Canadians entertain the idea before it collapses?

I asked participants, “Some people have compared what is happening to the United States today to the early stages of what happened in Germany in the 1930s – a government consolidating power, weakening democratic institutions and threatening neighbouring countries. What comes closest to your view?”

A plurality buys it. 42% of Canadians think “the comparison is accurate – we are watching a similar process unfold.” 37% of those under 50 hold this view, compared to 41% of those 50-64 and a majority (53%) of seniors. It’s also the dominant view of both NDP and Liberal voters.

28% of Canadians take a middle (but still critical) position: “the concerns are real, but the comparison goes too far.”

Just 13% of Canadians outright reject the comparison, and say it is “completely wrong – what is happening in the US is not comparable to 1930s Germany”. Almost all of this group are Conservative voters, but the Conservative voter base is split into 3 roughly equal components. 30% think the comparison is accurate, 28% think the concerns are real, but the comparison goes too far, and 27% think it’s completely wrong. CPC friends, have fun with that.

US Comparison to 1930s Germany
Q: Some people have compared what is happening in the United States today to the early stages of what happened in Germany in the 1930s — a government consolidating power, weakening democratic institutions and threatening neighbouring countries. Which comes closest to your view?

If we look past Trump, 42% of Canadians think the US is on a long-term path toward no longer being a functioning democracy, while 32% think the US will ultimately recover from the current period and will remain a functioning democracy over the long term. A not insignificant 25% of Canadians are uncertain either way.

Future of US Democracy
Q: Which perspective better describes your point of view?

Canadians anticipate broad consequences – or, at the very least, see them as possible.

A guiding question of last year’s research was – if Canadians think the US is no longer a true democracy, then what? What does it mean to live next door to a country that is… whatever the US is now?

Beyond an expected increase in Canadian pride, the most anticipated outcome last year was an “increase in refugees from the US.” 51% of Canadians thought this was “certain” or “likely” to happen. That expectation has since declined – 15% of Canadians think this is “certain,” 27% think it’s “likely.”

Expectations of the other two consequences tested have held steady. 40% of Canadians think it’s “certain” or “likely” that we could have conflict over Canadian territory, and 38% think we’re “certain” or “likely” to experience more extreme politics in Canada.

But the most important thing these data show is how uncertain Canadians feel. Relatively few think any of these outcomes are “certain” or “certain not” to happen. This ambiguity – the possibility – is politically powerful. It craves direction. Someone to say, “I got this.” Sound familiar?

Effects of US Political Environment in Canada
Q: How likely do you think it is that Canada will face the following because of the United States’ political environment?

The Canada-US relationship is expected to be fraught over the long term.

Just 13% of Canadians think the US can be relied on “a great deal” over the long term. 20% think they can be counted on “somewhat,” 32% say “not very much,” and 1 in 4 Canadians say “not at all.” Once again, not a blip.

Conservative voters are divided but are a little bit more likely to be optimistic about the Canada-US relationship. But if we look at the relationship with Poilievre’s own favourability, we again see why this issue is tricky. Those who like Poilievre the most tend to think Canada can count on the US long term. Those who are kind of *ehh* about him are divided, while those who dislike Poilievre are the harshest in their assessment of the Canada-US relationship.

Long-Term: Canada Reliance on US as a Stable Partner
Q: Thinking long-term – the next 10 to 20 years – how much do you think Canada can rely on the United States as a stable and trustworthy partner?

That pessimism shapes how Canadians want to approach the relationship going forward. If the next president wants to restore the Canada-US relationship, just 19% of Canadians would want us to fully “forgive and forget.” 34% would want us to “conditionally move on and restore our relationship but only if the US apologizes and makes meaningful changes.” The remaining 32% of Canadians want us to “continue on our new path,” and permanently reduce Canada’s reliance on the US and prioritize relationships with other countries. 15% aren’t sure.

Restoring the Canada-US Relationship
Q: If the next US president wants to restore the Canada-US relationship, what do you think should be our response?

Even if the next president were decidedly not Trump — if a Democrat were to win — just 12% of Canadians think it’s “certain” the Canada-US relationship will return to normal. At the same time, few are certain that the relationship won’t return to normal (10%). There’s a lot of ambiguity.

And while there is openness to things returning to normal if a non-MAGA Republican wins (57% say it’s at least possible), fully half of Canadians think the relationship is “certain not” to return to normal if a MAGA or Trump-aligned candidate wins the presidency.

2028 Presidential Impact on Canada
Q: If each of the following individuals or types of politicians were elected US president in 2028, how likely would the Canada-US relationship be to return to normal?

Canadians will happily maintain their travel boycott – if MAGA wins the next presidential election.

The one real-world manifestation of Canadians’ displeasure with the US, outside of voting, has been travel. In real terms, travel to the US has declined by more than a quarter vs. 2024, costing the US economy billions.

In self-reported behaviour, 38% of Canadians say they have completely stopped traveling to the US, and a further 14% of Canadians say they have traveled less to the US than they otherwise would. Just 16% of Canadians say the political environment hasn’t impacted their travel at all. Seniors are most likely to be boycotting US travel, but it’s still widespread across age groups.

Canadian Travel to the US
Q: Since Trump returned to office, have you avoided or reduced travel to the US?

It’s far from guaranteed that Trump leaving office means that travel will return to normal. 16% of Canadians haven’t had their travel impacted, and they expect that to continue. 1 in 4 Canadians don’t travel to the US anyway. But just 12% of Canadians expect to restart traveling to the US as much as they did before once Trump’s out of office. Just as many say they plan to avoid travel to the US even when Trump leaves (13%). And 25% of Canadians have a wait-and-see attitude – they’ll return only if someone who isn’t allied with Trump wins the presidency.

Post-Trump US Travel
Q: Once Donald Trump is no longer president, which best describes how you expect your travel to the United States to change?

Our new era.

There’s always an inherent limitation in asking people to forecast their own opinion or behaviour. How are they supposed to know? They’re not yet living in that context. But in doing so, we get a clearer read of where people are today.

Canadians are mad. It’s not surface-level. Many plan to stay mad, even if the next president wants to revert to the before times.

But as my dad, and every other boomer, would say… fool me once. Canadians might have been able to accept that Trump 1 was a blip, but America doubled down. A country that can choose that twice is unlikely to be viewed as a trustworthy partner any time soon.

In politics, and I suppose life, it’s always a big gamble to bank on a change in circumstances: to construct a strategy that relies on some uncertain event happening before everything else can fall into place. Waiting for Trump to leave office for political or economic change is precisely one of those risks.

The new normal is now. Plan accordingly.
