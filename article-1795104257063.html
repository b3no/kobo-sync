Tearing Down the Empathy Paywall

you don’t need a diagnosis to deserve compassion

Freddie deBoer
Nov 19

Back when I was in grad school, I noticed with some amusement that that there were academics in my field who were trying to get “Appalachian” going as an identity. I don’t mean to dismiss the fact that there are distinctly Appalachian identities, nor would I ever say that they aren’t worthy of academic study. What I found funny was how baldly they were engaged in a modern-day phenomenon that everyone knows about but which polite people don’t call attention to: the attempt to gain access to our culture’s contemporary circle of empathy, looking for a doorway into the status of victimhood to gain access to the treasures that lie within. The effort with Appalachian identity appears to have gotten nowhere, which is unsurprising - too white - but people do go on trying.

I thought of that when reading this academic article on the (bleak) state of patients self-diagnosing and resisting the judgment of doctors. Here’s a quote:

In many cases, patients strictly resisted the diagnostic outcome when it diverged from their anticipated self- or desired diagnosis. CPs [clinical psychologists] described situations where patients demanded that alternative diagnoses be removed from reports or explicitly asked for the desired diagnosis to be given despite not meeting the criteria:

“The peak moment was when a female patient said after the feedback session: ‘I now understand that I don’t meet the criteria for ADHD, but could you please give me the diagnosis anyway?’ When I asked what would change for her by receiving a diagnosis which she does not fulfil the criteria for, she said that she would also like to be ‘diverse in some way or another.’”

I would argue that anyone who spends any time engaging with these issues is aware that this sort of thing happens and for this exact reason - the desire to enjoy the benefits of being “diverse.” It’s a thing. None of this means that there aren’t very real inequalities and oppressions out there or that we shouldn’t be dedicated to ending them. But, well, the law of unintended consequences….

The broader tendency, where people try to create new “marginalized groups” to belong to so that they can enjoy the social and cultural blessings that accrue to such groups - well, it’s the sort of thing people get mad at me for saying without actually bothering to say that I’m wrong. It is an absolute classic in the genre of “That’s true, but you’re not supposed to say it, Freddie.” But of course people do that; I have a hard time believing that a single soul reading these words who doesn’t know people who behave that way. It’s ubiquitous, in the 21st century. Look at Rachel Dolezal or the never-ending parade of exposed pretendians. People fake ethnic and racial identities because there are incentives to do so. We so often find these fakers in academia because in academia there are clear (often explicit) advantages conferred on members of these identities and a strong social prohibition against challenging them. But occasionally, they are challenged, and occasionally, this results in scandal. (My favorite is “Jessa Bombalera,” a white woman named Kansas who successfully pretended to be a Dominican woman from East Harlem for more than a decade despite a comically fake accent.) This potential for career-ruining consequences makes racial and ethnic imposture less attractive. What you’d like is an identity category that anyone can join, with a little shameless effort, where the identity definitions are broad and frequently vague, and where we have a similar strong social prohibition against skepticism.

Disability fits the bill, and that’s why we are where we are. ...
