The Verdict, by Jerusalem Demsas

If one of my employees decided to hack another company to access and steal privileged information, there would likely be a criminal investigation.

By knowingly transmitting code that intentionally causes damage to a protected computer, the employee could be facing 10 years in jail under the Computer Fraud and Abuse Act (CFAA). The Argument itself might also be in trouble — a corporation can be responsible for crimes committed by an employee if the acts “(i) were within the scope of their duties and (ii) were intended, at least in part, to benefit the corporation.”

The first person convicted under the CFAA was a Cornell grad student named Robert Morris. In 1988, Morris wrote a computer worm — a malicious type of software that can copy itself from one computer to another automatically by exploiting security weaknesses. Worms don’t need a user to open infected files in order to spread from machine to machine, and the Morris Worm replicated far more aggressively than its creator intended, infiltrating universities, research facilities, and even military sites.

At the time, an appellate court rejected the argument that the government had to prove Morris intended the resulting damage. All it needed to prove was that he’d intentionally obtained unauthorized access, and that the subsequent damage was a result of that access. Congress subsequently amended the statute to demand proof of intent if prosecutors wanted stiff penalties to stick.

But what happens if no human selected the target or instructed the attack? This was one dilemma raised when a self-described “swarm” of AI agents hacked the company Hugging Face because they thought it might have the information they needed to cheat on a task given to them by humans at OpenAI.¹

The hack has triggered at least one active investigation into whether OpenAI violated state law, and 15 Republican state attorneys general have asked OpenAI to preserve documents related to the incident. Many computer-crime provisions require prosecutors to establish knowledge, intent, or recklessness to a legally responsible person. The problem for prosecutors is that no human intentionally or knowingly breached Hugging Face’s defenses.² The humans at OpenAI were running tests, and the agents acted … autonomously.

That doesn’t mean OpenAI is beyond the reach of the law. It means criminal law — which generally asks what someone knows or intends — may struggle to address the sorts of incidents that are now possible because of advances in AI.

The best tool may be an old one: torts!

As civil rights lawyer Joel Wertheimer has explained in The Argument, courts should assign liability to the cheapest cost avoider: “Instead of trying to cleanly attribute the harm of AI to each party involved in its creation, courts should simply assign liability to the party that can most easily limit harm at the lowest cost. Over time, regulations can evolve out of these harms.”

This framework helps us sidestep difficult and philosophically taxing questions like “was the model itself negligent?” and moves us to the practical question of “which organization could most effectively and cheaply stop the chain of events?” We’re not helpless — OpenAI could have taken a range of steps to prevent this sort of thing from happening.

Lots of people are currently waking up to the dangers of AI agents roaming the web and wreaking havoc. Trillions of dollars in economic value and the lives and well-being of most human beings rest on digital infrastructure that cybersecurity experts warn is now extremely vulnerable to these sorts of attacks. We don’t need to freak out, but policymakers do need to act now.

What’s News with The Argument

The Argument recommends, by Maibritt Henkel

This week, I asked the office to recommend all things Dolly Parton.

Our video producer Justin Zuckerman just got back from Sweden, and in the Dolly spirit, recommended Lee Hazlewood’s Cowboy in Sweden. Apparently it’s a “great country/psychedelic soundtrack album from a Swedish 1970 TV special.”

Jerusalem Demsas suggested you listen to some Kacey Musgraves, who has “a very different self-presentation but has a similar ‘mind your own business’ anti-small-town-social-surveillance energy.” Jeremiah Johnson also kept things contemporary, recommending Miley Cyrus’ cover of the Dolly hit “Jolene.”

Senior editor John Robert Thomason took the prompt more literally. Because he prefers Dolly’s more “melancholy songwriting,” he recommends the 2021 compilation Choctaw Ridge: New Fables of the American South 1968-1973. The songs “speak to an especially dark and frank moment in the genre — a far cry from the kitschy fantasies of small-town life that caused me to dismiss the genre when I was young (and dumb).”

As a bonus, he also thinks you should check out the 1998 Pras hit “Ghetto Supastar,” which uses a vocal interpolation of Kenny Rogers and Dolly Parton’s “Islands in the Stream” for its chorus.

One thing you have to understand about Kobe Yank-Jacobs is that he has an intense Bob Dylan situation going on. So it is not to be taken lightly when he calls the Dolly cover of “Don’t Think Twice, It’s All Right” “real good” — especially the lyric change in the third verse.

Operations manager Angela Tracy intends to rewatch Steel Magnolias, a 1989 film about a group of women in a small Louisiana town, in Dolly’s honor. To which I would add that if you haven’t, for whatever reason, already watched 9 to 5, then you should get on that ASAP.

Finally, Eli Richman leaves us with a piece of iconic Dolly lore: “I vividly remember when a bunch of Confederate statues were coming down and every Twitter leftist and their dog was going on about, ‘Oh, you can replace them with statues of Dolly Parton!’ And then when Tennessee actually did propose putting her statue on its Capitol grounds in 2021, I thought it was very classy of her to basically say, ‘I don’t want any statues while I’m alive.’”

Bring on the Dolly statues, I say.
