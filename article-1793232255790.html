Shaken and Stirred by the Search for the Next James Bond

The question may be less who will inherit the mantle than who in his right mind would want to.

By Anthony Lane

How was your weekend? Did you find an unexpected person in your bed? Did you slay a couple of heavies with your bare hands, then do it again, with extra venom? Did you save the world? If not, I have bad news: you are not the next James Bond. The quest for a new occupant of what remains, despite everything, the hottest seat in cinema—the role of 007—reached its latest climax on October 23rd to 25th, in London. Thither, “according to several sources,” in the words of The Hollywood Reporter, a chosen few were summoned to butch it out for the ultimate honor. Talk about a Bonding exercise.

Who those sources were cannot, of course, be revealed. Like purveyors of royal gossip, Bond whisperers never identify themselves; to do so would be to court the fate of Professor Dent, in “Dr. No,” who is shot after attempting—the fool!—to kill Bond with a mere tarantula. (The actor playing Dent, Anthony Dawson, had already tried to do away with Grace Kelly, in “Dial M for Murder,” and been stabbed for his pains. How epic can fails be?) Anyway, we are now informed, reliably or not, that the final contestants for the part of 007 include Jack Lowden, Callum Turner, and Josh O’Connor. Otherwise known, as any TV addict will tell you, as that guy from “Slow Horses,” that guy from “Masters of the Air,” and that guy with the ears who played Prince Charles in “The Crown.” Also allegedly in the running: Jack O’Connell, who, in “Sinners,” became one of the first vampires to dance an Irish jig. In a solemn aside, the Reporter adds, “It is unclear who else may be testing.”

In short, we haven’t a clue. In recent years, all too many names have drifted in and out of the frame: Idris Elba, Aaron Taylor-Johnson, Henry Cavill, Jacob Elordi, Richard Madden, Tom Hardy, Theo James, and Paul Mescal, although, given how miserable Mescal looked when he had to fight someone who was riding a rhino, in “Gladiator 2,” his odds may have lengthened. Age is another disqualifier. Elba, now fifty-four, was touted as a possible Bond for so long that his chance has come and gone, whereas Lowden, Turner, and O’Connor are thirty-six. A decade ago, the Irish actor Aidan Turner (you may have seen him in “Poldark,” on PBS) was all the rage, and his compound of intensity and levity, like that of his countryman Pierce Brosnan, appeared to fit the bill—one of Bond’s defining knacks, after all, is for orchestrating the victory of good over evil without taking either of them too seriously. But Aidan Turner is forty-three, and, like most of us, he will surely recall the crowning exchange, between hero and villain, that lends “The Spy Who Loved Me” its philosophical weight:

“Your time is running out, Stromberg.”

“Yours, too, Mr. Bond. Yours, too. And faster than you think.”

One man whose mortal time ran out this year, to the understandable dismay of his admirers, was Sam Neill. Born in Northern Ireland, he grew up in New Zealand before relocating to Australia, and that cultural backdrop, fronted by his other virtues, might have made him an excellent Bond. He was dashing, effortlessly amused, and quite unassuming in his command of our attention—so much so, indeed, that he specified how badly he did not wish to play 007. “I wouldn’t have been any good and I wouldn’t have enjoyed it,” he once said. In 1986, nonetheless, he went to Pinewood Studios, outside London, for a screen test. It survives online, enabling lunatics like me, who treasure films that never came to pass, to watch Neill intone the sacred liturgy of Bond. Entering his bedroom, he discovers that he is not alone:

“So, you’re Tatiana Romanova.”

“My friends call me Tania.”

“My friends call me Bond. James Bond.”

There is almost too much to unpack here. First, the intruder, lying in wait and in bed, is gamely played for the purposes of the audition by Fiona Fullerton, who only the year before had been in “A View to a Kill,” Roger Moore’s last movie as Bond. Second, where is the choker? The sequence is lifted from the second Bond film, “From Russia with Love,” in which, as Tatiana, Daniela Bianchi—lest we forget, a former Miss Universo Italia—wears a black choker and barely anything else. Details matter, as they did for Ian Fleming, who, in the original novel of “From Russia with Love,” not only bedecks Tatiana with “a quarter-inch black velvet ribbon round her neck” but also saddles her with an awkward, if well-meaning, gaffe:

“You are very handsome,” she said. She searched for a comparison that would give him pleasure. “You are like an American film star.”

She was startled by his reaction. “For God’s sake! That’s the worst insult you can pay a man!”

One wonders if this repartee, which was wisely omitted from the script, rang in the ears of James Brolin, who reënacted the same scene, with a British accent and a touch of aplomb, for his screen test, in 1982. He was up for the role of Bond in “Octopussy,” and his Tatiana, for the test, was played by Maud Adams. (No Bondian needs reminding that she had earned her spurs as Scaramanga’s doomed girlfriend in “The Man with the Golden Gun.” Do keep up.) In the event, although Brolin believed that the part was his, and even had orders to “prepare certain muscles for certain things I wasn’t adept at yet,” Moore returned for his sixth excursion as Bond in “Octopussy,” opposite Adams. The state of Moore’s muscles is unrecorded.

There is another snippet of Bond that has been used to challenge contenders for the throne. It comes from “GoldenEye,” and it consists of Bond introducing himself to Xenia Onatopp, one of his nemeses, and smirking maturely as he hears her name. In 2005, with “Casino Royale” on the cards, actors such as Sam Worthington, Antony Starr, Rupert Friend, and Henry Cavill (then a stripling of twenty-two) were invited to pronounce the words “Bond—James Bond” for the camera. Casual clips display their halting efforts. Eventually, all the young dudes lost out to Daniel Craig, who, in the course of five films, went on to make the role his own—and, it must be said, to demonstrate the toll that it took. Craig’s appeal was undimmed to the end, yet the joy slowly faded from his gaze, and maybe the question we ought to be asking is not “Who will be the next Bond?” but “Who in his right mind would want to be Bond?” Also, “Why do we still care who Bond is or isn’t?” Oh, and one last complicating factor: Bond is dead.
