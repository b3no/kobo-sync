The United States of Fear

In Edwidge Danticat’s “Dèy,” a mother survives a mass shooting but finds that the ordinary demands of life won’t wait.

Ron Charles
Aug 25
 
READ IN APP
 

The Tuesday Review

While I read Edwidge Danticat’s new novel about gun violence, six members of a family died by gunfire in Winfield, Kansas; another five were massacred near Lake City, Michigan.

It’s unlikely you heard much about these shootings. Held captive by the Supreme Court’s misreading of the Constitution and the deadly influence of the gun lobby, Americans routinely suffer such carnage. While thoughts and prayers are exchanged for campaign donations, politicians admonish mourners not to politicize the latest gun tragedy.

Indeed, so successful has that campaign been that even this mention of recent attacks and my brief characterization of our political impotence feel slightly hysterical, almost in bad taste. Enough with your editorial — get to the book!

Danticat must feel the same fury at this daily slaughter, but her novel Dèy is a gracious exercise in restraint, a story fueled by the tempered outrage that runs through so much of her fiction and nonfiction. She bears witness and, by the extraordinary empathy of her prose, makes us witnesses, too.

The first three of Dèy’s four sections are titled “Run,” “Hide,” and “F(l)ight,” a grim allusion to the advice American schoolchildren receive during active-shooter drills. The novel opens in early 2018, mid-rampage: “I dive to the restaurant floor as gunfire rips the air outside.” The narrator, a real estate agent named Magnolia, is shopping for her daughter’s birthday present at a mall when blasts echo through the building.

“Is this a mass grave?” asks a man sobbing next to her on the floor.

“Where else is left to run to now?” Magnolia wonders — a question that refers as much to the top floor of the Miami shopping mall as to this trapped nation.

But then sirens approach. Bland instructions blast over the PA system. A SWAT team shouts at the terrified diners to line up with their arms raised. Once outside, Magnolia breaks away from the group and runs barefoot to her car.

She tells no one what happened to her, even as the details dominate the news — nine injured, four dead, including the racist shooter. But Magnolia insists “the mall was one of many experiences I had that day.” She will remain “a woman determined to no longer give this event at the mall any space.”

The futility of that claim is enough to break your heart.

In an interview with Harper’s Bazaar, Danticat said she was inspired by her own frightening experience in 2017 when kids playing with the sound system in a Florida shopping mall created popping noises that sparked panic.

Dèy, which means “mourning” in Haitian Creole, takes place in the penumbra of those opening 10 pages. It’s a haunting feat of concision and elision. Still shaking from the trauma of what she experienced, Magnolia listens to her phone messages and gets right to work. A difficult client must be encouraged to accept an offer on her condo. Her mother, back in Haiti, is in the hospital, and Magnolia should comfort her and figure out what’s wrong. And then there’s the upcoming birthday party for her soon-to-be 10-year-old daughter, complicated by the fact that her partner recently moved out.

Life, in all its quotidian details, rushes along, completely uninterested in anyone’s trauma or near-death scrapes. The best Magnolia can do is press her palms to the floor of a condo she’s trying to sell. “There’s no stickiness here, no rattling chandelier, no gunfire, no mention of mass graves,” she thinks. “I remind myself to breathe slowly and steadily, matching each inhale to the shadows moving across the recessed lights.”

In the quiet sanctuary of Danticat’s prose, the complexity of Magnolia’s life gently unfurls. As usual, the author eschews certainty for the glimmer of implication, the indeterminate nature of so much real experience. Why, for instance, did Magnolia ask her devoted partner for a break? — a question much debated by everyone in her large, loving, obsessively gossipy family. How does the fear of her mother’s mental illness affect her own decisions? Can she facilitate the gentrification of Little Haiti while still mourning the neighborhood’s disappearance? None of these questions can be answered or ignored, even as Magnolia struggles to suppress the terror that still itches her skin.

I asked Edwidge Danticat, “What’s the greatest challenge of writing a novel about gun violence in America?”

If we didn’t all know the ghastly, repetitive details of these mass shootings, parts of Dèy would read like satire, which may be the point of Danticat’s matter-of-fact narration. How, she seems to ask, have we grown inured to such facile affirmations in the aftermath of each killing? What kind of nation would teach its youngest children this ditty to the tune of “Frère Jacques”?

Is your door locked?
Are your lights out?
Will you hide?
You will hide.

A persistent subplot, rumbling along offstage, concerns the violent collapse of civil order in Haiti, awash with American guns. Communicating with her parents and her half-brother largely by voice messages, Magnolia sees their imperiled lives as though she’s watching them through slats in a fence. Dèy never pushes the parallel, but the bloodshed spilling across the United States every week resonates with the violence ripping through her parents’ homeland, which the U.S. president dismisses as “a shithole country.”

And if you’re paying attention to the date, you may anticipate what’s about to happen on Feb. 14, 2018, in Parkland, Florida.

But this is determinedly not a thriller about mass shootings, details of which are laced so sparingly through these pages that you’ll taste the poison without being sickened by it. This is, instead, very much a novel about persisting, about remaining alive in a world that would have us be ghosts.◆

Dèy, by Edwidge Danticat (Knopf, 256 pp., $30)
