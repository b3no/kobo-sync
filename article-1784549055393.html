The Era of The "Stage Five Clinger" is Over

The stereotype that wedding-crazy women dragged reluctant bachelors to the altar is now unbearably retro, and if anything, reversed. What changed?

Believe it or not, we had our own version of “gender discourse” in the 1990s and 2000s. And yes, I was a child during most of this era, but this type of gender discourse was visible even to people who were not terminally online, because it was all over mainstream media and the general zeitgeist.

The dominant genderslop theory of the era was that women wanted marriage (but more specifically, a wedding, the groom himself was less important) and men wanted sex. It was assumed that women and men were engaged in some kind of nonstop bartering dance where women doled out just enough sex as was necessary to acquire marriage and money, and men doled out just as much commitment as was necessary to acquire sex. Under this framework, a woman’s ideal setup might be a monogamous marriage to a rich but castrated man, who lavishes gifts upon her but doesn’t bother her with all the icky sex stuff. And for men, the platonic ideal would be a naked mattress on the floor of a storage unit, a cold beer, and a rotating harem of impossibly beautiful and sexually adventurous women, ideally many at once.

Given that both of these outcomes were unattainable, men and women married each other because it was the least-bad option. But still, a terrible trade deal for both parties, or so went the trope. Women would have to endure a gross man pawing at them for fifty years, and men—largely seen as the gender who benefited less from marriage—would have to deal with nagging, monogamy, and boredom just so they could weasel in a crumb of pussy on their birthdays. At some point, it was assumed that over time the wife would get so old and fat that even the man’s desire for sex would wane, and nobody would be getting anything out of the marriage, hence the variety of cartoons which essentially boil down to, “My bitch wife is fat.”

This was the world in which I grew up—a world that told me that in order to find a husband, I’d have to trick him, or at least make it a really good deal for him (sex on demand? threesomes? going to the strip club with him and being “cool” about it?) because no man actually wanted marriage and parenthood for the joy or fulfillment of it. Nobody explained how this trickery would be done, because even in the “old” days of the 2000s, withholding sex specifically to get a marriage proposal was not a thing anyone was actually doing. In fact, some people encouraged young women to have sex more freely and cut back on the marriage talk because men just wanted cool, chill, low maintenance girls. As I wrote before, of the prototypical “cool girl:”

She was independent, but in a way that was thoroughly unthreatening to men: she was guaranteed not to bother them.

But times have changed, or at least…the vibes and stereotype has changed. As the story now goes: marriage, once every woman’s rabid and singular focus, is now a one-way ticket to resentment, exhaustion and the “mental load,” while men, mooning over marriage and babies all day, get to bask in all the “free domestic labor.” Parenthood is a fun side quest for men looking to build a legacy as if they were in Crusader Kings and a thankless slog for women—just look at how men versus women talk about parenthood on Twitter, or even just the dynamic between Bluey’s parents. A viral video format shows a woman about to try on an engagement ring, changing her mind when she is overcome with horrific visions of domestic servitude. And the “trad” section of the manosphere surprisingly agrees with Invisible LaborTok, as they complain that modern women’s priorities (social media, frivolous travel and “girlbossing”) are wildly out of whack as they refuse to settle down and start a family—a desire which is now male-coded.

In the 2000s, men were simple creatures who just wanted sex and beer, if not for all these crazy bitches trying to marry them. In the 2020s, men are simple creatures who just want marriage and kids, if not for all these crazy bitches refusing to marry them.

But how did women go from being the ball and chain to being the ones afraid of getting saddled with one?

Some of the most compelling evidence of this cultural shift is the reality show Love is Blind. Yes, I know, go ahead and laugh, but there is one particularly interesting pattern with how women on the show signal their desirability to men: somehow, this show has filtered for the tiny segment of the American population who want ten children.

For those uninitiated, Love is Blind offers people the chance to propose to (and potentially marry) someone they’ve never seen before, based only on “blind” conversations had in a pod where the participants cannot see each other. It’s an interesting premise, and it would be a whole lot more interesting if most of the participants were not conventionally hot. But anyway, when the topic of children comes up in the pods, the female participants often signal their desirability by claiming to want “a whole basketball team” of children, even if they’re already in their mid-thirties. The Reddit sub dedicated to Love is Blind has noticed the same thing—people blithely claiming to want five, seven or ten children, despite no workable timeline for this and no concept of what it entails. As far as I can tell, the men are always overjoyed to hear this from the women. The man will ask about kids, the woman will say she wants nineteen of them, and he will smirk and sit back in his chair, write something in a notebook (perhaps “19 kids” with a smiley face) and suddenly, he’s ga-ga.

I don’t think any of these people actually want that number of children. Okay, maybe I’ll believe five, but I have my doubts about ten. I think they are knowingly using hyperbole to signal their value to the other party as one of the rare women who will birth his massive brood. But what I find striking about this is that women would not have dared to do this in 2005—it would be the most obvious form of romantic self-sabotage. If a secular (or only mildly religious) woman told a guy she wanted ten kids on the first date, or even the fifth date, he’d flee for the hills and call her a “stage five clinger” provided he had seen Wedding Crashers, a movie which also pokes fun at marriage-obsessed women. Yes, Love is Blind is different from regular dating because everyone has already expressed interest in marriage by being there, but this degree of insincere trad signaling around gigantic families feels…very modern.

Similarly, when I watched How to Lose a Guy in 10 Days, a romcom from 2003 about a magazine writer who attempts to make a man dump her within (you guessed it) ten days, one of the ways she ups the ante is by openly fantasizing about marriage and children. Guaranteed man repeller! As Olivia Petter wrote in Vogue nearly twenty years later, the movie sent her teenage self a message that to ensnare a man for marriage, you must be as nonchalant about marriage and kids as a stereotypical man. Otherwise, like the main character’s “high maintenance” clingy friend, you won’t be able to keep a man. Only the “chill” girls get marriage proposals and plans for children—for which they aren’t desperate anyway. Hardly seems fair to the clingy crazies, but that’s life!

But none of this feels like the current moment. Per Love is Blind, men supposedly all want enough children to fill an entire queue for Taylor Swift concert tickets, and the only thing in the way is no woman who will agree to this. A woman who openly promises to birth a small city-state is signaling her desirability, not her clinginess or craziness. This stereotype, true or not (I vote “not,” this would still freak out most men) is also reflected in serious policy discussions about the birth rate. Last year, I wrote about how discussions of the falling birth rate only implicate women in the “decision” to have fewer children, as if all men would want an infinite number of kids if not thwarted by their wives.

But that’s…not really the truth. According to a survey I ran last year of approximately 1,000 men and women, once couples are married and have already had at least one child, they tend to be very aligned on how many kids they want (often two or three) and it’s women, more than men, who are likely to want four or more.

I also gave respondents the option to cite their ideal number of kids, separate from their current number of kids, and men and women are almost completely aligned on what that gap would be (usually about 1.5 kids—keep in mind many participants hadn’t finished having kids yet.)

However, when filtering down to only singles (granted, smaller sample size here) there is an obvious finding—men are significantly more likely to want four or more children than women, which I’m unofficially calling the Love is Blind Effect.

You also might notice that single women are more likely than single men to want to remain childfree. This could also be selection bias—given how closely a woman’s fertility is tied to time, a woman who doesn’t want kids may have less of an incentive to marry on a particular timeline than women who do (when filtering for singles under 30, roughly equal numbers of men and women wanted to remain childfree.)

To some extent, these findings are just selection bias—when filtering for parents only, you de facto filter against the voluntarily childfree, and you filter for people who likely have similar values to their spouses. But clearly, having five or more kids is much more appealing to single men than single women. Even though the majority of single men and women want similar numbers of children, for the relatively small percentage of men who want five or more kids, they will probably not find many available women who want the same.

Meanwhile, as far as marriage goes, there is not as much of a striking difference in interest between the genders. According to Pew, 72% of never-married men want to get married someday, and the same is true of 69% of never-married women. Women are slightly more likely to be unsure, while men are slightly more likely to be against it. However, when you sort by politics, there is a more striking shift. According to a previous survey I ran, left-leaning women are more likely to believe marriage is a “bad deal” for their gender than anyone else, although if you look at the Y-axis, that’s still only 34% of left-leaning women. Most people, regardless of politics, do not believe marriage is a bad deal for their gender.

Partly, outliers (and stories about outliers) are driving the cultural narrative, both in 1999 and today. Most men were not actually pathologically commitmentphobic Mr. Bigs in the nineties—they got married, perhaps even eagerly, and despite the cartoons indicating some degree of manipulation, nobody was actually forcing them to do so. Still, a shift in the narrative happened somewhere between 2005 and 2020—an especially dark and tumultuous era in genderslop history.

I would actually point toward hookup culture as the beginning of that shift—or a symptom of that shift. I know a lot of people (usually the same people who openly enjoyed hookup culture) will insist it “was never a thing” and, they are broadly correct in the sense that hookup culture was not prevalent everywhere, and was not reflected in any kind of national data. Like the stereotypical commitmentphobic man, hookup culture was not a real thing in the sense that “everyone was doing it.” But cultural narratives are driven by stories—often the most interesting stories—and given that hookup culture was mostly an upper middle class, college educated and mostly white thing, it made sense that it would be featured prominently in media, where many of these people worked, and become part of the zeitgeist to the point where multiple romcoms were made about it, often with the woman as the less-interested party. These movies directly challenged what we had seen so far in movies like the 1989 romcom, When Harry Met Sally, which features a man abruptly losing interest in his female friend after sleeping with her, while she catches feelings, all while dreading the possibility of turning forty while single.

But what was weird about hookup culture (and what people criticize about it to this day) was that it was seen as a “feminist” thing, despite the fact that it directly played into the hands of those supposed sex-obsessed bachelors of the nineties. This was, funny enough, no accident—the point of hookup culture was to flip that When Harry Met Sally narrative. Hookup culture, like all major cultural shifts, was an overcorrection from a rigid norm. After being bombarded with messages that women are obsessed with marriage and babies and men are obsessed with sex, some feminists got fed up and said, “Hey, maybe we can be obsessed with sex too!”

This was not just a series of random stories on forums. Hanna Rosin, now a senior editor for The Atlantic, wrote in 2012 that casual sex on college campuses was an empowering revolution being pushed not by horny college boys, but by self-actualized feminist women.

Spinning the “my bitch wife dragging me to the altar” jokes on their heads, it was common for hookup-culture era women to claim not to want commitment—perhaps ironically to be the “cool girl” and to garner male approval, or more commonly, in a modern “I don’t need a man” kind of way. And many of them may have truly felt this way, because they were in college and weren’t thinking about marriage, although most of them eventually got married anyway.

It was around this time (the 2000s leading into the 2010s) that the red pill got very popular, bordering on mainstream. One thing about me is that I am a red pill scholar, and it’s embarrassingly, the one type of social movement where I can claim to have “read theory.” I’ve even read the works of one of the red pill godfathers, Rollo Tomassi, despite him once telling me to go fuck myself. What can I say, it’s a special interest. I wish it were something like ethics or climate science, but unfortunately it’s the RooshV forum posts from 2012.

Anyway, all too often people group in generic misogyny with the red pill, when the red pill is actually a pretty specific ethos. The red pill is, if anything, a bit retro and nineties-coded (Rollo Tomassi himself is fifty-seven) in the sense that the prototypical red pill success story is more of a pickup artist than a patriarch. Red pill literature is generally not about how to find the perfect chaste virgin bride, and more about how to seduce the hairdresser with fake boobs in broad daylight. There is no emphasis on the importance of making women into mothers, and most of them are unambiguously anti-marriage. And the red pill, oddly enough, takes a “feminist” stance on women’s interest in sex—that they want to have it, ideally with a guy they consider very attractive, and like men, their attraction is completely amoral and cannot be negotiated or bartered.

The purest red pill content is stuck somewhere in between the Harry Met Sally world and hookup culture—they believe women want marriage and hot dangerous sex, but frequently choose different men for these pursuits. You guessed it: those men are the alphas (sex) and betas (marriage.) In this way, the red pill enjoys the “best” of both worlds.

This era of red pill content basically did what hookup culture feminists were trying to do at the same time—bust the stereotype that women were virtuous, innocent, marriage-obsessed, and mostly asexual. Hookup culture feminists said, “Women can be dirty and horny too,” and the red pill said, “We know.”

This era of sex positivity (even if, in the red pill’s case, it wasn’t always politically correct) eventually coincided with #MeToo and what we now call “Ban Men Feminism,” where hookup culture was no longer an act of feminist rebellion, but something that men did to women, perhaps without enthusiastic consent, or in the midst of some problematic power imbalance or age gap. Again, these are just vibe shifts and I’m sure people continued hooking up in college even in 2018, but the “cool” feminist slogan had changed. It was no longer cool to brag about how you “used men for sex,” but to talk about how you didn’t want to deal with men at all. Not for marriage, not for sex. (A quandary if you are heterosexual, which many of these women were.) And indeed, it became slightly in vogue for straight women to claim they wish they were gay.

The story and stereotype had changed again, made much worse by the pandemic-driven isolation and widespread social anxiety. Women weren’t empowered cosmopolitan man-eaters nor were we dragging men to the altar. They were complaining about men thinking it was appropriate to flirt with women at run club or pickleball. Where did men go from here? On their phones, mostly, and occasionally into the goon cave, but at least from their vantage point it seemed believable that women just weren’t into marriage or sex. I recently discovered that there is an entire incel-adjacent Reddit sub devoted to the idea that even heterosexual women are not attracted to men.

But no matter what the changing trope is, it feels worthwhile to point out that the woman who wants marriage more than her male partner still exists, even if it’s not the dominant narrative. When I wrote about women whose twenties are wasted, waiting on a boyfriend who keeps putting off a proposal while insisting it’s coming eventually, lots of young women told me they were in this position. There is an entire Reddit sub dedicated to women waiting for a proposal, with 47,000 weekly visitors. But, funny enough, when my article on the topic first came out, I was told that in fact, the vast majority of men are clamoring for young marriage, and if women are running into this problem it’s because they keep dating the small number of men who aren’t. Sure, Jan.

I am not sure what story comes next. At least from the standpoint of “Internet vibes,” which I know isn’t much (give me a break, I supplied multiple graphs already, what more do you people want) it seems like both men and women are fed up with the self-imposed isolation epidemic, social media addiction, easy access to dopamine hits anywhere and anytime, endless dating app scrolls, and anxiety about speaking to anyone at all, in any context outside of Discord. As I’ve said before, being online exposes you to the worst outliers of the opposite sex, and people should do less of that (Unless they’re reading my work. Those people should STAY IN LINE, quite frankly.) Men and women are frustrated with many of the same current cultural problems, and it’s encouraging that their interests are not entirely opposed.

But while I don’t think women are souring off marriage en masse, or that heteropessimism is the default state for most women, I did get some insights into what could be going on with single women via another survey I did. In this survey, singles could essentially long-form complain about dating. After analyzing the patterns between what straight men and women said, one thing was clear: men called themselves losers and women agreed with this assessment, essentially, “they’re not sending their best.” You could argue that women’s expectations are too high, but they were not citing income (in fact, zero women did). They were talking about things largely within men’s control, even if it’s hard: unwillingness to make the first move, porn addiction, immaturity, poor social skills, poor physical fitness or shallow conversation. Is there something about a dating app focused culture that makes men less charismatic and desirable than they used to be? Almost all the issues women cited among their male peers were things that could be traced back to a lack of socialization and everyday social “practice.” In other words, to quote the red pillers of yore, a lot of young men today just need game, but have no easy way of building it outside of apps. (I’m also not talking about cold approaching women in the grocery store. You can develop game just by knowing more people and interacting spontaneously with women in social settings). If anything is wrong between men and women today, it’s this—not drastically different levels of interest in marriage and kids.

Like all the stereotypes that came before, including the “stage five clinger,” there is some kernel of truth in today’s heteropessimistic narrative, blown out of proportion by media portrayals and tropes. And just like the stage five clinger of 2005, this narrative will eventually be replaced with something else. Is Clavicular ringing in a new era of looks-obsessed men (doubt.) Will women start embracing their heterosexuality in new and unexpected ways? (My vote is for “frumpy but horny,” since that’s at least something new.) But in any case, whatever trope comes next will not speak for all or even most people, just as the previous tropes didn’t—and we can assure ourselves that no matter what we see in The Cut, most people are painfully normal, as they’ve always been.
