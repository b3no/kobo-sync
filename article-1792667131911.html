The Democrats can't wait out God

Secularization isn't enough — if the left wants a durable majority, it has to persuade religious voters

Can Democrats wait out religious people?

America is secularizing — the share of Americans who say religion is an important part of their daily lives has dropped by 17 points over the last decade, according to Gallup’s polling. As a result, one school of thought argues that secular Americans — and the progressive causes and candidates they tend to support — just need to wait for the country to get less religious.

In Free Inquiry, a journal of secular humanist opinion, Executive Director of the Richard Dawkins Foundation for Reason and Science Robyn Blumner made this argument bluntly: “While things look bleak, we have to remember that this is a long game, and demographics are on our side.” And in 2024, Jennifer Rubin, then a columnist at The Washington Post, argued that Gen Z’s declining religiosity offered promising news to Democrats.

The Argument’s polling says that’s a bad bet.

Share

If you went looking for the Democratic Party’s weakest point, you might start with education, income, or race — a lot of ink has been spilled on the educational realignment that led to white working-class voters swinging hard to the right. But I’d give you a different answer: religion.

In our last two national surveys of 3,070 registered voters combined, religion persistently emerged as one of the biggest differentiators in political views. As an example, of the voters who said religion was “not too important” or “not at all important” in their life, 48% said that they were liberals and only 18% said they were conservatives. Meanwhile, among voters who said religion was very or somewhat important to their life, those numbers flipped: Just 18% said they were liberal, while 44% identified as conservative.

Religious voters are much more conservative
Voters who say religion is important to their life are far more conservative than those who say it's not important

Here’s a prime example of how this manifests. The Make America Great Again (MAGA) movement is, by this point, radioactive among wide swaths of America, with a net favorability rating of -23. By itself, that’s already pretty bad.

But the topline reality hides how it’s really a tale of two universes. With nonreligious voters, MAGA’s net favorability is at a shocking -54. Meanwhile, among religious voters, it’s much more evenly split, sitting at -3.

Religious voters are much more favorable to MAGA
"Do you have a favorable or unfavorable opinion of the following people or institutions? - The Make America Great Again movement (MAGA)"

It’s a similar story with Donald Trump, where 47% of actively religious voters still approve of his performance in office. Among all other voters, this number is just 23%.

Religious voters are much more likely to approve of Trump
"Do you approve or disapprove of the way Donald Trump is handling his job as president?"

This phenomenon isn’t limited to the president, either. When it comes to political support on the generic ballot, Republicans win the support of 58% of religious voters but sit at just 30% among nonreligious ones.

Religious voters are very Republican
"If the 2026 general election for Congress were held today, which party's candidate would you be more likely to vote for in your district?"

In other words, the conservative bent of religious voters might be the biggest electoral Achilles heel for the left. Democrats and liberals alike are fairly dominant among people who don’t care for religion. But that doesn’t mean as much when 61% of Americans say that religion is somewhat or very important to their life.

Religion is important to most Americans
"How important is religion in your life?"

What’s more, it’s not necessarily clear that religiosity has declined severely of late.

In fact, Pew Research Center’s latest Religious Landscape Study indicates that the shift has actually stalled. If secularization resumed at a pace of roughly 1 percentage point a year (as was the case from 2007 to 2014), then nonreligious voters wouldn’t make up half the electorate until nearly 2050.

And the electoral payoff of incremental secularization is smaller than you might think — even if you held current partisan margins constant, each percentage point of secularization would likely buy Democrats roughly three-tenths of a point in vote share. In other words, a decade of waiting, under the friendliest envisionable outcomes, gets Democrats a little over three points in margin.

A competent political movement begins by working with the fundamental reality of the country as it is, rather than waiting for what it might be in the future. The prevalence of religion has declined, but our polling finds that religious voters still constitute a supermajority of the electorate. This means that in order to assemble a true and durable consensus in the country anytime soon, Democrats will probably have to do a lot better with religious voters than they do today.

Perhaps the most pressing reason for Democrats to consider this angle is that not everything is a national popular vote. On Pew’s measure of religiosity, North Carolina, Texas, Georgia, and Kansas all rank in the top third of the nation. A less religious country might eventually make those races easier, but Democrats need to persuade the people who live there now.

This doesn’t necessarily mean that the party needs to make overt faith-based appeals to voters a part of its national platform. But if they do want to take advantage of Trump’s deepening unpopularity to compete in redder states that consistently evade Democrats, they probably do need to do something different from what they’re doing now; they’re still seeing their vote share trail Trump’s disapproval by an outsized amount with moderate and conservative religious voters.

Trump disapproval far outpaces Republican share among religious voters
Among moderate and conservative religious voters, Trump disapproval outpaces the Democratic generic ballot share by an outsized amount

Any improvement here likely begins with recognizing that many of these people have deep and genuine faith-based opposition to a lot of tenets central to the Democratic platform — and winning them will require compromise and common ground, rather than pounding the table and hoping for victory. (Just as liberals are not temporarily embarrassed communists, religious conservatives are not temporarily embarrassed liberals!)

The party might also benefit from recruiting more candidates like Sen. Raphael Warnock, whose profile as the pastor of Ebenezer Baptist Church in Atlanta helped springboard him to two Senate victories in Georgia, or senate candidate Adam Hamilton, whose candidate strength and profile as a United Methodist megachurch pastor have put Kansas into play despite an R+7 generic ballot in the state.

This wouldn’t actually be as new as you might think. The funny thing is, Democrats already have a long history of relying on religious institutions to win elections.

Does religion always help Republicans?

While our data is fairly clear on how religious voters are more conservative across basically every group, it’s not necessarily true that they’re always more Republican.

For white voters, religiosity (and, specifically, Christianity) generally correlates with a strong Republican lean, but for Black voters, it’s correlated with a Democratic one. Though our sample size is small, we see the same effect among Hispanic voters as we do with white voters — religious Hispanics are much more Republican than nonreligious ones.

How does Christianity correlate with vote choice?
"If the 2026 general election for Congress were held today, which party's candidate would you be more likely to vote for in your district?"

In other words, if you know that a white voter is Christian, the odds of them voting Republican skyrocket. And while Black voters are mostly Democratic in general, knowing a Black voter is Christian would make you even more certain that they’re a Democrat.

A big reason for this is the power of the Black church. Deeply ingrained in southern Democratic politics, this institution has served as a de facto political machine for Black voters for generations. Take a look at any Democratic primary in the region and you’ll see candidate after candidate flocking to Black churches to speak, earn support, and make their voices heard.

This form of organized politics helps Democrats punch way above their ideological weight, especially in the Deep South, because conservative Black voters are extremely religious. And the power of the Black church as a political machine helps explain why they are so much more Democratic than their conservative white and Hispanic counterparts.

Black conservatives are asymmetrically Democratic
"If the 2026 general election for Congress were held today, which party's candidate would you be more likely to vote for in your district?"

This sort of finding does give me pause on how the future of the Black vote actually looks — given that younger Black voters are much less religious than older Black ones, it’s entirely plausible that Black support for the Democratic Party weakens as older, more religious voters age out of the electorate. After all, across a full year of polling, we’re certainly seeing that younger Black voters are much less Democratic than older Black voters are.

Old Black voters are far more Democratic than younger Black voters are
"If the 2026 general election for Congress were held today, which party's candidate would you be more likely to vote for in your district?"

I’m not going to go out on a limb and make a prediction on whether that holds. But I do think it’s perhaps the most interesting political pattern to observe over the next decade.
