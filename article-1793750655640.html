On The Line: Why can’t our military just buy stuff?

Why do procurement projects take so long, cost so much, and so often fail to deliver what was originally promised?

Line Editor
Nov 3
 
READ IN APP
 

In this episode of On The Line, host Jen Gerson speaks with Jeff Collins, director of the Palmer Institute for Canadian Leadership in Charlottetown, P.E.I., about Canada’s military procurement problems, the country’s evolving national security strategy, and whether our newfound enthusiasm for getting our elbows up against the Americans makes any sense when it comes to national defence.

This episode is brought to you by BioCanRx. We’ve been partnering with federally-funded Canadian not-for-profit BioCanRx to learn how made-in-Canada immunotherapies offer new ways to beat cancer. This month we’re addressing common questions, like: are immunotherapy technologies dual-use?

While many immunotherapies are personalized using a patient’s own cells, the research and manufacturing infrastructure behind them can produce other important products, like vaccines. The people behind those research, manufacturing, and clinical delivery processes are also extremely capable and versatile. Greater support for translational research and clinical trials would help grow this workforce and build capabilities that extend well beyond cancer. In a health emergency, that capacity could also help Canada respond quickly to urgent needs for new treatments and vaccines.

Thanks to BioCanRx for continuing to partner with The Line to share this information. Visit BioCanRx.com to learn more.

Collins draws on his experience working at the Department of National Defence and elsewhere in the federal public service to explain why Canada has such a difficult time buying the equipment its military needs. Why do procurement projects take so long, cost so much, and so often fail to deliver what was originally promised? And what would actually have to change to make the system work?

The episode is also brought to you by the Forest Products Association of Canada.

For years, the Forest Products Association of Canada has called for action to reduce regulatory duplication and create greater certainty for mills, factories, and investment.

We’re encouraged to see the federal government taking action to make Canada’s regulatory system more coordinated and predictable, removing made-in-Canada barriers that have held the sector back. This is an important step for forestry and for communities across the country that depend on the sector.

Now, we need to move from promises to production. Canadian jobs, stronger supply chains, and new opportunities for communities from coast to coast to coast depend on it.

Canada’s forest sector is ready to do its part. Learn more at FPAC.ca.

Jen and Jeff also discuss Canada’s national security strategy and the growing pressure to reduce our military dependence on the United States. Political enthusiasm for greater Canadian independence is one thing, but the practical realities of continental defence, military integration and procurement are quite another. How realistic is an “elbows up” approach to our most important defence partner?

This episode is also brought to you by Cameco. Building Canada’s energy future means creating jobs for Canadian workers. That’s why the AP1000 reactor is the right choice for Canada: it is already operating and ready to deliver the power we need. Choosing the AP1000 means a reactor built by Canadians, creating skilled jobs in construction, fabrication and the trades while keeping more investment and economic benefits here at home. If we are serious about creating Canadian jobs and strengthening our economy, the choice is clear. To learn more, visit ap1000.cameco.com.

It’s a conversation about bureaucracy, military readiness, national sovereignty, and whether Canada can finally get serious about equipping its Armed Forces for a much more dangerous world. We hope you enjoyed the episode, and as always, like and subscribe.
