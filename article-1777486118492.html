Tom Perrotta and the Summer That Broke a Boy

In “Ghost Town,” a writer finally confronts the grief he avoided for 50 years.

Ron Charles
Apr 28
 
READ IN APP
 

The Tuesday Review

Tom Perrotta is best known for his comic novel Election — and for anticipating by 25 years the advent of Trump’s press secretary Karoline Leavitt, a comparison that hardly seems fair, since Tracy Flick sometimes tells the truth.

But most of Perrotta’s fiction is rendered in a different palette, the sad light of Sunday evenings in winter. Think of the yawning ache of Mrs. Fletcher, the unstaunched ennui of Little Children, or the unfathomable absences in The Leftovers. Victorians might have been baffled by Perrotta’s suburbia, but his melancholy would have fit them like a black glove.

Ghost Town drops deeper into Perrotta’s well of woe than he’s gone before. It’s a novel about grief — not the kind of grief you bend your life around but the kind so traumatic that you wall it off like a nuclear waste site, a place to be forgotten and avoided forever.

The narrator is a writer named Jay Perry, who, like Perrotta, was raised in an Italian American Catholic family in New Jersey. After college, Jay published several works of literary fiction that few people read before he inadvertently created the cheesy Ghost Teacher series for kids, which led to a lucrative children’s TV show. When we meet Jay at the start of the novel, the show is over, his writing career is stuck, and he’s suffering from depression that lingers like a sinus infection. He idly wonders how his obituary will begin.

Jay’s story of middling success tainted with the fetid scent of selling out could have made its own fine Tom Perrotta novel. But the heart of Ghost Town beats 50 years earlier before Jay — then known as little Jimmy — went to high school. These recollected sections are cast in the third person, as though he were using long tongs to handle still-hot coals. It’s an act of deliberate retrospection, an attempt to finally remember, record, and possibly understand what happened to him in 1974.

“I’d left that place a long time ago and I’d never gone back,” he writes. “I’d drawn a circle around my hometown — cordoned it off from the rest of my life — and that had served me pretty well for a very long time. Or who knows, maybe it hadn’t.”

His ambivalent attitude toward the value of reminiscence hovers over these pages, which progress with the deliberate care of someone searching for broken glass in the dark.

And who can blame him for being wary? Jimmy’s memories begin when his perfectly happy family is shattered by the loss of his mother. Too young to be informed of the diagnosis as her illness progressed, Jimmy is shocked to hear of her death during a Little League championship game. Somehow, the report of this tragedy spreads through the stands first. Everyone claps as he leaves the field, the way they do when an injured player limps off. Later, a sweet classmate sends a greeting card with a $10 gift certificate to Burger King: “I’m sorry about your mom!”

This is Perrotta at his finest, working in that cramped space between crying and laughing. He has a keen eye for those rituals of bereavement that provide meager direction to adults and leave a 13-year-old boy utterly baffled about what he should think, say, or do. Although their kindly priest drops by the house to offer prayers, the visits offer little comfort to Jimmy. “When it wasn’t sad it was creepy,” he thinks, “and when it wasn’t creepy it was boring.”

I asked Tom Perrotta, “How does grief distort adolescence in ways we only understand later?”

As the narrative stays tightly focused on Jimmy, we only see his father on the periphery, “a bleary-eyed guy with nothing to say, the one who couldn’t wait to get out of the house ... away from the memories he didn’t know how to live with.”

If you know Perrotta’s work, you know how effectively he can give motion to the suspended life of sorrow. This awful summer develops as subtly as twilight fades into night. Mourning that would stall another author’s prose is here gently animated by continually reopening a wound. Left adrift in that fog of despair, Jimmy spends a lot of time getting high and eventually falls in with another grieving kid who owns a Ouija board. Sure, the Church promises eternal life, but the spirit world delivers text messages.

For Jimmy, it’s a summer of illicit and utterly bewildering events. “He couldn’t understand what his father and sister had been thinking,” Perrotta writes, “how they’d left him to his own devices at a time when he needed them the most.” His mother’s death knocks him out of sync with his school friends, too. When one of them asks, “What the hell happened to you?” Jimmy just shrugs “because he didn’t even know where to start.”

That isolating quality of loss, its ability to make us feel set aside in a Fabergé egg of pain, is part of what Perrotta captures so well in Ghost Town. Jimmy may feel as though he’s suffered the worst calamity that can befall him, but the world of 1974, with all its cultural pathologies, keeps spinning — and he won’t remain untouched no matter how much he’s already suffered.

The ending is an absolute wallop, a tragedy hidden in the grass so subtly that no adolescent could have seen it coming, and no adult could ever survive it intact.◆

Ghost Town, by Tom Perrotta (Scribner, 288 pp., $28)
