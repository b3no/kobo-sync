An A.I. legislator running a cost campaign

Manny Rutinel is one of the few politicians to have spent his career trying to govern A.I. In the race to represent Colorado’s swingiest district, he’s not talking about it much.

One month before Election Day, Democrat Manny Rutinel spent the afternoon in his campaign office north of Denver — a collection of rooms in a nondescript Longmont strip mall decorated with mismatched chairs, photos of the candidate in a cowboy hat and with his mom, and notes of encouragement tacked to the walls.

That day, the gas at the station next door sold for upwards of $4.20, and diesel was running more than $6 a gallon.

“I think people see that every single day, and they’re like, ‘this is unacceptable,’” Rutinel told me, explaining that the residents of Colorado’s Eighth Congressional District — who will become his constituents if he wins his race — are furious about the everyday costs of living, from grocery prices to gas to insurance premiums to housing. He ties the first to tariffs, the second to the Iran war, and the third to the expiration of enhanced Affordable Care Act credits.

One family told him their plan on the Colorado insurance marketplace went from $500 a month in 2025 to $2,000.

“I think folks are extremely upset by what Donald Trump and Gabe Evans have done with their power,” he said of his opponent, the one-term incumbent Republican.

The race to represent Colorado’s swingiest district — which stretches from the left-leaning northern part of Denver’s metro area to conservative, rural Weld County and is home to the largest Hispanic population of any district in the state — has become one of the country’s most competitive. By Rutinel’s account, Republican-aligned groups, including the Congressional Leadership Fund (C.L.F.) and super PACs tied to President Trump and Elon Musk, are spending more against him than they are against any other House candidate. Certainly, the race is, by all accounts, one of the most expensive for the House this cycle.

His theory about Republicans’ focus on his race is that the incumbent has been a reliable vote for House Speaker Mike Johnson and C.L.F. wants to reward that loyalty. The district, which was created after 2021 redistricting, is known for volatility that’s unique in Colorado — recalculating precinct votes within the post-2021 boundaries finds that Barack Obama won it twice, then it flipped to Trump, then to Joe Biden, and then back to Trump in 2024.

Like most Democratic challengers running this cycle, Rutinel’s campaign is laser-focused on the cost of living. But he is also one of the few candidates in the country whose prior career in office largely focused on issues many are only just coming around to: A.I. governance and the housing supply.

Neck and neck

On Friday, for the second time in two months, Speaker Johnson visited Colorado to campaign for Evans.

“I’m absolutely convinced that he will defy history and win the district again,” Johnson said at an event for Evans. A recent poll of 400 likely voters from a Republican sponsor had Evans up two percentage points with a 4.9 point margin of error. The race, it seems, is neck and neck.

Evans, an Army veteran, former law-enforcement officer, and grandson of a Mexican immigrant who served in World War II, flipped the seat in 2024 by a margin of 0.74 percentage points. During his time in office, Evans has diverged from Trump: He pushed to maintain wind and solar tax credits amid federal cuts (before voting to unwind them), opposed increasing beef imports (the district includes significant ranching interests), and urged ICE to prioritize removing criminals rather than undocumented immigrants with clean records. He has referenced his bipartisan record on the Problem Solvers Caucus and, more recently, his work on bills regulating data centers and A.I. chatbots, the Colorado Sun reports.

Still, while Evans has differed with Trump on some issues, he has remained steadfast in his allegiance to the president in many instances when other Republicans have broken with him, the Sun reports. He voted for the SAVE America Act, opposed unwinding Trump’s tariffs on Canadian imports, and voted against resolutions to force Trump to end the war in Iran and one to send aid to Ukraine. He supported the One Big Beautiful Bill Act, which cut federal spending on welfare programs like Medicaid and food stamps, affecting an estimated more than 700,000 Coloradans. His support for that legislation has become a key target for Democrats looking to unseat the representative.

Rutinel explained that Evans’s incumbency advantage — an advantage that appears to be more of a disadvantage nationally for both parties during this midterm cycle — and party loyalty are much less important to the residents of this district than prices are.

The affordability argument is a standard pitch for Democrats running for office in 2026, but for Rutinel, it’s personal.

Running without a paycheck

Rutinel was raised by a single mom in Florida, on Medicaid and food stamps. He recalls being 10 years old, filling out the forms for those federal programs at his kitchen table. Two decades later, the young, Latino, Spanish-speaking former McDonald’s worker is running for office — without a paycheck. He said the campaign is a 75-hour-a-week job and that he isn’t taking “a penny” from it. Last month, Rutinel resigned from his seat in the state house — to which he was appointed in 2023 — to focus on this campaign.

“I have a lot of traits that are uncommon in the current congressional landscape, and I think that’s what people are really excited about: how personal this is for me, and how much I’m interested in fighting for the folks that grew up the way I did and are currently working the way that I have,” Rutinel said.

The 31-year-old candidate said that his appeal as a unique candidate may also be due to his age, which certainly has not hurt his ability to build an unusually national profile for a first-time U.S. House candidate in Colorado. His campaign capitalizes on his physical fitness, most recently running an advertisement that includes slow-motion video of him working out. His Instagram, which has more than half a million followers, features the candidate in soft-washed T-shirts and loose button-downs with a dimpled smile, doing pull-ups, swinging a baseball bat, shooting a basketball, and addressing crowds in a mellow tone.

But his age has also made the race more difficult, Rutinel said.

“There’s so many barriers for young people,” he said. “Running for office can be a really expensive ordeal. And the networks that we have are often other young people who are struggling to make ends meet, maybe living with their parents, seeing their rent go through the roof.”

Despite his own slim savings account, he said the stakes were too high to sit this one out.

“I’ve been able to make it through even worse economic situations with my background, so I know that I’ll be able to get through this and survive.”

The affordability resume

Rutinel’s limited legislative tenure has produced a long list of small, concrete wins. He helped eliminate discriminatory occupancy limits — “legalizing renting,” in his words — and passed a reciprocal food-truck permit that he said saves each truck about $3,000 per month, since they do not have to apply for a new permit in each locality where they operate. Among his accomplishments, he pointed to his sponsorship of an expanded state Earned Income Tax Credit and the Family Affordability Tax Credit, which, combined with other expanded tax credits, helped cut childhood poverty in Colorado by 40 percent, making it the state with the lowest rate of childhood poverty in the country according to a 2025 study.

As a state legislator, Rutinel also had an early focus on housing affordability and described himself as one of the few renters to walk the halls of the Colorado Capitol. He also has an economics background, having worked as an economist at the Army Corps of Engineers while earning a master of science in applied economics from Johns Hopkins University, which he received in 2018.

He said his approach to housing is “all of the above,” explaining that ensuring that working people can afford to rent or buy requires comprehensive supply- and demand-side action. He co-sponsored a 2025 law to create a $50 million revolving fund the state treasurer can use to lower construction and mortgage costs, reducing the prices of for-sale housing and providing additional funds to first-time and low- to moderate-income homebuyers. On the supply side, Rutinel said he has co-sponsored bills to cut red tape and enact permit and zoning reforms. He argues that Congress can, and should, do both.

On federal zoning preemption, Rutinel said he was skeptical that anyone is seriously considering it and that he would prefer to use federal funds to reward localities that reform. He envisions a fund for transit-oriented communities, where cities with the potential for transit could be financially rewarded for permitting dense housing along corridors.

Changing positions, consistent message

Rutinel’s last full-time job was as an environmental attorney. He graduated from Yale Law School in 2022 and moved to Colorado to work for Earthjustice. At an Animal Legal Defense Fund student conference, he described his job at Earthjustice as suing large meat corporations “for all their worth.” His history as an animal-rights activist and a vegan (he has since begun eating meat) has drawn some scrutiny from critics who believe that he shouldn’t represent a district that’s home to one of the country’s largest slaughterhouses.

Since running for the House, Rutinel’s views on other issues like banning fracking, canceling student debt, and enacting a single-payer health-care system have also shifted, the Colorado Sun reports. Having supported each of those positions in 2025, he told the Sun in May during his primary that he opposes a fracking ban, supports a public health-care option rather than Medicare for All, and does not support canceling all student debt — all more moderate views that make him a much more viable candidate in a moderate district.

Rutinel told the Sun that his core values have not changed and that his legislative record reflects his dogged fight for the working class. He told me that throughout the entire election, in both the primary and one month out from Election Day, his messaging has stayed consistent: “It’s always been about costs and how Donald Trump and Gabe Evans are making life impossible.”

While his moderation on some issues might be points for the candidate in the eyes of the Democratic establishment, Rutinel also sells himself as an establishment irritant — points on the board from the party’s anti-establishment left. He said he supports congressional term limits and a ban on congressional stock trading, and he takes no corporate PAC money, positions he said have rubbed some within the party the wrong way.

The issue nobody’s voting on

On policy, what really separates the candidate from others running for federal office, though, is his early work on the issues that have become urgent in 2026. On artificial intelligence, Rutinel’s record stretches further back than almost any other candidate I can think of. He called it “the defining issue of our time.”

Data centers, which seven out of 10 Americans oppose in their own communities according to Gallup, have come up among the residents of the district, where a proposal to construct the state’s largest one has been unanimously approved. Rutinel said voters worry about utility costs, the way that data centers might change their neighborhoods’ character, and their drain on natural resources. Voters also feel wary about the nondisclosure agreements that he said keep communities from having a say.

“They feel uneasy about the fact that we’re barreling down this future without guardrails, whether it’s data centers or A.I.,” he said. “What I hope is that technology works for humans, not the other way around.”

Rutinel began working on building policy guardrails for A.I. during his time in the state legislature. He co-sponsored a 2024 bill that required consumer protections for A.I., which passed but then was repealed and replaced. In 2025, he introduced a bill with a Republican co-prime sponsor to protect whistleblowers at frontier A.I. labs, specifically protecting those who report catastrophic risks. The bill made it out of committee with bipartisan support but met resistance he attributed to corporate lobbying.

He is looking forward to expanding his A.I. agenda at the federal level if he’s elected. He said the federal government must pass transparency requirements so it can investigate safety incidents, require pre-release testing for major model updates (analogizing to testing for new drugs and vaccines), and enact liability reform so companies bear the costs if their models either cause catastrophic harm or produce a near miss.

But, Rutinel explained, liability alone is not enough. For harms on the scale that worries him, he thinks that even the world’s wealthiest companies lack the assets and incentives to absorb the cost.

When I asked whether labs should stop developing their models, he said there must be “a coordinated pacing of the frontier” among large corporations in the U.S. and China.

As A.I. safety becomes both more urgent and more mainstream, Rutinel described his reasons for taking the issue seriously over the course of his legislative career:

“We’re barreling down a future where our privacy is going to be at risk, our children are going to be at risk, like our families potentially will be physically at risk if we don’t put the right guardrails in, and you know, nobody’s voting for this,” he said. “These are just large corporate executives that are deciding the fate of humanity’s future without allowing democracy to engage in that process, and that seems undemocratic, unpatriotic, un-American.”

The race is likely to come down to gas prices, health premiums, and Trump’s slipping approval ratings, not A.I. frontier-model governance. Still, the candidate is, and has been, holding both insurance premiums and unregulated A.I. as threats to Americans and their futures.
