Woke 1: Behaviors, Not Positions

Woke 1 is not defined by specific policies, but by tactics around those policies

Cartoons Hate Her
Aug 17
∙
Paid
 
READ IN APP
 

“Woke 1” is in the discourse again, mostly sparked by AOC laughing off Francesca Hong’s crazy since-deleted tweets calling to cancel Thanksgiving, by saying, lightheartedly, “Woke 1 was crazy.” She is not the first to say this. In fact, the year 2020 has become shorthand for a rigid, dogmatic, unhinged, very-online social justice movement also described derisively as “Woke 1.”

But as people joke about Woke 1 being crazy, others across the political spectrum have questions. In fact, it’s the same question, posed in different ways: which policies define Woke 1? The Right will say, “If you truly believe Woke 1 was so crazy, which precise positions do you now disavow?” And the Left will say, “If you think Woke 1 was so crazy, pray tell which positions were really that crazy?” The Right’s implicit accusation: you’re hiding the fact that you’re still Woke 1. The Left’s implicit accusation: you’re not Woke 1, ergo, you’re hiding some horrendous bigotry.

But they’re both making a major error by assuming Woke 1 was defined by its policies at all.

First, yes, Woke 1 was associated with unpopular leftist positions such as the now-radioactive “abolish the police” or prison abolition movements. This abolition rallying cry was hardly isolated to law enforcement. We had “abolition of the family,” and, even before the pandemic, a call to abolish restaurants. In a move that seems a bit too on-the-nose, they also wanted to abolish psychiatry. Oh, but don’t worry—Woke 1 didn’t want to abolish everything. They liked some stuff. For example, they made sure to defend looting as a form of political protest (in a 2020 book appropriately titled, In Defense of Looting).

But realistically, most “Woke 1” people did not believe all of these things. Plenty had less absurd political views, but leveraged particular tactics to intimidate others into no-questions-asked compliance. And that is what really defines Woke 1—not the precise positions, but the way people were coerced into adopting those positions (or pretending to) and the way illiberal, dogmatic and grievance-based conduct made it impossible to respectfully dissent or disagree without being kicked out of a rapidly shrinking tent.

This is not something unique to the 2020 Left. Leftists can hold their positions and still not embody this obnoxious-as-shit ethos. And on the other hand, even a right-winger has the capacity to start behaving in a way befitting Woke 1 (and plenty have, such as the officials who, even in 2026, are still scared to admit that Trump lost the 2020 election.) It’s the behaviors, not the positions.

But what were these behaviors?

The first rule of Woke 1 conduct is fairly obvious, and is honestly more of a baseline framework: every conflict or issue had to be framed in the context of the identities of the people involved. If two people got into an altercation, the person who held more systemic privilege via their immutable identities was in the wrong. Identities became shields to be deployed against accusations of wrongdoing. (I once saw a woman invoke her past as an “indigenous former sex worker” as a trump card to win an argument completely unrelated to either of those identities.) Criticizing or disagreeing with someone became risky if that person was perceived to be less privileged than you were. Consider, perhaps, that it’s actually racist to comment on Nicki Minaj’s history getting into feuds with other pop stars and not take her seriously as a political activist (This take didn’t age especially well, considering Nicki Minaj has since described herself as “Donald Trump’s number one fan.”)

The judgment toward the same basic scenario could easily change depending on the identities of who was involved. Let’s take: woman feels afraid of misogynistic man. Is she justified? Um…maybe? To decide on the verdict, Woke 1 must know the approximate marginalization levels of both people involved. Plenty of people validated the idea that a woman was in her right to feel “unsafe” just because she found out a man in her proximity voted for Trump and watched Joe Rogan. But that same woman would be derided as a “Karen” if she expressed feeling unsafe around disturbed homeless men (perhaps less privileged than her, but significantly stronger) screaming sexual or violent threats at her. (Remember, just avoid eye contact! Being aggressively sexually harassed by deranged men twice your size is a fun quirk of living in a city! If your kids witness this, it’s a teachable moment about their privilege!)

Disparaging a privileged identity could even be a tactic for a relatively privileged person to save their own ass, as shown by the tenured, white and well-off men in academia who reportedly bragged about refusing to hire white men. There was the option for men like this to hand their own jobs over to a woman of color, but they still wanted to enjoy their own high degree of privilege while getting pats on the back for being progressive. We also saw this topsy-turvy idea of privilege with successful writers taking aim at literal high school students who, for being conservative, were still fair game within their idea of “punching up.”

Note that not all the people employing this tactic wanted to do wacky Portlandia things like abolishing the family. Many of them probably held pretty normal, boilerplate liberal views. At worst, they were perhaps the type of people to hated “capitalism” in the sense that capitalism became code for having to work at all, but would probably have fled the country if an actual communist revolution took place. Again: it’s the behaviors, not the positions.

Another facet of Woke 1 conduct was the well-known purity test. At different times in Woke 1 history, there has been a go-to extreme political position or rhetorical question, with which one must agree or provide the correct answer (usually denoted with a simple or splashy motto—a right-wing version would be constantly asking people “how many genders” there are or “what is a woman?”) lest they be on the “wrong side of history.” Eventually, it became less about the position itself and more about the sport of intimidating people into pretending to agree with it.

A good example here would be the insistence that if you really cared about police brutality or systemic racism (as many normal people do) that you needed to take the extreme position of police or prison abolition—positions not actually held by most people of color. (In fact, a Gallup survey of Black Americans done in 2020 of all years, showed that 81% wanted the same or more police presence in their neighborhoods, even if they also supported police reform.) Many of the bullying attempts didn’t even create any real change other than making the cause look ridiculous—take, for example, the group of mostly-white BLM protestors who demanded random patrons of an outdoor restaurant (including young children) raise their fists in solidarity, only to harass and scream at a woman who did not agree with this tactic and refused to raise her fist. (This woman later claimed to support BLM, but did not approve of the harassment and intimidation.) Also, not to be that person but they were screaming in a stranger’s face, wearing flimsy surgical masks, during a pandemic.

Given that most regular people, even the people who supposedly stood to benefit from police abolition, didn’t actually want such a thing to come to fruition, my going theory is that most prominent people who said these things were actually just being pressured into it. Of course, this tactic didn’t always work: famously, during the 2020 BLM protests, Minneapolis Mayor Jacob Frey refused to capitulate to a group of protestors who demanded he commit to completely abolishing the Minneapolis police force. Meanwhile, other left-wing politicians claimed to support, at the very least, defunding the police, and are now doing damage control because they probably never really believed this in the first place. (Or did they? Were they capitulating then, or now?) Of course, that doesn’t mean everyone is absolved for saying these things just because they were driven by (bad) political strategy and a lack of integrity as opposed to genuine belief—just that I believe this intimidation tactic is why objectively ridiculous positions briefly looked like things you’d be ostracized for not adopting.

It’s easy to laugh off all of this as too-online paranoia, but if it were too-online and not actually happening, why would actual, real-life politicians be so deluded to imagine these social movements (supposedly conjured up by paranoid centrist pundits online) and then summarily change their positions a few years later?

Like most of Woke 1 conduct, this intimidation tactic is not unique to the 2020 Left. Many social movements across the political spectrum have leveraged some kind of purity test around an extreme belief, condemning anyone who did not take the maximal position, even if the actual number of people who agree with that position is minimal. Take, for example, abortion. Most people have pretty moderate views on abortion, and even many pro-life people believe exceptions should be made in cases of rape, fatal anomalies, and life of the mother. But many too-online pro-life advocates will require that you take a “no exceptions, ever” stance (very unpopular in the real world, by the way) and that if you don’t, you are actually a huge fan of “post-birth abortion.” (This, of course, also happens on the Left around the same topic. Let’s just say things did not go well when I said that I found flippant abortion jokes, or sassy cakes celebrating abortion, distasteful. Any nuance here, on either side, is not met well.)

Then, of course, there’s my least favorite part of Woke 1 (although admittedly, it’s hard to pick just one, it’s like perusing a buffet of different varieties of moldy peaches) which is the counterproductive and grievance-focused nature of it all, which ultimately alienated plenty of people who might otherwise agree with leftist or liberal views.

While I know many people today still claim that cancel culture “never existed,” it obviously did, because of people lost their jobs or reputations over harmless jokes overheard by a stranger or even completely imagined transgressions. And no, this was not just a “too online” phenomenon. At a company where I worked in 2021, we were all instructed to heavily audit our social media for any old jokes that might offend someone who might tag our employer and force the company to fire us. See also: David Shor—fired after a disgruntled Twitter user tagged his employer at the time—who didn’t actually tweet anything bigoted, and still somehow made his coworkers feel “unsafe” after posting a study on electoral responses to violent versus nonviolent protest.

You may be tempted to point out that some cancelled people did quite well for themselves after the fact, so maybe cancel culture wasn’t real. But surely, even if cancellation really was a boon for people’s careers, there’s still plenty to critique. Most obviously, if canceling someone just made them more successful, then it was a terrible strategy! But I think we all know that most of the time, it did not have this effect. And nobody could agree on exactly on what the length of a cancellation-exile should be, such as this comment I found about Alison Roman’s attempt to speak in public again a “short three years” after being cancelled for racistly “punching down” (ie: criticizing the brand strategies of Chrissy Teigen and Marie Kondo.)

Seems like if she were "canceled" we wouldn't be hearing from her 3 short years later. Maybe cancel culture is made up and being used as an excuse to sell this racist lady's b.s. to a bunch of gullible idiots.

Even if it never went so far as permanent life-ruining (and, to be fair, it often didn’t) the way Woke 1 people interacted with anyone who even lightly disagreed with them could not possibly have been done with the intention of wanting to build a coalition or engage in reasonable debate. I’m reminded of a Twitter pile-on I experienced in 2021, when I said that if you tell romantically unsuccessful men to “get off the apps and meet people in real life” but then demonize every real-life attempt to find a girlfriend (including wanting to date your friends, coworkers, strangers, or members of a hobby club, gym or shared institution) that you were giving them no possible way forward.

What I said was, obviously, true. Most of the people who replied—men and women—agreed with me too! But there was a vocal group of women who accused me of being “rapey” and “going to bat for poor, poor oppressed men.” Did these people genuinely think anyone was going to see this conduct and decide to join their team? Did they think even one singular mentally healthy straight man (or straight woman who enjoyed dating men) would be on their side? I haven’t run the numbers, but last I checked, I don’t think any social movement has succeeded which alienated upwards of 70% of the population, and even that estimate is extremely generous.

It makes you wonder: if a right-wing operative was trying to make the Left look as kooky as possible and alienate as many people as possible, how would they instruct everyone to behave? Presumably, they’d encourage perpetual fights over collecting Infinity Stones of marginalized identities, constant witch hunts around old off-color jokes, and bullying people into pretending to agree with increasingly ridiculous things, only to then turn around and say, “Nobody ever did any of that, but also, it’s good that they did.”

And now, a meta bonus: the idea that any critique of anything regarding Woke 1 is tantamount to secretly being three Donald Trumps in a trench coat. In fact, someone recently leveled that accusation at me for lightly poking fun of Francesca Hong’s anti-Thanksgiving tweet in an issue of Many Such Takes, even though last I checked she was running in a primary against Democrat David Crowley, not in a general election against Doug Mastriano. This is, unfortunately, just more evidence of my last point. Minor disagreements within the same political faction were frequently repackaged as complete defection and punished accordingly. If you thought “visiting Hawaii on vacation is genocide” was a crazy statement, you might as well have just gotten a swastika tattoo and called it a day. If you thought that violent crime was bad, you probably wanted to “lock up all poor people of color,” which also implies the offensive belief that all poor people of color are criminals.

But as we laugh off the craziness of Woke 1—and I agree there’s plenty to laugh about—we must remember that the conduct of this movement is not an isolated thing, or is it specific to any political party. This is not to give the people behind Woke 1 an excuse, but rather to say that when people ask, “Which positions do you disavow now?” They’re asking the wrong question. Someone can hold plenty of Woke 1 positions and still not actually be doing Woke 1. And someone can hold zero Woke 1 positions but embody a particularly repugnant Woke 1 mindset.

The issue is not holding bad positions—we can debate positions. We can put bad positions up for a vote, and if they’re truly unpopular and ridiculous, they’ll lose. The problem is when you aren’t allowed to question these positions at all, even when you know almost nobody supports them. Woke 1 is not about abolishing the police—it’s about the illiberal and dogmatic behavior that pushed people into pretending to agree with such a thing, and the fact that anyone tolerated that behavior in the first place.
