Republicans see a light at the end of the tunnel. It’s a train.

The last model update is here.

As Election Day approaches, Republicans are beginning to see a light at the end of the tunnel. Unfortunately for them, that light increasingly resembles an oncoming train.

Election modeling by The Argument and Split Ticket has long forecast this outcome. And now, on the day before the election, disaster looks hard to avoid for the Republican Party. In the Senate, where the odds of a Democratic takeover once looked fantastical, Democrats now have a 77% chance of flipping the chamber, with a projected gain of six seats. If that comes to pass, Democrats will hold 53 seats to Republicans’ 47.

Split Ticket's 2026 Senate Model

The House has long been projected to flip to Democrats. Some once thought midcycle gerrymanders in Texas and Florida would give Republicans a real shot at holding it, but the new maps haven’t insulated the GOP nearly enough.¹ As a result, Democrats have a 92% chance of flipping the House of Representatives, with a projected net gain of 24 seats.

A seat-by-seat view of Split Ticket's 2026 House model.

Republicans’ Senate odds have devolved rapidly, and it’s worth walking through the map in some detail.

How the Senate got away from Republicans

North Carolina’s Senate seat is the most likely on the map to flip. Former Gov. Roy Cooper has opened up a lead large enough for both parties to triage the race well ahead of the election. Our model rates his chances of winning at 98%. The real question is whether Cooper’s margin of victory will exceed incumbent Sen. Jon Ossoff’s in Georgia.

In Texas, James Talarico continues to hold on to a lead, even in the face of a fire hose of Republican spending bankrolled by Elon Musk. Talarico remains a favorite to flip the Lone Star State blue for the first time in over 30 years, thanks to a historically terrible opponent in state Attorney General Ken Paxton, plus a Hispanic rebellion against the GOP. As a result, our final model gives Talarico an 81% chance of winning, with the race rated Likely Democratic.²

His odds have only increased as the cycle has gone on. Several high-quality pollsters find a pretty sizable Talarico lead in its final survey — YouGov had him up by 4, CNN/SSRS found him leading by 6, and the latest New York Times/Siena College poll found Talarico leading by 7.

Megachurch pastor Adam Hamilton, the Democrat challenging incumbent Republican Sen. Roger Marshall in Kansas, has led consistently in polls over the last month. What should have been a safe Republican hold is now looking like a potential flip, thanks largely to a series of scandals: Questions have swirled about whether Marshall actually lives in Kansas or Florida, whether he’s using taxpayer funds to run his campaign, and whether he sued patients at his OB-GYN practice for small, unpaid debts.

As late as a few days ago, our model thought this race was a coin flip. But with the latest high-quality polls from CNN/SSRS and The New York Times/Siena College showing a clear Hamilton lead, the model now predicts Hamilton is a narrow favorite to win a Kansas U.S. Senate seat for the Democrats for the first time since 1932.

In Iowa — a state 3 points bluer than Kansas in our modeling — Republicans have nominated a strong candidate in Ashley Hinson. Though she’s facing a tough challenge in Democrat Josh Turek, we consider the race a complete tossup.³

In Ohio, former Sen. Sherrod Brown has seized upon hostility to AI and data centers, allowing the Democrat to open up a small lead over appointed incumbent Republican Jon Husted. While polls in Ohio usually overstate Democratic support, Brown’s lead has shown up even in surveys done by Republican pollsters, who typically correct for that skew. We think the race leans Democratic — and virtually every poll and model agrees.

Maine and Alaska also lean blue, with Democratic odds settling in very similar places in both states: 71% in Alaska, 73% in Maine. It’s worth going into detail in both states.

Let’s start with Maine. A lot of liberal readers will have PTSD from years of Susan Collins victories, even in blue environments — and it’s true that polls have historically overshot Democrats in Maine.

But our model picks up on two important factors that look like they push Troy Jackson over the edge.

First, our generic ballot model shows that Maine is effectively a D+14 state right now. That’s the bluest it’s ever been.

Second, candidate quality for overperformers like Collins decays over time, especially as partisanship accelerates. Today, we have Collins performing 9.7 percentage points better in margin than an average Republican incumbent would, which is 3 percentage points worse than her 2020 overperformance. While this would still be more than enough in almost every other swing state, it’s not enough for Maine. High-quality surveys agree — YouGov, CNN/SSRS and The New York Times/Siena College all have Jackson ahead by a modest but notable amount.

In Alaska, meanwhile, our model predicts a flip, but I’m not so sure. In recent weeks, Democrat Mary Peltola has been dogged by an unfriendly wave of headlines: From allegations that she mistreated her staffers (which I don’t think matters) and the revelation that her campaign propped up a spoiler candidate with the same name as incumbent Republican Dan Sullivan (which I do think matters), my guess would be that Peltola slightly underperforms the model’s margins.

The long shots

That leaves two stretch states for each party to hope for an upset win: South Carolina and Nebraska for Democrats, and Michigan and Minnesota for Republicans.

Though our model rates both South Carolina and Nebraska as “Leans Republican” with similar odds, I’d easily take South Carolina as the more likely upset, and our model agrees — Democrats have a 37% chance of winning South Carolina and a 31% chance of winning Nebraska. In the Cornhusker State, Dan Osborn has made a number of missteps in his second go at the seat, and he’s running against a stronger opponent while being more defined himself.⁴

Meanwhile, in South Carolina, Democrat Annie Andrews is facing a terrible candidate in Darline Graham. While we do expect the partisan lean of the state to save the GOP here, it would only take a normal polling error on a good night to see Andrews emerge victorious.⁵ (The New York Times/Siena College poll found this race tied.)

I’m considerably less bullish on the odds of a Republican upset in either Michigan or Minnesota.

While relatively strong GOP nominees in both of the above Midwestern states initially saw some nice polling, our modeling suggests that flawed Democratic candidates in both states will get saved by baseline partisanship. ⁶We rate both Michigan and Minnesota at “Likely Democratic,” with Democrats at an 85% chance of winning Michigan and a 91% chance of winning Minnesota.

Democratic nominee Abdul El-Sayed’s primary opponent Haley Stevens threw her party into chaos Sunday by endorsing the Republican nominee. I don’t think this will change the outcome. Simply put, El-Sayed leads by too much for a lower-profile figure like Stevens to shift things much at this stage in the race — nearly 1 in 4 registered voters in Michigan have already cast their ballots. Nothing surprises me any longer, but El-Sayed’s lead is sizable, and this isn’t a race that we think is likely to be decided by a few thousand votes; our model has him up by more than 4 points.

The last state worth discussing is Mississippi — where Democrat Scott Colom has run a phenomenal campaign, especially compared with the anemic one from the incumbent Republican Cindy Hyde-Smith. The simple reality is that Mississippi is a Trump +23 state. We expect Colom to come closer to winning this seat than any Democrat has in decades, but the partisan headwinds are just too strong to expect a victory in anything other than the bluest of tsunamis imaginable.

In the House, there is only one nonredistricting flip that Republicans can expect on election night: Maine’s 2nd Congressional District, which Trump won by 9 points in 2024. It is currently represented by moderate Democrat Jared Golden, who is retiring, leaving the door open for former Republican Gov. Paul LePage to potentially return to public office.

In virtually all other cases, the picture is somewhere from bad to catastrophic for Republicans.

Why I’m not freaking out about polling error

It’s worth approaching any election forecast with caution: Polling error is unpredictable, and several past elections have seen blue wave predictions that didn’t come to pass. But pollsters and modelers have adapted their methods, and polls have been pretty accurate in nonpresidential years like this one. Republicans actually haven’t been significantly underestimated by polls or models in the past two midterm elections (2018 and 2022).⁷

Our model is also somewhat robust to this sort of thing. We carefully model state, district and demographic correlations to account for systemic polling misses. The data all shows the same thing: Even with a heavy polling miss in favor of Republicans, they would need something exceeding the 2016 and 2020 errors that understated Trump support in order to become favorites in the House of Representatives. Meanwhile, in the Senate, a 3-point underestimation of Republicans (roughly the average error in late 2024 polls) would still leave the chamber as a tossup.

Lastly, part of the reason our model feels better about Democrats than you might think is that polling error is not fully correlated, especially in midterms (which typically see lower partisan polarization than presidential years). In other words, a polling miss favoring one party in one race doesn’t imply the same miss in all races. This is why, in our model, there is a 15% chance that Democrats win Texas while losing Maine.

In short, Democrats have some margin for error here.

For a while, top election watchers thought that Republicans could have a good night. As late as September, experts from Inside Elections were projecting that Democrats would gain only two to 10 House seats — even while models like ours, VoteHub’s and Silver Bulletin’s all had Democrats heavily favored.

To be frank, those ratings looked wrong even back in August. The environment was already indicating a blue wave. But there’s no need to relitigate that, because today, that divergence doesn’t exist. The Cook Political Report has upgraded Democrats five times in the last five weeks, shifting 75 of its House race ratings to the left.

In other words, the experts, the fundamentals and the models are all in agreement: This environment is a train wreck for the Republican Party.

The party has Donald Trump to thank for this. With the president’s net approval 23 points underwater in The Argument’s latest polling, the 2026 midterms are shaping up as the worst environment either major party has seen in decades. There simply isn’t any precedent for a good Republican night, given the kinds of numbers we’re seeing.

Historically, when the president is this unpopular, the party in power faces a massacre at the polls. If the numbers we’re seeing today are even remotely close to reality, 2026 will be no different.
