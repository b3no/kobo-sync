The People Want a Revenge Fantasy

Substack critics hate "Yesteryear," this year's buzziest book, for all the same reasons that everyone else loves it.

If you’ve been on Substack for even a few weeks, you’ve probably noticed that a lot of people really do not like Yesteryear, the hottest book of the year, which The New York Times urges its readers to buy.

Basically, the premise is this: a tradwife influencer named Natalie (not-so-obliquely based on the homestead influencer Ballerina Farm, or Hannah Neeleman) is transported back to the 1800s to live out the lifestyle she hypocritically valorizes online. Even people who hated the book admit the premise is good, and you get the sense many of them wish they had thought of it themselves. It’s such a good premise, in fact, that it will soon be a movie, starring Anne Hathaway.

Despite the sharp idea, Substack reviews (at least the viral ones I’ve seen—I’m sure there are others) have been nearly unanimously negative, citing that the author, Caro Claire Burke, has failed to extend any degree of empathy or humanity to the main character or women like her—and this is doubly disturbing when you consider that the main character is transparently based on a real person. Some writers have even gone so far as to compare the book to a “revenge fantasy that verges on torture porn,” lacking nuance and humanity for the main character, who you’re simply supposed to loathe, and slammed for not really doing the research into what fundamentalist Christian women (the author interviewed ex-Christian women for her research) are actually like. Writer Louise Perry goes a step farther and says that Caro Claire Burke has a problem with women.

But as much as people on Substack talk about it as if it were one of the most egregious pieces of fiction to be published in modern history—everyone else loves it. It has 4.2 stars on Amazon and 4 stars on Goodreads. One reviewer eloquently praises:

What in the ACTUAL tradwife MAGA MAHA conservative Christian manosphere clusterf*ck social media world do we live in that makes this bonkers book seem so REAL?

Look, I’m not here to make a judgment on whether or not Yesteryear was objectively good or bad. What I am interested in, however, is the fact that even if most people on Substack perceive Yesteryear as a one-dimensional revenge fantasy aimed at tradwives…that’s what a lot of people actually want to read.

One of the phenomenons I encounter the most frequently online is people demanding they be able to neatly categorize every content creator as political friend or foe before deciding if they like their work, even if their content isn’t political. I even have a friend who—maddeningly—knows the lore of every single Twitter account, and if I send him a funny tweet, he’ll reply something like, “I can’t really laugh at this, that guy is kind of an anti-anti-Trump guy” even when the joke itself is unrelated to politics.

A few weeks ago, when I went viral for something totally apolitical (posting a sparkly jumpsuit I sewed—yes really, that was it!) I stumbled upon a thread of people quibbling over what my political alignment was, because presumably whether they enjoyed my tweets needed to be verified by confirming I was not the Bad Thing. (Before you make a joke about women and catty tribalism, all people involved were, in fact, men.)

Funny enough, I found myself doing the same thing as they were doing, because I couldn’t figure out what type of man would look at a sewing project and then say, “I have to make sure she has the right politics before I decide how to react to this.” At first, I assumed they were conservative and wanting to make sure I was sewing clothes in a trad way and not in a crafty communist kitchen witch way. But—surprise!—they were kind of “edgy leftist” red triangle types who believed me to be a conservative, and therefore someone who should be avoided, mocked or blocked entirely. One suggested that I was conservative, but unaware of it myself (talk about mansplaining!) They had no evidence that I was a conservative except for one post where I referred to my husband as a “normie lib,” which almost feels like seeing someone tweet about the “goyim” and concluding they must be Jewish.

As I said, I’m not immune to this habit of obsessively filing people into political friend versus foe, but my instinct is driven more by anxiety than by tribalism. Then again, what do I do that isn’t driven by anxiety? Earlier in my Substack career, I received a few invitations to go on podcasts. I didn’t recognize the podcasters, so my first question was, “You’re not like….conservative, are you?” I was terrified that I’d be canceled by-association-by-association, and I needed to make sure that I never aligned myself with anyone who would make me literally Donald Trump by three degrees of separation. I have since loosened up on this because it’s silly—liberals will get nowhere by refusing to interact with anyone who isn’t a liberal or by further shrinking our tent (at this point, the liberals’ tent is about as big as a tent formed in a pair of jeans by a relatively small boner.) It also helps that Substack tends to be a pretty bipartisan place where people will interact respectfully across political alignments.

But most places aren’t Substack! I’m not terribly active on Instagram or Threads, but every now and then I get on there and realize that actually, Substack is a bubble! Most people fucking love the tribalism! Most people do not want to think about those who feel differently from them, and would much rather view them as one-dimensional genocidal maniacs with no inner world other than an insatiable craving for blood. After all, it’s much easier to revel in their humiliation and degradation if they deserve it.

Funny enough, some of the negative reviews of Yesteryear outside of Substack are disappointed that the book humanized Natalie too much toward the end, because the satire has no bite if we are forced to empathize with the target at all.

And on Threads, reviewers correctly identify that Natalie of Yesteryear is meant to be hate-able, but that’s exactly why they love it! This feminist found herself screaming “You dumb bitch” the whole time:

And then, of course, guilt by-association-by-association appears again:

The only women giving bad reviews to Yesteryear must be conservative tradwives who feel called out. Never mind that many of these women are openly liberal and went into the book very excited to read it for the “trad influencer culture is hypocritical” premise.

The difference isn’t that conservative women hate the book and liberal women love it, but some people (who I think are more common on Substack) prefer things with nuance, and have no desire to read a political revenge fantasy. They will read a thoughtful article or even silly satire about Trump’s terrible foreign policy, but do not want to consume anything that aggressively and one-dimensionally dehumanizes half the country. But I truly believe most people do want a revenge fantasy. There is a reason Yesteryear is popular outside of Substack. Most people on Threads would love nothing more than to see Hannah Neeleman herself fall face-first and naked into a vat of pig slop to Benny Hill music.

When people think about how our country is more divided and polarized than ever, they think about the actual political opinions we hold. But most people’s opinions aren’t that extreme. I think polarization is more about the degree of humanity we extend to people who disagree with us politically.

I know some people will read that and say, “Why should I extend humanity to MAGA when they literally want to kill me?” and I would say two things: this cuts both ways (conservatives often conjure up cartoonish, nuance-free ideas of what liberals are like and do not extend any understanding to them—trust me, I’ve been on the receiving end of it) but also, politics has descended to a place where every political opinion is viewed as life-or-death, where everyone believes the other side quite literally wants to enact mass murder onto their fellow Americans. I don’t doubt actual evil villains exist within MAGA—Stephen Miller et al—but I do not believe every Republican voter is a murderous maniac consumed with bloodlust, or agrees with everything Trump has ever said or done. (For example, plenty of Trump voters believed that ICE went too far.) It’s possible to engage with people (or just see them as human) when you disagree over politics. But when “politics” is invariably boiled down to “wants to kill someone, the question is who” then we’ve totally lost the plot.

I believe a lot of people falsely remember the nineties as a time when politics consisted solely of boring topics like zoning laws and tax breaks, and outside of David Duke nobody was really that problematic. Consider that Republicans and Democrats were not so polarized or antagonistic toward each other when I was a kid—while my family was mostly liberal, I had Republican family members, and generally we could expect to have Thanksgiving without major drama (about that, anyway.) But there were still plenty of emotionally-charged, polarizing political topics back then, and most people didn’t hyperbolize these debates into “this group wants to enact mass murder.” People used to disagree far more on the very emotionally charged topic of same-sex marriage. Now, most people—including some conservatives—are for it. And yet, back when this very emotional social issue was split in a more polarizing way, people did not hold as much animus for the other political team as they do today.

Now, I am constantly receiving “friendly heads up” about the fact that I briefly exchanged polite tweets with someone who has a problematic view—not something like “we should expatriate people of my least favorite race” but something that a boilerplate conservative would have believed anytime over the past several decades. That doesn’t mean I agree with these beliefs, only that I don’t believe that a person with these beliefs should be auto-blocked or ignored when they’re just saying “I liked your article." It wouldn’t even be a smart business move for me to refuse to associate with 70% of the country—and I say 70% because there are even many liberals who hold at least one belief that might be considered problematic to these people.

We do not want to view people as people unless they are on our team, and we justify this belief by insisting they want us dead anyway so they don’t deserve humanity. By exaggerating the depravity of our opponents’ beliefs, we can justify hating them and reveling in their torment. This is exactly why people actually really like Yesteryear. They did not want the nuance. They did not want to feel empathy for a conservative woman. In fact, they are angry at the moments in the book which prompt such a feeling.

I cannot fault Caro Claire Burke for writing this book, though, for several reasons.

I wrote a book which was professionally published in the 2010s, a piece of lore of which many of you are already aware. I’m not here to defend my book or say it was the best thing ever. I wrote it when I was twenty-five and now I cringe reading it (hell, I cringe reading my Substack articles from last year.) I will also go on the record and say that Caro Claire Burke in 2026 is a much better writer than CHH in the 2010s, so keep that in mind.

But one ridiculous roadblock I came up against during my editing process was that one of my characters was at least somewhat conservative-coded. Not a Republican per se, certainly not a Nazi, but a chauvinist who occasionally said regressive or ignorant things under the guise of “not being politically correct.” Basically, a real legalize comedy kind of guy, who was really the butt of many of these jokes himself. I thought it was obvious that this character was not meant to be aspirational, although I did attempt to humanize him. I wanted the reader to feel uncomfortable and conflicted, seeing this boorish man, starting out loathing him and laughing at him, but ultimately finding themselves inside his mind, realizing he’s more than a two-dimensional joke, and that despite his politically incorrect comments, he’s a full person with vulnerabilities and feelings. But instead, my agent and editor (and the nineteen-year-old intern working underneath my agent, who for some reason was in charge of providing feedback) kept telling me that it was essential that I have other characters repeatedly “call him out” so nobody would confuse his opinions for my own.

I thought this was absurd, naturally. I asked her why anyone would confuse a side character’s opinions with my own when the book also contained characters with progressive and feminist views. How could I hold all these views simultaneously? But it didn’t matter. If there was one vaguely right wing character, the whole book was too much of a risk. The intern in particular surmised that any over-the-top behavior from a feminist or left-wing character was satire, while the same absurdity from a right-wing character was obviously endorsement. I was forced to include moments where the “good” characters informed him he was being problematic, just so nobody got confused.

I don’t believe this was Caro Claire Burke’s journey. I don’t believe she initially wrote something complicated, and then her editor was like, “No, make this based on Ballerina Farm and then make her look like an absolute tit.” But even if she had tried to write something more nuanced, something that challenged us to feel conflicted, something that forced us to extend empathy to someone we also aren’t meant to like—I don’t know how many people would have bought it.
