The rise of the Hispanic moderate

New leadership can help a new majority finally emerge.

Matthew Yglesias
Nov 9
∙
Paid
 
READ IN APP
 

The biggest collective brain fart by the Democratic Party over the course of my career was almost certainly convincing itself that America’s rising Hispanic population share guaranteed a progressive hammerlock on American electoral politics.

This 2013 take from Ron Brownstein — that Barack Obama’s re-election proved culturally conservative white people had become irrelevant — had an unusually blunt headline but was typical of the spirit of the era.

This analysis never made a ton of sense, even on its own terms. In 2012, Obama won Iowa and Ohio and Michigan and Wisconsin and Pennsylvania, none of which had a particularly large Hispanic population share. This basic math was known and knowable at the time, but it was ignored amid emerging Democratic majority hype and a focus on non-pivotal states like Nevada.

But the notion that demographic changes meant Democrats didn’t have to do politics was misguided in deeper ways, and it helped set the party up for some serious tactical and strategic errors. Those mistakes led to blowing off not only the actual views of the kind of secular white working-class Midwesterners who were important to Obama’s wins but also to blowing off the actual views of most American Hispanics.

In 2020, Donald Trump shocked many liberals by making meaningful gains with Latino voters even while doing quite poorly overall. Then in 2024, with the national trends at his back, he did even better.

A huge part of the 2026 story is Hispanic voters coming back to Democrats. Trump’s own choices are the largest factor, but an important secondary one is Democrats developing a more sophisticated understanding of the Hispanic electorate. And part of that, I think, has been cultivating a larger crop of moderate Latino politicians. Slow Boring’s 2026 candidate slate featured three different Latino guys who wear cowboy hats. And if Democrats want to continue to win, politicians like that — people who actually represent their voters and know what they’re like — have to control the narrative and not let it be seized again by unaccountable NGO types spinning wildly unrealistic dreams of endless progressive dominance.

The false dawn

One serious problem with post-2012 hubris about Hispanic voters is that it kind of understandably radicalized Republican opinion against immigration.

People have a range of concerns about immigration, some more fact-based than others. But people also have lots of opinions about topics that aren’t immigration. The thrust of Brownstein’s argument was that catering to people with culturally conservative views on non-immigration topics was now unnecessary thanks to the rising Hispanic tide. This notion got rephrased by conservatives as an alarming and possibly potent narrative that immigration debates should be understood not as economic policy but as a progressive effort to perpetrate a “great replacement” of the existing electorate.

And at the same time, it generated a breakdown of ideological discipline in the Democratic Party.

With Democrats’ “old” support base, they needed to win over working-class white people who cared about tax fairness and health care and Social Security and Medicare but didn’t necessarily align with the cultural values of the kind of highly educated hyper-cosmopolitan white people I’ve been surrounded by all my life in Greenwich Village and Harvard Square and Logan Circle.

But what did we think all these second- and third-generation Hispanics were like? Did they share the cultural values of hyper-cosmopolitan educated white urbanites? Did that really make sense?

I once went to a block party in my D.C. neighborhood where they were handing out blue and pink balloons and little kids were grabbing them. Some of the adult attendees were white parents from the neighborhood and some were Latina or Afro-Caribbean nannies. A couple of adults objected to some kids having the “wrong” color balloon and started sorting the pink balloons to the girls and the blue balloons to the boys.

Do you think that the adults in question were the white Logan Circle professionals or the women of color?

Nobody has ever answered this question incorrectly. People are aware, on some level, that there is no racial magic that causes working-class Hispanic people to develop the cultural values and political opinions of Middlebury graduates. Right-wing politics, after all, exists in Latin America, and there’s no reason why people of Latin ancestry living in the United States can’t have right-wing opinions.

The momentary identity crisis among Democrats generated by Trump’s 2020 and 2024 gains with Hispanic voters has been ameliorated by evidence of a strong shift back to the party in 2026. But it’s important to understand who actually led this shift and why.

Hispanic politicians vs. Latinx staffers

An important figure here is Arizona Senator Ruben Gallego, who used to represent a safe blue House seat in Phoenix and who famously reacted to the 2020 red-shift with Hispanic voters with a social-media tirade about the term “Latinx.”

The controversy around this word was significant, I think, not because the word itself matters but because of what the word was a symptom of: the clout of people rooted in working-class Hispanic communities relative to that of people with Latin American ancestry rooted in generically progressive academic and NGO circles.

Gallego’s boldness in speaking about this mattered precisely because his House district was safe. The easiest thing in the world would have been to just continue communicating in a smart way with his constituents but go along to get along in D.C. and in national progressive circles. But Gallego wanted to run statewide and knew it would be smart to cultivate an image as someone who wasn’t a doctrinaire progressive. So he actually articulated what his constituents believed, namely that it was bizarre and insulting to revise the whole grammatical structure of the Spanish language for intersectional reasons.

Henry Cuellar and Vicente Gonzalez down in the Rio Grande Valley don’t have big social-media presences or do a lot of national press. But in the 2020 / 2022 / 2024 election cycles, they were some of the biggest electoral overperformers, and that involved things like being the only two House Democrats to vote for the Protection of Women and Girls in Sports Act.

It was at an event they hosted in D.C. that I first met Johnny Garcia and Bobby Pulido, two of the aforementioned Latino guys in cowboy hats, who won last week with the support of Blue Dog PAC and Majority Democrats. Garcia is an actual cop from the Bexar County sheriff’s department. Pulido’s magic trick for winning back Trump voters is to agree with them about select energy and cultural issues:

Warming to the theme, he said: “What plays well in New York City likely won’t play well among Democratic voters in the valley. The party has become too one-size-fits-all, and that’s a big mistake.”

His main takeaway from his months running for congressional office is that Democrats “let other people define us.”

“When I first told people in this district that I was running as a Democrat, their automatic assumption was that I was going to take away their guns, that I was for trans in women’s sports, that I was going to take away their oil and gas jobs,” he said. “That’s the narrative they were fed by Republicans.”

My third cowboy hat Latino, Manny Rutinel, was a pretty conventional progressive in the Colorado legislature, but he moved decidedly toward the center — notably, again, on energy issues — en route to flipping a Trump seat.

It’s now been a thousand years since anyone has heard the term Latinx. But I think a really important question as Democrats try to hold on to the Hispanic votes they regained is whether they’re going to let this story be told by guys like Gallego and Gonzalez and Garcia who are in touch with their voters or revert to the Latinx mindset.

Who flipped Texas?

To give a pointed, Texas-specific example, Congressional Progressive Caucus leader Greg Casar is also Hispanic. When the Texas state legislature gerrymandered the map, he was stuck in a strongly red district. His response was to muscle Lloyd Doggett out of a safe seat in Austin, from which vantage point he’s been lecturing everyone on what Democrats need to do to win.

The seat he skedaddled out of is the one that Johnny Garcia just won.

Notably, while Casar held a couple of big get-out-the-vote events this fall with Bernie Sanders, these were not events that Gina Hinojosa or James Talarico attended. Talarico, of course, is not Hispanic (he’s not even Italian), but he did run in close partnership with the Hispanic mods, a partnership that included the Latino Victory Fund doing joint Spanish-language Talarico/Pulido ads full of trilled Rs.

Chuck Rocha, who worked on all these Texas races, specifically confirms in a great op-ed that while Latino voters have a diverse range of views, the key to winning Latino swing voters is specifically “running politically moderate and somewhat culturally conservative candidates in heavily Latino districts that are rural or exurban.” He said that he’s from Texas and “in many parts of my home state, border security (done the right way), oil workers, cowboy hats, and big ol’ pickup trucks dominate.”

And he’s exactly right.

I’ve been with my father-in-law to the rodeo in Junction, Texas, and the Latinos in the crowd have more in common culturally with the white people of rural Texas than they do with my neighbors in D.C.

What makes this group of culturally conservative voters much more winnable than rural whites is, of course, that the Republican Party contains a thriving wing of white racists and Hispanics don’t like that.

Whether it’s Bo French panicking about Indian American football fans or Brett Kavanaugh giving the green light to racial profiling as an immigration enforcement method, there are some big non-economic beefs between non-white Americans and the conservative movement.

But that doesn’t mean this electorate wants 100-proof intersectional progressive politics.

Hinojosa is a progressive by Texas standards, which I think mostly comes down to her being a close ally of the teachers’ unions in the state legislature. But as governor, she’s not going to ban oil and gas extraction. She did this “Pronouns” ad to distance herself from progressive cultural politics. It remains to be seen how she’ll navigate these issues in practice, but she appears to have the same tacit understanding as Rocha and Pulido and Garcia and Gonzalez and Cuellar.

A big question in my mind is whether this new crop of culturally moderate Latino elected officials will do what Gallego did after 2020 and push the message out overtly so they can influence party-wide strategy rather than allowing their victories to breed complacency.

When immigration is personal

The biggest thing here is immigration.

The idea that originally sent Democrats over the cliff was the belief that taking left-wing stands on immigration would be so overwhelmingly popular with Hispanics that it would give them latitude to go maximally left on everything else, too.

Wisdom on the topic of immigration starts with understanding what the major immigration issue was prior to 2016 and how the nature of the issue changed.

Previously, a lot of Mexican American U.S. citizens had family members, friends, or acquaintances who were illegal immigrants, most of whom arrived in the late 1980s or 1990s. Those people were interested in immigration reforms that would directly help people they knew and cared about. They also worried about being personally caught up in immigration raids or dragnets or being subject to racial profiling. There was also an anti-immigration discourse that cast the presence of Hispanic people in the United States as a threat to the country, which naturally Hispanic citizens didn’t like or agree with.

But a shift happened in Trump’s first term and in Biden’s term, when the immigration issue came to be primarily about new arrivals — many of whom were not Mexican — making asylum claims.

To both the xenophobic and the kinds of people who use terms like Latinx or BIPOC, these are continuous issues. But from the standpoint of many Latino citizens, they’re totally different.

Many Latino citizens found it jarring to see not only a chaotic situation at the border but also a Democratic Party that was pivoting away from talking about assistance for mixed-status families and toward helping new arrivals from Haiti. It also appeared that many arriving through asylum or executive parole channels were receiving better treatment than the long-resident illegal workers Obama had pledged to help.

Then after 2024 Trump came back into office with a mandate to secure the border, and he not only clamped down on asylum but also initiated draconian interior enforcement, complete with the racial profiling and other things Latinos feared from pre-2016 restrictionists.

You could say, “well you guys should have known what you were getting,” but the fact is Latinos voted for Hillary Clinton in massive numbers in 2016 precisely because they feared mass deportations and Trump won anyway and then didn’t do it. So I think it was semi-reasonable of voters to assume his 2024 pledges were also bluster. They weren’t, though, and that helped set the stage for the backlash.

But it’s critical not to overstate this: Exit polls of Hispanic voters this year showed immigration as a much lower priority than the economy and also revealed that the minority of Hispanics focused on immigration were largely Republicans.

The distinction between “Latinos were mad at Trump because of ICE” and “Latinos were mad at Trump because of the economy” is important because it has implications for other policy areas. If you’re looking at a primarily ICE-driven backlash, you can address ICE abuses and then let the Sierra Club wild out on energy policy. But what we’re actually looking at is an economy-driven backlash. You still should address ICE abuses because ICE abuses are morally wrong. But you also have to actually deliver on the economic commitment, and that means consistently addressing all kinds of energy and regulatory issues with an eye toward the cost of living.

This is an inconvenient truth for a coalition built around the idea of an immigration-focused Hispanic voter. But it’s important to know the truth.

We now have a good crop of moderate Hispanic politicians who clearly do know. But the 2028 cycle will be a nationalized presidential campaign, not a series of localized battles, and we’re going to need to raise the voices of those who know how to address these voters and get as much of the party as possible aligned with politics that works.
