We can fix the Trump/Warren build-to-rent ban

The House of Representatives to the rescue?

Matthew Yglesias
May 20
∙
Paid
 
READ IN APP
 

It’s easy to forget now, but when the ROAD to Housing Act was first unveiled last summer in the Senate Banking Committee, it was like a Secret Congress miracle.

Somewhere down the line, Elizabeth Warren had gotten supply-pilled on housing and hired a really good staffer who was networked with all the smart housing analysts. On the Republican side, Tim Scott was willing to break some taboos around local control that had prevented any prior bipartisan housing-supply initiatives from gaining traction. Their bill had some language on mortgage lending that I was surprised Warren agreed to. But she did what serious legislators do and got to yes on a package that I’m sure she felt was flawed in key respects.

The land-use provisions were limited in their impact, but kind of visionary in their outcomes-oriented design and could have laid the groundwork for more forceful action leveraging larger grant programs in the future. Other good proposals that had been floating around Congress for a while — including, of particular note to me, chassis reform — got folded in.

And while Scott understandably wasn’t going to get into an overt fight with Donald Trump about it, suddenly the Project 2025 stuff about defending single-family zoning and Trump’s promises to defend the “suburban lifestyle dream” against apartments didn’t matter to Republicans on the committee.

Initially, the big outstanding question was about the House.¹ Remarkably, though, that side ended up working out thanks in no small part to the efforts of the new Build America Caucus.

Instead, the whole thing has been derailed because Trump, with some critical assists from Warren, has ginned up outrage around the fake problem of private equity buying up single-family homes and devised a legislative “solution” that will greatly reduce the construction of new rental housing.

The possibility that the anti-supply provision will be taken out keeps being raised, only for those hopes to be dashed. The latest drama is that even though key leaders in the House want to fix the problem and send a better bill back to the Senate, Trump and JD Vance are leaning hard on them to pass the Senate’s bill as written.

But the House, for now at least, is not taking Trump too seriously and seems to be trying to fix it. They’re right to do this, and I hope they stay the course in the face of continued criticism and opposition.

From fake problems to real ones

Private-equity investors “buying up” single-family homes en masse was a very real trend in the wake of the foreclosure wave that hit the country in 2007–08.

The issue here, as you may recall, is that banks had started making loans largely on the theory that we’d never see a national decline in house prices. This meant banks didn’t need to worry so much about whether any given borrower was creditworthy. If they couldn’t pay the mortgage, they would just sell the house at a profit and pay off the loan that way.

Of course everyone knew that specific places could experience real estate downturns linked to regional economic trends. But you could eliminate this risk by combining loans from diverse geographies into a single mortgage-backed security.

This proved to be overly optimistic, and the country soon found itself in a situation in which a lot of people couldn’t pay their mortgages even if they sold their home because the home was worth less than the value of the loan. As a result, lots of banks ended up foreclosing on lots of homes.

But while the threat of foreclosure is a crucial element of the mortgage lending ecosystem — you can get a loan on attractive terms in part because it’s backed by a hard asset — banks don’t actually want to own large portfolios of real estate. The crash induced a huge national credit crunch, so relatively few households could get loans to buy newly foreclosed homes from the banks that now owned them.

Enter some opportunistic private-equity investors with the idea of buying big swaths of homes and operating them as rental properties.

I think that conditional on the huge wave of foreclosures happening, this was clearly a better outcome than what would have happened absent private-equity interest. But clearly the whole process was highly undesirable, and all of these houses falling into the hands of private-equity firms was a visible signifier of a bad situation and significant policy failures.

For reasons that remain somewhat unclear to me, instead of anxiety about this peaking when institutional investment peaked and waning in later years as the situation normalized, complaints about institutional investors scooping up single-family homes grew more intense over time. On the whole, though, this has just never been quantitatively significant. Vastly more single-family homes are owned by small landlords than are owned by large investors, and vastly more single-family homes are owner-occupied than rented.

That being said, in recent years we have seen a new trend, which is investors (not necessarily private-equity firms) financing new construction of rental properties.

This has long been done in the form of apartment buildings, but it’s now happening with single-family subdivisions that typically feature smaller homes. Traditionally this kind of smaller, cheaper single-family “starter home” would have been built for sale, but since 2008 it’s been systematically harder for people with weaker credit scores to get a mortgage.

If people can’t get a mortgage, then they can either be homeless or else they can rent. You can rent an apartment, but it’s illegal to build apartments on the vast majority of residentially zoned land in the United States. So capitalism has done what it’s supposed to do and created a new wave of investments in purpose-built single-family rentals.

We should not overstate the scale of the build-to-rent market, but in some of America’s most important growth-friendly Sun Belt metro areas, it is a genuinely large share of new housing supply. You don’t need to be happy about the idea of families moving into exurban build-to-rent single-family home projects. But then you need to either make it easier for lower-income families to get mortgages² or else make it easier to build apartments.

Instead, what the Senate has done is respond to panic about a basically fake problem with legislation that risks the very real problem of cutting off an important source of new housing supply.

Dodging a solution

For a long time, the private-equity controversy and ROAD to Housing were proceeding on parallel tracks, with a lot of Warren-aligned people talking up the private-equity angle even as Warren herself was working diligently on the bill.

That changed in February of this year when Trump took time during his State of the Union to embrace the myth that corporate landlords were making housing expensive.

This ended the parallel track strategy. Populist Democratic senators now wanted a Trump-blessed private-equity provision in the housing package.

The initial rumors were that senators were going to write something that restricted institutional investors from buying single-family homes but retained a generous carve-out for new build-to-rent projects. This is not an idea that I would endorse on the merits, but it also strikes me as an example of slopulism done right. If people are really mad about something that’s mostly fake, you can address their concern in a bill that also includes lots of good provisions that address real problems.

What actually came out, though, was language that does have a carve-out for new-build projects but also restricts them so severely — most notably through a requirement that they be sold off within seven years — that it would effectively kill the build-to-rent sector.

As the bill came together, the Treasury Department signaled that it wanted this language changed, and Senator Brian Schatz tried to ride to the rescue by suggesting that it was some kind of mistake.

So there was a window when they could have changed it and created a situation where both Warren and Trump could claim they had banned Wall Street from buying up single-family homes without impairing build to rent and while doing a bunch of other good pro-supply things. But Warren smacked that suggestion down, and then as payback for his efforts to look out for the interests of renters, Schatz got slammed by the American Prospect as a pawn of the private-equity industry.

Over in the House, where Maxine Waters is the ranking Democrat on the relevant committee, they swapped out the build-to-rent ban so Matt Stoller of the American Economic Liberties Project decided Waters is the villain for bucking Trump on this.

More Perfect Union, in a tweet condemning the change, noted that after the Senate language passed, “Wall Street and housing developers immediately started lobbying the House, spending tens of thousands on key lawmakers,” a move that they say “appears to have paid off” in getting the House to make the switch.

A duel of perverse ideas

The bulk of the infighting I’ve seen about this has been among Democrats, but it’s worth underscoring how absurd and self-destructive Trump’s behavior has been.

One baseline impediment to good bipartisan bills passing is that “good bill that makes things better passes” is kind of inherently positive for the incumbent president.

I’m not going to say that if a good version of federal housing legislation passes, that single-handedly solves the administration’s myriad problems on the affordability front. But just as a bad war that explodes the price of energy ends up hurting Trump, a good bill that stimulates housing production would be good for Trump.

This tends to make it hard for opposition party members to “get to yes” on useful reforms. The fact that Trump is undermining himself in an area where it would be genuinely helpful to him to get a good bill passed is perverse, albeit broadly similar to how he keeps sabotaging energy-permitting-reform talks with his bizarre crusade against wind and solar power.

Then on the left, you have the More Perfect Union tweet complaining that homebuilding and finance interests stand to win if we allow new build-to-rent projects to go forward. They certainly do stand to win. It’s homebuilders who build homes, after all, and it’s investors who finance rental housing. As long as you have a market economy at all, any kind of beneficial economic activity is going to generate a set of “corporate” winners.

If we completely eliminate all private homebuilding and shift to a 100 percent social-housing model where all construction is done by public-sector entities, then you will still have NIMBYs who want to block new housing. Lobbying against them, you would find not private developers but private companies who manufacture construction equipment and building supplies. The NIMBYs will try to position themselves as fighting for the little guy against corporate construction interests and the exact same dialogue will recur.

Fundamentally, if we think that renting a home is a legitimate activity, then building and operating rental housing has to also be a legitimate activity. I don’t love the vision of new build-to-rent projects as the default for young families either, but the constructive policy alternatives involve making it easier to build apartments and get mortgages.

The House bill, unfortunately, has left out a couple of good ideas from the Senate legislation. But Waters and Schatz are right about build to rent, Trump and Warren are wrong, and the House should stand its ground on this point.
