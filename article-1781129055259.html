A market moves, with a crisis unresolved

Sales rebound and development continues, yet stalled reforms, shrinking budgets, and flawed affordability targets are leaving the housing shortage largely intact.

Last week, the House Appropriations Committee voted 34 to 27, along party lines, to advance its Housing and Urban Development spending bill for fiscal year 2027. The bill would provide $71.38 billion for HUD — a $5.94 billion, or more than 8 percent, cut from what was appropriated in fiscal year 2026.

This follows President Donald Trump’s budget proposal for fiscal year 2027, which would impose work requirements and time caps on assisted households and prohibit issuing any new vouchers.

ProPublica: The Low-Income Housing Tax Credit is building homes people can’t afford

ProPublica and Oregon Public Broadcasting published an investigation last week into the Low-Income Housing Tax Credit — the federal program that finances nine out of every 10 subsidized apartments built in the country and that Congress permanently expanded in last year’s budget reconciliation bill.

The program provides up to $15 billion in tax credits a year to help developers build apartments. But in Portland, Ore., the outcome is a surplus of apartments costing renters around $1,400 a month for a one-bedroom. According to Tony Schick of Oregon Public Broadcasting, that cost works for a family making $75,000 a year but is nearly half the monthly income of someone earning $35,000 at the local minimum wage. It’s a program that largely misses the poorest residents — those most at risk of homelessness.

The result is that nearly 2,000 of Portland’s subsidized units sat vacant and unused.

The core problem is how affordability is defined. To meet affordability requirements, developers typically need to make rents manageable for someone earning 60 percent of area median income. In Portland, that threshold pans out to about $75,000 annually for a family of four. That level of rent is close to what typical Portland landlords charge without a subsidy, Schick reports.

In one Portland suburb, a subsidized two-bedroom can cost up to $1,675, while the equivalent market-rate apartment is $1,525.

In addition to the federal credits, Oregon lawmakers over the last 10 years have provided their own development incentives through tax credits and loan deferrals. The state subsequently went from building about 1,800 subsidized apartments a year pre-pandemic to nearly 5,000 last year. But all that construction has failed to make a dent in the affordability crisis.

“This program is lacking its reason to exist,” Kirk McClure, a professor emeritus of urban planning at the University of Kansas and a leading critic of the tax credit, told Schick. McClure has advocated in Congress for reforms to the program, including redirecting the money to rental vouchers and narrowing eligibility to developers who build units that are in genuinely short supply.

Supporters argue that the tax credit remains the most effective way to increase the supply of affordable housing, since construction costs are too high for developers to profitably build apartments that can be rented at below-market rates without subsidies.

Now that the Low-Income Housing Tax Credit is permanently expanded, it is projected to finance more than a million additional units over the next decade using the same basic structure the investigation says is broken.

Existing home sales pick up in May

Existing home sales increased 3.2 percent month-over-month and year-over-year in May, according to the National Association of Realtors. The month saw a seasonally adjusted annual rate of 4.17 million sales, a median sales price of $429,300 — which is a new May record, according to NAR Chief Economist Lawrence Yun — and enough homes on the market to supply 4.5 months of sales at the current pace.

Yun credited improving affordability, noting that mortgage rates remain below year-ago levels and income growth is slightly outpacing home price gains in most of the country. The 30-year fixed-rate mortgage averaged 6.48 percent as of June 4, down from 6.53 percent the prior week and notably below the 6.85 percent average from a year ago. By June 9, the rate had climbed back to 6.65 percent as the U.S. conflict with Iran pushed oil prices higher and renewed fears of inflation.

The inventory level, which remains well below the six months typically associated with a balanced market, continues to put upward pressure on prices.

Housing near transit, mapped

The Urban Institute released a new interactive data tool last week that tracks housing development near transit, state by state and urban area by urban area.

It found that New York City is a national outlier, thanks to its unparalleled rail network. Between 1980 and 2022, the city added almost 700,000 housing units near rail — roughly equivalent to the entire housing supply of the Charlotte urban area today. Other leading cities were D.C., Los Angeles, San Francisco, Chicago, Boston, and Seattle, each adding at least 100,000 housing units near rail.

The Institute also found wide variation in transit access. Los Angeles and San Francisco provide transit service within a half mile of at least 90 percent of homes, while less dense, more sprawling urban areas such as Atlanta, Dallas, and Houston serve fewer than half of all homes within that range.

Bookmark the tool if you are following transit-oriented-development legislation at the state level.

Pritzker’s zoning reform stalls in Springfield

One of the most ambitious state-level efforts at housing reform this cycle fell apart this week. Governor JB Pritzker’s Building Up Illinois Developments (BUILD) plan ended the spring session without a vote amid opposition from municipalities. His statewide plan proposed withdrawing broad zoning authority from local governments, legalizing “missing middle” housing and accessory dwelling units, and setting hard deadlines for permit reviews.

Municipal leaders objected to the potential loss of local control over density requirements, the Real Deal reports. But Pritzker managed to salvage some money, securing $250 million in funding for “missing middle” and affordable housing programs in the state budget, Capitol News Illinois reports.

A separate Senate-passed bill targeting large institutional investors by proposing a graduated annual fee on those with more than 10 residential properties also stalled in the House, HousingWire reports.

Spanberger signs bills to boost manufactured housing

Last week, Governor Abigail Spanberger signed a series of bipartisan bills to boost supply and affordability.

The package includes a revolving loan fund for mixed-income housing development, a two-year pilot program designed to encourage new construction, cuts to the red tape that restricts manufactured homes, and protections for residents of manufactured home parks, including a right of first refusal if their park is put up for sale.

Washington school district plans housing for homeless families

Last year, 820 students in the Edmonds School District, which sits north of Seattle, were homeless. The Seattle Times reported this week that the district is partnering with nonprofit Housing Hope to build a 52-unit affordable housing development on surplus district land, specifically for those families with students experiencing homelessness.

The project is “likely” the first in the state that takes advantage of a 2020 law allowing districts to lease or rent out surplus property for affordable housing, according to the Seattle Times.

Wisconsin’s housing gap narrows for the wrong reason

A new report from Forward Analytics, the research arm of the Wisconsin Counties Association, revised the state’s housing need estimate down from 140,000 units to around 84,000 units by the end of the decade, and it is not because more housing is available. The reason is that the population of people aged 25 to 64 — prime working-age — is now expected to decline faster than researchers estimated, Wisconsin Public Radio reports.

The researcher who worked on the report was explicit that the decline is not good news, that the projected relief is “not because we’ve built more to become more affordable, but just because we project that there’ll be less people demanding housing,” he told Wisconsin Public Radio. “It’s signaling greater demographic pressures and challenges,” he continued.

It’s a preview of a dynamic that will become more common in slow-growth and shrinking states, where the housing shortage problem gets “solved” by population loss rather than construction.
