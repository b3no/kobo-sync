The Demands of Love

In “Stations,” Irish novelist Louise Kennedy tells a story of devotion, addiction and the limits of friendship.

Ron Charles
Nov 3
 
READ IN APP
 

The Tuesday Review

Stations, a new novel by the Irish writer Louise Kennedy, lays out a friendship that shapes a heart, and tears it.

Red Camelin is the first boy Róisín McCaffrey has ever spoken to. Not that she’s supposed to be speaking to him at all. “Actively unpopular” among her high school peers, Róisín has been commissioned by the hottest girl in class to pass a note to her rakish boyfriend, Red, one of the richest boys in town. It’s a courier task designed to emphasize Róisín’s subservience and sexual irrelevance.

But nobody told Red that.

He asks her for a cigarette and offers to walk her home. Soon, he wants to know her favorite book, song, and movie, and they’re drawing connections between the Beatles’ “Helter Skelter” and Joan Didion’s essay about the Manson trial.

Róisín can’t imagine what Red talks about with his girlfriend, “who had failed three subjects — including English.” But she likes the way he sounds: “Ironic, slightly formal. Always with the beginnings of a smile.... She had expected a delinquent, but he was curious, funny. Nice.”

Neither of these kids will ever recover from this moment of happiness.

When Kennedy was about Róisín’s age in the early 1980s, she dreamed of becoming a writer, but, as she once told an interviewer, she decided that writing “was something that magical people did.” And so for decades she worked as a chef until a friend pestered her into joining a writers’ group. Her debut story collection, The End of the World Is a Cul de Sac, won the John McGahern Prize, named after one of Ireland’s finest writers. Her first novel, Trespasses, published in 2022 when she was 55, was shortlisted for the Women’s Prize for Fiction and made into a TV series starring Lola Petticrew, Tom Cullen and Gillian Anderson. Now, with Stations, she’s confirmed her status as one of those magical people who conjure up stories that compete with our own lives.

Her Róisín is the kind of angry girl you want to assure life will get better, but you fear she’d just roll her eyes and flick a cigarette at your shoes. If you’ve ever had a teenager, or been one, you know this ornery, witty, self-deprecating girl who’s as cuddly as a Gaelic porcupine. And why not — she has a lot to be angry about. The Troubles have scrambled her family life. As she confesses to Red, her Catholic father had joined the Royal Ulster Constabulary in Northern Ireland, succumbed to well-grounded paranoia, and abandoned them. Róisín and her mother are barely getting by. Their move down south — a fresh start, ha! — has Mum working nights and Róisín feeling permanently friendless at a school where kids mock her accent, her clothes, and even her fatherlessness.

Although Kennedy narrates Stations in the third person, she stays so close to Róisín’s mind that we can feel the irritation flaking off her like dandruff, itchy and embarrassing. Only Red gets her, only Red makes her laugh, only Red gives her a reason to get up in the morning.

Romance feels inevitable, no?

No.

Róisín tries, for a time, to imagine that “her friendship with Red was on a higher plane than the grubby maulings of their schoolmates, all that boys-and-girls carry-on seemed so petty by comparison. She had ordered the copy of The Symposium because they had talked about platonic friendships.”

Sure.

There’s something else going on here, a species of devotion rendered all the more intense by their inability to get in sync romantically.

I’ll be your friend for life too.

You won’t, he said, turning to look at her, and that’s OK.

I will.

He reached for her hand. She moved it to his face, placing it on his cheek. For a moment she thought he was going to kiss her, but he laid it gently in her lap.

That poignant interaction, in various forms, is repeated again and again, until it isn’t.

Róisín realizes later that although they’ve been friends for over a year, “still he fobbed her off with small talk and pleasantries. He knew everything about her, but what did she know about him, aside from his taste in music and books and film?”

“You would not believe how good I am at keeping secrets,” Red tells her.

In the novel’s opening pages, that boast sounds like adolescent swagger, but it’s the knifepoint on which this tragic story turns.

Red always partied hard at school, but when he washes up in London and squats in an empty flat with no electricity, Róisín sees just how fiercely he’s gripped by addiction. It’s a shockingly debased period in their lives, and London, “this vast, indifferent city,” has no use for these young Irish immigrants who can quote Dickens but have no marketable skills.

Kennedy spares these characters none of the humiliations of their demons or their poverty, but she’s willing to suggest there’s also something profoundly thrilling about their Byronic freedom. The problem, as Róisín will realize, is surviving that kind of freedom and learning to corral her various resentments, disappointments, and self-recriminations into an actual life.

But what, ultimately, does she owe her brilliant best friend who pushes her away while grasping for her? And how much of Róisín’s impatience with Red is predicated on her ignorance of what happened to him?

I asked Louise Kennedy, “What interested you about exploring the limits of what we can do for someone we love?”

Kennedy constructs Stations as a series of crucifying moments, clearly dated, over a quarter century. Those silent gaps — some just a few days, one as long as 18 years — can roar with longing or pain, confirming our fears for these young folks or giving us a moment of relief.

Her prose style, though, is what really roots us to this story. It’s a seamless mix of narration and internal reflection. And the dialogue — without quotation marks — captures the erratic structure of real conversation, that haphazard tower of Jenga blocks we keep shifting to be heard.

Desire and melancholy pull so fiercely on opposite sides of this book that it’s hard to close it. At times the story is punctuated by extravagant grief. When Red “wept noiselessly into her hair until dawn,” I had to put the book down and take a few big breaths.

Late in Stations, Róisín texts a friend about a novel that depicts the sorrows of Irish emigration to England: “This book. I’m fkn destroyed.”

Exactly.◆

Stations, by Louise Kennedy (Riverhead Books, 288 pp., $28)
