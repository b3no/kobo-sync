Plus Netanyahu’s long-term effort to make Democrats hate him and the problem with wealth taxes

There’s a lot of Barack Obama talk in this week’s Mailbag, which reminded me of something that I think tends to get forgotten in assessments of Trump-era politics: It was Mitt Romney, not Donald Trump, who made a hard break with the Bush-McCain interest in some form of “comprehensive immigration reform” with a path to citizenship for the majority of long-resident undocumented immigrants. It’s true that Trump in his 2016 campaign amped up the rhetoric somewhat from where Romney was, but the actual policy gap between Trump and Romney on immigration was modest compared with the very large gaps between Romney and McCain on immigration or between Romney and Trump on Social Security or Medicare or marriage equality.

I bring this up because I think in a lot of ways the core difference between the Slow Boring worldview and the conventional wisdom among Democrats is that most Democrats see Trump’s success as a counterexample to the idea that moderation has political benefits while I see Trump as an illustration of the principle. He is dragged down by a lot of personal misconduct but boosted by having moved to the center to neutralize Obama’s best attacks on Romney. His immigration politics is way to the right of where Bush and McCain were but only modestly to the right of where Romney was.

Conversely, a lot of Republicans are convinced that Trump won where Romney lost because Trump is a scumbag and Romney was an honorable and decent man. This serves to rationalize all kinds of scumbag behavior when the simple truth is that Romney — or some other honorable and decent person — could easily have won if he had Trump’s views on Social Security and Medicare.

Greg M: Given that the least hospitable place on earth is 1000x more hospitable, manageable, more easily constructed, resourced, and populated than literally anywhere else NOT on earth, why the obsession with colonizing the moon or another planet?

Who is this question directed toward? I’m not moving to the moon, I’m with you.

I mentioned this in a totally different context in last week’s Mailbag, but I think your question is a great example of a topic where what I really want is a clearer index of what the conventional wisdom is. Are there a lot of people obsessed with colonizing other planets? Obviously Elon Musk specifically is very interested in this, but my sense is that he’s pretty idiosyncratic in this regard, just as you don’t see a lot of people who combine hard-right politics with enthusiasm for solar energy. But I might be wrong and tons of people are obsessed with colonizing Mars when they should really be thinking about why nobody wants to live in northern Canada instead.

Romulus Augustus: Could you suss out a bit on how Obama post 2014 election disappointed you? I feel the same way because I think he veered into a “proto Woke” policies on the sort of Kendist view that any disparity in school discipline was evidence of racism and his unliberal view on due process for young men accused of sexual assault on college campuses. I would also suggest he gave too much credence to the BLM narrative about the police at times during these years.

Are there other issues you had a problem with during those years and how do you rate him overall?

I might quibble on the margin, but I think the way the Obama administration handled B.L.M. was basically fine. Where I criticize late-Obamaism is on three fronts — all of which ended up making it a lot harder for Hillary Clinton to win in 2016.

One, as you allude to, is that in the summer of 2013 a woman named Catherine Lhamon came into the administration from a pro bono law firm called Public Counsel and took the job of assistant secretary of education for civil rights. I don’t think anyone particularly took note of this at the time as it is not a very high-profile job. But in the role, she innovated in the use of “dear colleague” letters to nudge institutional change via the threat of civil rights litigation without going through a normal rule-making process. And she used these letters to suggest that school discipline policies should be evaluated through an extremely simplistic disparate impact framework, to maintain an unusually low evidentiary bar (“preponderance of the evidence” rather than “clear and convincing”) around campus sexual-assault charges, and also to substantially launch the trans sports wars. I would dismiss this as just one fairly reckless person in a job people weren’t paying much attention to, but the Biden administration brought her back for a second stint in the role.

Second, the Obama administration moved away from the rhetoric of an “all of the above” energy policy and signaled this shift by killing the Keystone XL pipeline even though doing so did not meet any remotely reasonable cost-benefit test. Unlike the Education Department stuff, this did not have any particularly tangible impact on anything in the short term. But also unlike the Education Department stuff, it was a high-profile, high-salience shift on something elites were paying a lot of attention to and discussing. We have seen some shift back toward sounder views over the past 12 months, with candidates like James Talarico and Mary Peltola serving as star recruits while climate champions Martin Heinrich and Sheldon Whitehouse negotiate a pragmatic energy permitting deal. But this was a dead end that Obama walked us into over the objections of at least some of his senior policy advisors.

Last but not least, the Obama administration pursued a series of individually defensible immigration policies that, taken together, did not make sense or work.

We got the move away from Secure Communities (a program to facilitate jailhouse ICE pickups for deportation), we got the effort to accept refugees from the civil war in Syria, we had the early phases of efforts by migrants and smugglers to exploit the asylum system at the southern border as a loophole in immigration laws, and we had Obama trying to do a kind of executive amnesty in what became the DAPA program that was blocked by the courts.

The premise of the big push in 2013 for comprehensive immigration reform was that the border had been secured and it was now safe to treat long-resident illegal workers generously without incentivizing a big surge in new arrivals. The administration did not want to back off that talking point even though, factually speaking, the revival of the American labor market plus the discovery of creative uses of asylum law was undermining border security by 2014-15.

They didn’t want to disappoint people by saying “hold up, we can’t make any changes on interior enforcement until we get a handle on asylum claims” or “hold up, we can’t admit new refugees from overseas while we’re swamped with asylum cases at the border.” But I think that if Hillary Clinton had won the election (which she lost in part thanks to a sense that Democrats were going soft on immigration enforcement), she would have inherited an unworkable policy mix.

All three of these shifts — on civil rights enforcement, energy policy, and immigration — sort of underscore that I think people really tend to misunderstand how the 2016 primary played into the 2016 general-election result. There’s a tremendous amount of unfocused bitterness from Hillary Clinton’s camp directed at the Bernie Sanders camp, paired with a lot of smugness from the Sanders camp.

Given how close the election was and how much Clinton was hurt by the particulars of the mauling she took at the hands of the media over the emails story, it’s at least somewhat plausible that Bernie would’ve won. What’s not at all plausible is the idea that the American public thought Clinton was too moderate. America’s policy “mood” had swung to the right during Obama’s eight years in office, which is the normal pattern. Voters found a lot about Trump alarming, but they were looking for a Democrat who would course-correct toward the center the way George H.W. Bush did relative to Ronald Reagan or the way Mark Carney did relative to Justin Trudeau. Clinton initially seemed like a promising candidate for that since her husband had been somewhat more moderate than Obama and since Obama drifted left in his final few years in office. An optimal campaign would have involved Clinton putting some distance between herself and these Obama initiatives (she, after all, ran to his right on immigration in 2008).

What happened instead was that Bernie got in the race and then gained some traction, Clinton became obsessed with locking down endorsements from all the Progressive Establishment interest groups, and as a result, she ended up running on a bunch of promises to govern to Obama’s left.

It turns out to be very hard to win three terms in a row. If you are unpopular (like George W. Bush was in 2008), that’s a problem in and of itself. If you’re politically successful (like Bill Clinton was in 2000 or Obama was in 2016), this raises expectations inside your coalition. You can either fail to meet those rising expectations, the way Democrats did in 2000, which risks substantial third-party defections, or else you can do what Democrats did in 2016 and attempt to meet them, which risks alienating voters in the middle.

Matt: Center right neocons in the Commentary/Dispatch orbit have been claiming vindication for their suspicion that Obama was always more hostile to Israel than he let on, a “third worldist at heart,” restrained only by political reality. In addition to Obama’s recent comments, they point to his early embrace of Mamdani, which they argue had no political logic since unlike with AES, he didn’t have to win for Democrats to take the senate. They allege that while Obama wasn’t literally a Muslim socialist born in Africa, his background (including his ties to Jeremiah Wright) was close enough for him to feel an intuitive affinity for an actual Muslim socialist born in Africa (Mamdani). Do you think there’s any merit to this theory they’re spinning up?

I think you have to remember that Benjamin Netanyahu worked hard to torpedo Obama’s signature second-term diplomatic initiative, the Joint Comprehensive Plan of Action with Iran. He wasn’t just against it; he flew to the United States to accept an invitation from congressional Republicans to deliver a speech to a joint session of Congress to campaign against it. Beyond Netanyahu’s specific behavior on this, we had a great opportunity for the leaders of America’s pro-Israel organizations and major Jewish institutions to say something like, “Hey, we respect Israel’s right to elect its own leaders and make its own foreign-policy decisions, but we think that setting the bar for ‘supports Israel’ at ‘defers to all Israeli foreign-policy judgments even on questions that have significant stakes for the world unrelated to Israel’ is not reasonable.” But they didn’t do that.

At the time, the proponents of the J.C.P.O.A. pointed out that a non-J.C.P.O.A. scenario would involve either an Iranian nuclear weapon or else a costly regional war. The opposition, including Netanyahu, denied this and promised that a nebulous “better deal” was in the offing. Trump won in 2016, tore up the deal, and yadda yadda yadda we are now in the costly regional war.

This has had serious negative consequences for the economic well-being of the American people. It’s also had serious negative consequences for the economic well-being of people in basically every country of the world. That’s destabilizing European politics and giving a lot of ins for pro-Russian parties. In poor countries around the world, the problems are even more severe. There’s a basic cooking-fuel crisis in India.

I think it is probably fair to say that Obama feels very strongly that he was right about Iran, that Netanyahu was wrong about Iran, and that it reflects very poorly on pro-Israel groups that they (at best) stood by and (at worst) actively assisted his efforts to bring the world to this point. To me, that set of experiences probably does a lot more to explain Obama’s views than anything about Jeremiah Wright. Now of course the way right-wing Jews have always seen it, Obama’s support of the J.C.P.O.A. simply reflected his deep-seated hostility to Israel. From where I sit, though, the fact that he was completely correct that the alternative to J.C.P.O.A. was war and the fact that everyone hates the war ought to cause them to reassess their own interpretation of Obama — a guy who was just looking out for America’s basic interests in this situation.

Siddhartha Roychowdhury: Instead of taxing unearned income via the currently proposed wealth tax laws, would it make more sense to force divestment in small amounts — let’s say 1% every year, after individual stocks have been held for 25-30 years? Would that be more politically palatable and more market friendly?

I guess? But I think the drive to tax unrealized gains suffers from an unclear rationale. Obviously people have a lot of different views about tax policy. But among the set of people who favor a wealth tax or a tax on unrealized capital gains, I think everyone also agrees with the following more moderate ideas:

  1. Heirs (especially heirs to untaxed inheritances) should not benefit from the stepped-up basis tax break when they realize capital gains.

  2. If you address the issue of stepped-up basis, then the optimal tax rate on capital gains becomes higher and you should raise it to that higher level.

  3. You shouldn’t be allowed to do things like place illiquid, hard-to-value shares in private companies into a Roth I.R.A. and get huge tax-free windfalls.

  4. We should address the myriad estate-planning tools like Crummey trusts that allow people to legally avoid estate taxes.

  5. We should actually audit the tax returns of owners of closely held businesses so they can’t get away with disguising consumption as business expenses.

  6. We should cap the permitted size of a pass-through corporation and force all large businesses to pay taxes on the corporate level.

I completely get that this laundry list of nerdy technical changes is not as fun a thing to say as “Tax the wealth!” But the reason you end up with a laundry list of nerdy technical changes is that any kind of tax at all, in practice, ends up generating lots of nerdy implementation issues. Proposing a brand new thing is, from the standpoint of constructing a column or a slogan, a fun alternative to engaging with the details. But that’s not a real policy. Any kind of tax is going to feature tons of implementation details and tons of efforts to find loopholes. If you could create an idealized form of our present system of income and estate taxation (essentially what I am proposing above), is there any actual reason on the merits to believe it’s very important to be taxing paper wealth that is neither being consumed nor passed to heirs?

On a practical level, obviously, part of the issue is that people are looking for a magical funding source for big new programs.

But macroeconomically speaking this doesn’t work. If you try to pay for a large increase in society’s consumption of child care services, or health care services, or higher education services, or whatever it is you want by taxing non-consumed wealth, you are generating the exact same inflation problem that you would have if you paid for it by printing the money. It actually might be worse because, over and above the assets liquidated to pay the tax by taxing unrealized gains, you are encouraging the wealthy to save less and consume more.

Similarly, the idea of a wealth tax gets mixed up in people’s heads with the idea of attacking the political influence of the wealthy. But consider Elon Musk, who has given about $366 million to Republican candidates so far this cycle. Musk’s net worth is currently estimated at about $900 billion. Making him pay a 1 percent wealth tax would be neither here nor there from the standpoint of his ability to donate something like 0.04 percent of his net worth to Republican Party midterm candidates. What it would do is highly motivate billionaires to spend lots of money on attacking the wealth tax. Not just because they wouldn’t want to pay the tax but because, again, the existence of the wealth tax encourages the wealthy to save less and spend more. But what are they going to spend on? I doubt Jeff Bezos needs a second yacht, and Musk seems happy with zero yachts. They’re going to spend on politics.

I really think my six nerdy changes address what is addressable here and they do so by solving implementation problems rather than creating new ones.

An additional idea that I would consider is shifting the estate tax to be an inheritance tax. The idea would be that passing $1 billion to one person would incur a larger tax burden than passing $100 million each to 10 different people. I think it is a little bit underrated that concerns about multi-generational entrenched oligarchies are largely a function of family size. The reason the very top echelon of wealth in America is dominated by entrepreneurs rather than Walton heirs is that Sam Walton had four kids. Bezos (number two on the Bloomberg Billionaires Index) is a lot richer than Larry Page in third place, but because Bezos has four kids and Page only has two, each Page child stands to inherit substantially more money. Taxing inheritances rather than estates is not so much a question of tax justice for the Bezos children as just to encourage the super-rich, at the margin, to disperse their fortunes broadly. Maybe think of some nieces or nephews or cousins. The point is just that the process of intergenerational downward mobility normally involves an element of taxation but also just an element of spreading the wealth around within the family, and the tax piece should be designed to complement general dispersion of money.

Sasha Gusev: In a hypothetical D+5 election, is it better for Democrats to run a YIMBY candidate that will win by 2 or a NIMBY candidate that will win by 10? In other words, would you advocate for the candidate with the best policy that can still eek out a win or a candidate that will run up the scoreboard regardless of policy?

A candidate for what, for starters?

All else being equal, I would like to see members of Congress with good opinions on housing win, but I also don’t think federal candidates’ housing views are very substantively significant, and I wouldn’t want to blow political capital on it. If you’re talking about a governor of a badly under-housed state, then things are different. Housing scarcity is legitimately the number one policy issue in California, New York, Massachusetts, and a few other states, so if you’re not willing to run some risks on that, you’re not really in politics for any reason at all.

But I also don’t think there’s any general reason to believe that going from YIMBY to NIMBY generates an eight-point swing in a candidate’s favor. That’s probably true specifically in the NIMBYest places in the country, but we don’t in general see YIMBY candidates underperforming. For a Democrat, embracing housing abundance is a way to demonstrate some pro-growth, pro-business bona fides and some distance from the hard left. Or to put it another way, if you’re running as a YIMBY in a way that is generally alienating to most voters, then I think you’re probably doing it wrong.

Of course, you might have a solid housing champion who’s just politically unpopular for other reasons. Scott Wiener, for example, is probably way too progressive on non-housing issues for most voters even though he’s a moderate in the specific context of San Francisco. You could imagine a primary pitting someone like that against a generally more electable moderate who’s wishy-washy on housing. So the NIMBY has an electoral edge, but the edge comes from non-housing factors. In California, I’d definitely go for Wiener under those circumstances. But not in Wisconsin or Michigan or Georgia.

Pat T: How do Blue Dogs seem cooler?

This is why I feel that Bobby Pulido’s race is more strategic than the value of the single House seat would indicate. He is very cool and would be joining two existing great Hispanic Blue Dogs in Henry Cuellar and Vicente Gonzalez. Hopefully Johnny Garcia will also win. Cuellar and Gonzalez are not big national media figures, but Pulido is an actual super-charismatic star personality and “moderate Hispanics” is a much broader political tendency at the national level than the kind of quirky coastal communities like Jared Golden and Marie Gluesenkamp Perez represent.
