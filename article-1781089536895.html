The biggest issue in American politics doesn't exist yet

AI is everywhere. Does anybody care?

Lakshya Jain
Jun 10
∙
Paid
 
READ IN APP
 

Welcome back to The Argument’s poll series, where we survey Americans on the issues everyone’s fighting about. Our full crosstabs are available below the paywall at the end of this post. Our last surveys have asked about crime, the economy, gender issues, immigration, education and parenting, the lingering politics of COVID-19, immigration, AI, and free speech. The Argument’s full methodology can be read here.

There’s a giant political prize sitting in the middle of American politics just waiting to be picked up.

In The Argument‘s latest national survey of 3,008 registered voters, we asked which party people trusted more on a long list of issues. On every other issue we tested — including the economy, health care, crime, and corruption — at least 60% of voters could pick a side.

Then we asked about AI.

Share

Even though AI is everywhere, nobody cares about it yet. In fact, just 6% of voters ranked it as a top-two issue in our most recent polling. It’s close to dead last in voter prioritization at the moment, tied with climate change and above only LGBT issues.

Voters' top issues are cost of living, healthcare, and corruption
Which of the following issues are the most important to you? Please select two.

What’s more, the issue isn’t even politically polarized yet (unlike virtually everything else in America, at this point). Just 46% of voters could even pick a party that they trusted more on AI, which was easily the lowest number of any issue tested in our poll — and they split evenly between Democrats and Republicans.

Neither party owns AI as an issue right now
For each of the following issues, please indicate which party you trust more.

The same survey also showed that people are steeped in AI: Forty-two percent of voters said they use a chatbot weekly or daily, and a majority of users even said they enjoy it.

Most Americans use AI once a week or less
How frequently do you use AI in your day-to-day life, outside your job?

And yet, people are afraid of the consequences that may come with AI growth. As one example, 70% of people think that AI is “very likely” or “somewhat likely” to cause large-scale job losses within five years. And 27% of people believe that this same technology is “very likely” or “somewhat likely” to become so powerful that humanity itself goes extinct.

Voters are worried about the consequences of AI
Within the next 5-10 years, how likely do you think each of the following scenarios is?

It simply isn’t plausible to expect that it will stay a political afterthought. Instead, I think the more responsible read is arguably the bolder one: that AI is one of the most powerful latent political issues in America, and it has the potential to reshape the American political and issue landscape as we know it.

The electorate harbors deep and persistent reservations about the implications of AI. Large swaths of voters believe the consequences of AI could be disastrous and wide-ranging. And people aren’t really buying the bullish case for AI that CEOs and boosters alike are selling.

Voters are skeptical about the benefits of AI
Within the next 5-10 years, how likely do you think each of the following scenarios is?

In other words, the skepticism about AI’s effects is real and deep-running. And given how many people use it daily, this is not just an ill-informed set of opinions on something respondents have never seen before (like tariffs were before 2025).

The skepticism of the electorate on AI is not to be dismissed. Voters don’t believe that this technology, which has reshaped the world as we know it, comes without deep and troubling consequences.

Given the rate of AI advancement, and given the way in which AI’s importance has risen over the last few years, I don’t think that this depolarization or deprioritization is going to continue for too long. That means there’s a reservoir of opportunity for politicians to stake out a bold, concrete, and forward-thinking stand on the issue.

I should note that I don’t mean this in the traditional sense of “boldness” that politicians tend to espouse. I don’t actually think it’s super clear that hawkish, Luddite-esque views on AI are the way forward. People enjoy using it, and corporations across America have deeply embedded this into their employees’ workstreams already. Put simply, it isn’t going away altogether.

But voters also feel that if things continue on this track, there may not be many jobs left for the future. In fact, 61% of voters agreed with Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei’s stated views on the matter, that AI could wipe out half of all entry-level white-collar jobs in the next five years.

Americans fear AI could wipe out a lot of entry-level jobs
To what extent do you agree with the following statement: “AI could wipe out half of all entry-level white collar jobs in the next five years”?

So, it follows that voters are also surprisingly open to heavy federal regulation on AI automation in the workplace. A plurality of voters already believe that human workers in software engineering¹ and manufacturing should be protected from AI replacements via a federal ban, and significant chunks also support bans in other industries.

Voters oppose AI automation bans in most industries — except for software engineering
Some people have proposed banning the use of AI in certain industries to protect jobs. Other people say that such bans would needlessly hold back innovation. For each of the following industries, do you favor or oppose a federal ban on the use of artificial intelligence/automation to replace human workers?

Critically, it’s also not an issue that divides on partisan lines. In fact, in our survey, voters who backed Donald Trump in 2024 regularly voiced similar levels of support for federal bans on AI automation as Kamala Harris’ voters did.² That also indicates something to me: If AI is to become a future dividing line in American politics, it might also scramble the coalitions we’ve become used to in the Trump era.

Though Americans are famously more anti-government in attitude than many of their European counterparts, that doesn’t mean they want a free-for-all in cases like this. If AI does have large-scale, negative impacts on employment, our poll suggests that they will want their leaders to take action.

For instance, a federal jobs guarantee and a small “universal basic income” stipend both poll above water today. In fact, the federal jobs guarantee is among the most popular proposals I’ve ever tested in any poll, and notably polls better than it did in a Data For Progress poll from 2024.

A federal job guarantee is really, really popular
Do you support or oppose establishing a program where the federal government would guarantee a job to any adult citizen who wants to work but is unable to find a job?

What that suggests to me is that Americans may not be actively thinking about AI, but that there exists a real and serious underlying desire for action if things do get bad. And in the event that there are massive-scale job losses due to AI, our polling shows that not too many people want the market to freely dictate the course of events.

A plurality of voters favor job training programs as a response to AI-induced job losses
If there are widespread job losses due to AI, which of the following approaches do you prefer, even if none is exactly right?

These may be questions for the future, but it’s not as if AI has no electoral relevance today. Even at this early stage, some of its downstream and more immediate economic impacts are already obvious to voters.

For instance, Americans blame data centers for a spike in their power bills, with 62% of registered voters saying it had a “large” or “moderate” impact on electricity costs in their area. This has become a flashpoint in some 2026 races, especially amid an acute cost-of-living crisis. Once again, there isn’t a huge partisan split; majorities of voters of all political stripes believe this.

Voters think data centers are affecting local electricity costs
What impact, if any, do you believe data centers have on electricity costs in your area?

But this, again, is largely an indirect concern. Our poll mostly suggests that voters haven’t yet begun thinking of AI as a separate political issue, with the issue being surprisingly depolarized and of extremely low salience relative to its impact.

I wouldn’t bet on this holding. And it will be interesting to see what Democrats decide to do with respect to AI next year, given that they will probably take the House back. With Donald Trump’s approval sitting at an abysmal -23, our survey finds Democrats once again leading registered voters by 6 percentage points. Among voters who said they’re “probably” or “definitely” voting, that number rises to 8 points, which eclipses the 2018 blue wave margins.

Democrats lead on the generic ballot
If the 2026 general election for Congress were held today, which party's candidate would you be more likely to vote for in your district?

As we’ve covered already, that sort of environment would make the House an easy Democratic flip, while the Senate would be a toss-up. This means that with a split government looking like a probable midterm outcome, Democrats and Republicans will both have ample opportunity to stake out their party positions on AI in the buildup to the next presidential election.

That matters a lot. Because if AI keeps rising in power and impact alike, then I suspect that how both parties respond to this specific issue will have monumental impacts on our political landscape in a way that I don’t quite think anyone is ready for.

METHODOLOGY

Our poll was fielded between May 29 and June 3, 2026, and surveyed 3,008 registered voters across the nation. The sample was weighted to be representative of the universe of registered voters in the United States by race; age; gender; education; census region; race by gender; age by gender; race by education; modeled presidential partisanship by age, education and gender; and 2024 vote choice.

The margin of sampling error was plus or minus 1.8 percentage points. The design effect of the survey, which measures the loss of statistical precision due to weighting and design, was 1.20. Accounting for the design effect, the full margin of error was plus or minus 2.0 percentage points.

This survey was designed and weighted by The Argument. Data collection was performed by Verasight, and voter file data and weighting targets were obtained from Catalist. A full methodology statement is available on the last page of the survey PDF. A detailed explanation on how our surveys work is available here.
