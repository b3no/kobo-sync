Congress passed the biggest housing bill in a generation. Trump is holding it hostage.

The president said he will not sign it until lawmakers pass his SAVE America Act.

Halina Bennet
Jun 24
∙
Paid
 
READ IN APP
 

President Donald Trump Wednesday morning canceled plans to sign the 21st Century ROAD to Housing Act later that day, declaring on Truth Social that he would not sign it until Congress passes his SAVE America Act.

The House passed the bill with an overwhelmingly bipartisan vote of 358 to 32, which should have cleared the bill’s path to Trump’s desk. The Senate passed it 85 to 5 on Monday. Trump called passing the SAVE Act, which tightens voter eligibility rules, “a national emergency,” and the housing bill “of minor importance.”

Of course, under the U.S. Constitution, after a bill reaches the president’s desk, it becomes law automatically if he does nothing for 10 days, excluding Sundays, and Congress remains in session. If he vetoes before then, Congress clearly has the votes to override him — at least in theory. The president’s leverage here is nil, and regardless of his fight, it is likely that the bill will be enacted during the first week of July.

ROAD is, by any reasonable measure, the largest piece of federal housing legislation since the Housing and Community Development Act of 1992, which, among other provisions, expanded national affordable housing initiatives and reformed banking regulations. ROAD has had a long but productive journey amid intraparty clashes among Republicans, but its final passage by strong, bipartisan margins is a feat rarely seen by this Congress. Housing, it seems, has become one of the most urgent issues to tackle on both sides of the aisle.

The bill was negotiated by an odd-couple foursome — Senators Tim Scott (R-SC) and Elizabeth Warren (D-MA) in the Senate, Representatives French Hill (R-AR) and Maxine Waters (D-CA) in the House — and incorporates pieces of more than 60 separate bills, 36 of them originally bipartisan.

The substance, which leans heavily supply-side, includes:

  • Restrictions on large institutional investors (anyone who owns 350+ single-family homes) from buying more, with a carve-out for build-to-rent construction and a new Department of Housing and Urban Development office to help renters who occupy investor-owned properties navigate disputes. This was the most politically charged provision and the one Trump specifically claimed credit for, having spoken about his plans during this year’s State of the Union address.

  • Elimination of the permanent chassis requirement for manufactured homes. This piece could knock $5,000 to $10,000 off the cost of a manufactured home, according to an estimate by the Bipartisan Policy Center, and could make second stories and basements feasible.

  • A $200 million per year “Innovation Fund” that rewards localities for demonstrable zoning and permitting reform, plus a separate program tying Community Development Block Grant (C.D.B.G.) funding to housing production.

  • Streamlining of the National Environmental Policy Act across HOME, C.D.B.G., and U.S.D.A. infill housing programs. This was the most contentious item for House conservatives, some of whom wanted permitting reform that was more sweeping and felt this was a half-measure.

  • Lifting of the Rental Assistance Demonstration program cap by 100,000 units, creation of a Federal Housing Administration small-dollar mortgage pilot, reforms of the appraisal industry, and a temporary (not permanent) five-year ban on a Federal Reserve central-bank digital currency.

The Bipartisan Policy Center has the cleanest section-by-section breakdown if you want the full text of all 12 titles.

Drama has followed this bill all the way to the finish line. Punchbowl reports that Representative Anna Paulina Luna from Florida led a last-minute hardliner revolt during the House floor fight. She said Trump should veto the bill and threatened to tank unrelated rule votes if Speaker Mike Johnson moved it under suspension. Despite her attempts, the bill passed comfortably, with all 32 House “no” votes coming from Republicans and every voting Democrat in favor.

On the Senate side, the five no votes were Ron Johnson, Mike Lee, Rand Paul, Rick Scott, and Tommy Tuberville, with Lee specifically objecting that the ban on a Federal Reserve central-bank digital currency wasn’t permanent and Scott arguing that housing is fundamentally a local, not federal, problem.

This bill will help at the margins but is unlikely to move headline affordability numbers until a few years from now. Housing economists continue to point out that Washington doesn’t control zoning, the cost of labor and materials, and mortgage rates — each of which are bigger near-term levers than this bill’s provisions.

D.C.’s budget fight fills gaps in housing vouchers

The D.C. Council passed its $22 billion budget for fiscal year 2027 this week in a move that restores millions in funding that Mayor Muriel Bowser had proposed cutting as part of an effort to close a $1.1 billion budget gap. Notably, it includes housing vouchers that Bowser’s original proposal zeroed out entirely, the Washington Post reports.

Council members used roughly $450 million in additional funding — including $150 million from the city’s reserve funds — to preserve housing assistance programs and avoid reductions that advocates warned could worsen housing instability. The budget maintains current levels of voucher funding, though it still includes cuts to some housing-related programs, including emergency rental assistance.

The decision came with debate, with council leaders arguing the restored funding protects vulnerable residents, while Bowser and the city’s chief financial officer warn that relying on one-time funds and reserves could create significant budget challenges in future years.

“Several of the council’s budget choices, however well-intentioned, are steering the District toward a foreseeable and preventable $800 million problem next year,” Bowser wrote in a letter to the council last week, the Post reports.

California eyes an $11.25 billion housing bond for the November ballot

Governor Gavin Newsom, the California Assembly, and the state Senate struck a deal Monday to place the Veterans and Affordable Housing Bond Act of 2026 (SB 417) before voters this fall. The bill proposes $1.25 billion in self-supporting revenue bonds for the CalVet home-loan program plus $10 billion in general obligation bonds for affordable housing construction, rehabilitation, and preservation.

Officials cited by the state estimate that every dollar invested by the state pulls in roughly $4 in federal tax credits, local funding, and private financing, which is how they get to “tens of thousands” of units from $10 billion in spending.

This follows last year’s sweeping California Environmental Quality Act exemptions for housing, so officials are explicitly framing the latest push as moving from deregulation to investment.

Some state Republicans are questioning the necessity of borrowing additional money amid a large state budget and revenue increases due to the A.I. boom and subsequent success in the stock market, ABC10 reports.

“I’m the youngest member in the legislature, and everyone wants to borrow from the future to do today, but we can’t even do it today,” Republican assemblymember and vice chair of the Assembly Budget Committee David Tangipa of Fresno said to ABC10.

The market’s plot twist

There is an emerging, data-backed argument that the housing shortage narrative might not be as cut-and-dry as it seems, and that weakening demand could become a bigger force shaping affordability over the next decade.

The Mortgage Bankers Association put out a white paper arguing that an aging population, falling immigration, and rising inventory will structurally suppress housing demand for the next decade. Existing-home inventory rose 30 percent between 2024 and 2025; rental vacancy climbed from 5.6 percent in 2022 to 7.3 percent in 2025. M.B.A.’s own modeling projects 10.8 to 14.9 million units of supply growth from 2026 to 2035 — enough, they argue, to outpace demand and put real downward pressure on prices.

Harvard University’s Joint Center for Housing Studies released its annual State of the Nation’s Housing report last week. The shift from prior years is toward demand weakness, not just supply scarcity. Household growth fell to 1.1 million in 2025 from a prior pace of roughly 2 million a year.

Unsold new-home inventory rose 54 percent over two years to 127,000 units by January — the most since 2009. At the same time, affordability has not actually improved much: It now takes roughly $120,000 in household income to afford a typical home, and the share of for-sale listings affordable to a $75,000-income household has fallen from about half in 2019 to under a quarter today.

Cost burdens for renters hit a record high in 2024 even as vacancy rates rose. Aggregate supply and demand can rebalance even while the bottom of the market stays brutally undersupplied. The lowest-rent tier (under $1,000 per month, inflation-adjusted) has lost more than 7 million units since 2014.

The aggregate numbers suggest the macro housing shortage may be closing for reasons (demographics, immigration restrictions, economic anxiety suppressing household formation) that have nothing to do with policy success, while the low end of the market keeps getting worse because that’s a subsidy problem, not a supply problem.

Builders are feeling the demand slowdown directly.

KB Home’s second quarter earnings, released Tuesday, showed revenue of $1.11 billion, down 27 percent from $1.53 billion a year earlier, with net income falling from $107.9 million to $27.3 million. The stock traded down about 3 percent ahead of the release.

This follows an even uglier first quarter, when the company’s C.E.O. directly attributed softening sales to the Iran war hitting consumer confidence. KB Home, which was the first homebuilding company to be traded on the New York Stock Exchange, is leaning harder into its built-to-order model — 73 percent of net orders this quarter — as a hedge against the kind of unsold-spec-inventory glut that Harvard University’s Joint Center for Housing Studies is flagging nationally.
