The dismal economics and wild joy of parenting

Babies are a lot of work.

Two months ago, my family welcomed home our fourth, and he has thus far been wildly easier than the first three. He sleeps on his back, in his crib, for hours at a time — an ability I previously believed was a myth perpetuated by some sort of safe sleep industrial complex. He is sweet-tempered and takes his siblings’ manhandling with good grace, only crying when he has a legitimate complaint and stopping as soon as that complaint is addressed.

If he were our first baby, I would be unbearably judgmental of all those other people talking about how having a new baby is a several-months-long marathon of sleep deprivation.

Still, babies are a lot of work.

He is held almost all hours of the day. He generates an astonishing amount of laundry. For weeks, we were doing overnight shifts, with someone awake at all times to solve baby problems or be a baby sleeping service. He spends the whole evening nursing, and he goes through a lot of diapers.

If you were to ask someone who isn’t a parent what they know about parenting, they would almost certainly tell you they know that it’s hard. But while child care is hard, it is simply not true that it’s harder today than it ever has been.

However, it is harder today to know what parenting would be like than it ever has been.

A growing share of American families have only one child, so those children can’t see their nieces and nephews grow and get a sense from that of what parenting is like. Our cities have fewer children than even a decade ago, so young adults don’t see children in their day-to-day lives or get a concrete vision of what it would be like to raise them.

A lot of the most thoughtful adults I know are, for this reason, hesitant to have children. It’s a huge commitment to make, and it hardly seems wise to make it without having a real sense of what it will be like. And the fewer people who make the leap, the harder that sense is to get.

As my parental leave comes to an end, I want to make the case, as a parent and a liberal, that parenting is an incredibly worthwhile endeavor, one that is hard but joyful and full of meaning — and also one that we as a society could be making so much better.

The dismal economics of child care

Day care sticker prices are a bit shocking: In my county, infant care can run $26,800 a year. California, where I live, has gone hard on making it easy to open a residential home day care, but there still aren’t that many.

Day care is expensive because it requires an enormous amount of human effort. A single person cannot safely take care of more than a few babies. Many states allow each adult in a day care to be responsible for up to four infants, which honestly is a lot more than I could safely take care of myself. Therefore, eight hours a day of child care has to cost, at absolute minimum, one-quarter of an adult’s full salary and benefits.

The more broadly prosperous our society becomes, the more expensive that gets. On top of that, expecting that day care providers have licenses (or even degrees) raises the salary and benefits expectations of those workers.

This is classic Baumol’s cost disease. In my introductory economics textbook, it was introduced with an example of an orchestra: An orchestra cannot play a symphony without a certain minimum number of people, so labor-saving productivity enhancements are basically irrelevant. However, musicians can quit orchestras to do other things, like become lawyers or software engineers. As a result, orchestra wages have to keep up with musicians’ outside options, even though productivity in orchestras has not increased.

This was a poorly chosen example if the intent was to get me to take seriously one of the most important trends in labor productivity in the modern world, because an orchestra getting more expensive is simply not a big deal. Baumol’s cost disease is, in many cases, actually good for highly educated workers in low-productivity-growth industries, who will see their wages rise even though their productivity hasn’t, and if orchestras are more expensive, that’s fine. There’s no compelling government interest in making sure you can hear Beethoven’s 5th played live.

If child care is more expensive, though, that’s actually not fine.

I’m a liberal and embrace a pluralistic conception of the good: Having kids is one way to lead a good life but far from the only way, and I don’t want to try to engineer society to make people become parents if they don’t want to. But I do want society to be a place where the average person who does want to become a parent can.

Parenting is awesome! It is a valuable contribution to the world, it is demanding and meaningful and important, and it is, for many people, their most significant aspiration and life goal. It is absolutely essential that our society be one in which a pretty average person can realize that aspiration.

Instead, in my lifetime, we’ve made parenting much, much harder than it needs to be in ways that go way beyond the rising cost of child care.

Making a hard job harder

Parenting is more labor-intensive than it used to be. Sometimes I see people with the optimistic take that we’ve basically made parenting needlessly labor-intensive, that we could go back to doing what our grandmothers did and the kids would be all right — better off, even — if we relaxed. There are a few cases in which this is true — for example, the fact that it’s become taboo to leave 10-year-olds home alone is completely insane.

When The Argument polled American voters on parenting last December, 36% of respondents said it wasn’t acceptable to leave a child home alone for an hour until age 14 to 17, and a further 4% said it wasn’t acceptable until they were 18. For a kid who was babysitting at 12, it boggles my mind how quickly teenagers having some independence has become controversial.

Here, parents are simply shooting themselves in the foot. They are making their lives harder and not making their kids better off.

But not all of the added costs of parenting are like this.

Unfortunately, trade-offs are real, and there’s no free lunch. Our fourth baby sleeps in the crib, but this is very unusual in our experience: Absolutely none of his older siblings wanted to. The recommended sleeping environment to avoid SIDS is for a baby to be alone in a crib or bassinet on a firm mattress on their back with no blankets.

Babies hate this, I suspect, for all the same reasons you would hate sleeping on a firm mattress with no blankets. The sleeping environment babies want is snuggled up nice and warm. The problem is that sometimes they suffocate there.

I love being a cold-blooded technocrat in every conversation, but even I still flinch at saying “Have we gone too far in trying to help prevent babies from dying of suffocation?” I would take a bullet for my kids; am I really willing to say that it’s worth a small increase in the risk of them dying just so they and we can sleep better?

Of course, we all do this all the time, but rarely is it stated so explicitly: For instance, putting my kids in a car involves a small increase in the risk of them dying. If I categorically refuse to contemplate the risks, all I’ll be doing is ignoring the trade-offs I’m making.

So, to be explicit: Yes, there’s some increase in the risk of death that is an acceptable trade-off for sleeping better. Some organizations, including the American College of Nurse-Midwives and the Academy of Breastfeeding Medicine, have thankfully relented on this and published guidelines for relatively safe co-sleeping with your baby. But that’s the space we’re in: scary trade-offs, not free lunches.

Same thing with letting my kids bike all over town. Yes, kids did that all the time in the 1980s, and, in many ways, they were happier and healthier for it. Also, accident and injury death rates for children have fallen about 60% since 1980!

In the ‘80s, unsupervised kids mostly had wholesome and meaningful adventures. Occasionally, they tragically died in ways that would have been easily prevented if an adult had been present.

(This is part of why I’m such a rabid advocate for Waymos, one of the few interventions that would make children safer and freer at the same time.)

It’s worth it

I think it’s also important to acknowledge the ways in which parenting is easier today than ever before. The costs of modern intensive parenting are real, but so are the advantages of not needing to do laundry by hand or panic if your child refuses to eat the food that was their absolute favorite last week.

Parenting is hard. But the final element of my reflection on parental leave is that parenting is also wonderful beyond comprehension.

My 6-year-old will see his baby brother and wander over to lie down next to him and talk (in a way he doesn’t usually want to talk to me) about whatever’s on his mind: Why pasta is the best dinner or how rockets work or why it was a good idea to blockade the South during the Civil War. The baby watches him, rapt.

Recently, at my sister-in-law’s wedding, my 2-year-old wandered around telling everyone who would listen about her latest astonishing mathematical revelation, which is that 5 + 4 = 9 and 4 + 5 also = 9. My 9-year-old recently spent an afternoon with me designing a flowchart for her school newspaper to help her classmates decide which of the books she read this summer they should read first.

My life is good in ways I never expected and unexpected in ways that do me good.

Parenting is good. And we should ensure that, for everyone who wants it, it’s also possible.
