Prison Song

Aug 27
 
READ IN APP
 

Toxicity, System of a Down’s second studio album, was released September 4th, 2001.

Now, whoever was calling the shots at their label was probably smart enough to make sure Prison Song didn’t get released as a single, but Chop Suey!, Toxicity, and Aerials were regular players on MTV and Rock Radio.

I was in seventh grade, and all I wanted to do was grab a brush and put a little makeup to hide the scars and fade away the shake-up. I loved System of a Down. In fact, most of my peer group weren’t averse to the burgeoning Nu Metal genre that System of a Down got lumped into (in spite of definitely not being Nu Metal).

Thanks to the limited scope of mainstream media, there weren’t so many options, and most of us were resigned to saccharine pop, hip-hop, or nu metal. Whenever Korn or Limp Bizkit managed to beat out a Pop or Hip-Hop group on Total Request Live, it was a big deal for those of us who were not of the Pop Persuasion.

But, Back to Toxicity… Kind Of

An entire week after Toxicity was released, the September 11th attacks happened. In its wake came the Clear Channel Mandate, which was less an edict and more of a strong suggestion for radio stations under the Clear Channel umbrella (i.e. a shitload of radio stations) to avoid certain songs that might convey sensitive imagery after the attacks.

These suggestions ranged (confusingly) from John Lennon’s “Imagine” to (less confusingly) the entire discography of Rage Against the Machine. And of course Chop Suey! by System of a Down.

In December of 2001, I traveled to McAllen, Texas with my parents by way of the Amtrak train TEXAS EAGLE to visit my Grandpa for Christmas. Flying after 9/11 was avoided. Before I left, my sister provided me with a chainmail necklace that she had made, and a burned copy of Toxicity. I listened to it on repeat by way of a metallic red Discman for the entire train ride¹. And a train ride from Chicago to McAllen, Texas is really fucking long.

System of a Down trended toward the incredibly political side of the compass² when it came to their music in the same vein as Rage Against the Machine, and had already spent basically their entire career rallying for the US to simply acknowledge that the Armenian Genocide happened. And in the wake of the September 11th Attacks were openly vocal in their opposition of the War on Terror and the high-handed implementation of the Patriot Act…

While they dipped their toes into it with their next album, 2002’s Steal This Album!, going so far as to have the music video for “Boom!” be directed by Michael Moore.

The sentiments seemed to have solidified once they released 2005’s Mezmerize and Hypnotize albums.

In 2005, I was a junior in high school. And System of a Down was winning a Grammy for the best hard rock performance: B.Y.O.B. (Bring Your Own Bombs)

I remember sitting in my Television Production class talking about this album with multiple people. About how much we loved it, and as always, how cool it was that for one moment the airwaves cracked open and allowed something that wasn’t Pop music have a turn in the cultural zeitgeist. And I preferred it to Green Day’s American Idiot and a lot of other protest music occurring in the morass of George W’s presidency…. Lest we not forget the Black Eyed Peas’ chart-topping, and relatively safe Where is the Love? or P!nk’s Dear Mr. President.

System of a Down was almost a fluke in this ecosystem. Mainstream “protest” music (if you can call it that) tended to lean into “the president is stupid” or the sentiment that “We just need to come together and learn to love each other!”

System of a Down’s lyrics openly identify the systems (lol), who benefits from them, and how those who benefit operate within the system. They were far more confrontational than they were therapeutic. Bands that operate this way usually don’t get play on the radio. You weren’t going to hear Anti-Flag’s “Anatomy of Your Enemy” or “Justin” by Against Me! on the radio. But you would hear “B.Y.O.B”.

Over Twenty Years Later

It turns out… The machine my peers were raging against was, in fact, their parents grounding them.

It’s weird, sometimes, popping into social media and seeing what everyone is up to now. The same people who I once bonded with over a band, didn’t appear to actually be listening to the lyrics. They were just angry about being thirteen. They liked feeling edgy in the monoculture of boy bands and safe Hip-Hop that primarily dominated the airwaves. They were just as touched by “Chop Suey!” as they were by “Break Stuff.” Now some of them are touched by songs like Ordinary by Alex Warren (gag).

I’ll be incredibly frank here. I started thinking about System of a Down because I logged into Facebook and saw an old classmate posting Bootlicker Country music. I can vividly remember being in eighth grade with them and they were a System of a Down fan. They had Toxicity on CD (and not a burned copy, like I had). And now they’re posting Bootlicker country? How the heck did that happen? As I quietly went insane, wondering how you get from point A to point B, I fired up YouTube and started playing System. It still holds up for me.

In 2005, if you were young and countercultural-ish, George W. Bush was stupid, the Iraq war was bad. System of a Down was cool. So was Slipknot. So was Korn. If your parents agreed with it, you chose to disagree with it, because you were thirteen and your parents were gay and lame. Your parents were trying to build a prison and you wanted to break stuff.

There was no need to be curious about what the noises you put in your ears meant beyond the visceral feeling of BIG EMOTIONS and who you could bond with over it while popping into Hot Topic to get weird pants. And then they, inexplicably in the years between college and having children they turned into their own mothers and fathers. The prison, constructed³.

Or perhaps, they did listen to the lyrics and stopped caring.

The unfortunate thing about the late ’90s and mid-2000s ecosystem, where we did have bands like Rage Against the Machine and System on our TVs and radios, is simply that youths were and will always be fickle. I watched my peers hop, skip, and jump from nu metal to pop punk to emo to, inexplicably, dubstep. The Thing At The Current Time, while never seeking any deeper understanding of the content (if there was deeper understanding in the first place).

I don’t find this dissimilar to what The Barking Years had this to say about Normies and Woke:

“The normie did these things because the other people around them were doing these things and the normie is consensus-seeking animal.”

There always seems to be a pushback when social movements get to irritating and loud. When anyone lodges criticism against one side or the other, they present their perceived enemy as a caricature. Beliefs are like fashion trends. Once you see someone low-status wearing the same outfit you don’t want to wear that outfit anymore and you move along.

Some people might be very confused about why I call this publication (haha) “Normieworld.” By all accounts, I carry normie conventions. I’ve got an email job. I’ve got a home. I have a blue-collar husband, and I know how to make casseroles. Casseroles are delicious. I need a job to pay bills. Apartment life sucks compared to owning a home. And also, drug money is used to rig elections and train brutal corporate-sponsored dictators around the world. I hate the status quo. I hate “That’s just how it works!” passivity.

The conventions of normalcy sometimes get pushed as antithetical to any form of countercultural belief. The punks, goths, and emos of yesteryear changed their outfits, and quite a few of them also became passive members of society because they believed—or started believing— that’s just what you do.

I am not surprised that Limp Bizkit ended up being a mainstream band in that little pocket of time. I am surprised that System of a Down did, and how they persisted through the Bush years with relative success. In the now, Rock as a genre doesn’t really exist as it did when I was a kid. And any act that does speak to power (or whatever) of any genre doesn’t chart like System of a Down did. It just doesn’t happen even if there’s not a lack of artists doing that.

I feel bad that the people who enjoyed them at the same time I did, for whatever reason — Be it a lack of understanding or apathy in the wake thereof— seemingly not carrying that energy through their lives.

Drug money is used to rig elections and train brutal corporate sponsored dictators around the world. And you should feel really mad about it, even if you have a mortgage.⁴
