The seven most important midterms of all time

A subjective — but objectively correct — list

Wanted to draw everyone’s attention to the Substack Midterms Hub which is going to have content from Slow Boring and many of this platform’s other fine outlets today, tomorrow, and beyond — a great place to go for updates, results, commentary, analysis and more.

One week before Election Day, Nate Silver’s model said that Democrats had a 76 percent chance of taking the Senate tomorrow, while Lakshya Jain’s model said they had a 65 percent chance.

I know the people who do this kind of work mostly focus on questions like “does this model give good gambling advice” and ignore metaphysical issues. But I’m always interested in what these claims really mean. Statistically speaking, to get enough power to reliably distinguish between these forecasts, you’d need to test them across 100 elections, which you obviously can’t do because this is a real-world event and not an experiment.

In other words, while we’ll soon know which party controls the Senate, we will never know which model was correct.

And the models themselves don’t clearly distinguish between claims about epistemic uncertainty (things we do not know about the underlying state of the electorate) and a more metaphysical uncertainty (the ability of events-to-come to shape the future). Almost nobody actually cares about this, but even as a devotee of quantitative models who consumes them heavily, I find something unsatisfactory about this.

So, with nothing more meaningful or satisfying to say about the events that will unfold this week, I thought it could be interesting to take a tour of some of the most significant midterm elections of American history.

Midterms lack the personalistic drama of American presidential elections, but outside of foreign policy, Congress really is the main driving force of the American constitutional system, and midterm votes often bring big changes. The phenomenon of “midterm backlash” is something we’re accustomed to, but while it now operates more or less as a law of the political system, it’s not an actual law of physics — whether any given backlash emerges is somewhat contingent and actually drives much of the drama of American politics.

So, with that in mind, here are my picks (in chronological order) for the seven most consequential midterm elections.

1826 — the first midterm brushback

The 1824 presidential election featured the breakdown of the Virginia dynasty dynamic that dominated the early American republic and a passing of the torch from the founding generation to a newer one. Part of that breakdown was that for the first time, there was a strong multi-candidate field, a basically non-party election, and an Electoral College deadlock that was resolved by the House of Representatives.

This is arguably how the Constitution was “supposed to” function in the minds of its authors.

John Quincy Adams got Henry Clay’s supporters to back him in the congressional vote, and then Clay became secretary of state in the Adams administration. This kind of thing happens in all kinds of democracies and is a very normal part of representative government.

But supporters of the defeated Andrew Jackson castigated it as a “corrupt bargain” and began the work of organizing the country’s first modern political party — the Democrats — to elect supporters of Jackson all across the country.

Heading into 1826, pro-Jackson forces already held a majority of Senate seats, and they gained another. They also picked up nine House seats (there were only 213 members at the time) and flipped control of the majority there. What’s especially noteworthy is that though Jacksonian political power was anchored in the South, the party organization was genuinely national — Martin Van Buren of New York was a key organizer, most of the Jacksonian midterm House gains came in upstate New York, and the 1826 Senate pickup was in Maine — and created a quasi-stable political order that lasted until the Civil War.

The actual policy stakes of these 1820s controversies are a little low by contemporary standards, but I think the 1826 midterms really set the pattern of how American institutions actually work in practice.

1862 — the Union prevails

If you’re familiar with the broad patterns of American politics, it’s not a big surprise that Abraham Lincoln’s Republican Party lost ground in the 1862 House elections.

Notably, though, they retained their majority and actually gained ground in the Senate. As a result, Lincoln and the Republicans were brushed back but fundamentally able to continue governing. Appropriations, appointments, and so forth could continue on a party-line basis, and Republicans were granted two more years to prosecute the war and make the case for Lincoln’s re-election.

This is sort of the inverse of 1826.

The election results themselves were not particularly dramatic, but the lack of massive backlash was very consequential. Indeed, the Lincoln administration’s policies end up becoming more radical: The Emancipation Proclamation came in January 1863 and General Order 143 organizing the United States Colored Troops came in May of that year.

1874 — the end of Reconstruction

As an alternate history fan, I get a lot of questions about the disputed presidential election of 1876, which was resolved on terms that put a stop to Union military involvement in the South and basically ended Reconstruction.

I think the smart take on this, though, is that Reconstruction was basically cooked by the huge rebuke dealt to the Grant administration in the 1874 midterms.

The sweeping Democratic gains — nine Senate seats and 92 House seats — were not necessarily because of opposition to Reconstruction. A lot was in the mix in this race, notably the financial Panic of 1873 and corruption. But the baseline question was whether a Democratic Party that had fully incorporated barely reformed traitors and rebels in its Southern wing could be acceptable to Northern public opinion.

You could easily imagine a world where the answer is not yes and the cross-sectional Democratic Party basically doesn’t get rebuilt.

But what we saw in the 1870s was that Northern white views on Reconstruction were mixed at best and that lots of Northern whites agreed with the basic idea that re-incorporating white Southern elites into the American political system was more important than securing the rights of loyal African American Southerners. The 1874 outcome not only made the logistics of Reconstruction hard to maintain but also fundamentally convinced Republican leaders that this was a loser of an issue for them.

1918 — Wilson repudiated

Woodrow Wilson’s second term was an odd duck. He was narrowly re-elected in 1916 on a pledge to keep the United States out of the Great War, but his Democrats lost seats in Congress and actually fell short of a majority in the House of Representatives. Despite Republicans winning more seats, though, Democrats retained control of the chamber via a coalition agreement with Progressive Party and socialist members.

By Election Day 1918, America had entered the war and the public was sick of the Democrats.

They lost 22 House seats, the Progressive Party was wiped out, and Republicans picked up six Senate seats and four governorships while a bunch of state-level prohibition initiatives passed. These results doomed Wilson’s hopes for the United States to enter the League of Nations and set the stage for Republican domination of the 1920s.

In some ways this is a typical “six-year itch” midterm — we normally see the president’s party do especially badly in the midterm elections of his second term. But I think it’s noteworthy because the dynamic whereby Wilson led America into the war, America won the war, and then America walked away from the victorious wartime leader’s preferred resolution was pretty odd.

It’s also hard not to note that the fighting ended basically a week after the election and to wonder if the results would have been different had Germany signed the armistice sooner.

I’m not sure that world history would, in fact, have turned out radically better had Democrats done better in 1918 and America joined the League of Nations. But F.D.R. and his Secretary of State Cordell Hull and many other New Deal figures definitely believed that, and it played an important role in their thinking about the United Nations.

1938 — the failed purge

This was in some respects another typical “six-year itch” midterm where Republicans gained eight Senate seats, 81 House seats, and a staggering 12 governorships amidst a fairly broad-based backlash to the Roosevelt administration.

On the other hand, this still left Democrats with large majorities in both houses of Congress. Just on the numbers, it superficially looks like a replay of 1862, where what’s really significant isn’t the losses but Democrats’ ability to retain control.

The real story of this midterm, though, and what makes it unique, isn’t in the general elections but the primaries.

F.D.R. was frustrated by the number of conservative Democrats, especially from the South, who were blocking some of his New Deal program ideas despite his willingness to go along with Jim Crow — a willingness that extended to being unwilling to even verbally endorse an anti-lynching bill.

Roosevelt decided to dedicate a fireside chat to the need to defeat obstructionist Democrats in primaries, and he actively campaigned against several incumbent Democratic Party senators.

This attempted purge basically backfired. Almost everyone he targeted won re-election, and having been targeted only made them angrier at the White House. The fact that the purge was tried and failed emboldened other Southern Democrats who had some misgivings about the administration to defy it. In effect, a bipartisan “conservative coalition” took control of Congress in these midterms and mostly held on until Lyndon Johnson’s presidency.

But this midterm became mega-consequential beyond even those particulars simply because it established a strong bit of conventional wisdom that it was unproductive for a sitting president to publicly pressure same-party members of Congress in this way. Donald Trump successfully taking out John Cornyn and Bill Cassidy in primaries is so striking in part because it repudiates the lessons of 1938.

1994 — the birth of the modern House

Republicans gained a lot of seats in 1994 in a way that basically sealed the fate of the more progressive strands of Bill Clinton’s thinking.

What’s more important here, though, is that Republicans disrupted 40 straight years of Democratic House control.

For most of the post-New Deal period, the real question in the House was whether you’d have the aforementioned bipartisan conservative majority. The Reagan Revolution of 1981-82, for example, happened during a period of Democratic control of the House. But conservative “Boll Weevil” Democrats demanded that Tip O’Neill allow votes on Reaganite measures that split the caucus and often joined with Republicans to pass them. At other times, like during the Great Society or the late Nixon/Ford years, progressive majorities in Congress passed liberal bills. Republicans actually winning the majority fundamentally shifted how Congress operates toward a more parliamentary dynamic. The minority party now works relentlessly to try to win the majority, and the majority party exerts ruthless control over the floor.

Everything modern observers know about “how Congress works” is downstream of tactics Newt Gingrich used to win control in 1994, of the fact that they worked, and of how he chose to wield power after winning it.

2014 — the one I’m personally mad about

This election still upsets me whenever I think about it. Democrats lost nine Senate seats, some of them extremely closely, and hanging over the whole thing was an insane media panic about Ebola that vanished immediately after the story had done its necessary work for the G.O.P.

I think conservatives have lodged some valid complaints against liberal media bias over the years, but the way this midterm was handled, the coverage of Hillary Clinton’s emails, and the monstering of Joe Biden over ending the war in Afghanistan are the three huge counterexamples.

And precisely because the mainstream press does tend to have a liberal ideological inflection, negative coverage hurts Democrats way more than it hurts Republicans because soft Democratic voters care a lot about mainstream press coverage.

There was all this mockery of Mark Udall for constantly warning people that control of the Supreme Court and the future of Roe v. Wade could be at stake in the election, and he was 100 percent right. When I asked Claude if this one belonged on my list of the seven most important midterm elections, it told me no, that the only real stakes were the judiciary and that 1986 was very similar in that regard. Just so you all know that this is a human-written column, I rejected that bit of feedback. That year was a blip that turned out to be consequential for the Robert Bork nomination; 2014 eliminated the moderate wing of the Democratic Party.

We’re now having a refounding moment for Common Sense Democrats, but today’s reborn moderates are clearly in the tradition of Barack Obama rather than that of the old-time “to the right of Obama” moderates who had their last stand in 2014.

Honorable mentions

A lot of people talk about the 1974 Watergate midterm, which was certainly important in the sense that a lot of prominent 1980s and 1990s Democrats got their start in 1974. But I don’t actually think that crop of late-1970s Democratic majorities accomplished much that was interesting or noteworthy.

The 1810 midterms saw Henry Clay win his first congressional race and then get elected speaker as a freshman (a weird occurrence), and he was then an influential spokesperson on behalf of going to war with Britain. But I don’t really think the War of 1812 was consequential in the long term, and while Clay was a very important historical figure, I don’t really think that initial two years in the House was the important part of his legacy.

The 2006 midterms will always have a special place in my heart because I was there for them, but I think in the grand scheme of things, they’re a pretty standard-issue “six-year itch” story.

The 1866 midterms that delivered a decisive anti-Johnson majority in Congress and led to his impeachment are a cool story, but since he ultimately wasn’t removed from office, I think the significance is somewhat limited.

The 1998 and 2002 midterms that broke the pattern of the president’s party losing ground are both interesting, especially since they happened so close together. But I don’t think anything particularly huge happened as a result of them, and it really is just a coincidence that they occurred one after the other — it didn’t actually represent a departure from the historic pattern.

It’s also interesting to go way back to 1798, when John Adams’s Federalists gained seats in the midterms. But of course that was not exactly the wave of the future — the party lost the presidency in 1800 and eventually disappeared.

So, conceding that 2014 is a bit of an idiosyncratic choice, that’s where I stand. And we’ll see what happens tomorrow.
