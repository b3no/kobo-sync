Will we know when AI is taking our jobs?

AI is making economists get creative

AI’s impact on jobs could happen really fast, but economists’ ability to tell us what’s happening in the labor market is often delayed.

You might be used to hearing about employment numbers regularly: New jobs data comes out from the Bureau of Labor Statistics on the first Friday of every month, and it appears in news stories about the unemployment rate and the number of jobs gained or lost. But if you read the news and learn that the tech sector lost 57,000 jobs in the last year, that doesn’t necessarily mean that 57,000 software developers were displaced by AI coding agents.

Monthly jobs data tells us what happened — it does not tell us why it happened. To isolate causes, economists often need to wait for more data to accumulate.

“National statistical systems are not designed to track task-level AI reallocation at monthly cadence,” economist Pedro Serodio recently wrote on X, introducing his report on AI’s labor market impacts in the U.K. “The unprecedented speed of [AI] adoption means that aggregate figures may take a long time to reflect real-world impacts.”

Even one of Anthropic’s recent economic reports opens on a note of humility: From offshoring to the China Shock to the automation of factories, the report warned, the effects of past economic disruptions are still being hotly debated years after the fact.

Analyzing this disruption as it happens is a major challenge. It is not impossible, though.

If economists could collect data that more closely reflects real-world conditions, they could tell us more about what will happen, rather than what has happened long after it’s over. That’s why Alex Imas, an economist at the University of Chicago, has called for a “Manhattan Project” for more real-time economic data, and his sense of urgency is shared by other economists I spoke to for this story.

From scraping internet data to interviewing workers regularly, economists are attempting novel methods to keep up with change in the labor market. Below are the ones that I found most promising.

What is job “exposure” really?

So far, the most common measures have focused on AI’s capabilities — what tasks it could do — and not how it’s used in the real world.

The foundational paper on how AI will affect jobs was published by OpenAI in 2023, called “GPTs are GPTs.”¹ Its headline finding was that 80% of jobs could have at least one-tenth of their tasks exposed to AI, and 19% of jobs could have at least half of their tasks exposed.

People hear “exposure” and think, “This job is disappearing tomorrow!” but it’s more complicated than that.

Share

Exposure is measured by taking a job, breaking it down into a list of tasks and then seeing whether AI can speed up completion of that task by 50% or more.

If a task can be sped up by 50-plus percent it is considered exposed. If a job has a lot of exposed tasks, well, it tends to be considered at risk.

This is not how automation works in the real world:

If AI speeds up your work, your company might sell more of what you make. If each tax accountant can handle twice as many tax filings, their company might accept a lot more clients, increasing the need for accountants. (It depends on whether there is untapped potential in the market.)

Another thing that happens is people shift to the more important tasks within their job: If a management consultant automates slide deck creation, they might spend more time with clients, which could mean each consultant managing a bigger client portfolio. (Once again, depending on the demand for consultants, this could lead to their firm serving more clients overall.)

In other words, “exposure” can have some upsides. You can’t tell what will happen to a job based on whether AI speeds up its tasks.

What really matters to economists is the net effect of all these small changes: They need to understand how workers are using AI, how that shifts burdens between workers, and how that relates to overall demand for the products in that industry. AI will simply have different effects in different industries based on all of these details.

Option 1: Usage Data

One obvious way to handle this, then, is to look at what people ask AI to do.

This is what reports from Anthropic’s economic team examined. They anonymized user queries and matched those queries to tasks on the same list as that foundational exposure paper. This method looked at real AI usage, but in order to analyze the findings, researchers had to make some pretty big assumptions.

For instance, to figure out whether a query was augmenting or automating work, they basically guessed based on whether a user iterated on the AI’s output or vice versa. In essence, if a user asked for advice on doing their own work, the researchers classified the use as augmentative. But if a user asked AI to do the work itself, the researchers classified that as automative.² In the earliest of these papers, Anthropic concluded that 57% of uses were augmentative while 43% were replacing human tasks.

Whether or not someone asks a follow-up question just isn’t that good of a proxy for whether the task they are doing is being replaced. For instance, you could ask Claude to draft an email for you and then just edit it in Gmail yourself. Assuming that no chat response is the same as replacing the task is a big guess.

This makes one of the major limitations of usage data clear — you don’t know what’s actually happening outside of the Claude window or what’s happening off the computer altogether. That’s what matters most: how workers reallocate their newfound time from productivity gains.

Another limitation of AI usage data is that since the data is anonymized, researchers don’t know who is employing the chatbot.

If a software engineer requests a coding task, that’s different than if an accountant requests the same task. The accountant is doing something new, outside of their traditional role. But all you can see with usage data is what kind of task was requested, not which kind of worker requested it.

But the main issue is finding out what’s happening offline, outside of the Claude window, and that suggests another possible method: asking workers themselves.³

Option 2: Interviewing

Even before AI, executives at big companies had the challenge of understanding exactly how their employees were working. AI deployment only accentuates the need. To understand how the private sector has tried to solve this, I spoke with Christophe Martel, who co-founded a software business designed to help large companies interview employees about their work.

Martel’s foundational premise is that the important information “doesn’t live inside tasks, it lives between them.” This is in contrast with both approaches above, which start with a list of tasks and ask whether AI could do them or (in Anthropic’s case) whether AI is doing them. Martel believes the critical detail lives “in the handoffs, the judgment calls about which tool or data source to trust, the policy exceptions that require another team’s sign-off.”

The detail lives in “workflows.”

This is why capability-based measures, and even usage-based measures, fall short — they tend to suppress the complexity of real human organizations in favor of easy-to-measure lists of tasks. Those measures ask whether a single step had AI involved, while interviewing lets you see if speeding up a single step really advances the overall outcome.

“The question is not ‘did the step complete,’” Martel emphasized to me, “But ‘did the sequence of steps actually produce the outcome it exists to produce’”

In general, the recent research papers that mirror Martel’s interviewing approach seem to agree, but their challenge is scaling the approach so they can collect enough data to speak to the economy as a whole. Interviewing is both subject to low response rates and, according to Martel, depends heavily on how well you can specify questions to the worker’s real-world work. (Specifying questions to workplaces naturally limits the ability to aggregate data across all workplaces.)

One paper tried to overcome this by designing LLMs that can interview employees in what’s known as a “semi-structured” format, the benefit of which is “adaptively diving deeper into unanticipated participant responses.” It allows workers to share the rich details of their work.

Another recent paper mimicked the “semi-structured” interview approach and combined it with a richer picture of human-AI collaboration. Rather than assuming AI can either speed up a task or not — like the GPTs paper — it divided automation into a scale. That scale ranged from AI doing everything to the human and the AI collaborating to the human needing to guide the AI through each step of a task.

This paper also asked these workers what they want in terms of AI taking over tasks. It finds that workers actually have a positive attitude about AI automating 46.1% of work tasks, “even after explicitly considering concerns such as job loss and reduced enjoyment.” Generally, those tasks clustered into low-value, repetitive tasks, which emphasizes the kind of job augmentation that leads to workers focusing on their more important tasks.

Option 3: Scraping job postings

The third approach is to see what companies want by looking at their job postings data.

Revelio Labs is a well-known economics research firm that pulls job postings off the internet, deduplicates them, and breaks them down into the tasks in their job descriptions to pick up on trends. In essence, rather than using the same government database that the papers above use to itemize tasks — O*NET, widely used across labor economics — it makes its own.

The advantage is that it’s dynamic, tracking the job market as it evolves. And, crucially, it doesn’t just track job titles; it pushes down to the level of tasks. This is important because when automation or augmentation happens, what’s really happening is a change in the composition of work, which later has effects on roles overall.⁴

By doing this, Revelio can report findings like the fact that “leadership skills” appear to command a growing premium on the job market, particularly in AI-exposed fields. This suggests that as AI takes over certain rote tasks, the other parts of workers’ roles are becoming more valuable — a clear sign of augmentation.

The limit here is that job postings are a noisy signal: It’s rare that you look back at your job posting after you start and it matches the tasks exactly; sometimes companies post jobs but never really attempt to fill them; other times the posting is buffed up to be more appealing to the job candidate.

“When you have large-scale technological disruption like AI, the forward-looking nature of the postings becomes a real strength,” Caelan Wilkie-Rogers, a Revelio analyst, emphasized to me. “Employers write postings for the jobs they see themselves needing.”

Option 4: Broader, more frequent price data

If AI makes law firms 10 times more productive, they have two options, either fire 90% of their staff or provide 10 times as many services (or somewhere in between). How do we know what will happen?

In order to figure this out, economists need to figure out what consumers will do when the price of legal services (or anything else) drops. Historically, when weaving made textiles much cheaper, textile employment still grew!⁵ Why? Because humans just all started buying way more clothes.

Share

To figure out in advance whether industries are more likely to expand or contract when productivity-enhancing technology gets introduced, researchers have to find out what consumers do when prices drop. But that requires knowing what the prices of a variety of goods and services actually are.

There are some areas of the economy where we have very good real-time price data — something like the cost of eggs is knowable on a weekly basis because there is really frequent data on fast-moving consumer goods like this.⁶

But, while that data exists for eggs and Doritos, it doesn’t exist for software, legal services, or the outputs of other industries most subject to AI’s effects — and gathering it could be the key for economists to reverse engineer how AI will play out in the labor market.

In theory, we should be in a golden age for that kind of information.

According to data from the Federal Reserve Financial Services, by 2024, credit and debit payments were 65% of all payments. In a cash economy, the only data that is easy to collect at point-of-sale is consumer scanner data. Now, we buy so much digitally that we should be able to collect a wider range of this information.

Unfortunately, the data is scattered across various payments processors, consultancies, and private companies, which would require a “Manhattan Project” for economics data to solve.

There is no pink slip

It should be clear at this point that economists actually have a really difficult job to do. Automation doesn’t work in the way people imagine it does: No one walks into your office with a pink slip and says, “We’re sorry, but AI can do this now.”

Instead, it could look like this: Workers get faster and companies don’t need to hire as much. At first, maybe wages go up because everyone is providing more value in the same amount of time, but later, salaries level off as demand for the role weakens.

Or maybe salaries don’t level off and new roles’ productivity spurs more firms and more demand for labor.

Labor economics is about modeling the balance of these forces.

While no method of measuring AI’s impact is perfect, life and politics don’t wait for researchers to develop perfect statistical methods and survey tools. Decisions are being made right now about how and whether to regulate AI and safeguard American workers.

Economists — and the rest of us — are in a race against time.
