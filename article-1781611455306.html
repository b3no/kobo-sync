I Dress For My Personality, And My Personality Is Annoying

I'm realizing the way I dress has been just as socially detrimental as my lack of social cues--perhaps they're the same thing. But how much do I really need to change myself for other people?

For a long time now, I’ve joked that I’m obsessed with social dynamics because I am not naturally gifted at socializing. Call it high-functioning undiagnosed autism, call it just being annoying, but it is what it is, and I didn’t choose it. This lack of talent forced me to examine social dynamics in a way most people never have to. I “taught myself” how to be likable (or at least not extremely off-putting) at the age of twenty-eight (I wrote about that—and what I learned—here). And when I wrote about why nobody comes to my parties, one of my favorite podcasts, Search Engine with PJ Vogt, reached out about it and asked me if I wanted PJ to leverage his journalistic skills to figure out why my parties have such low yield. (I’m underplaying it here but I’ve been a parasocial PJ fan for years so I was actually hyperventilating with excitement.)

Going into the episode interview, I kind of already knew why RSVPs have been a challenge—a lot of people don’t like me. I’m a lot. I am, as many people would kindly put it, “insufferable.” I don’t annoy people to be malicious, it’s just how I am. And as I’ve tried harder to tamp down this part of me, my social life has gotten better, so obviously this isn’t just my unfounded insecurity. It’s not terribly uplifting to say, “the moral of the story is don’t be yourself,” but that masking at least a little, at least at the beginning, has obviously been the right move for me.

But one thing that came up on the episode—and in previous conversations—is that the way I dress puts people off, especially women. In the, at times, not-so-nice Reddit thread that discussed the episode, multiple people specifically cited my attire as off-putting, immodest, and even “yikes.” The same has been said bluntly even by women who subscribe to me here, often women who love everything else about my work (so I know they’re just speaking honestly and not trying to be mean.)

Although I don’t write about it nearly as often, I’m realizing that the struggles I’ve faced with socialization are not unlike the struggles I’ve faced with fashion, and in many ways, they are interconnected since my fashion appears to be my number two social detriment, right behind impromptu Trump impressions. Although I love fashion and take great pleasure in it, I can’t deny that two of the things I love to do—plan outfits and socialize—are two things at which I am horribly incompetent when combined.

A lot of people who struggle with fashion are people who don’t actually enjoy fashion. Their problem is pretty easy to solve. Once you give them the basics, the clothing that says, at a maximum, “I am a normal human being who wears shirts and pants that fit me and are appropriate for the occasion,” they’re fine. Eventually, they may come out of their shell and experiment with a fun scarf or something. The same goes with people who suck at socializing. Most of the time, they aren’t the same kind of people who love socializing, and they’re bad at it precisely because it’s not their thing. But I am, as many people would say in a variety of situations, “something else.” Similarly to how people might describe my socialization habits or personality, I could never be accused of doing too little with my clothes.

But every time I think about this, I return to one main question: to what degree do I owe it to other people to change myself? It’s an amoral and simple cost/benefit analysis. I can be “who I am” (which very few people like, at least not if it’s all at once) and then have people mostly dislike me, or I can tweak things and be liked. And surely, all of us have had to do this from time to time. None of us attends a job interview wearing sweatpants, so should I really be flummoxed about the idea of consciously covering up and toning down the brightness and patterns (and perhaps, unsolicited standup comedy) if I’m going to be around other moms?

It’s no surprise that fashion and socializing would occupy the same obsessional part of my brain, because for as long as I can remember, I used fashion as a way to connect with other people—while getting both the “fashion” and the “other people” part egregiously wrong, most of the time.

As a little girl, when my parents brought me to restaurants, I would walk around the tables with my coat open like some kind of subway flasher, hoping random adults would compliment me on my dress. I meticulously picked my outfits for school as early as I can remember (by first grade, I was obsessed with “giving Little Princess schoolgirl” although I didn’t phrase it that way exactly.)

I put tremendous effort into my clothes—even at an age far before I was concerned with boys or popularity. I wanted my clothes to say, “I have a story!” and of course, little kids can’t be “bad dressers” because they look cute in basically everything, but as you can see here, at the age of six or seven I thought it was a really good idea to pair blush pink, dark green, black and…cobalt tights? And I loved waist belts before I even had a waist!

As I got older, fashion unsurprisingly became less of a wholesome form of self-expression and a bit more self-conscious. Suddenly, I had to worry about popular girls, cliques, “aesthetics” that denoted certain social strata, and of course, boys. What kind of person did I want people to think I was? When I was six, I was usually just goofing around with colors and pretending to be various forms of Victorian orphans. By ten, I was really worried about whether or not my fake Tiffany’s heart charm necklace from Claire’s was believable. By twelve, I was worried that tube tops were making my boobs look too small and I’d never get a boyfriend.

And my fashion was already a big social detriment, if not my main problem, by age twelve. My Goth phase in seventh grade did me no favors—instead of being embraced by a cool group of Goth kids (which, at my school, didn’t exist) other girls just spread rumors that I was having sex with grown men outside of school. Meanwhile, I thought I was giving Elvira realness! I knew just enough about fashion and socializing to try really hard and nevertheless be bad at it in a way that was doubly embarrassing because I clearly gave such a shit. Sometimes, I’d even make a good first impression, or even a good second impression, but things would fall apart. Often, I was painfully aware the shift had happened! However, I was unsure exactly why. I would come away from social interactions, aware that things went badly, but not sure what I had done wrong.

I have one example of this happening, although in this case you might be wondering how the hell I was too obtuse to notice. My only defense was that everyone involved was very drunk. When I was about twenty-five, at a party in San Francisco, everyone was drinking and being silly, sharing increasingly ridiculous stories, so I thought it would be an appropriate time to tell a story too. Without going into detail and alienating all of you, I will confirm that the story was incredibly gross, but not about me or anyone these people would ever meet. The men didn’t seem to care, but the women actively shunned me for the rest of the night. Despite the fact that the disgusting story was obviously the reason why, it didn’t occur to me for a long time until one of the women actually told me, while she was apologizing for “how much shit we talk about you.” Shit? We? TALK, as in ONGOING??

Okay, okay, I’ll tell you. I knew a girl who had a diva cup dislodge spill out onto an unsuspecting man during oral sex. You’re welcome.

Anyway, the same is true with fashion—not the oral sex part, but the “I did something wrong and I don’t know what” part.

Fashion is obviously much lower-stakes than socialization. It feels good to wear nice clothes and enjoy how you look, but there is no fashion equivalent of the loneliness epidemic, even though I think barrel jeans are a crisis in their own right. But for better or worse, we dress for other people. For the most part, we do not dress the same way at home by ourselves as we do when we attend a party or a gathering. Perhaps most of us do not consciously think, “I want someone to notice my clothes and think I’m an XYZ type of person” but I think for most people it’s effortless, so there’s nothing to consider. Most people are normal people who wear normal clothes, and they never have to think about it beyond finding the right size, flattering colors, or dressing for their body types. “Being yourself,” is very easy if “yourself” is roast chicken with mashed potatoes. But me? I’m pickled herring with a side order of sea urchin. 10% of people will really like me, 80% will say, “Hmmm, not really for me,” and another 10% will go into instant anaphylactic shock.

It stands to reason that my clothes would reflect my personality. And shouldn’t they? But in my instance, my personality is such an acquired taste (and so are my clothes—or they were, for most of my life) that I couldn’t very well keep dressing like this and then wonder why women didn’t like me. (Photo from 2013—Modcloth and the Valencia filter gone wild.)

And then comes the defensiveness. The cope. The explanations. Most of the people who didn’t like me were women, so I assumed they were jealous. (In hindsight, there were probably plenty of men who didn’t like me either, but I wasn’t trying to make friends with men so I never noticed.) I ignored the fact that women prettier than I was still had lots of female friends, and told myself I must be conveniently bumping into really toxic women all the time. It did not occur to me that fashion was almost exactly the same as social skills. I could be/dress “myself” and accept the consequences, or make changes. Nobody owes me friendship, or really anything beyond politeness. I was sort of like the bizarre version of an guy who wants a girlfriend but refuses to talk about anything other than maps because it’s his special interest. Women are not obligated to date Map Guy. Map Guy also is not obligated to change his behavior to placate women. But he will remain single if he doesn’t—or unless he’s really lucky and stumbles upon a woman equally obsessed with maps.

It all reminds me of the TV show, What Not to Wear. This was a reality show I watched in the 2000s, where badly-dressed people were subject to fashion interventions (often at the behest of their concerned families and friends.) Dutifully, fashion experts Clinton Kelly and Stacy London would do their best to convince the contestant that their “fun” clothing was actually embarrassing, and give them a makeover. Typically, this would actually be more of a make-under, especially when the subject was a woman, especially a woman over a certain age who was, you guessed it, too much.

In particular, I think about an episode starring a flight attendant named Denise, who Stacy accused of “doing a character” of a bimbo or airhead and using her over-the-top makeup and clothing to signal her ditziness. Denise was (or at least claimed to be) happy with her clothes, and said that they reflected how “goofy” she is.

But—perhaps to avoid grappling with something very uncomfortable—Stacy and London insisted that the way Denise was dressing wasn’t really her, but was a character, even though she convincingly stayed “in character” for the entire episode. They did not want to come out and say, “They way you are is bad and you should change,” so they hand-waved it away by claiming the “real” Denise was being obscured underneath a fake Barbie persona.

After her makeover, Stacy assured Denise that actually, her personality shines through more, because nobody is distracted by all her ridiculous accoutrements. Perhaps genuinely, or perhaps for production, Denise claimed to love the makeover, which was flattering but scrubbed everything remotely Barbie-ish about her. I can only breathe a sigh of relief that they did not give her the standard “dark burgundy bob with side bangs” that every other contestant got.

Of course, by conventional standards, Denise looks much better after the makeover. But you have to ask, at what cost? At the beginning of the episode, she is resistant to changing her look. She enjoys being a character. She enjoys being “goofy.” And as much as Stacy claims that Denise’s makeover helps her personality shine through, I’m skeptical.

But also, being honest, I’m not sure I’d go out of my way to befriend Original Denise, for the same reason I think people are justified in not wanting to befriend Barbiecore CHH from 2024.

As the podcast episode and the entire Substack have made clear, I have a big personality, despite the fact that I also happen to be extremely deferential. It’s hard to explain—I’m extroverted, funny (well, according to my number one fan, myself) and I’m a performer. I’ve had to tone this part of myself down so that people feel like they’re having a conversation with me and not experiencing extended standup comedy, listlessly weaving in and out of various “bits.” While I don’t have a tendency to emulate Donald Trump with my attire (aside from one time that I moonlit at a friend’s birthday party in a Trump costume when I was seven months pregnant—don’t worry, I was asked to do this) my clothes showcase my personality. I wear a lot of color. I don’t hide. I have to consciously remind myself not to appear like I’m wearing a literal costume, and not to buy a never-ending supply of purses shaped like fun little things. I am become quirk chungus. I could never, ever be described as doing “too little” with my outfits the same way nobody could ever accuse me of being a wallflower at parties (can you even be a wallflower if you’re two verses deep into a Creed-voice rendition of With Arms Wide Open?) And that’s much more embarrassing than being a shy girl or an all-beige quiet luxury kinda chick, because those people aren’t trying hard enough to fail so spectacularly.

But I think what’s most embarrassing for me to admit, even to myself, is that both my personality and fashion sense (plus the way I write about them) signal, “Please like me.” It’s giving Emily in Paris who couldn’t get any bat mitzvah invites twenty years ago. Why else would someone do a highlight reel of their “best” jokes every time they meet someone, dress in at least three bright colors with every outfit, and feel the need to showcase their figure even for the most mundane events? And hell, is there anything more embarrassing than needing to be liked? Well, there is: needing to be liked and failing.

Since I used to publish so many of my outfits publicly, and now I rarely do, most people who aren’t paid subscribers probably have an incorrect idea of how I dress, especially how I dress for things that aren’t date night. But changes have been made! Here we have four outfits I wore for things where I knew I’d be socializing with people other than my husband, who would probably prefer I wear a full-body spandex catsuit at all times:

I like to think that I’ve been able to hit what I’m just going to call a “Denise mid-point.” I feel like my personality is showing through. These outfits aren’t boring. They feel flattering, fun, and I get to play with silhouettes and trends that I find interesting. At this point in my life, I genuinely enjoy wearing them more than the nonstop bubblegum pink and bodycon, mostly because I got bored with that and nobody wants to dress the same, predictable way forever—yes, even if that way is something zany and fun. But I don’t think any of my new outfits signal, “Histrionic woman obsessed with herself and male attention,”which I’m now perfectly aware is how my previous outfits came off.

It’s hard not to second guess myself when I know I just have very poor instincts for certain things. Obviously, the social stuff is more important for my day-to-day life, but it’s hard to dismiss the fashion stuff since they feel so interconnected. But just as I don’t think women need to contort themselves into a particular body shape to appeal to men, because men actually like a variety of body shapes, it is not appealing to me to completely change my aesthetics or personality to appeal to people who would never enjoy anything authentic about me. Self-improvement must be, well, an improvement of the self, not a complete obfuscation.

I will never be the woman who goes to a party and just listens to other people’s stories. I will also never be the woman who wears The Row neutrals with a tasteful black Khaite jacket (I insist this is because I am more dynamic and fun, NOT because I can’t afford it. Don’t put it in the papers that I can’t afford it!) But it also feels necessary to put checks and balances on the most extreme parts of my personality and my aesthetics—my blind spots where I know something is “off” but I can’t figure out exactly what.
