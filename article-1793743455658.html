The Revenge of the Institutionists

And the pitfalls of the liberal resurgence

Justin Ling
Nov 3
 
READ IN APP
 

“Public institutions are largely the lengthened shadows of private men,” the arch-conservative columnist James J. Kilpatrick wrote in 1976.

It’s an observation that rings true of Kilpatrick himself. Two decades prior, Kilpatrick popularized the idea of nullifying Brown v. Board of Education. He devised all kinds of clever rhetorical devices to encourage powerful people to do so, and it worked. State legislatures across the South, desperate to preserve the disintegrating Jim Crow system of racist laws, declared the landmark desegregation ruling of null force and effect. Kilpatrick’s advocacy had helped spur the “massive resistance” to the ruling: That took the form of white Americans attempting to blockade Black students from attending school.

The Southern resistance, met with a horrified public and a U.S. government willing to send in the National Guard, eventually, slowly, dissipated.

Kilpatrick remained in the public square, with a nationally-syndicated column, acting as a voice for the stubborn part of the South which defended its racist institutions from outside attacks. Even in 1976, Kilpatrick bemoaned “coercive orders, manipulating little children because of the color of their skin,” that is to say, orders that bus Black children to integrated schools “have aroused enormous resentment in communities with large minority populations.”

But Kilpatrick’s observation on the nature of institutions was about something slightly offside of segregation. A month before, the Church Committee — a Senate committee tasked with investigating abuses of the U.S. security and intelligence apparatus — had released its voluminous final report. It found a sprawling effort to foment division inside leftist and Black liberation groups; it laid bare a lawless campaign of international skullduggery and murder; it uncovered brazen spying and abuse of powers; it revealed how the FBI surveilled some reporters even as it recruited other journalists and columnists as assets. The U.S. security state had led a systemic effort to infiltrate and undermine civil society.

It was an “ugly record,” Kilpatrick wrote. But, he implored, “don’t over react.” FBI Director J. Edgar Hoover, whom Kilpatrick recalls meeting “only a few times,” may have had some “obsessive prejudices,” sure, but “he emanated power the way that some women emanate sex.” Seemingly losing sight of the fact that he’s supposed to be criticizing the man, Kilpatrick writes: “He did not merely reign; he ruled.”¹

In Kilpatrick’s mind, institutions needn’t serve the broader public, so long as they were run by great white men.

Then came Jimmy Carter. He came out of nowhere, dispensing with Dixiecrat (in Kilpatrick’s mold) George Wallace, to rack up early primary wins. Kilpatrick denigrated him as a dreamer who capitalists ought to fear. On the contrary, Carter was a real institutionalist. He wanted to fix things: And the FBI and CIA were a great place to start.

On the campaign trail, Carter called both rotten institutions a “national disgrace.” But instead of campaigning for their destruction and dismantling, Carter decided to cast his own shadow over those institutions. “The first thing we need to do is make sure that the FBI is completely professionalized once again,” he told NBC that year, “and removed from politics.”

Once president, he tapped Admiral Stansfield Turner — who had been stationed at a NATO post in Italy — to run the CIA. “Lots of people think President Carter called me in and said, ‘Clean the place up and straighten it out.’ He never did that,” Turner said. Instead, Carter was hands-on. “He wanted to understand the mechanisms from our satellites to our spies to our methods of analyzing what was happening.”²

Carter could have kiboshed covert activities entirely. He didn’t. He imposed new rules and ethics on how intelligence operatives and assets behave in the field. Rather than mind-control experiments, they got into the business of republishing the works of dissidents living behind the Iron Curtain, even distributing fax machines and tape recorders to help them reach the world.

In 1977, a strange missive went out to the CIA station in Pretoria, South Africa. “I was instructed to begin unilateral operations against BOSS,” the station chief recounted. BOSS: The Bureau of State Security for the Boer regime. It was now the CIA’s job to kill apartheid.

Carter turned to the CIA’s Middle East analysts with a different kind of mission: They were to surveil Egyptian President Anwar Sadat and Israeli Prime Minister Menachem Begin. Carter wanted to head into the 1978 Camp David negotiations armed with as much information as possible, in the name of getting both sides to a peace deal.

A year later, Carter signed Executive Order 12036. It banned CIA wiretapping of Americans, established new oversight of its foreign intelligence operations, and expanded a ban on assassinations. Two years after that, Carter introduced an FBI Charter: It would be a new constitution for the bureau, one which would forbid its wanton invasions of privacy and prevent it from political subterfuge.

President Jimmy Carter believed in institutions. He believed they could be fixed, improved, overseen, managed, and made to do good — even if they had been a source of awful misdeeds. But he believed for those institutions to work correctly, they needed good information and the right kind of people running them: The kind who shined light instead of casting long shadows.

Politics often gets boiled down to a fight of left-vs-right. These days, perhaps we align things more generally on a populist-establishment axis. But I would argue there is a whole other dimension that, at least at this moment, matters far more: Institutionalist vs. anti-institutionalist.

The distinction is pretty easy to make: The former wants to protect the institutions of society we, the Western world, have spent centuries building, especially as it means repairing them; the anti-institutionalists want to break down, weaponize, and co-opt them to their own ends.

Kilpatrick is as good a totem as any of the kind of man who wants institutions to work only insofar as they work for him. He and his Dixiecrats tried to use their institutions to hold back modernity, and they failed.

Carter is a sterling example of an institutionalist — and he, too, failed. His FBI reforms fizzled, and he was drubbed by Ronald Reagan, a president who would weaponize the CIA into a new era of illegal and unethical abuses.

The push-pull between institutionalists and anti-institutionalists is as old as politics itself. But, at least since the arrival of Donald Trump in 2016, it’s fair to say that the institutionalists have been losing ground.

The tempo has shifted in recent weeks. Students in Serbia ousted their mini-Putin, a kleptocrat who had subverted and cannibalized state institutions for his own benefit. Lula, in Brazil, beat back a challenge from another Bolsonaro, intent only on demolishing the rule of law for his clan’s benefit. Alberta has spurned a crackpot plan to separate from Canada. And the U.S. midterms, taking place as I finish this dispatch, look set to put real limits on Trump’s ability to pervert and profit from U.S. democracy.

Good news abounds.

But this week, on a very special Bug-eyed and Shameless, we take a look at the fundamentals. Because there is lots of reason to believe that this is little more than a death rattle for the institutionalists.

Unless we change the game.

Bug-eyed and Shameless is an institution. That means you have to support it.

Upgrade to paid

In late 2024, a phalanx of Serbians stood silently in the streets of Novi Sad — they didn’t make a noise for 14 minutes. A few weeks later, a cadre of students at a university in Belgrade did the same: For 15 minutes this time. By early 2025, similar protests had cropped up across Serbia, now observing 16 minutes of solemn protest.

They were remembering the dead, those crushed under the weight of a fallen canopy at the Novi Sad train station — and protesting the rotten state corruption which allowed it to happen. First they were remembering the 14 dead, but lengthened their protest to reflect the growing death toll.

The state, an increasingly despotic hybrid regime run by President Aleksandar Vučić, flailed in response. First it sent goons to beat the protesters, then its construction minister resigned, then it declared a campaign to root out corruption in the construction industry — proving, by accident, that it had been sitting on a mountain of evidence of graft infecting the entire industry.

The protests continued, and gained steam. Students, unions, regular people took to the streets and blocked buildings, demanding accountability for the disaster. Blockades at schools turned into the nucleus of a new democratic movement: Each protest cell (plenum) held assemblies to debate and vote on initiatives. Leadership was rotated often and often remained anonymous, for fear of Vučić’s secret police. They started with four demands: Release all the information about the station collapse; prosecute those who beat the protesters; release all those detained in the protests; and increase higher education funding by 20%. The government insisted those demands had been met – the protesters disagreed.

Estimates say the protests may have attracted as many as 325,000 people. As their street protests coalesced into a more formal movement, they began to think of electoral politics.

The leaders of the protests could have put themselves on the electoral lists, running as outsiders to oust Vučić’s party — a populist surge to defeat fascism. That’s not what they did. Instead, the plena met to decide who would make up those lists. University faculties would nominate candidates, students would vet the candidates, and open votes selected the roster of candidates.

They called it the Student List — its logo a sassy owl, in a graduation cap, pointing an accusatory finger — but none of its candidates were students. They were professors, athletes, actors, a former central bank governor. The party had no leader, just a lead candidate: Cardiologist Ilija Srdanović.

“This movement has no individuals who rise above the collective, nor does it cultivate a cult of personality,” their website reads.³ “Our actions are collective, considered, and accountable to society. We take part in this struggle solely for the idea and the common good, creating space for togetherness, compassion, and solidarity. The students are winning!”

Srdanović, a political neophyte, declined to debate Serbia’s strongman leader and chose, instead, to speak directly to the public about how he wanted to fix the country’s broken healthcare system. “In the future,” Srdanović promised, “patients will no longer be used as political props.” The focus, he said, would be on fixing the healthcare system — not lavishing money on political goodies. Rebuilding the institutions for the public good, not wielding them for personal gain.

In their election platform, they pledged to root out corruption, liberate the media from political control, to drag the education system out of the dark ages, and more.

While Vučić held large rallies — marshaling all the resources of kleptocracy to line the squares with supporters — the Student List went small. They held smaller rallies across the country every night, sometimes impromptu, and dispatched their army of activists to knock on doors: Hitting hundreds of thousands of households in a matter of weeks. Towards the end of the campaign, marches set out from across the country, heading towards Belgrade.

Under Vučić, Serbia had a prolonged economic boom. Even as he put the country on track to join the EU, he finagled ample support from Russia and China. He rigged elections and undermined the independence of the media, turning the state into a vehicle for clientelism and corruption. He set his sights on Kosovo and Bosnia, with plans to force them into a greater Serbia. Serbia became a state where party affiliation became a prerequisite for getting your kid into the right daycare. Serbians felt the benefits of growth, but grew furious at the scale of the graft.

In the lead-up to election day, polls painted two radically different portraits of what was transpiring. Pollsters allied with the government showed Vučić’s United Serbia coalition cruising to victory. Others had the Student List ahead, usually by a hair. It was hard to know which reality was real.

Then came the election itself: Marred by slanted media coverage and intimidation by government goons, in a system built by the government to protect its power, the Student List posted a remarkable victory nevertheless. The students captured 53% of the popular vote and won a clear majority in the Serbian parliament. They had vanquished Vučić. He whined that the same Deep State which roiled Trump’s administration has also come for him. Nobody is buying it.

What comes next isn’t clear. The students have indicated they will not put sanctions on Russia, that they will continue welcoming cooperation with China, and that they are unlikely to drape themselves in the EU flag — noting, bitterly, that European leaders continued glad-handing with Vučić amidst their struggle to remove him.

But they say, too, that foreign policy won’t be their focus. They intend to get their own house in order, and are wasting no time — they’ve already demanded the resignations of prominent Vučić cronies.

The students claim to be in the business of making institutions work as designed. They now have a shot at doing so because they encouraged the public to ignore the globalist boogeymen and the state media, to overcome the disenchanted fatalism which says change can only trend towards the worse.

There, they have discovered the real Achilles heel of modern reactionary politics. Be it Vučić’s Serbia, Viktor Orbán’s Hungary, Vladimir Putin’s Russia, or Donald Trump’s America — or the little fascists hoping to join their pantheon, like Marine Le Pen in France or Alice Weidel in Germany: The goal of modern populism is to destroy and blame institutions in equal measure.

It is a clever bit of magic, really. Smashing the regulatory state and defunding the public accounts via tax cuts begets a sugar-rush economic boom. That boom is used to buy favor and loyalty. Public agencies and departments, defunded and weakened, slowly come under influence of the new regime and allow for the kind of graft and theft they once fought against. A captured media market and a flurry of social media soldiers push the narrative: These institutions, their power stemming from a globalist project, only want to take. I want to give. If that doesn’t take, the high-velocity rage machine seeks to make you mad about everything, everywhere, all of the time.

This sleight of hand has a real effect on our understanding of systems. It is not that the people of Serbia were stupid or uniformly brainwashed during the decade of Vučić’s reign. Rather, they experienced the economy growing, their lives improving, as they watched their leader do battle with a wretched system he blamed for holding back these good tides. His corruption was well-known, but at least this corruption was making their lives better. So they tolerated it.

But this corruption implicates everyone, eventually. You cannot live in a kleptocracy without experiencing both its pitfalls and its largesse.

As Anne Applebaum writes in Autocracy, Inc.:

Applebaum: Kleptocracy and autocracy go hand in hand, reinforcing each other but also undermining any other institutions that they touch. The real estate agents who don’t ask too many questions in Sussex or Hampshire, the factory owners eager to unload failing businesses in Warren, the bankers in Sioux Falls happy to accept mystery deposits from mystery clients—all of them help undermine the rule of law in their own countries and around the world. The globalization of finance, the plethora of hiding places, and the benign tolerance that democracies have shown for foreign graft now give autocrats opportunities that few could have imagined a couple of decades ago.⁴

This goes further, though. The citizens who accept the baby bonus cheques, the veteran who takes pride in the state’s generous investments in the military, the patriot who relishes the focus on collective history, the factory worker who appreciates the state subsidy.

Populists, once they seize power, do everything they can to make the population contented as they dismantle and repurpose those institutions. The regulatory state becomes arbitrary, the state media becomes loyal, the social safety net becomes an act of charity by dear leader, international relations become strictly inter-personal, the corporate state is divided into loyalists and enemies. Once this transformation happens, kleptocrats hate to leave.

But the success of the Student List — TISZA in Hungary, Lula da Silva in Brazil, United List in Latvia, the establishment parties in Sweden, amongst others — is clear evidence that people are frustrated with our institutions but even more hostile to the idea that burning them down will achieve much, either.

But it’s not all good news.

Perhaps my favorite part of the EU Parliament building in Brussels is the Debate Zone. It is a small corner of the sprawling, brightly-lit ground floor of the building. It features a series of cameras pointed at desks and podiums — giving the impression of a miniaturized version of the debate stages assembled in the various European capitals around election time. But this Debate Zone (my nomenclature, to be clear) is year-round: A spot for Members of the European Parliament to host impromptu dust-ups between their various factions.

It’s not the only dedicated spot for MEPs to show off in front of the cameras. The spacious campus is lined with spots which allow the European politicians to film stand-ups. Walking through the parliament one day this summer, just before the start of the Fall session, all of these spots were conspicuously empty. Nobody films in here anymore, a staffer told me. Indeed, I spotted one MEP wandering around the quad outside the building — under a massive DEMOCRACY IN ACTION banner — their outstretched arm holding the camera, delivering a tirade about some initiative or another.

Why use the institutional infrastructure to debate your fellow politicians when you can be, literally and metaphorically, outside, ranting directly to your social media fans? Twitter and TikTok denizens no longer want their politicians to stand stoically in front of flags and banners — they want authenticity. They want politicians rambling to camera from the driver’s seat of their car, or wandering the streets.

The politician’s perpetual quest to appear like regular folk isn’t new. But it is rather new that these wannabe-folksy politicians find themselves competing for all of our attention. It is uncouth and uncool to appear inside the institutions which they represent — and, in some cases, which they want to destroy — and so they fashion themselves influencers.

Influencers are a massive class of people, most with modest audiences, who contextualize and demystify politics, science, the daily news, finance, and much else — to wildly variant degrees of quality. Some have podcasts, some have TikTok accounts, some have TikTok pages where they pretend to have a podcast. YouTube, Discord, Twitch, Instagram, Facebook, Truth Social, Kick, etc. The social internet has radically flattened and decentralized the information space, shrinking the fourth estate as a new class of citizen-journalist multiplies.

The positive spin on the state of public information is, of course, that it has been democratized. More people are participating in the production of news, information, analysis, and politics, this thinking goes, so it’s more democratic.

But there is no obvious spot, in this strange new informatic landscape, for institutions. The influencer economy is a non-stop, 24/7 competition for attention. That competition is generally managed by algorithms, themselves run by Big Tech. This Hobbesian free-for-all means that no one figure can get too big.

I have written a lot over the past few years about why this is bad. We are uploading our politics and society to the internet, I believe, and that means we are surrendering control of it to people who don’t give a damn fig about democracy. We are giving the keys to our institutions to Mark Zuckerberg and Elon Musk, and I needn’t spend another thousand words explaining why that’s bad. (Dispatches #119, #137, #144, #153)

But there have been recent victories for the institutionalist class, Serbia included. And they were wins earned specifically by doing an end-run around this new uber-online, influencer-first political battleground. The fall of Vučić was precipitated by a mass, grassroots mobilization campaign, organized in-person and delivered door-to-door. In Brazil, Lula’s campaign used a WhatsApp group — comprised of, by the end, more than 10 million members — to relay marching orders. They called it Lula’s Army. Supporters mobbed the streets and managed, through coordinated action, to actively alter algorithms in their favor. Lula won an election that all projections had him losing.

Another data point comes to us from Alberta. The province was thrown into a bizarre independence referendum thanks to its paranoid premier (Dispatches #23, #24). The vote, legally, could not trigger independence — so it was a vote on whether to hold an independence referendum. Despite the polling, many worried the public would vote to do just that.

That fear was driven, in particular, by a “passionate community of 114 accounts,” McGill’s Centre for Media, Technology, and Democracy found. These are influencers “unencumbered by traditional forms of political accountability,” the study found. And that crew of a hundred-odd accounts were responsible for half of all digital engagement in the lead-up to last month’s vote.

Of those 114, nearly 60% were in favor of separating from Canada — and they were heavily rewarded by their chosen social media platforms. “Pro-independence accounts outperform anti-independence ones by a ratio of roughly 8:1,” the researchers found. “Posts about Alberta separating from Canada, irrespective of position, receive more than double the engagement of comparable other-topic posts.”

To follow the Alberta independence referendum on social media — Twitter, YouTube, TikTok, Instagram — you would think that the pro-independence camp was about to take the vote in a cakewalk. Indeed, many of the separatists reported that three-quarters of their social media feeds were pro-independence: How could the final results not look the same?

Then the results came in: 75% voted against holding an independence referendum. The institutionalists, particularly those still in major political parties who campaigned hard to kill the initiative, had won.

All of these are wonderful examples of the politics of institutionalism succeeding. Real human-to-human connections have won out. IRL campaigning beat the algorithms.

But it’s hard to get excited about these victories. It feels like, yes, politics is finally finding ways to overcome the distortive effects social media has on politics. Long gone is Barack Obama’s digital-first hope-and-change election and all the inspiring power of the Arab Spring: Now, liberals and democrats are desperately figuring out how to reach people online — just to tell them to log off. (Dispatch #136)

These victories come, however, just as AI threatens to wildly change the game.

A man picks up the phone. “Hello Omer,” the voice on the other end says, “this is Benjamin Netanyahu.”

But it’s not the Israeli prime minister. It’s an AI agent mimicking him. The faux-Netanyahu is on the phone to answer any questions Omer may have about the upcoming election.

The campaign knows full well what Omer is likely to ask, because they have built an extensive digital profile of him — as they have with every Israeli voter. Using technology developed to track (and kill) Iranian nuclear scientists, Israeli political strategists can link phone numbers to individuals to their online behaviors.

This kind of data collection has been going on for years, now. But the volume of data has simply been too massive for humans to exploit at scale. AI has changed that game.

It has changed how content is produced, too. An Israeli research group found that fully 1-in-8 social media posts about the election were made by AI. Thousands of bot accounts, powered by LLMs meant to make them sound authentic, fanned out to generate partisan slop. Campaigns hired outside consultants to set up astroturf websites, existing exclusively to poison LLMs — like ChatGPT and Claude — into providing information favoring particular candidates. This effort, run by multiple campaigns, was aimed at turning the internet space into a fundamentally unreal place. Underpinning the effort was a belief that whoever could trap voters in the most compelling fiction could win their vote.

Israel’s Knesset vote was “the first real AI elections in the world,” an Israeli expert told the Wall Street Journal. And the elections were a horrifying look into what’s waiting for the rest of us.

If Israel was first, the United States is second. Ahead of these midterms, hundreds of AI slop ads have flooded onto American TV sets, blurring the lines between what is real and what is generated. Unlike in Israel, there is no federal requirement to disclose that the ads — funded, often, by PACs and dark money groups — were generated by AI. (Some states do require it.) Campaigns now obsessively “sloptimize” their websites to be scraped by AI bots. Nearly 4-in-10 Americans say they’ve used AI to help decide who to vote for, even as they admit they don’t trust AI to relay credible information.

This race to make American elections artificial is largely a one-sided affair. Of the staggering $137 million spent on AI ads, 88% has come from Republican candidates and groups.

This is all the official slop. Shadowy disinformation networks and Russia-linked influence campaigns have also made ample use of AI — though they have been orders of magnitude smaller, at least thus far.

One particularly strange manipulation of reality came from a firm called Wedgewood Polls. The company, polling aggregator and news site FiftyPlusOne alleges, released a slew of midterm election polls — and they were all fake. It’s not the only case of this deception. While these firms have tried to claim their fake polling data was part of “a short-term social experiment,” more likely they were an attempt to juice prediction markets. In other words: The reality of elections is being manipulated to fleece degenerate gamblers.

In a world of institutions, this would be manageable. The establishment press could be a voice of truth and debate, even as online chatter spins into madness. Election regulators could monitor and crack down on illegal hoovering up of voters’ personal information, defined clearly in law. Campaign finance laws could limit campaigns’ ability to flood the zone with slop. AI regulation could set standards about what political information LLMs share with the public during elections.

But no jurisdiction has institutions capable of all those things. Israel raced to implement measures to mandate AI-labeling, Canada and Europe have good campaign finance rules, the United States is trying to harden its systems from AI skullduggery. But, generally speaking: Nobody is ready.

And even if they were, how could one stop a rush of water on a floodplain? In a world of infinite influencers, of a flattened news environment, where information is delivered at lightspeed from all directions, how can we defend ourselves?

We hit an ominous milestone this summer. It appears that artificial online traffic — both bots and AI agents — have surpassed human activity on the World Wide Web. Whereas bots tend to crawl the internet, scraping and observing it, AI agents interact with it. They converse with us, and each other. They hack and exfiltrate.

“The transition from a human-centric web to one heavily influenced and populated by AI is no longer a future projection, but a current reality,” cybersecurity firm HUMAN wrote in its state of the internet earlier this year.

This has an enormous, dangerous, effect on our politics. The relationship between real and fake, agentic and human, malicious and helpful, manipulated and authentic is hopelessly blurred. All the anti-institutionalist decisions we’ve made in recent decades have left us woefully ill-equipped to deal with it.

We’ve had a few good weeks of news from the struggle against reactionary, anti-establishment populism. Serbia, Brazil, and Alberta all show that real-world organizing — done specifically to challenge the internet’s control of our politics — works.

But I see no evidence that we are interested in learning those lessons and opting to campaign in the meatspace instead of uploading more and more democratic infrastructure to the unreal space of the internet. Indeed it’s been the opposite: In the United States and Israel, we see how kleptocrats and wannabe autocrats have no qualms about wielding AI to protect their power. There is every reason to think this will become more, not less, effective.

So celebrate the wins while we have them.
