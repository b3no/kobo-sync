The Art of Falling

Autumn’s dazzling farewell and a haunting new story by Niall Williams.

Ron Charles
Nov 6
 
READ IN APP
 

The trick to catching 24 billion tons is not to catch them all at once.

The trees, they have mercy. Every autumn, they drop their leaves on us like incendiary Post-it Notes, little missives of mortality too lovely to believe.

The poet Gerard Manley Hopkins asks, “Are you grieving / Over Goldengrove unleaving?” The sorrow that tints fall is what makes the season so exquisite.

In Nature’s Magicians: How Leaves Conjure Up Our World, Jonathan Silvertown calls leaves “the real currency of life.” But it’s their death throes that dazzle us.

Cool nights, sunny days, and well-timed rainfall have rendered the foliage around Washington as gaudy as I can remember it. For a few weeks, Mother Nature luxuriates in the sentimental palette that Thomas Kinkade gets mocked for. Every morning, I channel my inner golden retriever and barrel through crinkly piles my fastidious neighbors have raked and blown to the curb.

Naturally, this burlesque show is not entirely for our aesthetic revelry.

“Once thought to be waste products like the trash left behind after a party,” Silvertown writes, “the vivid colors of autumn leaves are manufactured components with a job to do.” Those red and yellow pigments help protect the leaves as the tree recycles nitrogen and kills off its “photosynthetic machinery.”

It’s a good death.

One of my favorite writers, Irishman Niall Williams, has published a wee book called O Now! Not a novel, not even a novella, it’s a bound short story originally written for BBC Radio.

The scene opens at the end of October when, as happens four nights a week, Bat Considine wanders over to Ganga Crowe’s thatched farmhouse to chat by the fire. Their conversation is heartfelt but not robust. A 68-year-old bachelor, Bat doesn’t have a lot to say. Ganga, well into his 70s, can barely hear. His black-and-white sheepdog, Joe, watches without comment.

Aside from reading the obituaries aloud and occasionally responding, “O Now!” the two men are “waiting to see which of them would die first.”

“What I’d like to know is why the world exists,” Bat announces. “What I’d like to know is the mind of God.”

Ganga offers no immediate response to this grandiose desire, but his friend proposes an arrangement: “The one of us that goes first, who finds out the answer, he should try and send back word to the one left behind.”

In the season of Samhain, that arrangement is almost immediately put to the test.

A soupçon of dark humor keeps this story from curdling into tragedy, but the grief that winds through these tender passages cuts deep. As the undertaker does his grimly intimate business, “the physical realities of the human body made impossible the thought of the soul.... Meaning was gone out of the world.”

But even as guests arrive for the wake, the surviving friend keeps waiting for a revealing message from the realm of imperishability.

You can trust O Now! to lead you somewhere dear because it takes place in Faha, the County Clare village of Williams’s classic novel This Is Happiness.

“Even in Faha,” the narrator concedes, “resurrection was rare.”

But on this sorrowful November day, Williams draws a miracle from the sky as common and lovely and irresistible as fallen leaves.◆

Books Go to the Movies

  • Being Heumann, starring Ruth Madeley, is based on the 2020 “unrepentant memoir” by Judith Heumann, who fought for federal civil rights protection for people with disabilities. Mark Ruffalo co-stars as her antagonist, Secretary of Health, Education, and Welfare Joseph A. Califano Jr., during the 1977 occupation of a federal building in San Francisco. Opens in select theaters today; streams Nov. 13 on Apple TV.

  • “We can have lots of good fun that is funny!” Again? Maybe not. The Cat in the Hat is back in a new animated version inspired by Dr. Seuss’s classic. The film opens today in theaters with Bill Hader voicing a mischievous feline who can turn back time. Judging by the trailer of this earnest abomination, I would not watch it in a box. I would not watch it with a fox.

  • Blake Draper is the only actor in a biopic called A. Rimbaud, playing in select theaters. (Interview with director Patrick Wang.) For nearly three hours, Draper portrays the 19th-century poet as he interacts with musical sounds that represent the various people in his brief life from France to Africa. Il faut être absolument moderne, indeed!

P.S. Ordinary People, Judith Guest’s debut novel about the devastation caused by a boy’s death and his brother’s attempted suicide, is 50 years old. In an anniversary edition released this week, Amy Bloom writes, “By revealing the struggle, even the flailing, beneath a smooth suburban surface, Guest made many readers see their own families more clearly — and with more compassion.” In 1980, Robert Redford directed a stunning adaptation starring Donald Sutherland, Timothy Hutton, and Mary Tyler Moore, who delivered a performance that exploded her range.◆

The Dame Truth

“I could never do a one-woman show,” says Judi Dench, “because I’d bore myself stupid.”

That surely isn’t the only lie in this utterly charming new memoir by Judi Dench. The world’s most beloved actress is back in conversation with her longtime friend Brendan O’Hea for Is It Too Late to Make a Run for It?

With gently prodding questions, O’Hea leads Dench from her childhood in York, which seems to have been a series of zany antics, to her late-career triumphs, like having a garbage truck named after her — Dame Foodie Dench — “and very proud of it I am, too,” she says.

In between, she talks about her Quaker faith, which gave her “the discipline of silence.” She describes finally falling in love with Michael Williams after knowing him for years — “God, he made me laugh.” And she confesses to hating her role in Peter Shaffer’s The Gift of the Gorgon — “I didn’t know what the hell was going on half the time.”

Gracious, mischievous, and with a purse full of tales comic and tender, Dench is an irresistible raconteur, and it’s astonishing how effectively this apparently casual format conveys the crisp lilt of her voice.

It’s not too late to make a run for it — it just came out Tuesday. Open to any page, sit back, and enjoy.◆

Underlined

A passage from something I’m reading.

It had never been obvious how two people invisible to each other should begin a conversation. To break the silence, some vocal utterance was needed. As if it were natural and preordained, people learned to pick up the telephone with the formerly obscure word hello. But it was neither natural nor preordained. Bell and Watson had said “Ahoy!” as though they were at sea. Sailors might also cry “Halloo!” as a call from a distance. The word — or sound — had existed as an interjection, with no formal spelling: The Oxford English Dictionary records nineteenth-century examples of hallo, hullo, hillo, ello, and olla. It might have been a variant of shouts used by hunters pursuing game. It could be a greeting or a summons. Now it became a meme and spread virally. After a while, it seemed that all telephone conversations began with the same question: Hello?

From “The Telephone: A New History,” by James Gleick (Farrar, Straus and Giroux).◆

Picture This

The title of David Duff’s delightfully macabre picture book, I Will Eat You in the End, may become a catchphrase in your house.

The story begins with two pink worms celebrating the glory of the day. But then a dinosaur steps on one of them.

“Sorry,” the dinosaur says. “I didn’t see your friend.”

“Don’t worry about it,” the worm says. “My buddies and I will eat him later.”

Thus begins a hilariously deadpan explanation of what worms have done for the environment since life first got complicated. The whole book is like a cherry-flavored tombstone.

Desperate to make up for stepping on his friend, the dinosaur agrees to keep an eye on a bird that threatens to eat the pink worm.

“Together, they watch the life cycle unfolding around them,” Duff writes, while Marianna Coppo’s elemental illustrations carry the story along with understated wit.

Then the worm notices something fiery in the sky.

“That doesn’t look good,” the dinosaur says.

“It’s very bad,” the worm agrees.

This clever picture book introduces the youngest readers to the wonders of decomposition, the ingenuity of nature, and even a little paleontology.

The final pages jump ahead about 66 million years. Can you dig it?◆

This Week’s Poem

I know Ha Jin as the author of such powerful novels as War Trash and Waiting, which won a National Book Award. But lately, I’ve been reading his poetry.

In his new collection, Running Away, the Chinese American writer explores the terrors and hopes of people fleeing home, searching for a new one, much as he did when he decided to remain in the United States after the Tiananmen Square massacre.

Crossing the Rio Grande

I asked her how long she has been on this trek.
“A month,” she replied in a soft voice.
I said again, “There must’ve been moments
when it was hard for you to continue.
What made you trudge all the way from Ecuador?”
She told me, her eyes flickering while
her head bowed, “I often felt my country followed
on my heels like tigers and wolves.
We were afraid of being deported to Cuba,
which would repatriate Chinese runaways,
so I forced myself to move on.”

“How old are you?” I asked again,
amazed by her lined face.
“Sixty-one,” she said calmly.
I went on, “Do you think you can make it in the US?”
“Whatever the cost, I must live a free life!”

We reached the edge of the Rio Grande,
which a group of Mexicans had just crossed.
At the sight of the muddy water
the woman dropped her wheeled suitcase
and tied a yellow plastic bag to her belt.
She said she had to cross the river to join the others
so they could scale the American Great Wall together.
“What if the Border Patrol catches you?” I reminded her.
She smiled and said, “That means
I have reached safety.”

Without turning her head, she waded into the water,
leaving her blue suitcase in the grass
and swimming to the other side,
where a few Mexicans were beckoning,
ready to pull her ashore.

From “Running Away.” Copyright © 2026 Ha Jin. Published by Copper Canyon Press, 2026. Reprinted with permission of the publisher.◆

Let Your Fingers Do the Walking

Reading James Gleick’s new cultural history of the telephone (see Underlined above) reminded me that I owe you an explanation for how my wife ended up selling condoms.

It was the early 1990s. I was teaching, we were strapped for money, and Dawn was going bonkers taking care of our two small children. One day while driving them to Grandma’s, she heard an ad on the radio:

“Do you need extra cash?”

I do! she thought.

“Do you have a car?”

I do! she screamed.

A few days later, she found herself in a windowless conference room learning to deliver phone books door to door.

She came home with our van filled with hundreds of phone books, fliers, and plastic bags.

The first outing with the kids’ red wagon began cheerily enough, but the work was slow and tiring. She could pull only so many phone books at a time. The bags were supposed to be filled in a particular way and hung on residents’ front doors just so.

The pile of phone books in the van never seemed to diminish.

Dawn insisted it was against the rules for anyone to help her. No phone-book deliverer had ever taken the job so seriously. But it was clear she’d never finish in time. So we all pitched in, illicitly.

As the deadline approached, the weather turned colder. It rained constantly. All four of us got colds. Ever the pragmatist, I wanted to dump the phone books in the woods, or go live in the woods.

But on we trudged, the children whimpering: Phone book—flier—plastic bag. Phone book—flier—plastic bag. On damp, chilly nights I can still feel the weight of those gray volumes on my soul.

We did eventually finish, though. And as the tale of our ordeal spread among friends, one of them — who ran a business supplying condoms for free distribution — took pity on Dawn and gave her a job mailing out samples.

Every six weeks, a refrigerator-sized box would appear on our front porch with the words CONDOMS: DO NOT EXPOSE TO SUNLIGHT printed on each side.

Not a single neighbor ever mentioned it.◆
