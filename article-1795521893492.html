The case for cyberpocalypse prepping

If this is our new normal, we are collectively underreacting

On Jan. 5, 2024, a door plug blew out on a Boeing 737 Max. No one was hurt, but terrifying videos made it clear we were very lucky that the seat next to the door was empty. For the next several months, minor aviation incidents — a windshield cracking upon landing, a “mechanical problem” forcing an airliner back to its gate — made headlines. It felt like Boeing planes were falling apart everywhere. But they weren’t; we were just paying more attention to minor safety incidents that happen all the time.

So for the last several months, I have been watching reports of frightening cybersecurity incidents pile up and trying to figure out the answer to a very high-stakes question: Is the “cyberpocalypse” upon us? Are things actually getting worse, or are we just paying more attention?

Share

The headlines are certainly alarming: The world saw its first $6 billion heist (of cryptocurrency, of course). Europe reported a string of cyberattacks on supermarkets. A Norwegian oil and gas operation experienced a targeted cyberattack. The FBI was hit with an intrusion into its national criminal justice database. Illinois blocked “an apparent cyberattack attempt … at the Algonquin Dam on the Fox River.” The West Virginia legislature suffered a possible ransomware attack. The list goes on.

Cybersecurity is one of the first arenas where AI has dramatically changed the game: LLMs can identify software vulnerabilities far faster than human hackers. That might not be as big a problem as it sounds if defenders can generally patch problems before they are exploited. But it could also lead to a world where the cost of doing business online is permanently higher — or where services we’ve grown to count on as reliable go dark for weeks, wreaking economic and social havoc.

The number of critical vulnerabilities being discovered is already way, way up — and we’re still in the early days of AI hacking. Fixing each vulnerability takes time from engineers working on the patches, and then it requires every single person with an affected device to download the update.¹

If this is our new normal, then we are all collectively underreacting to the risks these vulnerabilities could pose to critical infrastructure. At minimum, it’s worth thinking about: If my family was without power, without running water, without electronic payments, or without internet access for a week, would we be okay?

I’m not a prepper. I don’t want to ride out the apocalypse in a bunker; I want to prevent it through policy. But it’s a good idea to be in a position to weather these disruptions: Have cash, food, and water on hand. It’s pro-social to be prepared for emergencies; you will reduce the demand for emergency assistance and be in a position to help your neighbors.

But even as I pitch preparedness, I have to acknowledge that not all indicators point in the same direction.

The case for fear

We know very definitively that the number of critical vulnerabilities being discovered in software is way, way up.

The dramatic rise began suddenly this spring, and it corresponds extraordinarily well to the moment when OpenAI and Anthropic developed AI systems that are super-hackers capable of identifying software vulnerabilities that humans never found. At first, those systems were not publicly released; OpenAI and Anthropic instead partnered with major companies to address the security vulnerabilities secretly.

But close observers of AI warned that this situation would not last. Depending on how you measure it, American AI labs are perhaps six or seven months “ahead” of their counterparts in China in AI cyber capabilities, and China has made its powerful models open-weights — meaning they are available for anyone to download and run themselves.

Open-weights models have tons of advantages for human freedom, flexibility, and sticking it to big corporations, but they have one big downside: There’s no way to prevent their use for crime. Even if an open-weights model is trained to refuse to shut down hospitals in cyberattacks, it’s easy to train it to instead get to work.

So the fear was that once the highest-tier models were made open-weights, we would see a spike in such incidents. By some metrics, we have. The investment firm Bridgewater released the following chart of cyber incidents, which indeed shows a massive surge very shortly after open-weights models became widely available.

“We see attacks continuing to inflect upward in spite of the race to secure software and the internet, and don’t think the potential scale of cyber warfare is well appreciated because of how hard it is to track these attacks systematically,” the company concluded.

The increase in cyber incidents adds up to a much higher risk of a large event causing massive social disruption. Bridgewater, for instance, accompanies that chart with a startling claim: “We are managing money based on a one-in-three chance of an economically destabilizing attack within the next year.” Sentinel, an independent forecasting group with a more precise definition of an “economically destabilizing attack,” arrived at a similar probability: “By the end of 2028, will an outage cut power, telecoms, digital payments or water to 1M+ people in an OECD country, excluding natural disasters, for a week? Forecasters estimate that there’s a 33% (10% to 60%) chance.”

This would, to be clear, be totally unprecedented in modern U.S. history — and yet it’s regarded by serious analysts as a quite likely outcome for the next two years.

Initially, the hope was that there were a fixed number of bugs that new tools could identify — that there would be a sprint to fix them all, after which we would have secure software going forward. But few of the cybersecurity experts I’ve spoken to in the last six months still believe in that optimistic vision. Instead, they seem increasingly resigned to a world where cyber defense has a narrow advantage for the time being — and only then if companies are spending big to red-team their own systems and fix them before outsiders can find anything.

“There is a part of me which says, yeah, there is a Y2K-like period, where you adjust for it,” said Microsoft CEO Satya Nadella recently. “The only challenge with that is, if the models keep getting more and more capable.”

Of course, that’s exactly what’s happening: The models are indeed getting more and more capable. That means we’re likely not in a one-off sprint to fix everything, but a permanent race as newer and more complex vulnerabilities are discovered by more and more powerful AIs.

“What we are seeing is a new normal,” OpenAI board member and retired Gen. Paul M. Nakasone, the former head of the National Security Agency and U.S. Cyber Command, reportedly said earlier this month at a Vanderbilt national security conference.

I think he’s probably right, and it’s why I’m taking steps to prepare for something worse.

A wave that has yet to fully materialize

There are some grounds for cautious optimism. If you want to put a number on how concerned you should be about something, it’s good to look at what it costs to insure against it. And the price of insurance against cybersecurity incidents, as reported by one major provider, was actually down 4% in the third quarter of 2026, the 13th consecutive quarter it has fallen. I have been keeping a close eye on cybersecurity insurance markets since, in theory, they should take into account all of the above — and at least as of the third quarter, they very clearly were not losing any sleep.

What should we make of this? Are cyber insurers walking blindly into the biggest threat to their business in history, or is everyone else overreacting? Maybe cyber insurers don’t have to think about situations that would leave 1 million people unable to make digital payments for a week, as those people aren’t buying their insurance? (The cost to, say, Amazon, would be pretty minimal, because demand would be disrupted but would not decrease.)

So maybe these declining prices are compatible with expecting significant disruptions. But that seems too convenient. It’s hard to imagine insurers coming out ahead in a breakdown in the financial order, however short it may last. So they seem to be betting — and betting real money — that this is not a serious risk.

And there are real data points they could point to in support of the idea that the huge increase in critical vulnerabilities detailed above will not turn into a lasting surge in cyberattacks — that for the most part, defense is outpacing offense. One security analyst looked at 1,400 fixes Microsoft had rushed in a major October patch and pointed out that only one of them had actually been exploited in the wild. That suggests a world where software has a ridiculous number of vulnerabilities, of course, but perhaps also one where defenders have a huge asymmetric advantage at finding them.

A big company can usually devote more resources to attacking its own infrastructure than outsiders will be willing to, and the company can use the same AI tools that the outsiders have.

There’s also some evidence that Americans aren’t experiencing significantly more cyberattacks in their day-to-day internet usage. Epoch AI, the same research group that found critical vulnerabilities rising sharply, polled American adults in June and again in September asking whether they had experienced a cybersecurity incident. It then asked specifically about everything from unusually convincing spam to personal identifying information leaks and whether hacks were disrupting regularly used services. They found no difference — if anything, a nonsignificant decrease — between June and September.

The difficulty of reconciling all these indicators, which seem to measure the same things but pull in opposite directions, has made me pessimistic about our future understanding of AI risks. That’s not because I think cybersecurity issues will doom us. My best guess is that it is a serious and growing problem, and that tackling it will raise prices and entail supply chain disruptions over the next year. But I ultimately think those disruptions will occur on a much smaller scale than, say, the logistical challenges seen in the early days of the COVID-19 pandemic, and therefore not be catastrophic or civilizational in scale.

Instead, I’m pessimistic because it’s hard to answer the simple question “Are we seeing a massive rise in cyberattacks?” through any methodology besides waiting to see what happens. That does not bode well for the much more complicated question we will soon need to answer: What will the consequences of recursively self-improving AIs be?

And while the stakes of cybersecurity are likely measured in the tens of billions of dollars, the stakes of getting recursive self-improvement right are ultimately much higher. So it’s not a great sign that there’s so little consensus to date on the effects of open-weights AI hackers.
