Maybe Men and Women Shouldn't See This Much of Each Other

Young men and women now get full, free access to the worst of the opposite gender's locker room talk. This is bad.

When I was first getting interested in boys and dating, I had absolutely no clue what boys said about girls.

Okay, I had some ideas. I heard through the grapevine that they thought certain girls were hot. They had some kind of NFL draft of the hottest girls in our high school and I discovered that was why they kept calling this cute sophomore “Reggie Bush.” I’m sure they said other things—far worse things—to which I was never privy. And honestly? If I had heard every weird, perverted or downright offensive thing that teenage boys said about girls, I think it would have fucked me up, and done major damage to my views of men as I got older. But luckily, I heard very little of it.

Similarly, I think it’s probably good that the boys didn’t hear what girls said about them. When my friend’s boyfriend broke up with her, I cheered her up by drawing a comic of him getting mounted by a buffalo (to think of all the money I could have made if I agreed to commission such images.) Most of the time, when we talked about boys, we were just bullshitting and being edgy to amuse each other—a phenomenon I refer to as “highfivesexuality,” or a proto stage of sexual development that should end by adulthood.

I also think it’s good that boys never saw or heard any of this.

Unfortunately, today, if you are on social media, you will be served a constant stream of the absolute worst of the other gender’s “locker room talk.” The stuff that young people are able to see just by logging into TikTok or Twitter for two minutes is worse than everything I ever heard boys say in four years of high school, even through gossip. And we’re kidding ourselves if we don’t think that experiencing the opposite sex this way—through their most inflammatory members saying or doing the most inflammatory things—is doing real damage to today’s young people and how they view the opposite sex, fueling myths and stereotypes that aren’t remotely true.

Take, for example, the belief that a majority, or even a sizable minority, of young women are engaging in sex work or deliberately scamming men for their money. This is easy to understand if you are terminally online. If you log online, you will immediately see loads of sex workers. Social media is a prime way for sex workers to market their content or services, so it makes sense that you’d see them. Remember “pussy in bio?” Sometimes, all it takes is an innocent photo of an attractive actress, and immediately your replies will be full of sex workers (or bots pretending to be sex workers) posting themselves for comparison.

But lots of young men seem to believe that “most” Gen Z women are doing OnlyFans, or frequently being “flown out to Miami” by wealthy men looking to buy sex. One can’t help but wonder if these men are experiencing women primarily from being online, and are therefore exposed to an inflated percentage of sex workers.

Exhibit A:

Most women who need money do exactly what men do—jobs unrelated to sex. If you truly believe that your average woman is doing sex work, I want to say you’re too far gone. But more charitably, I believe today’s young people are devastatingly out of touch because they’ve been fed a curated feed of slop regarding the opposite sex, while being farther away from real-life interaction with the opposite sex. Given their limited real-life interactions, they have very little evidence to counteract this nonsense.

But there is no data to suggest this perception—that Gen Z women are uniquely promiscuous, if not doing sex work the majority of the time—is true. Gen Z is the least sexually active generation in recent history, something that holds true for both men and women. Nearly half of Gen Z have not had sex (keep in mind this number isn’t that shocking, given that some zoomers are minors, but teens are still having less sex than they used to.) Average lifetime sexual partner count isn’t very high either—somewhere in between 4 and 8. I guess it’s possible everyone is lying, but I’m having a hard time wrapping my head around a woman who openly produces porn on OnlyFans and does full-service sex work, yet lies on anonymous surveys about only having slept with five men.

Prolific sex worker Bonnie Blue, who claims to be pregnant (I am still a truther on this) has gone viral multiple times for teasing some gross sexual stunts that will be performed at her baby shower. The correct response to this is, “Bonnie Blue is gross” or perhaps even “I don’t believe Bonnie Blue is actually pregnant.” However, a common response I’ve seen is the famous copypasta: “Women will say shit like this and then wonder why the entire planet and every major religion has imposed strict social restrictions on their sovereignty since the dawn of time.”

Really? Women? Most women are undergoing golden showers and gangbangs at their baby showers? I must have attended some unusually boring ones.

This is why discussions of modern-day hookup culture make very little sense. I’ve written publicly about hookup culture (wherein very young women and teenage girls were pressured to be more sexually open than they wanted to be, and promiscuous sex was the norm) but this culture was not something that permeated most people’s lives, even when it was at its peak in the nineties and 2000s. I witnessed it because I was surrounded by upper middle class, highly educated white people who seemed to be patient zeroes for it. But no matter how big hookup culture felt in 2005 on certain college campuses, it wasn’t reflected in any kind of countrywide statistics about partner count. Your average woman in 2005 was not partaking in it, perhaps just perusing magazines at the grocery store which talked about it. Given that people are having even less sex today than back then, I am especially incredulous that hookup culture or mass sex work are problems ruining an entire generation of potential wives.

And yet- some people seem to believe that today’s young people (especially women) are engaged in nonstop debauchery. It’s easy to believe that if your understanding of women comes from guests on the Whatever podcast and drunk women being interviewed by men with microphones on the street at 1 AM about how much money a man needs to make to date them, condensed into buzzy, short-form video.

Plus, even a lot of the “dating” content that riles up young men online is also just advertisements for sex workers. Sometimes, it’s not even real. Take, for example, this AI-generated Facebook account that seems to be specifically focused on a “trucker girl with smelly vagina” fetish that I did not know existed:

TFW the trucker that just drove past you has an unshaven 🐱 and guys keep refusing to eat it:

Anyway, this is an extreme example, and I like to think today’s young people are able to clock this as AI, but there is plenty of other content out there—content that technically features real people, but is specifically engineered to rile up viewers and make them believe this generation of young women is hopelessly soulless, materialistic and sex-obsessed.

When you encounter a young man online who is opposed to getting married, or who is wary of getting involved with a woman in any capacity, and you ask him why, 99% of the time he will not mention any experiences with women he knows in real life. He likely won’t even mention experiences his friends have had. It used to be that if you were bitter about women, you had an ex who cheated on you. Now, all the evidence is “this gross woman in a short-form video said a man needed to make $300K to date her and she wasn’t even hot.”

I’ve dwelled on the obsession with sex work and promiscuity, but the content that tricks men into believing ridiculous things about women goes way beyond that. You have endless content by women deliberately trying to rile people up, claiming that men under six feet tall are repulsive (the vast majority of men are under six feet tall, and find love.) I once saw a tweet that bragged about having sex with one guy, then when the other guy noticed the first guy’s semen, she claimed to already be wet. I have my doubts this even happened, and yet, men everywhere were responding with some variant of, “See? This is why I can’t date!”

Yesterday I covered Ban Men feminism, or the variant of vaguely buzzy/liberal (but not actually radical) feminism that insisted most straight women would be happier on an all-female commune, reading Buzzfeed listicles and drinking wine, then being forced to interact with a—ew—man. Of course, when I published this, I got the usual onslaught of, “Was anyone really saying this, or just a few weirdos online?” While I maintain that Ban Men feminism had legitimate cultural power especially within the types of media millennials were consuming in the 2010s, I’m not sure it matters. Loads of performative, just-venting or ridiculous misandrist content is all over the Internet. In an ideal world, like all the Ban Men stuff, it remains a joke among women and never goes further—the boomer man “I hate my bitch wife” jokes of the 2010s, if you will. But thanks to social media, I am convinced that most of the people who saw this content were actually men.

Most ragebait involves women, and angers men, but there’s plenty of this content with the genders reversed, which could be in part why young women report increasing rates of heteropessimism. Perhaps it’s even a vicious cycle—the men who watch ragebait content about women begin saying increasingly nasty things about women, and then other young women (not the subjects of the ragebait content) start believing that all young men are embittered misogynists.

When I ran a survey to test this hypothesis, young, single straight women were actually more likely than their male counterparts to report worrying that the opposite sex was engaged in behavior that made them unsuitable for relationships (69% vs. 20%). And certainly, it’s impossible to write about “both” men and women feeling worried and embittered without being honest about the fact that serious injuries and death from intimate partner violence and sexual assault overwhelmingly go in one particular direction, even if not 100% of the time. (When I wrote about this obvious fact, I did get some crap for “not believing male victims,” which is a totally separate and untrue accusation—but you can see what I’m saying about the Internet bringing out the worst and dumbest in us.)

But while I’m sure some young women are worried about those serious things, sexual and intimate partner violence are not new, nor are men committing these crimes at increasing rates. Instead, today’s young women are increasingly bombarded with young men saying egregious things online—usually things they would never say in person. The “her body, my choice” joke that began with Nick Fuentes and circulated online among teenage boys was frequently used as an example for why “boys these days” are hopeless and girls are justified in wanting nothing to do with them. And to be clear, yes, the joke is repugnant, but I’m reluctant to say it means anything about the suitability of an entire generation of men. I would actually put money on the fact that if you stopped a random twenty-year-old guy on the street, he will be unlikely to have ever watched a Nick Fuentes clip, and probably doesn’t even know the joke exists.

Not only were most young men not making that joke, but despite the unfortunate popularity of people like Nick Fuentes and the Tate brothers, we are looking at the issue (again, as always) through an especially online lens. In fact, boys age 18-29 are more likely to hold their mothers, their fathers, their teachers, and checks notes Barack Obama (who was President when many of these men were children) in high esteem, than people like Andrew Tate.

Is it bad that 31% of these men like Andrew Tate? Sure, but that attitude should not be held up as an example of “young men these days,” nor is it reasonable to declare you are scared to date because everyone loves Andrew Tate. Most young men these days idolize normal people! However, if you spend too much time online you will genuinely begin to believe that your “average” Gen Z man is a groyper and aspiring rapist.

These young women are also witnessing content about bad men—and not just the clearly-horrible violent ones, but the ones that appear much more common yet insidious. Take, for example, the videos produced by prominent influencer Paige Connell, which my friend has nicknamed “the queen of Invisible LaborTok.” Paige Connell occupies a big space in the online ecosystem, and her content may very well be relatable to married women whose husbands believe their time is more valuable than that of their wives’, only put in the bare minimum of effort, and still pat themselves on the back for playing with their kids sometimes. You know, the guys who have been living in the same house for ten years but still need to ask their wife where the cast iron pans are and who don’t know the name of their child’s dentist.

These guys exist in great numbers, women are married to them, and it makes sense for these frustrated women to watch content about their predicament. But this content frequently makes its way across feeds of women who are not in this predicament. A newly-married female friend of mine (whose husband is not, in any way, Like This) watched one Paige Connell video that came across her Instagram feed. She reported that after watching this one video (to which she could not relate) her entire Instagram feed was full of women complaining about their bad husbands. She texted me and said she somehow got lost in “I Hate My Husband Gram” and couldn’t escape. She was already happily married, so this was nothing more than a funny experience for her. But for a young woman who isn’t married and has no experience with men at all, it’s hard to imagine that mainlining this kind of content is going to be good for her. It’s the same reason I would not advise a young single man, who has never had a girlfriend, to watch videos from disgruntled ex-husbands about having to pay alimony.

The Twitter/X algorithm also seemed to change recently to be even more laser-focused on specific things as opposed to a curated feed on a variety of your interests. For example, I made the mistake of engaging with a few tweets about the semi-recent hantavirus outbreak, mostly out of curiosity. Almost instantly, my entire feed was awash with doomsday predictions about a global hantavirus pandemic that would kill half the world’s population, with seemingly everyone in the world convinced that hantavirus was airborne just like covid. (They moved onto ebola being airborne the week after—one must wonder if there is any disease they don’t believe is airborne.) I was almost ready to start hoarding toilet paper in the midst of an OCD-fueled anxiety frenzy when I talked to my brother and told him that “everyone is freaking out about hantavirus.” He said he hadn’t seen anything about it save for one article in the news, and nobody he knew was talking about it either. Similarly (but less upsettingly) one Twitter drama about culturally appropriative ice cream took over everyone’s feeds a few weeks ago.

If you engage, perhaps even just a few times, with ragebait content about the opposite sex, it will dominate your feed and it won’t even take long. You may not even notice it’s happening. And I know it’s easy for many of us to say “They should just log off! They should realize the Internet is real life!” but that’s much easier said than done. Many of us have been influenced by what we’ve seen online, even if not in this space. There is a reason social media is so effective for political purposes, both good and evil. Even smart people can be influenced by an algorithm that hijacks something they care about. And if you are young, inexperienced, and anxious about what marriage and dating might mean for you (or whether it’s worth it to pursue these things at all) the algorithm will quickly identify this weak spot and bombard you with “evidence” to sway your decision. For some people, that evidence may be a constant stream of trad birth rate anxiety, discouraging young women to get jobs and telling young people that having fifteen kids while living in a tent in the woods is “the way.” This content is also stupid, but there are many barriers to actually doing this. There are very few barriers to being disaffected, lonely and bitter—in fact, many people are doing that right now without trying.

This type of online content—aimed at both young men and women—is the reason that people believe ridiculous things about the state of modern marriage. I dispelled those myths here, but basically, the only thing that’s gotten uniquely “worse” about marriage in modern times is the fact that fewer people are doing it—and depending on who you ask, that might be a good thing (fewer people marrying who shouldn’t be married, I suppose.) But other pessimistic statements just aren’t true. Yes, it’s true that women do more childcare and housework than men, but today’s men still do more than ever. “Unhelpful lazy husbands” is not a modern or new phenomenon, and women in the prior generation had the option to eschew marriage and didn’t, so it’s not like this is the first time women have been able to say no to these men. Marriage is also not a recipe for a dead bedroom—they happen, but most marriages involve people having roughly the amount of sex they want to have, and married moms over forty are actually more sexually satisfied than anyone else—especially younger single women. Married men also have more sex than single men, even when evaluating good-looking and wealthy single men. The divorce rate is not “skyrocketing,” and is in fact, the lowest it’s been in some time (in part because people are more selective about who they marry.) Infidelity is also not skyrocketing, and as you’re about to see in one of my upcoming articles, is not the reason for most dead bedrooms.

This is not to say that every heterosexual relationship is great, and everyone should get married tomorrow, preferably to the first warm body of an acceptable gender who crosses their path. But most humans don’t want to be alone. The drive to partner, and quite frankly, to fuck, will always be there. It’s one thing to feel negatively about dating and marriage because you were horribly betrayed in the past, and another thing to be the type of rare person who simply prefers being on their own. But it’s an entirely other thing to have the attitude of an embittered divorcé when you are a nineteen-year-old who has been on two dates in their entire life.

And what’s perhaps most upsetting and daunting about this entire trend is that the cycle of embittering talk is self-sustaining. Many of the “I can’t date—just look at how horrible men/women are!” examples are of other relatively inexperienced men and women, repeating their own misperceptions about how bad the opposite sex is. When you see a tweet from a young man saying he’ll never get married because his wife will gain fifty pounds and stop having sex with him, he learned that from someone else, and that sort of talk is now being served to his young female counterparts who will use it as evidence that men just aren’t worth it.

In a world where none of us have the option to spend all day online riling ourselves up, not only would we not see any of this, but it would matter less if we did. Young, straight people want to have sex with each other, and they’ll never fully be able to turn off that desire, only feel increasingly angry and anxious about it. This is why I wrote that a heterosexual sex strike (which was being proposed by a woman who was married to another woman!) will simply never happen. A lot of these young people will not psyop themselves into truly “going their own way” and happily abandoning their sexual and romantic urges to live out the rest of their lives with friends. They’ll just be horny, lonely, and worst of all—mad.
