The Unbearable Burdens of Childhood

Chang-rae Lee’s “A Tender Age” takes the despair of a boy seriously.

Ron Charles
Aug 18
 
READ IN APP
 

The Tuesday Review

Chang-rae Lee’s new novel, A Tender Age, recalls a period of childhood freedom impossible for today’s iPhone-toting kids — or their anxious parents — to imagine.

“In our 1970s town,” Lee writes, “the adults, for whatever reason, whether they were working a job or not, left us to our own, and to one another.”

It’s true. After scarfing down peanut butter and jelly sandwiches, my friends and I would tromp into the Hundred Acre Wood behind our subdivision and not think about returning home until dinnertime. Our long unsupervised days were full of capture the flag, fort-building and -wrecking, and, if the older kids could get their hands on them, firecrackers. But Lee’s story burns nostalgia off that era like a mean kid frying an ant with a magnifying glass.

The narrator, Jeon-Gi, is a Korean-American man looking back half a century to the summer he turned 11. “When I was a kid,” he says, “there were some rough times.” Just what an Olympian act of understatement that is will only gradually become clear.

Jeon-Gi and his buddies live in a diverse, working-class neighborhood north of New York City. “We were young enough,” he writes, “that the bonds felt fierce, like in a tribe, tourniquet tight, yet our tribe was fueled by constant disagreements, contentions, sometimes outright bloodletting, which made everything dire and fragile.”

Lee offers a clear-eyed vision of the playground’s regal hierarchy, both its essential comedy and its horrible cruelty. Although the dimensions of the arena are cramped, the stakes are vast. These boys and girls play mildly injurious games, learn new swear words and slurs, and adhere to stringent standards for sneakers and haircuts. “A chunky kid, bordering on fat,” Jeon-Gi is generally happy, popular enough, but, he notes, “to be a kid was to be bullied.”

Which becomes the irritant that rubs Jeon-Gi’s soul raw. He can handle the typical picking-on just fine, but when Tommy Reilly, a creepy, violent boy from a different apartment building, starts taunting him, he grows alarmed, then paranoid. It’s a crisis that reveals to him, more clearly than ever before, how little control he has over his own life. Unable to tell any adults about his predicament, and with Tommy Reilly growing ever more threatening, Jeon-Gi begins to fantasize about how he might scar his antagonist, even kill him.

In the claustrophobic panic of the boy’s mind, every step to defend himself feels justified, but when he’s pushed too far, he erupts in a way that finally can’t be ignored, that shocks his gentle parents and threatens to derail his life. How perfectly appropriate that Jeon-Gi’s name literally means “electricity,” that elemental force for good or destruction.

“This is the nature of certain youthful happenings,” he writes, “how they can become a knot in the flesh that every other occurrence must bypass in order for you to know yourself, in order for you to grow.”

This was also the nature of childhood in an era when therapy was less common and school psychologists less ubiquitous. Jeon-Gi has no words to confess what he’s feeling or what he’s enduring. As a straight-A student, he’s disappointed to fail the test for mental illness. Nonetheless, his implacable silence becomes further evidence of his deviance, which plunges him into a world of wildly ineffectual and even abusive counseling. (The scare-’em-straight day trip to a prison is a chapter I will never forget.)

I asked Chang-rae Lee, “What do you think adults tend to forget—or sentimentalize—about being 11 years old?”

Lee, the author of six previous novels, has spoken of A Tender Age as his most autobiographical work of fiction. That’s not to suggest that the shocking events of Jeon-Gi’s life are Lee’s or that the parents and the sister in this novel are the author’s actual family members. But the intensity of the child’s need to assimilate, that fierce urge to reject one’s loving mother with her funny accent for the bleaching immersion of the American melting pot, feels tragically real in these pages.

And it’s the voice of the novel that proves most alluring. An 11-year-old protagonist poses a special challenge, one that reaches all the way back to that imperiled boy floating down the Mississippi on a raft. Twain, like so many novelists who followed him, maintains the illusion of the child’s consciousness through the charming vernacular of a boy’s speech.

Lee takes the opposite approach. His narrator throughout is a mature adult articulating with casual elegance and solemnity the boy’s inchoate terrors and desires. He claims, “How little you can fathom the consequence of a time,” but in fact this entire novel is an uncommon fathoming of those consequences. It’s a haunting act of ventriloquism, defined by self-consciousness and steeped in regret. What, he keeps wondering, brings out the evil in other people, in himself? Here is a person at the moment he recognizes himself becoming “a totally different creature,” as though the butterfly could describe its own chrysalis.

This older perspective initially risks disrupting the immediate sense that we’re experiencing the struggles of an 11-year-old. In the second half of the novel, when Jeon-Gi is sent to a Korean summer camp for two weeks that will end up shaping the rest of his life, his perceptive analysis of his campmates, his fully formed appreciation of their nobility, their wickedness, and their guile, can seem almost preternatural.

What I quickly grew to admire, though, is Lee’s willingness to consider the boy’s tribulations with the seriousness they deserve. He’s trying to capture that ephemeral stage when self-loathing first condenses in the fog of childhood guilt. While drinking bug juice and making lanyards and smelling his tentmates’ farts, this narrator is intellectually and emotionally equipped to convey what the boy feels but won’t be able to articulate for decades. The fear, the desire, the resentment, the shame that young Jeon-Gi experiences are no less painful, no less overwhelming simply because he’s experiencing them on a rainy campout. Only the deadening effect of familiarity and our falsely sentimental regard for adolescence allow us to pretend that a kid’s dirt-covered longings are somehow less existential than our own.

After Lee’s strikingly different novels about spies, veterans, suburbanites, war orphans, and dystopian wanderers, A Tender Age is another reminder that he’s one of the most insightful and wide-ranging authors in the country.

Making predictions about how our literary canon will develop is a fool’s errand. After all, just about every novel fades into obscurity, and some of our most famous ones have risen from it. Literary persistence depends largely upon school curricula — the choices, good or bad, made in thousands of high school and college English classes. But as someone who taught and loathed John Knowles’s A Separate Peace for several years, I wish I could go back and talk with my students about this infinitely finer novel that traces the unspeakable pain that twists around the spine even while we’re still reaching for the life ahead.◆

A Tender Age, by Chang-rae Lee (Riverhead, 368 pp., $30)
