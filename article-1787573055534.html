An Alien In A Skin Suit

When I was six, I told my school I was an alien in a skin suit and they believed me.

I feel like I’ve used one particular term far too often in my writing, and it’s “alien in a skin suit.” Most of the time, I say this when I’m referring to how I feel about myself, especially with regard to my well-documented social ineptitude and how I sometimes feel like I have to put on a convincing impression of a human to get people to like me (as I’ve learned, if this impression is a literal Trump impersonation, it doesn’t play as well—after all, the guy’s approval ratings aren’t so hot.)

But perhaps I’m so inclined to repeatedly say “alien in a skin suit” because one of my most formative memories involved quite literally telling everyone that I was an alien in a skin suit.

Most of you know I grew up on the Northeast, but what many of you don’t know is that I was born in Los Angeles. My memories of Los Angeles are fragmented, even with my freakishly good memory. My Los Angeles memories may be incomplete but they are specific: the wrought iron gates in my neighborhood, a fire truck play structure at the playground made of always-too-hot silver metal (I promise I have some memories that were not related to types of metals.) I recall, very faintly, my brother being born (despite my parents waiting until his birth to know his sex, I was so confident he would be a boy that I didn’t even bother to ask my dad about it when he returned home from the hospital.)

But it feels like my sentience and memory really flipped on after my family (which by then included my newborn brother and six-year-old me) moved across the country. And one of the first things I remember vividly, in my entire life, was my decision to tell my entire first grade class that I was an alien—something they actually believed.

I entered first grade at my new school shortly after we moved. I have a summer birthday, so my parents faced a decision about whether to send me to first grade at just-turned-six, or hold me back. They decided on the former. My mom tells me that she assumed I would be “bored” in kindergarten because I was so incredibly smart, but unfortunately for her I remained bored in school for the next eleven years, and not because I was smart.

I immediately surmised I would not fit in with my first grade classmates. As the youngest in the class, I was prone to oversensitivity. My most embarrassing memory was probably when I loudly farted during storytime, and instead of pretending nothing happened, I whipped around, blamed the most troublesome boy in the class, and then had to go in time out for “farting and lying about it,” all the while sobbing hysterically. At one point, my first grade teacher gave me a literal medal for “going a full day without crying,” which sounds like an award that Mr. Krabs might have bestowed upon SpongeBob, much to Squidward’s exasperation.

I had a few friends here and there, but I felt like the other kids had all this shared history from kindergarten and earlier, and it felt difficult to break into these groups. Many of my classmates even attended daycare together, and had mothers who hung out together (which, from what I remember of those moms, mostly involved discussion of various low-fat diets). I noticed other little quirks too, which made me feel different from these kids. They seemed very interested in having money, and asking gauche questions about how much money people had, something which just hadn’t occurred to me.

My hobbies weren’t the problem. My interests were pretty typically little-girlish in every possible way. The year before, I agonized over how I would go as both a puppy and a princess for Halloween, eventually forcing my mom to sew me a very unsettling dog costume with eye holes, which I wore underneath a purple satin princess dress. I liked Barbies. I liked baby dolls. I especially liked Samantha, the American Girl Doll I was gifted to distract me from the fact that my new little brother was about to get a lot of attention (The first thing I did with her, recalling things I had witnessed upon visiting my mother in the hospital after his birth, was to stick some kind of lighted lamp post from my Barbie house between her legs and tell her I was checking her stitches.)

But at some point that year, I saw some kind of community puppet show which featured various alien characters who had invaded Earth and were living amongst the humans. These aliens mostly looked like humans too, as they had disguised themselves by wearing human clothes, or perhaps actual human costumes (I forget). Think Invader Zim. Before this show, I had never been interested in outer space or aliens. The closest I came was my short-lived Power Rangers phase in preschool which was more social contagion than anything. But for some reason, I found this puppet show fascinating, and immediately developed an obsession with aliens. Not just aliens, but aliens who were able to walk amongst humans by wearing human costumes. Aliens who could look just like humans—who could even be beautiful or stylish or cool—but still secretly be aliens.

When I got back to school, I had a particularly memorable gym class, because our teacher, Mrs. Thompson, was suspiciously absent. Before I get into what happened in this class, I need to talk to you about Mrs. Thompson. And before I get into that, I should tell you that my only friend from elementary school with whom I’m still in touch frequently texts me about Mrs. Thompson and it’s been thirty years.

Mrs. Thompson was essentially what would happen if you took a female drill sergeant, dishonorably discharged her for being too mean, and then put her in charge of teaching six-year-olds how to play softball. Alternatively, a far less original description: The Trunchbull in a pastel polo shirt. It’s possible Mrs. Thompson was uniquely mean to me because I was terrible at sports (she repeatedly complained that I “wasn’t trying” because she couldn’t imagine anyone actually sucking this much ass at dodgeball) but other, sportier kids also reported unpleasant run-ins with her. In particular, most of us experienced some trauma from her sitting on a lawn chair on the hottest day of the year while making us perform the pacer run fitness test, loudly blowing her whistle to humiliate the children who had lagged behind. A rumor spread that she knocked a kid off the climbing rope just to kick him in the balls. At the risk of sounding like an idiot: yes, this was a made-up story, but what does it say about Mrs. Thompson that everyone believed it to be true?

Another important factoid about Mrs. Thompson was that despite appearing, sounding and acting as manly as possible, she had a ridiculously gargantuan ass—think “diaper at the splash pad”—and somehow not in a sexy way by the standards of any decade of beauty standards or any culture. (I would never make fun of this feature today, but the same could not be said of me when I was six, and we are in the mind of six-year-old me.) Said ass was often concealed with belted knee-length khaki shorts, and rested below her trademark polo shirt, a pair of sporty polarized sunglasses, and a hairdo that must have come to fruition when she went to a barber shop and said, “Give me the John Kerry.” And in case you’re wondering, no, Mrs. Thompson was not a lesbian (or at least not an open one.) She was married to a man, and was somehow married to a different man before him.

Anyway, on the day I returned to school after the alien puppet show, Mrs. Thompson wasn’t there. She always made us line up on the floor in our assigned “squads” and we were so terrified of her wrath that we dutifully stayed in our squads as we waited for her to arrive. A good amount of time passed by and Mrs. Thompson wasn’t there. So finally, I decided to tell everyone a completely fake story about what I suspected had happened to her, if only for the two motivations that have driven most of my social decisions: a desire to amuse myself, and the insatiable hunger for attention.

“I actually know what happened to Mrs. Thompson,” I said. “She’s going to come to class, but it won’t really be her. It’ll be an alien in a skin suit.”

As the kids argued with me (and, honestly, fair) I was steadfast in my conviction that Mrs. Thompson had been abducted by aliens, turned into some kind of skin-suit-clone thing, and the “real” Mrs. Thompson was hanging out in a space ship far above the atmosphere while phony alien Mrs. Thompson would be returning to Earth any minute.

“When you see her,” I warned them, “She will say she isn’t an alien. She will try to prove to you that she isn’t an alien or even say things that the real Mrs. Thompson would say. She will look and sound exactly like Mrs. Thompson. But that’s because the alien also wiped her brain, so the alien knows everything about Mrs. Thompson. In fact, the more she tries to say she isn’t an alien, the more you know she is.”

At some point, a kid asked me how I knew all this, so I performed my big reveal. “It’s because I’m an alien in a skin suit too, and I know the aliens who abducted her. In fact, they’re my real parents.” A lot of the kids believed this part of the story right away. Like, Oh, okay, never mind, that checks out.

Sure enough, Mrs. Thompson showed up a few minutes later, claiming to have been at the “dentist.” A likely story. Right on schedule, the students began accusing her of being an alien. Muhahahaha, I chuckled. But just as I had warned them, “Mrs. Thompson” was suspiciously familiar with the lore of the real Mrs. Thompson. She was annoyed right off the bat, but became genuinely distressed as she ran around to all the students demanding they feel her arm to prove she was a real human being. (Of course, a skin suit WOULD feel like that. Next!)

I forget how the class ended, but somehow she figured out that I was the person who made this up. Her response was to have my parents meet with her after school so she could inform them of this grave misbehavior. Unfortunately for Mrs. Thompson, my parents burst out laughing when she told them, and praised me for my creativity and commitment to the bit.

Eventually, I think the kids realized the Mrs. Thompson alien thing was a prank, but most of them still believed that I was an alien. It helped that I spun up elaborate lore about my alien family, explaining how my parents were merely human surrogates enlisted by the aliens to raise me until I could return to my home planet. I told them I was a benevolent alien and had no intentions of “invading” Earth, just of witnessing human behavior and understanding human society. I enjoyed the attention as they asked me questions about what it was like on my home planet, how I got into my skin suit, and what I looked like out of it.

This experience was the closest I had gotten to any kind of “popularity” that year, and naturally, I reveled in it. As a current friend of mine often says, I am at my best when I’m “holding court,” vaguely entertaining a group of people as opposed to participating in a multi-way conversation. I’m sure that as I weaved tales of my alien past, I was fairly entertaining—perhaps even likable—as long as the kids believed these stories were true.

But at some point, they wisened up and realized I was lying. I forget the details of this part, but I do remember that after my ruse was uncovered, I spent years—quite literally years—trying to prove that I was deliberately misleading people about the alien thing, and did not actually think I was an alien. This distinction is important—one option is kind of obnoxious, the other is downright insane. Unfortunately, everyone went with the “she’s insane” explanation.

This very brief era in first grade followed me all the way to my pre-teen years, where my elementary school combined with three others to form a much larger middle school. I hoped that entering this new school, with 75% of my class having no idea who I was, would give me the opportunity to reinvent myself as something other than the alien skin suit girl. I even got my mom to purchase an arsenal of sparkly butterfly clips, in the hopes of becoming the “butterfly clip girl” and drowning out the alien thing. But as soon as I met new kids and shared my name, they would ask me if I was “the girl who thought she was an alien in a skin suit in first grade.” And all I could really say was, through deep embarrassment, “I pretended to be an alien. I didn’t actually think I was one.” It’s kind of like when you ask your boyfriend if he was flirting with other girls at the club and he responds, “Well first off, it wasn’t a club, it was a lounge.”

Eventually, people stopped talking so much about the alien thing because there were plenty of other social errors to mock. At one point in sixth grade, we all had to create Medieval lordship names for ourselves and, oblivious to the fact that everyone else just chose their actual surname (I didn’t realize that was even an option—what a stupid project!) I had chosen “Pennote” because it was a combination of pen (writing) and note (music) only for everyone to make penis jokes or pronounce it like a type of pasta that originated from penne (granted, my school was at least 30% Italian, I was asking for that.) As I entered puberty and began understanding how I could dress to look less like a cute little girl and more like an edgy teenager, kids spread rumors that I was having sex with adult men outside of school, and worse yet—paying them! Can you imagine anything more embarrassing than being a twelve-year-old girl so hideous and repulsive that she must compensate pedophiles to molest her? Anyway, the list went on.

For many years, I convinced myself that my classmates believed this ridiculous alien story because they were stupid. And surely, some of them were. But looking back now, maybe everything about me made it fairly believable. Maybe they picked up on something within me—the same thing that made me feel so inspired by watching a puppet show about humanoid aliens. And now, when I think about whether I pretended to be an alien in a skin suit or whether I actually thought I was one, I don’t really know.
