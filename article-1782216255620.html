"Be Yourself" Only Works For People Who Aren't Annoying

In defense of letting the freak flag fly at half mast.

Cartoons Hate Her
Jun 23
∙
Paid
 
READ IN APP
 

Somewhere along the way, people got “geeky” and “unlikable” mixed up.

Geeky people aren’t unlikable. Well, they can be, but their geekiness is not the reason. Stereotypically geeky interests in anime, comic books, fantasy franchises, D&D or anything adjacent are actually incredibly common, if not a great jumping off point to make friends. I concede that being a geek can be challenging during adolescence, when (depending on your generation) you felt judged if you weren’t the Hollister polo shirt type, but once you’re an adult, being a geek is basically a one-way ticket to infinite hobby clubs and avenues to make friends. If you’re scared to talk to people, that’s going to make things harder, but being a geek is not the problem in that case. Geeks are not, in any real sense, “weird,” if only because there are so many of them.

Back when I was in high school, I noticed this. The geeks always had friends! Simply “being a geek” would not cause social isolation and friendlessness. And because this wasn’t a 1980’s movie, nobody got stuffed in a locker by a letterman-jacket-wearing guy named Brad who pounded his fist into a cupped hand and said, “Well, well, well, if it isn’t the dweeb brigade.”

When people hear that you are struggling socially because you’re “weird,”(as I have) for some reason their assumption is that you must be a geek (or some other variation of non-normie) and therefore you should just “be yourself” and find other weird people to befriend. But people have flattened “weird” to include “enjoys comic books” alongside “everyone who meets this person is immediately put off by their objectively unappealing behavior.”

For people whose “weirdness” begins and ends with the fact that they aren’t into sports and they enjoy watching anime or doing improv, yes, they should be themselves because that’s actually not that weird at all. But for other people—often neurodivergent or just “socially challenged” people who are weird not because they like fantasy novels but because they do annoying things—being yourself (while making zero adjustments and implementing zero filters lest you “dull your shine”) only goes so far.

In some ways, I actually wish I was a geek. I’ve been socially inept my entire life—something I’ve written about in great detail, and I deliberately made changes to my social behaviors as I got older after realizing that perhaps, I was not just “quirky” but extremely irritating.

People can sometimes tolerate geeky and weird people, because we associate poor social skills with geeky interests, and when we see a geeky person who can’t hold a conversation very well, we’re like “Okay, this checks out.” But most geeks are fine socially. Maybe they wouldn’t click with a total and utter normie, but they click with each other. In fact, it really feels like the geekier extroverts I know have the biggest, most full social lives. Being a geek often, but not always, involves lots of hobbies and fandoms, which inherently connects you with other people.

I am, unfortunately, something else. I’m actually not very geeky. I have some weird interests, like bizarre Internet subcultures and red pill blogs from 2011, which would not appeal to most normies, but they also are not boilerplate geeky interests. To top it off, I do not dress like a geek (although for most of my life I haven’t dressed like a normie either—the kindest way to put it is a “Polly pocket costume,” and the meanest way of putting it is, “Nurse, she’s escaped again,” which is, I suppose, its own category of weird which doesn’t rise to the point of fitting into any particular subculture.)

Being weird is not really a problem, if by “weird” you mean geeky and not normie. Being socially inept and off-putting is the problem, and I would argue that social ineptitude comes off worse in people who cannot explain it with being geeky:

People make the same mistake with “theater kids.” I was never seriously into theater (and at the risk of splitting hairs in the stupidest fucking way possible, I’m more of an improv person) but I’m closer to a theater kid than a geek. If a creative, theatrical person is disliked, people assume it’s because they’re too creative and theatrical, and they just need to meet other jazz-handsers. And yes, some people will simply loathe theater kids for that reason alone, but most won’t. If everyone hates a theater kid (and I say “kid” to encompass adults with theater kid energy too) it’s likely not because they enjoy the lyrical craft of Stephen Sondheim.

There was a theater girl in my high school who everyone found extremely annoying. Even I did, and as an annoying person, I like to think I justifiably give people some wiggle room in this area, but she plowed through it. She believed she was the best actress in the school, but wasn’t really very good, and was prone to bursting into tears when she failed to get lead roles (to her credit, she was low on physical vanity—she was eager to play old ladies and bridge trolls and villains—but she was ruthlessly competitive and hated the idea of having a small part.) When we had a debate project and we all had to pick a topic about which we were passionate, she chose the abolition of child labor laws because they prevented underage actors from getting enough work.

A couple times, I tried to be friendly with her, but she was so stuck on herself and her future as a Broadway actress that she didn’t even seem to want friends. She only cared about theater, to the point that when she didn’t get the titular role in Hello Dolly, she ran around the school like a ghastly Victorian apparition, wailing in the hallways. Students traded notes on where they had seen her crying, coming to the conclusion that she had been on a crying tour throughout every inch of the campus.

Perhaps unsurprisingly, this girl was diagnosed with autism in her early twenties, and ironically, is one of the only theater kids from my high school who is still doing theater today. But what’s important to note about her behavior was that “enjoying theater” and “not being a skinny blonde cheerleader” weren’t her problems. Her problem was that she was deeply annoying. Other theater kids had plenty of friends!

If she wanted to be well-liked, I would not have advised her to stop doing theater, straighten her hair, wear preppy clothes, and change her entire personality. But I would have told her that showing interest in other people, being able to carry on a two-way conversation, and regulating how she displayed emotion (read: not having a tantrum in the hallway over a casting decision) would have gone a long way. There was an a capella group in my high school who got lunch together every day and frequently burst into song together. Cringe, right? But guess what—they still did not like her, but they liked each other. She was surrounded by other artsy kids who should have been “her people” and nobody liked her because she was annoying.

But the best example I can give of the dreaded, “insufferable” quadrant of “socially weird but not geeky” is Jenna Maroney of 30 Rock fame.

Jenna is about as far from geeky as you can get, despite her voice acting in the world’s first outer space themed porn video game. She’s a pretty and slender blonde actress with late-2000s barrel curls, who wears conventional, flattering clothing and who would never be caught dead in a Gamestop.

And of course, Jenna is an exaggerated comedic character, so she behaves even more outlandishly than anyone would in real life—even anyone socially inept. For example, she begins a charity project called “Camp Jenna” to teach little blonde girls how to be mean, and uses a loophole to sell her “used” underwear by rubbing the crotch of new panties with lunchmeat. But comedic hyperbole aside, the themes that might translate to real-life Jennas are there. Jenna is, above all, vain—she has a major crisis of confidence when she is cast in a Gossip Girl type show not to play Tartine, the twenty-year-old socialite, but her “elderly” mother who is dying at the old age of forty. She is so insecure and self-centered that she requires her co-star in a Christmas special sing off-key so she doesn’t sound bad by comparison. She is so in love with herself that when she finally finds love, it’s with a drag performer who enjoys doing drag shows in a Jenna Maroney costume. While I can’t say I relate to all of that (I don’t think I’m particularly catty or arrogant) I can relate to the fact that Jenna is always performing and really loves getting validation, to the point that she misses out on how other people might be feeling or what they might be doing.

There are glimmers of humanity throughout the show where you understand why someone might like Jenna. Liz Lemon, 30 Rock’s main character who is also Jenna’s boss and best friend, does like her, even if she also finds her exhausting. But Jenna is routinely excluded and mocked by other writers and cast members for being vapid, narcissistic, and well…annoying. Because she is.

People don’t dislike Jenna because she’s a theater kid. They dislike her because, whether it’s intentional or not, she sucks the life out of the room, makes everything about herself, and comes off as arrogant and stuck-up, even if this is merely a veneer for her vulnerability and insecurities. (This is also why we enjoy watching her, and the irony is not lost on me that some people might read my work for the same reason.)

If Jenna Maroney were a real-life person, and came to you, and asked you what she could do, not to make everyone like her (impossible) but to make fewer people dislike her, would you honestly look her in the eye and tell her to “be herself?” To “just find her people?”

You certainly wouldn’t tell her to give up singing or acting, dye her hair brown, or start getting really into sports. Those things would all constitute changing her entire persona, and they wouldn’t really help her socially anyway. But you probably also wouldn’t tell her that everything she’s doing is great and nothing needs to change. You’d probably tell her to step outside herself more often, to be kinder to people, to show more interest in other people, to celebrate other people’s wins more often and be more supportive of her coworkers, tone down the constant performance…and she could do all of that and still, in the major ways, “be herself.”

A lot of people probably don’t ever really think about changing their social behavior in any major way because most people never have to think about this. It’s the same reason that effortlessly thin people with fast metabolisms have never had to diet or take GLP-1s. Most people just aren’t that annoying! Perhaps they won’t click with everyone (and that’s also normal) but it won’t be hard for them to find people who enjoy being around them. Even if “their people” constitute 20% of the population, that’s still a lot of people. In my unfiltered case—and in the case of people like Jenna Maroney—they number is more like 0.2%. Change must be made, unless we truly don’t want friends at all, and truly don’t care if people dislike us. Which…

When most people encounter someone like Jenna Maroney (or, frankly, me) they offer platitudes about being yourself, either because they don’t want to be mean and tell someone to change, or because the very idea of a socially inept person “hacking” their personality to skinwalk with the normies makes them feel deeply uncomfortable.

I actually think for many people, the latter explanation makes more sense than not wanting to be mean. It’s the same reason that some women are so viscerally repulsed by men trading notes on how to develop “game.” Even if these men aren’t cheating on women, abusing them, or doing anything malevolent, it makes women very uncomfortable to imagine that the suave, dapper, charming fellow is secretly a weirdo loser who’s putting on a James Bond act. But for some people (who are often neurodivergent and struggle to pick up on social cues) putting on somewhat of an act is necessary. It doesn’t mean changing their entire personality, lying about their interests, or being less “geeky.” It means learning social skills, which at first can come off as awkward “masking” but eventually does become second nature, just the way reading begins with sounding out the words but eventually an adept reader would have a hard time seeing a sign without immediately knowing what it says.

I know this example might be a bit more polarizing because not everyone likes the show, but on Love on the Spectrum, a reality show about autistic people dating through matchmakers, a few dating coaches help autistic adults navigate the social cues of the dating world. These people aren’t afraid to tell their clients what to say and do, but they’re also not forcing them to change their entire personalities. If an autistic woman has a special interest in Disney princesses, the dating coach will not saying, “You’re an adult, you must throw out all your Disney paraphernalia and pretend to like home improvement shows,” but instead will offer advice like, “Make sure to ask your date about what he enjoys too, so you don’t just talk about Disney princesses for an hour straight.”

In a moment that felt very close to home, one of the women on the show (Emma) entertains a date with a very startling (and good) Donald Duck impression. He’s…not into it. And from how awkwardly their date had already been going, it was fairly obvious he wasn’t going to be into it, but Emma didn’t pick up on that. However, she later had a date with another guy, and she introduced the Donald Duck impression after a bit of more natural conversation. And he liked it! It’s possible the second guy was always going to like the impression (He enjoyed doing impressions himself too. Be still my beating heart!) But Emma adjusted her strategy so she didn’t risk immediately alienating someone who might enjoy the impression eventually but needed some small talk first.

Most people—even most autistic people—will not hire a dating coach or go on a reality show. But many people who struggle socially will eventually look at their interactions this way and wonder what can be changed. And a lot of other people—the people who have never had to think twice about how they appear to others—really don’t like that.

I’ve probably written about it far too much at this point, but when I was on the Search Engine podcast to investigate why I have a hard time getting party RSVPs (because, surprise, I’m annoying) the advice coming out of the podcast from my dear brother and husband, and from many of the commenters on the podcasts’s subreddit, was that I should “just be myself” and “find my people.” (Another social mistake: I was apparently not supposed to read the subreddit, in the sense that this is apparently a major breach of Internet etiquette, although to my credit I didn’t engage, mostly because I’m permanently banned from Reddit lmao)

But one thing struck me about the less-supportive comments on those Reddit threads about the episode. The people saying that I should have “been myself” and I was doing something wrong by wanting to change are the same people joking about how “insufferable” and “exhausting” I am. They already admit they’d hate me if we met, but then bristle at the idea of me taking that feedback into account to change my behavior. They do not want me skinwalking amongst them. They want me to resign myself to being irredeemably unlikable, to be a loser whose faults exist to make others feel less lame, and just remain widely disliked. Sounds legit.

One nuance that many people didn’t pick up from the episode was that I’ve already changed quite a bit of my behavior from my low point in my childhood through early twenties. As fun as it is to joke about, I do not break into musical impressions anymore and haven’t for years. People grow up, and part of growing up is no longer being extremely drunk at parties every weekend and genuinely just becoming a less chaotic and immature person. But I’d be lying if I said I wasn’t born with some degree of social challenges, and I’ve consciously worked on those things. (One example I’ve given before is recording facts about people in a spreadsheet so I can remember things for future conversations—something which has also been practiced by politicians.) And keep in mind that my brother and husband are both very supportive of me learning basic social cues and becoming a more considerate and less self-centered person, but this was sort of lost as the episode was condensed. Their argument (with which I mostly agree) is that I should not try to pretend to be a “normie,” and completely disavow my love of Trump impressions. As if!

But efforts to mask—or at least admitting that any masking is happening—bother people. They do not want to see how the sausage is made, because they are perfect sausage machines who deploy zesty Andouille perfection without thinking. And as I said before, the kneejerk reaction to tell people to “be themselves,” has a lot less to do with not wanting to dull the sparkle of quirky neurodivergent people, and a lot more to do with normies (yes, even geeky normies) being aghast at the idea of a true weirdo hiding amongst them and learning their language. But this is, for better or worse, how some people need to learn social cues because they don’t come naturally. And learning social cues doesn’t mean erasing your personality or pretending to be a different person. It’s like being a hippie but still deciding to shower sometimes when you realize the patchouli and four-day-old body odor is bothering everyone else in your hot yoga class. The shower doesn’t make you a different person, it just makes you smell better.

Moreover, if by “yourself” you mean your full, unfiltered self…you can still be that person, but you don’t need to be that person immediately, to everyone you meet, in every context. I am not the same person around new people as I am around my brother, and that’s completely normal. The only thing weird about this is the fact that I admit it in a way that comes off as calculating or phony, but…everyone does this to some extent. This is why “small talk” exists. Perhaps it comes naturally to a lot of people, but it doesn’t come naturally to me, and there’s a big flashing sign in my brain that says, “You are now performing small talk to increase the probability of this person liking you enough to ascend to the next level of closeness. Do not deploy Trump impression until further notice.”

This is hard to imagine for those who struggle socially because they are too shy. Their entire problem is having too much of a filter and never letting go and putting themselves out there. But if, like me, you constantly put yourself out there to the point that you read, as some of my critics have put it, as a “desperate child doing a cringe performance,” then dialing it back can absolutely happen without becoming a different person.

Jenna Maroney could still be Jenna Maroney and have lots of friends. She could come into the writer’s room and instead of complaining about the newest hot young actress who might be unseating her, ask them about their weekends and genuinely listen. She could be more self-effacing and self-aware, even poke fun at herself for being such a diva. She could still be funny and extroverted, doing that goofy thing where she harmonizes when everyone sings Happy Birthday, but also make room for others. And she could do all those things…and still be herself.
