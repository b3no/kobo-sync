The Edu-Optimists, Part II: CREDO, the Pro-Charter Research Shop That Won’t Take No Effect for an Answer

how “days of learning” explains the education reform shell game

Freddie deBoer
Aug 25
 
READ IN APP
 

Folks, I’ve retconned my post on Raj Chetty to include it in a new series, titled The Edu-Optimists. (I have the power to do that, here, in my little fiefdom.) In the next week or two I will publish part three, on Sal Khan and Khan Academy and the failure of Khanmigo.

In 2023 the Center for Research on Education Outcomes, known as CREDO, released its third national study of charter schools. The research covered 1.8 million students across twenty-nine states plus Washington D.C. and New York City. CREDO’s study purported to offer the most definitive evidence yet about the relative school quality of charter schools as compared to traditional public schools. Certainly our media, political establishment, and the nonprofit sector have acted as though what CREDO has to say is definitive; the 2023 study is, I believe, the most-cited piece of charter research in existence. Every pro-charter politician reaches for it, your Jonathan Chait neoliberal “reform” types treat its findings with near-mythic reverence, people who otherwise have no exposure to educational research wave at it as the final word on the subject. When the average person talks loosely as though there’s some sort of generic charter advantage, they’re usually referencing CREDO research, even if they don’t know they are.

This is all rather surprising, given that CREDO has not produced robust evidence in favor of charter schools, not in the 2023 study or its precursors. As we shall see, even if we assume that CREDO has solved the problem of the fundamentally non-random samples inherent to the task of comparing charter to public - which we should not, because they have not - the evidence supplied by CREDO in the favor of charter schools is so marginal and so noisy as to be meaningless from a policy perspective. And yet the message that the media took and shared from that report - the good news!, the vindication!, the headlines in Education Week and the New York Post!, the press releases from the National Alliance for Public Charter Schools! - was that the typical charter student was the beneficiary of a meaningful learning boost compared to their traditional public school counterpart. Why did this message spread so widely and resonate so loudly, if I’m right that even CREDO’s own numbers show very modest differences? Because of a remarkably savvy bit of marketing in how CREDO’s numbers were presented. The report did not show its findings in terms of effect size differences between charter and public, which would be the field’s standard way of sharing results. Rather, the outcome that was shared again and again was that a typical charter school student in the dataset had gained sixteen additional “days of learning” per year in reading and six in math. For a few months there you couldn’t read educational media without running into those numbers: sixteen additional days of learning in reading per year, six in math. It became a bit of holy writ in the endless quest to make bad students good with ACCOUNTABILITY and market reforms.

Now, me, I immediately gravitate to that last bit: what on Earth is a “day of learning,” and since when is that a statistical measure?

“Days of Learning”: A Jury-Rigged Metric Invented for the Purpose of Inflating the Effect

If you share my confusion over that metric, don’t blame yourself. “Days of learning” is not merely an unusual measure of student performance; it’s a boutique metric that CREDO invented themselves for their charter studies. It’s like YouTube changing how views are counted so the numbers look bigger.

This should raise a number of red flags, most importantly when it comes to comparison with other research. We have standardized metrics in social science research for a reason. One core reason among them is mutual interpretability, the ability to compare outcomes involving different interventions that are measured with different instruments and in different units. That’s why effect size exists, and various expressions of effect size have served as the dominant measure of interest in all manner of human research for decades precisely because effect size enables ease of comparison. If you want to compare the influence of the same intervention when one study uses grades as the DV and another used test scores, effect size enables you to do that. If you want to compare two different types of intervention, effect size enables you to do that too. There are some caveats, as there always are, but the basic, elegant logic of effect size makes the measure remarkably flexible and portable: effect size tells us how a given intervention moves average outcomes along the distribution of outcomes. If you want to know more there are plenty of simple explainers of effect size out there; here’s mine. There are some fields where effect size is used less often (economics, for example) but the interpretability and flexibility of effect size metrics like Cohen’s d are powerful advantages. Not surprisingly, then, it’s the dominant reported measure in education research.

So if effect size is both useful and common, and is in fact the dominant measure of interest in the relevant field, why would CREDO come up with its own boutique measure? Why invite the potential for confusion with such consequential research? Occam’s razor suggests a simple answer: because CREDO is a pro-charter organization that’s intent on convincing stakeholders that charters have meaningfully superior outcomes when compared to traditional public schools… and their own research demonstrates the opposite. Their own numbers, in fact, suggest that the returns on charter schools are meager even if we ignore the selection problems that continue to plague charter advocacy. Here’s what sixteen additional “days of learning” in math and six days in reading looks like in terms of effect sizes, what we find when we pull back the curtain:

Those numbers, you may notice, are quite small! That’s not a .28 in reading, it’s a .028 in reading. That’s not a .11 in math, it’s a .011 in math. Effect size is always contextual, but these are very small effects no matter the context. If you have any familiarity with effect size, you might look at these numbers and be shocked by the various headlines that insisted that, finally, the pro-charter crowd had its silver bullet. The actual, real-world impact of the intervention being measured here, attending a charter school, is just about nil. CMO affiliated, for the record, stands for charter management organization, which are nonprofit organizations that run networks of charter schools. The numbers are slightly better but still close to zero in practical terms, and of course restricting to CMO schools reduces the sample size and degrades the generalizability of the study’s findings. The virtual charter finding aligns with similar research and contributes to the belief that virtual K-12 schools should not exist.

But perhaps these numbers are not intuitive to you. Fair enough. I think this is exactly what data visualization was invented for. Remember, a simple way to think of effect size is to think of the distribution of all outcomes (in test scores or in grades or in whatever else) and then visualize how the studied intervention moves the test group along that distribution. Here’s a visualization showing an effect size of .5σ - that is, one half of one standard deviation, which will move an average result a little less than 20% across a normal distribution:

Fairly intuitive, right? If orange is test and blue is control, you can see how this intervention (whatever you want to imagine it is) has moved the averages and the percentiles. The size of the effect is clear, as is the degree of overlap. Well, here’s CREDO’s own numbers in standard deviations, showing the difference between the traditional public school averages in blue and the charter averages in orange:

So, yeah, there you go. That’s the difference that has been lauded as the smoking gun in the charter school debate. That’s the revolutionary potential of charter schools, which we’ve staked billions of dollars, the rights and working conditions of millions of public school teachers, and the future of our educational system on. And that’s if you assume that CREDO’s analysis is correct and they’ve effectively solved the selection issue, which I will shortly argue is not the case. With all the inevitable statistical noise, with the minor corruptions inherent to any model, this is what we get - an effect size so small close to half of the studied charter population did worse than the average public school student and which indicates a negligible effect on actual, meaningful learning outcomes.

This, of course, is why the days of learning metric was invented: to obscure this exact reality. When the usual metric doesn’t say what you want it to, you just come up with a new one. And that, friends, is the pro-charter movement for you.

The Metric Itself is Misconceived and Inconsistent

Even if we set aside the flimflam inherent to coming up with your own unit in order to exaggerate the size of the effect you’re studying, days of learning is poorly conceived in its own right, as multiple critics have pointed out. It works like this. CREDO takes an effect size, measured in standard deviations, and converts it into the equivalent fraction of a 180-day school year. They estimate how much standardized test growth a typical student makes over a typical academic year, treating that amount as 180 “days of learning.” (There’s no federal standard, but 180 days is the length of the school year in 31 states and the District of Columbia.) They then express the charter school effect as the corresponding number of days. So, for example, that reported reading effect of 0.028 standard deviations becomes roughly 16 “days of learning.” Is that a little weird and convoluted? Indeed! And it brings a number of issues with it.

The whole approach requires vertically scaled tests, and the tests aren’t. To convert a standard deviation into a fraction of a year of learning, you need an assessment whose scale is continuous across grades, so that a point in fourth grade means the same thing as a point in fifth. Right? Right. Days of learning as a metric that is applied to schools across the K-12 range implies and requires a single continuous interval scale; tests need to be scaled consistently if you want to make those kinds of longitudinal comparisons. Right? Right. Well, guess what? Many of the state standardized tests that CREDO pools aren’t vertically scaled at all. Without that consistency across the age range, the translation inherent to “days of learning” lacks reliability and thus validity. Mark Weber has made this argument years ago and CREDO just kept on using the metric.

A “year of learning” is not a constant! Honestly, this is the one that should end the conversation right here. Many researchers have established that annual achievement growth, measured in effect size, is large in early grades and shrinks steadily thereafter - the reading gain from first to second grade approaches a full standard deviation, while by high school annual gains are a small fraction of that. (See Bloom, Hill, Black and Lipsey 2008, for example.) This, among other reasons, is why so much effort is invested into early childhood education and why Head Start is often treated as a holy cow. Well, if one standard deviation is worth about one year of learning to a second grader and something like four or five years to a high school junior, again the metric’s basic assumptions just break down. CREDO pools students across the whole K–12 span and applies a single conversion and there just is no defensible way to do that. I think you can argue that “days of learning” means something different for every child it’s applied to, but even if you think that goes too far, it certainly means something different for students of different grades and ages. But then why on earth is it being used to describe outcomes in an dataset that includes grades K through 12?

CREDO changes the metric based on new NAEP results, which makes their own metric inconsistent across their reports. In the 2015 CREDO online charter school report, the effect size to days of learning conversion was 0.01 SD = 7.2 days of learning. But by the 2019 South Carolina study, CREDO was using 0.01 SD = 5.9 days. In the 2023 national report it became 0.01 SD = 5.78 days. This is because, as stated, CREDO uses NAEP scores to calculate what a day of learning means quantitatively. But NAEP scores change, and thus so does their formula. So take exactly the same 0.028 SD effect in reading from the 2023 study. Under the older CREDO conversion, 0.028 SD is approximately 20.2 days. Under the 2023 conversion, 0.028 SD is approximately 16.2 days. This is more than enough difference to make meaningful comparisons between studies a real headache. Which is why we shouldn’t use the metric! Effect size eliminates all of this inconsistency; it compares the effect to movement along the distribution of scores and thus doesn’t require any artificial conversion.

Expert reviewers identified all of these problems years ago. Andrew Maul and Abby McClelland, in a National Education Policy Center review, called the conversion insufficiently justified and concluded there was no substantial performance difference between the two sectors. NEPC’s list of objections to CREDO’s method runs to eight items, of which the days-of-learning conversion is only one; the others include insufficient matching variables to support causal claims, systematic exclusion of some lower-performing charter students, no correction for multiple significance testing, and yes, trivial effect sizes. Robert Slavin, who was nobody’s idea of an anti-reform zealot, asked a simple but telling question: why days? Why not additional hours of learning, or minutes? The answer is obvious: because the unit was chosen to present the results in a particular light, to make the result look meaningful to people who don’t check the numbers or ask where they came from. Sixteen days sounds like three weeks of school. 0.02 standard deviations sounds just like what it means: that there isn’t any meaningful difference.

As I said above, the problems goes beyond the metric; the underlying research itself has serious issues. As is so often the case in education research, it’s fundamentally a question of nonrandom selection and how to account for it.

The “Virtual Twin” Construct Doesn’t Work

Before you can argue about how an effect is reported, you have to ask whether there’s an effect to report. Since all education questions are ultimately selection questions, especially when it comes to charter schools, researchers have to have some sort of mechanism to address the non-random distribution of students into different schools. CREDO’s answer to that problem is a technique they call the virtual twin: for every student in a charter school in their sample, CREDO assembles a synthetic counterpart out of the records of students in the traditional public schools that feed that particular charter. (Let me concede upfront that this is a clever idea.) The twin is built by matching on a variety of student variables: grade level, gender, race and ethnicity, free-and-reduced-price lunch eligibility (ie socioeconomic class), English learner status, special education status, and importantly, the prior year’s test score. After matching for those variables, whatever difference in growth that shows up afterward is attributed to the school.

That last bit might remind you of my critiques of Raj Chetty’s research, and it’s problematic, to say the least. This subtractive approach to ed research, where you attempt to control for a variety of factors and then assume whatever’s left is controlled by schools and teachers, is permanently vulnerable to omitted-variable bias - the things you measure and control for aren’t necessarily the only things that distinguish what’s being compared. Matching can make the groups look more alike on paper, but it can’t make an observational study behave like a randomized experiment. That’s why we do random designs whenever we can, and the fact that we often can’t is a big part of why ed research is so hard to do. Meanwhile, the genuinely random studies we have concerning school quality consistently find no effect.

Part of the problem is that variation in academic ability within any demographic group of interest dwarfs the variation between cells. “Low-income Black fourth-grade girls, not ESL or in special ed” doesn’t describe a population of similar learners; it describes a population that spans a very broad range of academic outcomes. There will certainly be racial and socioeconomic differences in averages between one demographic group and another, and the persistence of these differences has been the obsession of our national education conversation for longer than I’ve been alive. But the gap between the top and bottom performers in that group are far larger than the averages between any two groups. This has all manner of consequences that are rarely commented on, but here the salient point is simple: a demographic code can’t amount to a measurement of a child. That’s why CREDO adds its test score element, which is an admirable idea that doesn’t really work, either.

The prior-score match - comparing demographically similar students who are also matched on test scores, within a certain tolerance - is supposed to address complaints that demographic information tells us nothing about individual talent variation, But it doesn’t, for reasons any psychometrician could tell you. A single year’s score is one noisy measure, subject to test-day illness, to guessing, to the specific items sampled, to any of the challenges inherent to testing. In particular, one test score can’t tell you if a student is ascending or descending or stuck in place, academically. For a summative test like the SAT, which is developed and administered to tell colleges where a given student is near the end of their high school career, this isn’t a big deal. Here, it’s a real problem. Two children who scored identically last spring can be on entirely different trajectories: one who tested at his ceiling, one who tested well below what he can do, one on the way up, one on the way down. If a student who is in the process of moving down the performance distribution matches with one who is moving up, CREDO’s research will look worse for the school that gets the former student as they assess future learning. In fairness, this could just as easily disadvantage charter schools as traditional public schools, but either way it’s a problem for analysis.

Again, this is why we randomize - if the mismatch between a student and their “twin” was random, it would wash out across 1.8 million records. But that mismatch isn’t random because the thing that determines which child ends up in a charter school is precisely the thing the covariate set does not contain. The entire reason we’re all going through all of this rigamarole - and I have some sympathy for CREDO here - is because we know children are nonrandomly sorted into charter schools, even when slots are given out by lottery. As has been repeatedly demonstrated, the act of a parent choosing a charter ensures that the students who attend are not the same population as a nearby public school, even before we get into all the chicanery. A parent has to know the charter school exists, has to want out of the local public school badly enough to act, has to find the application online, has to meet the deadline, often has to attend a mandatory information session. Then the parent has to solve transportation issues, because charters often don’t have buses, to sign the parent contract, convince the kid to tolerate the uniform policy, absorb the phone calls about demerits, and (crucially) not withdraw the child over the following years. Those are all filters, and every one of them ultimately filters students in or out based on family income, household stability, parenting, a given kid’s own talent and engagement… the exact variables that tend to be correlated with superior academic outcomes. (This might all be related to a confounding third variable, talent, but that’s a different essay.)

Making this problem more acute is an artifact of CREDO’s noble attempt to adjust for student ability: a student can only be matched if they have a prior-year score in the state system to match on. Kids who change districts during a school year, those who enter from out of state, those who cycle in and out of homelessness, those who are chronically absent - in other words, the most unstable and disadvantaged students in the system - are simply not in the dataset. NEPC’s reviewers (linked above) flagged systematic exclusion of lower-performing charter students as one of their major objections; CREDO has never addressed this point in a satisfactory way. This issue takes the research’s biggest strength and turns it into a weakness. CREDO’s 1.8 million student sample size is impressive, but increasing sample size only makes a biased estimate more precise, not more correct - that is to say, a larger n of nonrandom samples narrows the confidence interval around an incorrect number. The reason methodologists value admissions lotteries is that random assignment among applicants genuinely does solve this and other problems. Everyone in the lottery pool has already passed through the various filters; the lottery’s randomization is the only thing separating winners from losers. That’s why it’s insane that for most lottery-based charter schools the only oversight that exists is essentially the honor system! And, to take it a step further, lotteries only exist where schools are oversubscribed, and oversubscribed schools are the most desirable, most heavily resourced, most reputationally successful schools in the sector…. Selection issues multiply.

Well. It won’t surprise you to learn that I don’t trust any of it.

This is all made more troubling by the potential influence of CREDO’s funders. The Hoover Institution, the far-right thinktank that runs CREDO, does not publish a list of the project’s donors. Funders, apparently, only appear in its reports when they specifically ask to be acknowledged. That’s not standard practice for a research center; indeed, knowing who funds research is a pretty fundamental and basic part of establishing credibility. We know who paid for the 2023 national study because the report names them: the Walton Family Foundation and The City Fund. Those are the only two named funders. Are there are others? Don’t know; CREDO won’t say. But those names should give you pause in and of themselves.

The Walton Family Foundation - yes, the Walmart Waltons - is the largest private funder of charter school startups in the United States. They splash around money in the K-12 space for the explicit purpose of replacing traditional public schools with charter schools. Its charter grantmaking isn’t some side quest or just one part of a diverse set of interests, it’s the central project of the foundation’s education work. WFF spends hundreds of millions of dollars to advance the charter agenda, primarily by sending grant money to charter management organizations, to the Charter School Growth Fund, and to charter advocacy groups. A Walton family member sits on the boards of both the Charter School Growth Fund and the KIPP Foundation. And the funder with that ominously vague name, The City Fund? That organization was cofounded and financed by the Netflix billionaire Reed Hastings, who is as rabid a critic of public schools as you’ll ever find. The City Fund (which has also been the recipient of a lot of Gates Foundation cash, as I understand it) exists to convert public school districts into “portfolio” systems - that is, systems in which a large share of schools are charters. It sets its own institutional targets in terms of the percentage of a city’s schools that should be under such management. Don’t let the generic name fool you. The City Fund exists to close down traditional public schools.

So: the two organizations that paid for the definitive national assessment of whether charter schools work are, one, the largest private financier of charter schools in the country and, two, a vehicle created by billionaires for the express purpose of converting districts into charter portfolios. The problem here is obvious, inherent, and existential. The funders of the research stated institutional purpose is to fight to spread the schools that the research purports to evaluate! Put it this way: if the American Federation of Teachers paid for a major ongoing study of comparative public and charter quality, would the education reform crowd take those results seriously? Would The Atlantic trumpet their findings? Would the New York Times represent the conclusions as objective and definitive? No. But that’s exactly what we’re meant to accept with CREDO’s findings.

And CREDO seems broadly indifferent to the kind of basic checks and refereeing that exist in most published research. CREDO’s reports don’t go through a peer review process; CREDO’s reports don’t include literature reviews or explicit limitations sections, which any academic journal would require; CREDO doesn’t release its data for other researchers to review. We get the findings CREDO chooses to report, analyzed in the way CREDO chooses to analyze, and if you want to check their work, well, too bad. We’re all held hostage to those institutional practices. To put it all together, the most influential studies in the charter research literature, which have served as justification for the largest structural change to American public education since desegregation, amount to unreviewed, unrefereed, impossible-to-replicable reports produced by a shop funded exclusively (as far as anyone can tell, given the lack of transparency) by funders who have worked tirelessly to champion charter schools. If a pharma company ran its own unreviewed trials, declined to name its funders, refused to release patient-level data, and didn’t have any external review, we wouldn’t act like those results were the objective scientific truth. We’d call that marketing. And that’s what CREDO ultimately is, marketing for charter schools.

Look, credit where due: CREDO’s first national study, in 2009, found that charter schools underperformed traditional public schools, in defiance of what the whole world of “No excuses!” accountability-worshipping media establishment was predicting. That study was an embarrassment to the sector that funded it and the ideologues who had championed the research before they knew what the outcomes was, and CREDO published it anyway. Its study of virtual charter schools was brutal and disqualifying, finding losses so large that a lot of people who should have known better dropped their support for virtual charter schools. The 2023 report itself admits that special ed students do worse in charters than they do in traditional public schools, that charter schools in ten prominent cities underperform in reading and eleven in math compared to matched public averages, and that so-called stand alone charters show no advantage in math at all. I appreciate their candor, though it makes “days of learning” more baffling.

No, as frustrating as they are, as much as they’ve branded themselves an independent arbiter when they clearly have an agenda, CREDO isn’t corrupt, just idealistic and blinkered in a deeply distorting way. If nothing else, I believe in director Eric Hanushek’s integrity, even though I think he’s fallen into the politician’s syllogism when it comes to charter schools. (My strong suspicion is that Hanushek knows that educational outcomes are dominantly the product of individual student talent factors that schools can’t affect but feels that he can’t say so directly.) I’m not really arguing that they’re cooking the books in a deliberate way, but something more ordinary and insidious: an institutional apparatus in which a funder, a host institution, an unreviewed publication process, an unreleased dataset, and a jury-rigged unit of measurement all lean in the same direction. Every individual influence might be small, but you add it up together and you have an organization that’s just going to bless charters. Nobody has to tell a lie, under those conditions, to distort public understanding. You just have to make a hundred small defensible choices, each of which happens to point the same way, and then hand the result to the media, who’ll only see Stanford’s name and the impressive-sounding “sixteen additional days of learning.”

Somewhere around the end of the last decade, the people running the pro-charter movement found themselves with miniscule effect sizes, after fifteen years of trying, against all of their own assumptions. They didn’t change their point of view. That’s the reality: after decades of the most aggressive, bullying policy advocacy imaginable, after years of assaulting dedicated public servants and the schools they worked in, after endlessly shouting “No excuses!,” the charter people were entirely unable to demonstrate the superiority of their preferred systems. The individual schools they lauded as exemplary were riven with selection problems due to both admissions (selection from students going in) and attrition (selection from students going out), and anyway their techniques never scaled. Large-scale research, including the first, famously negative CREDO study, consistently failed to find anything like results to match the rhetoric. The only honest take is that the educational research and policy world doggedly pursued pro-charter findings for decades and have essentially nothing to show for it.

Unsurprisingly, charter advocates have never admitted to that failure. Too much had been staked on the idea that charters were clearly and consistently superior - an awful lot of money donated by billionaires, the reputations of foundations and thinktanks, careers in nonprofits and politics and academia, entire state policy regimes, a vast and angry and loud war on public schools pushed by conservatives and the useful idiot liberals who helped them. So CREDO, unwilling to lie and unable to prove a charter advantage that just isn’t there, came up with its own bespoke measurement of educational efficacy, a way to juke the stats. There was always something sweaty and desperate in doing so, but it worked like a charm - the media and the politicians and the thinktanks ate it up, and now your average informed person thinks there’s a generic charter advantage when there simply isn’t one. And so, in the name of educational efficacy, we all now live in an intellectual world where in the discourse about charter schools, nothing became sixteen days, and then sixteen days became a headline, and then the headlines became a legislative finding, and the legislative findings became laws, and on down the chain until, somewhere, a public school closes and teachers lose their jobs, all thanks to the machinations of people with a lot of money and clout who will not ever stand up and admit that they were wrong.
