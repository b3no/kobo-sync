The Politics of Fantasy and Entitlement Are Driving Everyone Insane

imperfect reality can’t win in a culture of fantasy

Freddie deBoer
Sep 29
 
READ IN APP
 

Catherine Liu has a new piece out looking at the unconditional victory that “the personal is political” has scored in progressive spaces. As Catherine says, it’s not just that left-leaning people believe that there are political valences in personal experience, but that the personal has “entirely consumed” the political; in our media, there appears to be zero interest for any political story that is not read through the emotional life of a specific individual. I think this is another example of a place where “Woke 1.0” won, simply and completely, regardless of how things have shifted in other ways. Most younger people can’t even conceptualize of a politics that is not fundamentally personal in nature. And if there’s any essential corollary to this observation, it’s that in the 21st century, the personal is inextricable from the aspirational - that is to say, from fantasy. More than a decade ago, essayists began warning that Instagram presented a manufactured, unreal vision of what life was like, as though the world was all thirst traps, artfully arranged brunches, and sunsets. Those essays seem quaint to me now. The Instagram fantasy vision won, in a rout, and increasingly left-of-center politics is hobbled by its inability to resist it.

If you’re me and believe that politics is best understood as material and impersonal, this is all not great. But you don’t have to share my hangups to see the core problems here: online life has broken people’s basic apprehension of what life is like and what we can reasonably expect, and social justice politics and therapy culture have declared it wicked to ask anyone to accept life’s limitations. For a good example, consider the absurd story of the “bad dad” at the San Francisco Giants game and the wife who’s been excoriated for defending him.

A family had the misfortune of going mega-viral last week, for the sin of attending a baseball game. The game’s television broadcast showed images of a mom returning to her seat, carrying her baby in a carrier, while balancing a pile of ballpark food. On her arrival, she gave her husband an opportunity to eat. The announcers calling the game jokingly called her the mom of the year and moved on; the internet, I’m afraid, acted the way the internet acts. The video clip escaped its specific context and became a bizarre rallying point for a certain kind of quasi-political gender slop. (The story went sufficiently viral that I only had to type in “San Francisco Giants” and the story was the first thing autofilled on Google, YouTube, and Twitter.) This all became exactly as unhinged as anything else online does. The mother, social media insisted, was clearly an unappreciated and unhappy woman who should definitely divorce her husband. Sleuths began rooting out their real identity, and the husband’s image was replicated in a thousand memes that insisted that a 30 seconds of video taken in a public place must represent his entire character. The mother, however, felt differently, sharing her own perspective on Substack. (Local San Francisco media has since verified that the Substack post is authentic.) Contrary to what the mob was suggesting, the woman, identifying herself only as Erika, insisted that he’s a great husband and father, that he had been dealing with a lot, and that managing both the baby and the food for a few minutes wasn’t a big deal. She further suggested that maybe people need to learn to give others the benefit of the doubt.

And, of course, everyone accepted what she had to say, recognized that it wasn’t their business, and moved on. lol no, they just got madder and more unhinged. Her Substack comments filled with invective; social media insisted that she was a dupe, a traitor to other women, deluded, suffering from Stockholm syndrome, and most importantly destined for misery. That last bit was hit very, very hard: that it was impossible that this woman could be both sincere and correct about her own marriage and her own partner. Her future misery was thus not just inevitable but deserved. A very brief, out-of-context bit of video taken in the inherently weird space of a major league baseball stadium was enough to make all of these people experts in her life, so deeply understanding of her family and its various foibles that they could immediately and unrepentantly dismiss her own perspective. This is all Very Online, in the worst possible sense. It was also, I’m afraid, largely conducted in the argot of modern fake progressivism, the weird way people drape their various worst instincts and most vicious selves in the idiom of progress, equality, and justice. The people demanding that this woman be made to pay for the sin of defending her husband and family weren’t merely engaged in an internet pile on, no. They were engaged in feminism, at least in their own delusion.

I’ll give them this! Their behavior was of a piece with far too much of the contemporary progressive world. The whole ugly show was absurdly presumptuous, galactically self-righteous, toxically embittered, utterly bereft of humility or restraint, filled with bullshit faux-political insight, and most of all, miserable. Because that, I would say, is what’s actually behind the ever-growing corpus of Dump-Him-Girl feminism and the broader world of forcing bad online behavior into a vaguely left-wing silhouette, misery and the desire to share it. The woman in question is telling you that her husband is a great partner and wonderful father! That can only result in anger if you’re not really concerned with the family in question, but are instead making an increasingly-ubiquitous demand in 21st century life: I’m miserable, so you have to be, too. I’m not living the life of my fantasies, so I must consign you to unhappiness in your real one.

For the record, saying that a stranger whose life you know nothing about should divorce their spouse is weird and stupid and low character. Saying that a happy family unit should be ripped apart by divorce, and a very young child denied the very many and real benefits that come from a stable two-parent household, based on whim? Yeah, you’re a bad person if you engage that way, as thousands did. And to be very clear, that marriage and family no doubt have the same problems any of us have. I’m not even someone who’s opposed in principle to the overstuffed “Here’s Why The Family is Bad” genre of lefty essays. Some of them make some fair points. The trouble, of course, is that none of us lives in The Family but rather in our families, actual families, ordinary imperfect families. And part of the reason so many people are so miserable is because the internet has enabled them to believe that they can not only achieve the unachievable but that they are entitled to achieve it. “Hey, that guy looks like a shitty husband, I don’t have an ideal partner, and the internet tells me that any instinct to stay with a less-than-ideal partner is the hand of injustice, internalized whatever.”

Whatever is true of that particular family, there are lessons here about being judgemental, about minding your own business, and about kindness. There’s also the simple reality that in a culture that increasingly refuses to accept the profound limitations of real human beings, no one is going to look good enough.

I recognize that, to some, this looks like the same justification for why people are poor and why women get sexually harassed at work and why cops get away with murder. Hey, life’s not fair! Sorry, you have to accept mass incarceration. But, well, those things are not the same. “You have to live with preventable structural injustice” and “You can’t expect to have everything you ever wanted and you’ll be unhappy if you do” are not the same. And we have to be adults and do the work of separating one from the other, no matter how tempting it is to talk ourselves into believing that anything we don’t like is necessarily a political sin.

I am sympathetic to what Nwanevu says, here. His point about AI is sound and he’s perfectly correct that if Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez had demanded that staffers feed someone’s chameleon, that would be represented as unhinged misconduct. But Yglesias is poking at something that the American left-of-center finds itself entirely unequipped to deal with: lots of fundamentally unpleasant elements of life are genuinely inevitable, and a political movement that cannot reflect on that fact honestly is setting itself up for failure. We have, in contemporary progressive politics, a glaring problem when it comes to separating out systemic injustice from the ordinary unhappiness of adult life. Black people being unable to get mortgages on terms appropriate for their financial records, women being passed over for shots at the highest level in companies even as they come to dominate the lower rungs of the ladder, disabled people who use wheelchairs being unable to access public transit thanks to a lack of accommodations, gay people facing assumptions that they’re more likely to be child sexual predators when that isn’t true - these are structural, material injustices of the type that the left exists to fight against. The persistence of poverty is a societal choice and we could choose to do something different, there’s a lot of aspects of gender relations that are unjust and could be changed, and so on. But it’s also the case that we’re inherently fallible creatures in an indifferent universe and a permanently imperfect world, and I simply do not see any incentive for left-wing commentators to tell people that some problems have no solution other than resilience and a willingness to reconsider our expectations. The money is all on the other side - you’ll never grow broke telling people they can and should have everything they want.

So look at the ne plus ultra of confusing unhappiness with injustice, The Cut.

The New York magazine vertical The Cut was once a publication that was consistently willing to tell its readership things they didn’t want to hear. But over time, since the pandemic in particular, it’s become a parody of the progressive insistence that everyone should have everything they want, all the time. The average Cut essay argues that any failure of the world and of life to fulfill the dreams of its readers is necessarily a mark of injustice, rather than a simple expression of the basic nature of human existence. It’s a storehouse of essays of a particular type, the kind that insist that you should be having exactly the kind of sex you want with all the people you want to no matter if you’re in a monogamous relationship or not, that the only reason you may not get that dream job is misogyny even if it goes to another woman, that you should dump your husband if he ever fails to live up to your loftiest desires, that you should diagnose yourself with any disorder if that makes you feel better, that your boss is definitely a sociopath and your ex-boyfriend certainly a narcissist, that every momentary setback is a matter for Congress, that the natural state of things is for all of us to float along in a state of validation and emotional security, at all times, and if you don’t you’re a victim. If you think I’m wrong about this, I challenge you to go through the recent archives of that publication and find pieces where the reader is told, in one way or another, that their expectations are too high, their entitlement too capacious, that what they want is unrealistic and childish. Go look. Go look!

There are two glaring considerations here, gender and commerce. The Cut is part of “women’s media,” whatever that means. And the problem with saying the things that I’m saying is that it’s is all susceptible to allegations of policing women’s desires or whatever, that this is all simply gender war. Personally, I have said here and elsewhere and will continue to say that the tendency to endlessly raise expectations and sanction entitlement is a remarkably wide-ranging and universal phenomenon; though it might not seem like it, ultimately the very male cultural obsession with meme stocks, crypto, and sports gambling is an expression of the same politics of entitlement. (These dudes believe that the fact that they’re not already rich is some sort of cosmic error and so pursue a get-rich-quick approach to life, convinced that being told to slowly save money in an ordinary job is a horrible insult.) Yes, the “if your partner is failing to meet your most extravagant fantasies, dump them” essay genre is dominantly written for and by women, but that’s only because the kinds of guys who base their perception of women on OnlyFans don’t read or write essays. There’s more than enough fantasizing and entitlement in male culture these days, trust me. The tricky part is that because feminism has for so long identified very real structural sexism and unjust impediments to women’s flourishing, there’s a built-in resistance to being told that some of our demands are virtuous politics and some are self-destructive entitlement. But we are self-deceiving creatures, all of us, men and women.

What’s depressing about all this is that it’s functionally the same conversation we were having fourteen years ago, when The Atlantic published its notorious “Can Women Really Have It All?” essay, which went viral to a degree that simply doesn’t happen these days. In it, a hard-charging career woman expressed her doubts that ambitious women really can balance career and home aspirations. In the endless commentary that followed, the most sensible people made some version of the same point: that while there are certainly still structural gender barriers to women having both career success and family happiness, the ultimate reason women can’t “have it all” is that no one can have it all, that that’s an absurd, unrealizable standard, the kind of aspiration that leads to misery. At the time, I thought maybe that sentiment might win. The opposite has happened; the sense that any limitation on our desires is a matter of injustice has fully metastasized. And the people who are most caught up in those feelings will inevitably pay the price. Entitlement is a funny thing; its most obvious destructive effects afflict those around us, but oftentimes the most pernicious outcomes are for ourselves. A failure to properly understand what we can and can’t reasonably expect is a truly debilitating personal weakness. It makes every realistic success into a failure to live up to fantasy. As Liu says, “A general anti-humanism disguises a contempt for ordinary language and ordinary people” - and the ordinary is what we all have to learn to accept.

The reason why this problem is so hard to shake is not due to any inherent gender dynamics, but because of the other reality I mentioned, commerce. There’s a book that’s become very popular, it’s got a title like Adult Children of Shitty Parents or something. Bestseller. And its message is that if you have a bad relationship with your parents, good news - it’s their fault and not yours. This exonerative service has become the financial engine of almost all of the meager number of nonfiction books that are still financially successful. You’ll note that there are no examples of the obverse. There is no NYT bestseller titled Actually, Your Relationship with Your Parent is Bad Because You Are a Toxic and Selfish Person. And that is a function of an incredibly unhealthy dynamic in contemporary culture. You can’t get rich telling people that, in fact, they’re single and unhappy because they keep cheating on their partners and they keep cheating because they’re bad people. But speaking as someone who has spent a lot of time in the “recovery community,” I assure you that that is the kind of talk most people really need.

Today’s deranged essay in the Cut concerns Heated Rivalry, the book-and-television franchise that has ignited the passions of an army of dissatisfied women, some of whom have left their real-life partners thanks to the vision of raw animal passion that fictional world has shown them. Monia Ali of Fandom Exile has been documenting the dark side of Heated Rivalry psychosis for some time. What I find quite remarkable about the Cut’s piece is that it simultaneously tips its hat to the utter insanity that the HR fanfic community has produced and yet refuses to simply arrive at the obvious conclusion: that many people, in 2026, have lost their ability to sort fantasy from reality in a profoundly unhealthy way. That’s what Heated Rivalry is, after all, a fantasy, make believe, just as much as the perfect partner and the dream job and the ideal life are all expressions of fantasy. The author of the essay, Jen Ortiz, walks right up to the ledge of admitting to all of this - to accepting that the Heated Rivalry fantasy is indeed a fantasy and that the people who have become addicted to that fantasy are hurting themselves and others. But before she can commit, she goes skittering off in the other direction, unwilling or unable to actually come to the most obvious and necessary conclusion. It’s as if the essay, the essayist, and the publication are unable to embrace the simple reality that as adults we must learn to leave the imaginary behind and embrace the real.

I have no idea how any of this gets fixed. No piece like the one I’m writing now could ever be published in a mainstream publication; the disingenuous allegations of misogyny (or any other convenient bigotry) write themselves. But I do know that we can’t go on like this. The collective misery, the rage at the real, the absolute and utter refusal to accept that this is just what life is and that disappointment is unavoidable… it can’t continue forever. The trouble is that you can’t convince a junkie to stop being a junkie. Not with reason and certainly not while protecting their feelings. As Ortiz writes, “Addicts do tend to rationalize their addictions.” Yes, they do - and when it comes to insisting that living anything short of your ideal fantasy life is a form of social injustice, our whole culture is an enabler.
