What is a woman and who gets to decide?

Two feminists debate the importance of sex

Elizabeth Grace Matthew and Maibritt Henkel
Nov 13
∙
Paid
 
READ IN APP
 

Welcome to Mad Libs. This is an irregular debate column where our columnists, contributors, and staff writers (or even you, dear reader) can duke it out over the big ideas we’re discussing in the metaphorical pages of this magazine.

Today’s Mad Libs grew out of the firestorm over the publication of The Argument’s Maibritt Henkel’s “My weekend with the ‘sex-realist’ feminists” late last month.

No issue divides liberal feminists from their sex-realist counterparts quite like — you guessed it — gender. Sex-realists emphatically reject the idea that womanhood has anything to do with identification, arguing instead that it has everything to do with biology.

For sex-realists, feminism begins with the bodily differences between men and women. For liberal feminists, the point of feminism is to make those differences matter less.

So we invited Elizabeth Grace Matthew, a sex-realist feminist who writes the newsletter Restoring American Adulthood, to debate Maibritt directly on how feminists should think about sex and gender.

Elizabeth Grace Matthew:

As a sex-realist feminist, I believe both that women are fundamentally, intrinsically equal to men and that women are definitionally, biologically different from men.

Right-coded anti-feminists reject the first principle in deference to the neopagan misogyny of gender essentialism; they believe that men are superior to women and that the sexes must have distinct roles. Meanwhile, left-coded mainstream feminists reject the second principle in deference to the pseudoscience of gender ideology; they cannot define the word “woman.”

This dominant form of feminism today rejects the reality of sex difference. In so doing, it makes three mistakes:

  1. It abandons protections and accommodations for women on the mistaken belief that sex exists on a spectrum.

  2. It conflates recognizing biological differences between men and women with believing those differences should determine how men and women behave.

  3. It aspires to make men and women more alike rather than allowing each woman to flourish on her own terms.

Sex is not a spectrum

Approximately 0.018% of newborns are intersex (i.e., cannot be classified as male or female or observed classification is inconsistent with chromosomal sex). Until quite recently, the American Psychiatric Association estimated that up to 0.014% percent of adult males and 0.0032% percent of females manifested gender dysphoria (i.e., experienced distress due to the incongruence between their “gender identity” and biological sex). Among children, presentation of gender dysphoria typically occurred between ages two and four. , according to the journalist Abigail Shrier.

The recent spike in gender dysphoria (especially among adolescent females) is a costly, dangerous social contagion. Broad adoption of terminology that attempts to treat these aberrations as norms (e.g., “sex assigned at birth”) is insidious propaganda.

Acknowledging that this emperor is wearing no clothes (and that if his imaginary outfit were a dress, he would still be a man) does not and must not mean failing to treat the small number of people with these conditions humanely. Perhaps no one has been clearer about the totality of that moral reality than the late Pope Francis, whose compassion for people struggling with gender dysphoria did not equate to acceptance of gender ideology.

Sex realism does and must require recognizing, and ordering society to accommodate, the binary reality of sex: In comparison to men, women are on average more sexually vulnerable as well as smaller, weaker, and slower. So, no male, however he identifies himself, should have access to a women’s prison or locker room, or compete on a women’s sports team. As the WNBA recently (unwillingly) demonstrated, to maintain otherwise is to maintain that women’s prisons, teams, and locker rooms have no reason to exist.

Moreover, women alone menstruate, gestate and birth babies, and breastfeed. So, sexual mores, parental accommodation, and infant care should not reflect perfect sameness between the sexes. To maintain otherwise is to embrace a disembodied fantasy.

Sex realism is not gender essentialism

“Gender” is a useless, sophistic concept. It refers to aesthetics and activities that are normed in our culture by sex. “Norms” would suffice.

Anti-feminists weaponize gender to lionize rigid, sexed roles: Men should be breadwinners, and women should be homemakers. Meanwhile, many mainstream “trans-inclusive” feminists weaponize gender to reject the unique physical and sexual vulnerability of women and accept the fallacy of “gender identity” (heedless of the attendant homophobia).

While it’s true that many sex-realist feminists acknowledge the sexes’ overlapping distributions of personality traits and preferences, believing men and women are different doesn’t mean believing women belong in the kitchen. Indeed, sex-realist feminists differ in our own societal and domestic roles.

Sure, many sex-realist feminists believe that if we had a women’s movement that acknowledged and supported both women’s biological distinctness and our holistic equality — and that called both women and men to complementary care work accordingly — there would be more early marriages and big families. Amen.

But sex-realist feminism does not require this belief. It requires only a reality principle and an investment in women’s genuine well-being.

Freedom is not androgyny

Mainstream feminists do not trust women with real freedom; their goal is not to facilitate women’s interests but to perpetuate a norm of androgyny. They know that, blessed with the economic and cultural freedom to do so (as I have been), many smart, educated, ambitious women will prioritize hands-on motherhood (as I do).

Sex-realist feminists, by contrast, trust women with actual agency. Women are not pawns in the left’s crusade against an erstwhile patriarchy, and liberation is not a contest to see how much like men we can become.

Women are also not foot soldiers in the right’s war on feminism, with victory measured by how many of us return to traditional gender roles. Our goal is every individual woman’s full flourishing via equality of opportunity that does not measure success by sameness of outcome.

I can advocate for economic and cultural policies and practices that support working mothers and stay-at-home mothers. I can say “Godspeed” to every girlboss and to every homemaker, irrespective of the national percentages of each.

I believe in each woman’s holistic equality. And, with it, her true liberty to fulfill her own unique vocation.

Maibritt Henkel:

The term “sex-realist feminism” implies that other feminists are unrealistic about sex, so let me clarify where I stand on biology.

It is true that the vast majority of humans fall into one of two chromosomal categories, XX or XY, and have unambiguously male or female genitalia. Many physical capacities are determined by this genetic difference, such as the male capacity to produce sperm or the female capacity to birth babies.

My own feminism is rooted in the belief that it is morally fine for people to disregard these biological capacities as completely as they wish. More than that — this is a freedom that warrants society’s protection. Because even if sex is rarely a spectrum, the extent to which all of us opt in and out of our biological predispositions through our social gender identities is.

From what I can tell, Elizabeth, you largely share my intuition that people’s opportunities in life should be determined as little as possible by their bodies. This is why you disavow gender essentialism and explicitly proffer a feminism that accepts both the “girlboss” and the “homemaker.”

But let me ask you more explicitly: To what extent does your feminism — and your definition of “woman” — include women who never have children, either by choice or because they are infertile? Or women who do not menstruate, as is the case for at least 20% of hormonal IUD users? Or women who get bilateral mastectomies, like roughly half of American women with the BRCA mutation?

If women who do not give birth, do not get their periods, and do not have breasts are all still women, then excluding trans women from the category “woman” because they potentially have a penis seems arbitrary.

In this sense, trans-inclusive feminism is not just about trans women. While they are the ones most obviously welcomed by a women’s movement that defines “women” based on gender identity rather than biological sex, all women who do not live in accordance with what binary sex appears to entail stand to benefit. Because while you insist that sex-realist feminism does not prescribe how women should behave, many of the biological experiences you consider definitionally female are, in fact, choices.

Not everything is a choice, like lung capacity or grip strength, and yet sex-realist feminists are wrong about women’s bodies being the primary reason they are vulnerable to violence. There are plenty of strong, tall women who have been assaulted by flabby, short men. What gave Harvey Weinstein the power to pressure women into sexual favors wasn’t first and foremost his biological sex; it was his control over their careers and his institutional immunity.

Likewise, under the sex-realist paradigm, the violence committed against trans women doesn’t make sense. After all, they have the same genetic advantages as cis men. Nonetheless, trans women experienced violent victimization at roughly four times the rate of cis women (not to mention the fact that most homicide victims in the United States are…men). The fact that trans women are, as you insist, biologically male does not protect them from these physical threats, which statistically dwarf any documented threat trans women have posed in locker rooms. But proper legal rights and increased social acceptance might.

None of this means leaving behind cis women who choose to act according to their biological capacities. I support laws that entitle nursing mothers to time and space to breastfeed, for instance, and cultural norms that compel people to stand up on the bus for pregnant women. Here, reproductive biology is relevant, but I’m not sure gender is.

What matters when it comes to protecting pregnant people is, well, whether they are pregnant, whatever pronouns they use. The same goes for protecting people who are menstruating, going through menopause, or predisposed to ovarian cancer.

The gender-neutral language you deride as “androgyny” is what actually keeps policy tied to the experiences that need institutional support, rather than simply the category “woman.” For instance, male employees might want to take time off work to care for th eir young children just as much as their female colleagues.

The way I see it, my feminism offers protections to a much wider range of women. It cares less about identifying one essential trait that all women share and more about defending women’s right to live in radically different ways.

Once we set aside the accommodations we agree on, this debate seems to come down to who belongs in women’s prisons, locker rooms, and sports teams. Those are important questions, but they’re much narrower than your claim that feminism must organize society around the binary reality of sex.

What else would that require in practice? If there’s a broader program, spell it out. If not, why present these disputes as a wholesale conflict over women’s freedom and flourishing?

Elizabeth Grace Matthew:

This response misstates, misconstrues, and misunderstands my argument. That’s mostly because it’s predicated on fundamental misunderstandings of both the reality of human nature and the purpose of civilization.

I do not believe that “people’s opportunities in life should be determined as little as possible by their bodies” because I am not living in a fairy tale. We are embodied creatures. Does that mean that only women fold clothes? No. Does it mean that only women birth children? Of course. It also means that men who are 5 feet and 1 inch tall can’t play in the NBA.

I do not deride “gender-neutral language.” In fact, I stated that gender is a wholly useless concept. Language does not construct reality. That’s why, if you’re pregnant, you’re a woman, no matter “your pronouns.”

Of course women who don’t menstruate or have children are women. The distinction between not menstruating because you have an IUD and not menstruating because you’re a man is not “arbitrary.” It’s like the distinction between not walking because you’re a person whose legs are broken and not walking because you’re a snake, which definitionally means that you have no legs.

Aberrations, malfunctions, and life experiences don’t change chromosomal or biological reality. You can’t offer “protections to a much wider range of women” because (as the former NBA players petitioning to play in the WNBA have recently, humorously, demonstrated) there is no such range.

Any broader program for the flourishing of women must take into account why we need feminism in the first place. Socialization doesn’t produce male violence and rape; it mitigates it. Without sufficient social construction, it’s “nature, red in tooth and claw,” in which men oppress and rape women while strong men oppress and kill weaker ones. The ongoing need for feminism reflects women’s asymmetric reliance on male virtue, which a functional civilization produces by both inspiration (religion) and coercion (law).

We no doubt agree, Maibritt, on many policies to aid women, mothers, and families. This dispute remains so important, nonetheless, because it isn’t ultimately about such matters of policy or opinion, but about fact.

In your worldview, sex is subordinated to the faux-enlightenment of “gender.” So, power differentials between the sexes originate not in biology but in social systems. Logically, from that perspective, a social system of utopian androgyny could and should eventually obviate all need for women’s sex-based protections.

I know, by contrast, that we’ll always need feminism. The actual, sex-realist kind.

Maibritt Henkel:

I asked what sex realism requires beyond same-sex prisons, locker rooms, and sports teams. You answered that our dispute “isn’t ultimately about such matters of policy or opinion, but about fact.”

But it was you who wrote that a sex-realist feminism requires “ordering society to accommodate” the sex binary. That’s not a claim limited to facilities that play a marginal role in our daily lives. You wrote that “sexual mores, parental accommodation, and infant care should not reflect perfect sameness between the sexes.” These are not so much factual observations about human biology as normative prescriptions about how our bodies ought to serve the “purpose of civilization.”

There is no way to read this except as requiring different sexual rules and parental roles for women and men. That is precisely the gender essentialism you say you reject.

I never disputed the fact of biological differences. Sometimes sex matters more than gender, for instance in elite women’s sports. But this debate is about whether feminism serves women best by advocating for them as a biologically defined group that explicitly excludes trans women. My position is that feminism should protect women’s freedom to disregard their biological capacities in whatever ways they feel they are limited by them. This project is fortified, not weakened, when it accepts trans women as women.

The institutional accommodations that feminists support should follow from the multitude of embodied experiences that women can have, whether or not they choose them, and whatever biology they were born with. A woman without a child doesn’t need parental leave, but a man who wants to spend time with his newborn does. So perhaps parental leave should be available on (gasp) gender-neutral terms.

But our deepest disagreement may be that your feminism is grounded in “women’s asymmetric reliance on male virtue.” I, like all men and women in a free society, am reliant on liberal institutions that retain a monopoly on the legitimate use of force. The majority of men are not violent — and they fear the few who are, just as women do.

Indeed, men do not grant women their rights and freedoms because of “virtue” — most men have a shared self-interest with women, and most cis women have a shared self-interest with trans women. We may arrive at the need for common rights from different places, biological or otherwise, but we arrive there all the same.
