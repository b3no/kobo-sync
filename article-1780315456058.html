Weight Isn't The Dealbreaker You Think It Is

70% of America is overweight, and most of those people are married--so why do so many people complain about their dating prospects being too fat?

If you spend a lot of time online listening to men talk about dating in 2026, you will see many of them repeat that overweight women are “ruining” the dating landscape. Even men who claim not to be picky will say they “don’t ask for much”—just someone who is “fit and active.” (Cue: the guy from Love Is Blind who repeatedly asked women if he would be able to lift them onto his shoulders at a concert.) Others will cut to the chase with “no fatties.” They will tweet out photos of women they apparently deem too heavy to be on the apps at all, with the same degree of shock and horror as one might feel if they had seen these women not on a dating app, but hiding in their shower with a knife.

Of course, the Internet is primed to show us rage bait, and I have long believed these men represented a particularly nasty vocal minority—not that it was unusual to prefer slim women, but that it was unusual to be so incensed that heavier women exist at all.

But something still didn’t add up. If over 70% of the country is overweight, and the vast majority of people get married, aren’t these men actually far pickier than the average guy, despite claiming to be “not that picky” or even on the incel spectrum? The average guy, after all, is fat, and is married to a fat woman. Even when I interviewed a group of non-misogynistic incels, most of them subtly mentioned they wouldn’t date an overweight woman. And what about women? Surely, if women are the choosier sex, they’re choosy about weight as well. So why do we never hear them complaining about “too many fat guys on the apps?”

Luckily, I had lots of data on this—not just from my recent digital body type rating survey, but from other studies I conducted earlier. Finally, I have an answer to the question of just how much a dealbreaker weight is for men and women, and the nuance that everyone seems to be missing.

One particularly ridiculous thing about “my dating apps are full of fatties” discourse is that you almost never hear this feedback from women despite the fact that obesity isn’t actually that gendered. Yep, that’s right. Despite the fact that it’s mostly men who complain about an epidemic of overweight women, there are nearly as many overweight men. However, like many genderslop contradictions on Twitter, this might boil down to class. As I wrote in a previous (somewhat related) article:

According to a 2011-2014 study done by the CDC, obesity correlates with income differently for men and women. Richer women are thinner than poorer women (29% of highest-income bracket women were obese compared to 45% in the lowest income bracket) but for men, the obesity rate was flat across income groups (32% at the lowest, 33% at the highest.)

Basically, if a guy sees lots of overweight women around him, and not as many overweight men, that says something about where he probably lives and the median income of his region. He probably doesn’t live in Manhattan or Los Angeles, for example. (It makes sense too, if you live in a region where most men work physically demanding jobs while women don’t—the women will be fatter.) And honestly, if you’re upper middle class and live in a large coastal city (as is the case for many people of Twitter and Substack) you likely won’t see many young, single obese people at all—maybe a few chubby people, but looking around you, you will not see evidence of any kind of obesity epidemic. A matcha epidemic, yes. An epidemic of “Timothee Chalamet lookalike contests” where nobody actually looks anything like Timothee Chalamet, yes. But an obesity epidemic? No, that will be relegated the news and CDC headlines, not your daily life.

This could explain why Twitter and Substack are so plagued by weight discourse that does not really recognize what the average American looks like. Most of the takes I see in these places are from highly educated, upper middle class people, who probably don’t interact with that many significantly overweight people on a day-to-day basis. That’s probably why I keep seeing notes and articles about how “everyone” is rapidly shrinking to fit into a size quadruple-zero via GLP-1s. Everyone? Really? If you parachuted into the middle of Kansas, everyone would look like Kiera Knightley after norovirus?

So in keeping with that observation, it’s entirely possible that the extreme aversion to any excess weight in a romantic partner isn’t actually a common thing, but a thing that’s been amplified on social media platforms frequented by relatively slim, upper middle class people.

But there’s an even more important distinction that must be made, which is that “chubby” and “obese” are not the same thing, and when people talk about “overweight” people, they often don’t really mean those who are just chubby. As it turns out, the vast majority of singles are open to chubby partners, with a huge dropoff when it comes to an imagined obese partner.

Despite all the talk about how height is the hardest obstacle for men to overcome, women are overwhelmingly more open to dating a man exactly their height than dating a man who is obese. And dating someone who was “overweight but not obese” was appealing to the majority of all cohorts of singles.

The same held true when I ran my digital body type rating survey, which had a much better sample size. One reason I used the (admittedly, imperfect and Smurfish) body generator was that I could input exact weight and height measurements to make sure that when I was designing an “obese” or “chubby” body that I wasn’t just eyeballing it and was instead referring to a specific BMI. This was exactly because I wanted to measure people’s openness to dating chubby people, instead of grouping everyone with any degree of excess body fat as “overweight.”

What I discovered was that there was, in practice, a huge gap between the reaction that singles had to chubby bodies vs. obese bodies, and unsurprisingly, people were more open to heavier bodies depending on their own weights. So yes, with most of this country existing somewhere on the “overweight” spectrum, we can basically assume that overweight people are marrying each other. Not great fodder for rage bait, but it’s true.

Just two examples of how “chubby” versus “obese” showed up in my survey, based on BMI (although there were other bodies that fell into both of these categories.)

For women, the first body was accepted by 35% of men and 59% of lesbian/bi women, while the second was accepted by only 17% of men and 30% of lesbian/bi women.

And for men, the first body was accepted by 66% of women and 69% of gay/bi men, while the second body was accepted by 23% of women and 11% of gay/bi men.

Another finding sticks out, which is that men were much harsher about women’s weight than women were about men’s weight, even if they were overweight themselves, which could explain why the “fatties on dating apps” content always seems to come from men, who aren’t any less likely to be overweight.

For example, chubby women’s bodies received a 50% average acceptance rate from men who were slightly overweight (ie: their “body match”), whereas chubby men’s bodies received a 83% average acceptance rate from women who were slightly overweight. In other words—single women are more likely to date their body match than single men.

There are two reasons this might be true. First of all, the digital body generator I created was imperfect, as I said, and I received feedback that the women’s bodies looked less realistic than the men’s bodies. Some veteran naked lady experts informed me that the hips bones looked unnatural and the boobs weren’t defined enough. Perhaps if the men and women were rating real people (something on which I know Aella is working—I’m not brave enough to ask people to send me their nudes!) we would see a different pattern.

However, I did notice that lesbians and bi women who rated women’s bodies were about as picky as straight women were about men’s bodies, and far more accepting than straight men were of women’s bodies, while gay men were picker than straight women and even less forgiving of obesity than straight men—which makes me think that the delta between straight men and women’s weight-pickiness could not be boiled down to “the female bodies rendered in a uniquely weird way.” If something was wrong with all the women’s bodies in the dataset, we would see lesbians rating them lower, and gay men likely wouldn’t have been even pickier about obese bodies than straight men.

It’s also entirely possible that women could be pickier overall, while being less picky than men specifically in the arena of weight.

Why do I think women are pickier than men overall? Well, I have eyes, ears, and I live in the world. But also, I ran a different study based on a fake dating app that did include real photos (which hinted at body type, but were mostly focused on faces.) Even the most attractive men barely cracked 60-70% right swipes, while certain women were nearly unanimous.

One fun tidbit from that study: women were picker about men’s age than men’s height. Adding five inches to a man’s height did very little to improve his right-swipes with women outside his age bracket.

But anyway, what wasn’t shown in these photos, or really any others in that survey? Body types. Let alone full-frontal nudity. You could basically make out if someone was skinny or fat (and almost all submissions were from people who clearly weren’t fat, anyway) but beyond that, you didn’t really get a good idea of actual body shapes and BMI. And perhaps that’s the one area where men really are pickier.

But even if men are pickier about weight than women are (and I believe that they are!) there is a huge delta between bodies that are mildly or very overweight, and the discourse keeps flattening this distinction. Keep in mind that my survey respondents tend to skew middle class to upper middle class, so these men might be surrounded by a higher percentage of slim women and therefore less likely to consider chubby women. But even then, about 40% of men who rated their own bodies as “average” were open to dating digitally-generated chubby women. There may not be a universal, conventional preference for chubby women, but clearly enough men are open to it that it’s not an obvious dealbreaker either. The same was true for chubby men. They were not the preferred body types among any of the female cohorts, but they were still commonly accepted.

In practice, this means that if you’re chubby, losing weight to get to an average or slim BMI is probably smart for your dating strategy because there are far more chubby people than people who prefer this body type. But does it mean nobody will be attracted to you? Absolutely not. And while it’s not a common preference, it’s still a preference for a minority of people.

But what none of these surveys were able to capture—and what basically no survey will ever be able to capture—is the intangible chemistry between two people. Women will readily admit such a thing exists, and some men will, but a sizable number of men insist that they are simple, humble creatures who desire nothing more than a really hot woman and couldn’t care less about the rest. How noble! When I hear this, it’s generally giving “hasn’t actually dated hot women.” There are plenty of hot women who can’t seem to sustain a relationship, or who take a while to find the right person, and the reason is not that they’re too hot or too picky. I know some naysayers will be quick to tell me that any hot woman who is dumped was simply not hot enough for that man, and I guess I can’t disprove it, but that’s such a ridiculous premise that I shouldn’t have to. Most men with options will not pursue a woman—even a hot one—if there is no chemistry.

If men only cared about looks, no woman would ever get dumped by a guy and then date someone else who was more attractive than the previous guy. And yet, this happens! Because not only are looks more subject to “type” than sex data nerds such as myself like to admit, but people—yes, even men—care about things other than looks.

I also don’t mean to create the false dichotomy of “hot, skinny bitches” and “nice chubby girls.” I keep seeing this too, among certain Internet-brained men, where they muse about the decision to date a “bitchy 10” or a “nice 7.” (Hilarious that “7”—still above average!—is the ugliest they can fathom even in this scenario.) But I’m not really talking about being nice or being a bitch. I’m talking about chemistry, which is much more subjective. If you don’t have chemistry with someone, it doesn’t mean they have a bad personality—they might have great chemistry with someone else.

Even people who say they would never date a chubby person are probably not considering what would happen if they had amazing chemistry with a chubby person, or if they fell in love with an average-sized person who became chubby ten years later. Perhaps obesity is more likely to be a dealbreaker, especially with slim or fit people, but it’s still impossible to measure hypothetical chemistry.

This is exactly why I worry about the effects of dating apps on today’s singles. Chemistry is often instantaneous and inexplicable, and when you spontaneously meet another person in real life, you can experience chemistry that you might not have experienced just looking at a photo of the person—yes, even if you are a straight man. It’s completely true that you don’t have to be a Chad (or a Stacy, for that matter) to find a romantic partner, but I wouldn’t be surprised if the photo-first dating app format means that people are swiping left on (or swiping right but not prioritizing) people with whom they would actually have the best chemistry. And no matter how nerdy I want to get with these studies, I’ll never be able to prove precisely how that chemistry works—or what we’ve lost by disregarding it.

Zooming out on the rest of America, yes, the majority of the country is overweight and partnered. Clearly, excess weight is not a dealbreaker for most people. But most of these people got together with their partners before the advent of dating apps—when they might have noticed a woman’s smile and laugh alongside her body size. Today, people are distilled to two dimensional images, with no chance to allow their personalities (and hints of chemistry) to shine through. While the chubby versus obese distinction is unfairly flattened in the discourse, and while a mildly overweight person might not struggle much at all, the dating app medium is doing a disservice to anyone who is not abundantly, conventionally hot. And that—more than the idea of fatness being a literal dealbreaker—is more of a concern.
