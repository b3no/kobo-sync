You can't diversify the finish line

The war on standardized tests is shooting the messenger

Kelsey Piper
Jul 20
∙

READ IN APP
 

My grandmother — whose family fled Austria in 1939, when she was three — grew up in New York City. When she was in middle school, she passed the extremely selective test to attend Bronx Science, one of New York City’s specialized public high schools. From there, she went to Barnard and then to law school in an era when very few women did.

So while I live on the opposite coast these days — much to her consternation — I have always had a special place in my heart for New York City’s competitive specialized high school system, an engine of immigrant mobility and opportunity. Admission to these schools doesn’t require an essay or an interview, nor does it consider any facts about who you are. You just take a test and prove what you can do.

These are some of the best public schools in America — and right now they’re at the center of the same type of debate that is playing out at public schools all over the country: If a program has unequal benefits for children, should we destroy it?

Share

The most competitive of NYC’s specialized high schools is Stuyvesant. If the thing you want from high school is the opportunity to take the most challenging courses available alongside peers who are extremely smart and hardworking, Stuyvesant is probably the best school out there: It offers not only 30 or so AP classes but also several years of undergraduate mathematics courses.

And in the 2026-27 admitted class of 777 Stuyvesant students, only 21 are Latino and only three are Black.

City Councilman Lincoln Restler responded to these results with a call to end fully exam-based admissions: “We urgently need state legislation to modify the admissions process,” he wrote.

Restler is hardly the first to call for the exam-based schools to go; Bill de Blasio, while serving as New York City mayor in 2018, proposed replacing test-based admissions with admitting the top 7% of each middle school. Mayor Zohran Mamdani, who himself attended Bronx Science, had entertained SHSAT abolition during his campaign for state Assembly, but this week he took a much smarter approach, calling for more work to ensure that all middle schoolers are well prepared for the SHSAT.

Before we dive into that, let’s be clear on one thing: part of why there are so few Black students at Stuyvesant is that there is a nonprofit program, Prep for Prep, which helps low-income and disadvantaged minority and immigrant students earn scholarships to some of the nation’s most prestigious private high schools. Some of the Black and Latino students who might otherwise be at Stuyvesant are instead snapped up by private schools. That paints a much less troubling picture: It’s not that almost no Black or Latino students are strong academic performers, it’s that there are a few different paths to the elite and Prep for Prep is capturing some of them.

But still, it’s definitely the case that far fewer Black and Latino students post high scores on the SHSAT than white and Asian students. And unsurprisingly, some have responded by calling for Stuyvesant and similar schools to stop selecting their student body based on test scores.

In 2020, similar concerns about racial disparities led the University of California Board of Regents to drop test scores for admissions to California’s best colleges, and these concerns have also led school districts all over the country to restrict or end their gifted and talented programs.

In both cases, the problem that they subsequently discovered was that you don’t actually fix disparities by refusing to measure them.

Never ban the test to hide the results

There are a few different critiques one could make of an academic test. One is that it doesn’t really capture what we care about. For instance, maybe it fails to predict academic success. When the UC Board of Regents banned the SAT, a prominent argument was that the SAT was much less predictive than grades of student performance in college.

This turns out not to be true: At top schools, SATs are more predictive than grades of college success, and in the absence of any external check, it’s easy for some schools to award A grades to students who know almost none of the material expected of them.

When Black and Latino students score worse than white and Asian students on standardized tests, the tests’ biases are not the only factor. The score disparity remains when tests are well-designed, fair, and strongly predictive of later academic success.

This sucks, obviously! We would like to figure out how to deliver the highest-quality education to all American kids, and we would like an elite that is drawn from every quarter of American society. So it’s discouraging that disadvantaged students aren’t learning the skills they need.

But getting rid of the tests that demonstrate this doesn’t change real material disparities that exist between Black, Latino, white, and Asian students.

Black kids in America are much less likely to be reading at grade level. They are much less likely to take advanced math classes and less likely to take and pass AP tests. But taking away the AP tests wouldn’t mean they knew the math. And taking away the SAT wouldn’t teach them how to read.

If this bothers you, you are — I hope and assume — bothered by the actual reality of kids’ preparedness, not by the fact that somebody measured it. There is a real problem here, and we absolutely cannot solve it by bluffing or by trying to create more and more institutions that don’t check whether or not we have solved it. We have to actually teach kids to read and to do math.

The war against gifted programs has not led to any educational improvements — indeed, it has correlated with a substantial across-the-board slump in test scores that has been most brutal for the students who started out weakest. (I don’t think this was caused by the retreat from gifted programs, though I do think a decrease in test-based accountability has contributed.)

And it’s politically a disaster, with Asian voters in particular swinging dramatically against Democrats whenever the party considers gutting advanced academic programs. But it keeps popping up, I think because the discomfort over these disparities is so profound and the easiest path to relieving that discomfort is simply hiding the evidence.

How to actually get more Black kids into Stuyvesant

Where should our energy be directed if we want to actually solve, rather than hide, the struggles of disadvantaged kids? I’ve written about the answer for kids who are struggling to learn to read: high-quality phonics instruction, teacher training, and the other reforms that are working in Mississippi and Louisiana. But while universal literacy is really important, it won’t move the needle on admissions to Stuyvesant — for that, you need to better serve really bright disadvantaged kids.

In the 1970s, there were a lot more Black students in NYC’s competitive schools. What were we doing differently? For one thing, as one Black Bronx Science graduate pointed out, we had more gifted and talented programs in middle schools. Those nurtured and challenged the brightest students and set them on a path for the competitive schools. When those pathways vanished, the share of Black students in the exam schools started to collapse.

We should work much, much harder to make sure that advanced coursework — for the kids who could, if challenged, reach Stuyvesant readiness — is available within our public school system.

Right now, many students who are taking advanced math are doing it outside school through expensive extracurricular programs. The inequities downstream of that are incredibly obvious. If we want every bright kid to have a chance, we have to offer advanced math in schools.

You can’t hold out for representation

Realistically, even doing a lot better at teaching all students is not going to produce high schools that are exactly representative of the city as a whole. In my grandmother’s day, new-arriving Jewish immigrants were wildly overrepresented in the specialized high schools. There are still a lot of Jewish people living in New York City, but they’re no longer wildly overrepresented in those schools.

That’s to some extent a first-generation immigrant thing: parents and students pursuing extreme academic achievement as a route into a new country’s middle class. First-gen immigrants are probably always going to be wildly overrepresented (and this is, of course, true regardless of race — African immigrants do very well in matriculation and graduation rates).

As long as there are any disparities anywhere else in our education system, those will show up at specialized schools. You should expect disparities at Stuyvesant unless you’ve eliminated them literally everywhere else — every difference in income, in parental free time, in interests and abilities, in access to books and museums, in exposure to violence, in nutrition, in everything.

If we can only tolerate Stuyvesant in a world where its admission test does not show us any disparities, then we can only tolerate it as a capstone project in a world we already made perfect in every way.

But the specialized high schools are the exact opposite of sprinkles on top of an already-perfected society. They work — well — in a deeply imperfect one. They’re a small enclave of intense meritocracy, offering opportunity to the kids who can benefit from it, in a wider society that has frequently been hostile to it.

Everything that is said today about how Asian students dominate these schools was said about those excessively studious, personality-less, presumptively untrustworthy Jews back in the 1950s. The “holistic admissions” now being entertained to save Stuyvesant from being overwhelmingly Asian were first invented to keep out the Jews.

But Jews did not destroy New York City’s elite schools, and Asians won’t either. The graduates of these schools in their day benefited from their rigorous education and went on to make the world a better place — and today’s graduates will do the same thing if we let them.

Not every school should be Stuyvesant, but some schools should be. For them to persist, we do not need to become comfortable with the educational system failures they highlight. But we need to avoid blaming those failures on the tests that expose them. And we need to not condition competitive, excellent education on solving everything wrong with the rest of society.

Get to work on that, by all means! But in the meantime, leave Stuy be.
