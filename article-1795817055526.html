The Supreme Court’s dissenters on the law’s “terrible dilemma”

Three of the Court’s four women justices would have struck Quebec’s Law 21 down for infringing on women’s equality

Paul Wells
Nov 27
 
READ IN APP
 
Karakatsanis, O'Bonsawin, Martin: The s. 28 Justices

The Supreme Court of Canada today upheld most of Quebec’s Bill 21, which bans teachers, police officers, government lawyers and other officials from wearing prominent religious symbols. In practice, its predominant effect is to prevent Muslim women who wear head coverings from teaching in the province’s public schools.

I don’t like the law and I don’t like the effect of this decision. But I’m encouraged by the thought that the law will need to be re-approved by Quebec’s National Assembly every five years, and that there is continuing debate among Quebecers about whether the law makes sense. I’m not at all sure the law will still stand in a decade. Slim consolation for people directly affected by its provisions today.

Today’s ruling divided the Court in ways that reflect gender and geography. All three Quebec justices (of the seven who considered the case) upheld Bill 21, whereas three of the four justices from outside Quebec would have struck down its central provisions. Three of the Court’s four women would have used Section 28 of the 1982 constitution as the basis of their decision to strike down the law. Therein lies a tale. Even though these dissenting justices didn’t form the majority today, I want to tell their story because I suspect we haven’t heard the last of it.

Here’s Section 28 of the Constitution Act, 1982, as it reads today.

28. Notwithstanding anything in this Charter, the rights and freedoms referred to in it are guaranteed equally to male and female persons.

It’s quite sweeping. “Notwithstanding anything.”

But that’s not how it read at the beginning of the patriation debates. The version that emerged from the 1981 fed-prov negotiations that led to repatriation of the Constitution with a Charter of Rights and an amending formula was less sweeping: “Notwithstanding anything in this Charter except section 33, the rights and freedoms referred to in it are guaranteed equally to male and female persons.” In other words, Chrétien, Romanow and all Canada’s governments except René Lévesque’s sidelined Quebec government — all the men of the so-called Night of the Long Knives — originally believed the equality of men and women should be subordinate to the “notwithstanding” clause. Legislatures would be able to suspend the guarantee’s protections by simple majority vote.

This upset a lot of women, and in a national and cross-partisan lobbying effort without precedent, activists joined to force Parliament and the provinces to change their minds. There’s a film about it. Here it is. Watch it sometime. It’s a reminder that Parliament can matter if citizens decide it had bloody well better matter.

Now, the thing about s. 28 is that it had never been fully tested at the Supreme Court before today. There’s another provision for general equality, s.15, whose wording specifies that it includes equality by “sex.” What’s unique about Bill 21 is that it takes s. 15 out of the game by invoking the notwithstanding clause, s. 33. That leaves only s. 28 as recourse for anyone who worries that the Quebec law infringes equality.

The Court’s majority on this question. — Wagner, Rowe, Côté and Kasirer — say s. 28 can’t trump the notwithstanding clause. Wagner, Rowe and Côté say that’s because s. 28’s equality provisions only work in conjunction with other enumerated rights — you don’t have freestanding equality as a man or woman, but only in understanding “the rights and freedoms referred to in [the Charter].” In their interpretation, if a right is eclipsed by the use of s. 33’s notwithstanding provision, so is the guarantee of equality. It’s a rock-paper-scissors argument: 33 covers 28.

No way, say Karakatsanis, O’Bonsawin and Martin: 28 crushes 33. Otherwise s. 28 couldn’t really be said to “guarantee” anything. I suspect this is not the last we’re going to hear of this argument.

Two of the s. 28 dissenters, Ontarians Andromache Karakatsanis and Michelle O’Bonsawin, wrote their reasoning on these matters together. The third, Albertan Sheilah Martin, wrote separately. Here’s some of what they wrote.

“There can be no dispute” that Bill 21’s core provisions “infringe the fundamental rights and freedoms of many Quebecers,” Karakatsanis and O’Bonsawin write. The law puts “devout Quebecers in a terrible dilemma: betray their religious convictions or be excluded from avenues of public service and employment.”

But so what? By invoking s. 33, the “notwithstanding” clause, Bill 21 suspends those rights and freedoms. Yes, counter Karakatsanis and O’Bonsawin, but it only suspends those rights: it doesn’t snuff them out. They don’t cease to exist. That means courts retain the option to name the rights being suspended — to “promote democratic accountability by informing citizens how their representatives have chosen to temporarily override rights and freedoms.” In this, Karakatsanis and O’Bonsawin are joined by Kasirer and Martin, establishing for any future legislative use of s. 33 the judiciary’s right to name the rights being flouted.

But they also call, unsuccessfully today, for a broad interpretation of s. 28 in its first real test. The equality provision in s. 28, added after the original constitutional deal as a direct result of widespread public pressure, “is an express commitment not to undo Canada’s decades-long efforts towards substantive sex equality,” the two write. “It is the unmistakable guarantee that women’s constitutional equality is non-derogable.”

What does denying those equality rights mean? “Lost employment opportunities, deferred ambitions, and the pain of knowing that full participation in public life is conditional on abandoning parts of their identities,” Karakatsanis and O’Bonsawin write. “At the heart of this case are people who seek to contribute to the communities they call home, yet find themselves excluded from public service because of how they practice their faith.”

Sheilah Martin chose to retire from the Court in May, just before her 70th birthday rather than serving until the mandatory age of 75. The Judges Act lets judges participate in rulings where they’ve heard the arguments, for six months after their retirement. Martin will reach that post-retirement limit on Monday. This will be one of her last sets of reasons. That’s neither here nor there, but I found it interesting.

Martin’s dissent is more categorical on s. 28 than Karakatsanis and O’Bonsawin’s. “It is clear from its face that s. 33 cannot suspend the sex equality right protected under s. 28. Substantively, s. 28 confers a free-standing right that exists “[n]otwithstanding anything in this Charter” and cannot be rendered inoperable by s. 33 of the Charter.”

In fact, that’s why Martin sees no reason to list the other Charter rights the Quebec law infringes. It infringes s. 28, so to Martin, no further detail is needed.

“The battle that women fought to ensure s. 28 found its place in the Constitution is inspiring,” Martin writes. “The intention of these equality seeking women and men, and the Parliamentarians who adopted their proposals, was so clearly to enshrine an inviolable guarantee of equal rights that superseded both ss. 1 and 33.” The point of rewriting s. 28 to strengthen it, after the entire rest of the new Constitution had been put to bed, “was to give sex equality rights a special place in the Constitution and to make clear the now uncontroversial point that women and men are equally protected in our free and democratic society.”

No debate ends here today. All the debates continue. Did Wagner and his colleagues on the majority permanently cap the application of s. 28’s equality guarantee? I doubt it. Does s. 33 remain available to legislatures willing to use it? Yes. Will they be less likely to use it, knowing courts will probably list the rights being suspended in ways that are sure to attract widespread public attention? I hope so.

Today’s ruling contains more words and less unity among factions than most of what the top court produces. Little is settled. After a long trip through the courts, Bill 21 is back in the domain of politics. Very well then: the law’s opponents now have a debate to win.
