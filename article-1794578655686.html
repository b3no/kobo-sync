Everything you’ve ever wanted is in this newsletter.

More than 50 years before Napoleon Hill published Think and Grow Rich, Miss Watson told Huck Finn that he would get whatever he prayed for.

“But it warn’t so,” the boy reports from the laboratory of his own experience. “I tried it. Once I got a fish-line, but no hooks. It warn’t any good to me without hooks. I tried for the hooks three or four times, but somehow I couldn’t make it work.”

Huck’s failure might have been the end of positive thinking in America — the equivalent of Dr. Johnson kicking a stone to refute Bishop Berkeley’s subjective idealism. But of course, that’s not how the universe manifested itself on this fresh, green breast of the new world.

For better or worse — I would say better and worse — the United States has been the world’s most fertile soil for positive thinking.

The philosophical musings of the Concord Transcendentalists helped birth the New Thought movement and let loose a hive of reformers, visionaries, charlatans, and peddlers of earthly salvation.

Hill’s bestseller appeared during the Great Depression, when the idea of thinking one’s way to wealth must have sounded much better than selling pencils on the street corner.

During the Cold War — that geopolitical battle between modes of thought — Norman Vincent Peale published The Power of Positive Thinking.

We were already a nation of people cruising down the course of miracles. See it, be it.

And why not? Doesn’t our thinking determine our reality? Remember that Milton himself wrote:

The mind is its own place, and in itself
Can make a Heaven of Hell, a Hell of Heaven.

How many disciples of positive thinking remember that this claim is made by Satan?

By the grace of the Over-Soul, we’ve now arrived at the 20th anniversary of The Secret, by an Australian television producer named Rhonda Byrne. The buzz has died down considerably since the MySpace era, but at the time, Oprah and other celebrities helped The Secret sell tens of millions of copies.

In a new introduction to the anniversary edition, Byrne claims, “Over the next twenty years, we saw neuroscience, psychology, and quantum physics make discoveries that completely aligned with The Secret’s message — that the mind is a powerhouse and creates our lives with every thought we focus on and believe.”

Of course, no such alignment between Byrne’s gooey metaphysics and any of these scientific fields has been established. But the universe doesn’t need me to chill The Secret’s vibe. If these lozenges of inspiration soothe some readers’ despair, I’m genuinely glad.

What’s more prominent to me now — in my advanced age — is the book’s unabashed narcissism. Its pages itch with self-absorption. By my count, the phrase “you want” appears more than 100 times.

“You are the most powerful transmission tower in the Universe,” Byrne promises. “The Secret can give you whatever you want.”

That’s where Huck’s fishhooks catch in my mind.

The last thing I need is to be given whatever I want. What I need — what the world needs — is a faith or an ethic that turns my mind away from my incessant wants.

That’s not Byrne’s frequency. In a particularly cringeworthy passage, she writes, “One of the most powerful uses of gratitude can be incorporated in the Creative Process to turbo-charge what you want.”

Turbo-charging what we want sounds like a line from Madison Avenue’s vision board.

Resistance lies cradled in the Thanksgiving season, which is why it’s my favorite — family without the presents, pie without the guilt. But gratitude is a rich, complicated virtue. It can sweeten a life or curdle into superiority.

I’m reminded of how often my wife must tell her students not to write their college application essays about how traveling to a poor country made them realize how grateful they are not to live there.

The value of gratitude, it seems to me, is not the satisfaction it confers but the compassion it activates.

But that’s no secret.◆

Through Knowledge, an Old Heart Becomes Young Again

What most Americans know of Iran is endless war, defiant mullahs, and, more recently, higher gas prices.

Iranian-born Hamid Rahmanian knows there’s more — so much more — and he’s determined to prove it one dazzling creation at a time. His inspiration and obsession is the Shahnameh, “The Book of Kings,” completed around 1010 by the epic poet Ferdowsi.

What The Odyssey is for Greeks, the Shahnameh is for Persians, a vast canvas of foundational myths, heroes, and cultural values.

“There are beautiful love stories,” Rahmanian tells me. “There are tragedies, there are endless battles, betrayal — all the human qualities you can find.”

For more than a dozen years, he’s been producing stunningly illustrated translations, puppet shows, animated films, shadow plays, stage performances, and audiobooks based on the Shahnameh. In 2014, he was awarded a Guggenheim fellowship in film.

And now he’s just released his third pop-up book, The Myths and Epic Adventures of Shahnameh (order).

About 1,000 people spent months assembling these books by hand, each one containing some 200 individual pieces, but honestly I can’t imagine how they were created without the magical assistance of some kindly jinn.

Open this box-like book to any of its eight stories, and these fantastic realms bloom off the page as they once did for God.

The first story shows King Qumars being swept up into clouds of glory by winged angels and horned divs.

Turn to the love story of Zaul and Rudabeh, and the maiden’s tower soars a full 18 inches off the page. There’s Zaul hanging from an actual rope attached to her balcony.

Are the characters adorned with real bird feathers? Of course they are.

Every story is briefly told along the bottom of its spread, where smaller and even more intricate pop-ups hide.

Why, I ask Rahmanian, is every part of this book so much, so spectacular, so awesome?

“Because,” he tells me, “you have to scream so loud as an immigrant to be heard.”

(Rahmanian wrote to 38 reporters, editors, and feature writers about this book. Only one committed to writing about it, and he just has a Substack newsletter.)

“Iran is like a symphony, an opulent symphony, but you only hear drumbeats and nothing else,” Rahmanian says. “There are so many different instruments.”

He hopes to follow in the footsteps of Edith Hamilton, who made Greek mythology accessible to generations of people. “The idea is to create an accessible translation and also an entertaining object that people — kids, adults, any age — can engage with the Persian mythology.”

This is as enchanting as paper and ink can be — a miracle of artistic design and engineering, which carries with it the promise of cultural illumination at a time when the world thirsts for it.◆

Wish You Were Here

If you’re not getting cheery postcards from Anne Tyler, Billy Collins, and George Saunders, it’s your own damn fault.

The Common, a literary journal based at Amherst College, is holding its 12th annual postcard auction.

From now till Dec. 1, you can bid on the chance to receive a personalized postcard from dozens of favorite authors (full list).

Is it really better to give than receive? Do both!

For the right price, Ann Patchett will drop you or a friend a note from her bookstore.

Got a conspiracy theorist in the family? Get Dan Brown to send them a postcard and blow their mind.

Edwidge Danticat might win a National Book Award next week — you could be hearing from her soon!

In years past, authors have drawn little pictures on their postcards, composed short poems, and squeezed in wee stories.

A postcard and a 65-cent stamp could make somebody’s day and help keep a literary journal thriving.◆

A passage from something I’m reading.

Ojibwe writer David Treuer addresses his new book to his three children:

When you’re told — in our books, our schools — that we died because of our lack of immunity; that it was a matter of survival of the fittest; that what happened “was going to happen anyway”; know that they aren’t speaking to you. They are speaking to themselves. They don’t even see you, and so how could they speak to you? They are trying — helplessly, habitually — to unsee their true nature and the nature of the country they created; to remain illuminated by their own myth of innocence. But they are not innocent. And they are not harmless.

Picture This

Duel, by Shawn Harris, is a chance to double your fun. It’s a delightfully clever picture book that begs to be imitated by young readers — and adults.

The story describes two rival queens in neighboring castles who want to rule the whole hill. Each queen selects her bravest knight to face off and win the kingdom in a duel.

Even the youngest readers will notice that the queens, the knights, and their boldest horses look exactly the same.

Harris has created these illustrations by spreading paint on one side of the page and then folding it over to create a mirror image — a duel image. (He demonstrates the process in this video.)

What’s especially fun about his technique is that everybody succeeds. You may not produce images as fine as Harris’s, but even the messes turn out to be surprisingly lovely.

If you’ve got kids around over the holidays, get a copy of this book along with some paints and paper. Your refrigerator will soon be covered in wet butterflies.

Like the queens’ trumpeters, your young artists will hold “their horns on high and blow a hearty toot.”◆

This Week’s Poem

In the long title poem at the center of Benjamin Gucciardi’s new collection, Arguments, a man joins a group of climate activists. Their mission is deeply earnest and certainly illegal.

Yes, the Earth’s fate hangs in the balance, but the calculus for a parent is complicated.

“I think of my daughter growing up without her father,” Gucciardi writes. “I think of my daughter growing up without fresh water.”

I’m so moved by the way he captures the wonder and bewilderment of fatherhood, particularly in those early, disorienting months.

Nocturne

A voice in my daughter’s room.
A voice singing sweetly in my daughter’s room.
On the other side of the wall, I lie in my bed,
a T-shirt over my eyes to block the neighbor’s floodlight.
Rain falls in long gray threads against the window. My daughter
draped on the bosom of the voice drinking the song
and the rain pooling in the stroller I left outside
not predicting weather. The possum that lives in our yard
crouches beside it. The moon up there somewhere.
The voice is smiling. The night has no lips.
My wife asleep beside me doesn’t hear a thing,
has never seen the possum whose tail reaches out
from under the stroller thick as a watermelon vine.
The voice feeds my daughter, the rain has no hands.
In the mirror in my room, my face.
Pooling in the corners of my daughter’s lips,
is it rain? Is it the juice of one ripe melon? I should go to her
and hold her but I don’t want my footsteps
to scare away the sound.

From “Arguments: Poems.” Copyright © 2026 by Benjamin Gucciardi. Published by Persea, 2026. Reprinted with permission of the publisher.

“Apparently there is nothing that cannot happen.”

— Mark Twain

Four years ago, we were snickering about AI’s ridiculous hallucinations. Now we’re blotting our bloody lips.

“If AI is magic, it’s a magic that gets imposed on us,” said Damien P. Williams. “It’s a magic that gets done to us. It’s a magic that happens to us without our consent. And we call that kind of magic a curse.”

Williams, an assistant professor of philosophy and data science at UNC Charlotte, was one of 10 speakers at a symposium on creative writing and AI at the Mark Twain House & Museum in Hartford, Conn., last Saturday.

The spirit was feisty; the prognosis grim.

Bhamati Viswanathan, a visiting law professor at Suffolk University, called AI “one of the greatest thefts of labor ever.”

Best-selling thriller writer David Baldacci, a benefactor of the Mark Twain House, is one of the plaintiffs in a lawsuit against OpenAI and Microsoft.

As an experiment, he asked ChatGPT to write a Baldacci novel. It immediately wanted to know which of his iconic characters to include and then spat out 54 chapters in three and a half minutes.

The writer conceded it sounded a lot like him. And why shouldn’t it? It was allegedly trained on dozens of his copyrighted novels.

These systems are “good, bad mynah birds,” he said. And they swoop in everywhere.

He even had to remind his publisher never to use AI to generate jacket copy or cover images for his novels.

“Is AI intended to be complementary, supplementary, or replacement?” he asked. “We need to have a choice. We need to have oversight.”

Baldacci recalled that when he was trying to get a sense of how Claude works, the system told him, “Even with your impressive intellect and your obviously very vivid imagination, I don’t think that you fully comprehend what’s coming.”

Or, to translate it from the original Borg, “Resistance is futile.”

Lydia Millet — on hand to accept the Mark Twain American Voice in Literature Award — said, “We need to be talking in almost revolutionary terms about how we can resist the worst aspects of this thing that is actually invading our personhood.”

And it’s not just writers who feel threatened by “creative” AI. Emily Hockaday, editor of Asimov’s Science Fiction magazine, said that reams of AI-generated stories have made working as an editor “really miserable.” Synthetic submissions clogging up her inbox are now good enough to require careful, time-wasting detective work.

Meanwhile, genuine human writers toiling away in the vineyard are constantly bombarded by AI scams.

Baldacci, who’s sold 200 million copies, said he gets about five AI solicitations a day.

That’s nothing, said Erin Bartram, associate director of education at the Mark Twain House. The museum receives emails all the time: “Dear Mark Twain, we think you have potential. We’d like to market your book.”◆
