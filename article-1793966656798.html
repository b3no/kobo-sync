Energy isn't just about climate change

Is anyone serious about affordability?

Arnab Datta and Skanda Amarnath
Nov 6
 
READ IN APP
 

If you’re a Democrat, the term “energy policy” may as well have been “environmental policy.”

Should we expand natural gas? Let’s figure out how much this will increase or decrease emissions.

Should we shutter a nuclear plant? Consider the risks of radioactive waste.

Should Congress pass a permitting bill to make it easier to build infrastructure? Time to consider the impact on endangered species.

With the midterms in the rearview, ascendant Democrats face their first major policy opportunity of the new era: the Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act (BAAJA). BAAJA may be the most ambitious regulatory overhaul of our lifetimes, and the debate on the Democratic side about whether to support it has at times seemed mostly focused on its implications for the climate.

BAAJA, which not only has bipartisan congressional support but also the president’s backing, would overhaul federal permitting procedures, set the stage for faster interstate transmission build-out, and curb the ability of judges to block infrastructure projects indefinitely (a concern that extends beyond energy into the realms of industrial policy and public transit). We think that it would be a net positive for the climate. But evaluating this bill solely on its climate impact is like evaluating a car solely on its fuel efficiency, without considering safety, reliability, or cost.

Cheap, clean, and consistent: It’s hard to get all three

Traditionally, energy policy has been a delicate balance between three objectives: affordability, reliability, and sustainability, the so-called “energy trilemma.” Different sources of energy can fare better on any one or two objectives, but they often struggle to excel across all three.

For centuries, energy access has been enabled by the relative affordability of coal energy, at great cost to the physical environment (our air quality). Nuclear power is uniquely clean and reliable, but efforts to affordably construct new capacity have been a historic challenge. Wind and solar fare well on sustainability and on local estimates of affordability, but making that power more reliable when scaled comes with steeper system costs.

For the past two decades, that emphasis has shifted in the Democratic Party, where the dominant paradigm has become sustainability — narrowly understood in terms of restricting fossil fuels to mitigate climate change. Consequential policy decisions have been shaped without sufficient consideration of affordability and reliability goals.

One major example of misplaced priorities came in late 2015, when President Barack Obama determined that the Keystone XL pipeline — which would transport Albertan crude directly through to the plains states, ultimately reaching Gulf Coast refineries — did not serve the national interest and denied its permit. The move was largely driven by pressure from climate activists, who described the project as a “carbon bomb.”

This was a highly dubious claim; the Keystone XL pipeline would have simply added capacity to an existing pipeline system. When that existing network hit capacity, Keystone XL would have avoided the substitute at the time: transportation via rail, a more expensive (and hazardous) alternative.

The State Department acknowledged in its 2014 environmental review that the pipeline was unlikely to change how much oil sands crude was extracted. Now, without Keystone, much of that oil still reaches global markets and still travels through the U.S. and to the Gulf Coast refining complex, just at greater cost and less efficiently.

In short, the decision sacrificed the affordability and reliability benefits of more efficient access to Canadian heavy crude, leaving us reliant on imports from unstable countries such as Venezuela or nations in the Middle East. It also signaled to one of our closest allies that the United States could not necessarily be counted on as a dependable partner for long-term energy investments.

Then newly elected Prime Minister Justin Trudeau stated he was “disappointed,” while Alberta Premier Rachel Notley expressed dismay at “the way the U.S. government chose to characterize our energy exports,” questioning the logic as the U.S. continued to import millions of barrels of Albertan crude through other means.

The Obama administration’s opposition to the project was a sore point in its relationship with then-Prime Minister Stephen Harper; a former U.S. ambassador later described an “ice age“ between his office and Canada’s cabinet ministers over the dispute.

After the Trump administration gave Keystone XL new life, President Joe Biden made things even worse on his first day in office in 2021. He revoked Donald Trump’s permit after construction had begun and after Alberta’s government had spent nearly $1 billion on the project.

Alberta’s premier at the time, Jason Kenney, called it a “gut punch” to U.S.-Canada trade relations. While President Trump is rightly criticized for his harm to the U.S.-Canada relationship, the Keystone decisions were also a symbolic turn signaling that the U.S. could not be relied upon as a close trading partner.

The discussion surrounding nuclear has also exhibited this pathology. With support from the Cuomo administration, New York closed the Indian Point nuclear plant, which had supplied around one-quarter of New York City’s electricity, after a years-long campaign by environmental groups. But maintaining the supply needed to meet the state’s electricity demand required replacing that production.

Much of it was replaced by power plants burning natural gas, and the state’s power-sector emissions rose. Convinced, wrongly, that nuclear power was an environmental menace, green activists spent decades closing and blocking plants, seemingly indifferent to the fact that the replacement for this power was mostly natural gas, which increased emissions.

Another example came in January 2024, when the Biden administration decided to halt approvals for new liquefied natural gas (LNG) export terminals, following a pressure campaign by climate activists. This was less than two years after American LNG had helped Europe survive the loss of Russian gas after Russia invaded Ukraine, which allies noticed. Public officials and stakeholders from Europe through Japan, who had come to expect reliable U.S. supply, publicly raised concerns.

The pause had no discernible near-term benefits for the climate. At the time, LNG export capacity was already being restricted, not by the government, but by the market. Even though the government had already issued permits to increase the global LNG market by 40%, those facilities were not being built because of high costs and an uncertain market environment.

The pause did not tangibly restrict construction of facilities, but it did communicate to importers that the availability of American LNG was contingent on the narrow (and weak) considerations of sustainability. A district court stayed this “pause.” Trump ordered it lifted on his first day in office.

Sometimes, this prioritization reflects institutional design.

The Senate committees responsible for writing BAAJA — which also have oversight and jurisdiction over the laws it amends — are the Energy and Natural Resources committee and the Environment and Public Works committee.

Furthermore, the staffers responsible for preparing memos and briefing members on this bill will often be environmental or energy (or sometimes public lands) staffers, and the Democratic bench here is often populated by the former staffers at myriad environmental and climate organizations that advocate exclusively along these dimensions.

The people have voted, and they care about the cost of energy

Cost-of-living concerns — whether the price of gasoline and diesel or inflation attributed to surging data center construction — dominated voter priorities going into this election. For years now, as inflation has surged, Americans have been begging for relief, first under the Biden administration and now under President Trump. Trump, for his part, has recklessly pursued a cost-raising foreign policy without any Congressional authorization.

It’s telling, then, that some of the loudest opponents are not asking how BAAJA will affect families’ budgets.

Here’s what we know about how the bill will impact affordability: Independent analyses show the economic effects would be positive — the Rhodium Group estimated a 3% to 5% reduction in electricity prices by 2040. And our organization, Employ America, estimated that the bill would create over 100,000 industrial jobs over the next 10 to 15 years.

The bill would immediately deliver on the messages of candidates who supported an “all-of-the-above” energy policy — from James Talarico in Texas to (likely winner) Mary Peltola in Alaska to Adam Hamilton in Kansas to Annie Andrews in South Carolina. Shrugging off a substantive bill that takes the approach voters validated would be an own-goal.

And yet, Rep. Jared Huffman, D-Calif., incoming chair of the House Natural Resources Committee, wants to use Democrats’ midterm leverage to extract concessions and negotiate for a better bill — one that would be more restrictive for oil and gas.

Even setting aside the fact that Democrats have no evidence that President Trump is willing to sign an alternative permitting bill from them, this strategy completely ignores what voters have asked their representatives to make their top priority. Voters want a holistic approach to energy policy, one that ensures the energy system is affordable and reliable. BAAJA doesn’t need to be rewritten to accomplish this.

For too long, Democratic energy policy has been seen exclusively through the lens of climate or environmental policy. But energy policy has always been integral to multiple issues. And losing sight of the trilemma’s balance has produced consequential mistakes, sometimes even at the cost of the very climate goals those decisions were supposed to advance.

Addressing affordability means making things affordable

The affordability crisis has made the imbalance between cost, reliability, and climate concerns untenable. Electricity prices in many states have spiked; the Energy Information Administration estimates wholesale electricity prices rising by 11% in 2026, and we are experiencing another sharp increase in gasoline and diesel prices driven by the Iran war.

This isn’t just about your utility bill. Energy is a key input for almost all sectors of our economy, from manufacturing to agriculture to food services.

Too often, the left takes for granted that clean energy is the most affordable. It’s true that the cost reductions in wind and solar, coupled with accelerations in battery technology, have been significant, but this relative cost-effectiveness is conditional on other factors.

The value of solar and wind depends highly on geography and on the costs of transmission and other supporting grid assets — the very assets that BAAJA is designed to make cheaper and easier to build.

We know that failing to include affordability as a key consideration in energy policy — or even just appearing to not consider it — comes with political consequences.

The Biden administration, for instance, was clearly unwilling to vocally embrace oil and gas production, and it made several attempts to block fossil fuel production on federal lands. Even where pragmatism prevailed, climate-centricity and overt hostility to oil and gas were the rhetorical norm, often putting the administration’s messaging at odds with the energy expenses that voters were more directly concerned with.

BAAJA is an opportunity to demonstrate a shift in how the Democratic Party approaches energy policy — away from climate-exclusivity, toward an appropriate balancing of affordability, sustainability, and reliability.

BAAJA is more than just good energy policy

While BAAJA is primarily concerned with promoting a more balanced energy policy, it also has positive knock-on effects even outside the energy domain. Its reforms to the National Environmental Policy Act would limit the “litigation doom loop” that puts public-sector investment at a disadvantage with the private sector.

The bill would also exempt federal financial assistance below a set threshold from triggering review — meaning future industrial-policy efforts in the mold of the CHIPS and Science Act could proceed without a NEPA fight. It would require data centers to pay for the transmission their load requires rather than pushing those costs onto ratepayers, one of the most direct affordability provisions in the bill.

The bill could fundamentally improve public transit policy by “relaxing the procedures that make it harder to repair, rebuild, and modernize public transit.” Yet the defensive crouch of certain climate advocates has left these benefits underexamined.

Despite these wins, BAAJA’s champions on the center-left can sometimes seem too focused on proving that their bill is good for the climate, themselves underrating the benefits to other major priorities. There are simply more organizations eager to weigh in on climate advocacy among the center-left relative to energy policy overall, which elevates the emissions-oriented argument for BAAJA.

These arguments may be largely valid, but they also reinforce the idea that emissions reductions should be the central measure of the bill’s worth.

Fortunately, one of the bill’s sponsors, Sen. Martin Heinrich, D-N.M., demonstrated the right approach in a recent home-state op-ed emphasizing the economic benefits from the bill:

“If we have any hope of cutting energy costs and creating more good-paying jobs to help hardworking New Mexicans support their families, then we need to be able to build big clean energy projects in a matter of years — not decades.”

This is the strongest case for the bill: It would make it substantially easier to build more of the reliable energy infrastructure that serves as a platform for broad-based economic development. Democrats, who will soon control both houses in Congress, can take this opportunity to demonstrate their focus on improving Americans’ lives.

Take the affordability and economic policy win.
