There's No Way Every Woman You Date Has BPD

When therapy culture comes for the "crazy ex-girlfriend" archetype

Cartoons Hate Her
Jul 8
∙
Paid
 
READ IN APP
 

Earlier this year, I wrote There’s No Way Every Man is Avoidant Attached, about the fact that men who are simply not-that-interested in a particular woman are recast as “avoidant attached” so that the woman in question can maintain (at least for a while) a relationship that probably should have ended, or absolve herself of responsibility after the fact. At the time, I wondered if straight men did anything like this, but straight men don’t have a habit of posting about attachment theory. So I shrugged it off as something weird that only women do.

After all, “fake” or exaggerated and speculative diagnoses is a tendency that, at least if you go by the stereotype, is more of a woman thing. When people post about parents with an insufferable desire to be “special” getting their high-functioning kids diagnosed with autism (note that I do not agree with this descriptor) they are usually referring to the mothers, not the fathers, even if in reality, both parents of mildly autistic children are often involved in the diagnosis. When people refer to adults who make a self-diagnosis “their entire personality,” they are typically referring to women with ADHD or mild autism, not men. The idea is that women crave attention and have a stronger desire to be a special main character than men. And perhaps, inundated with this stereotype, I didn’t stop to think about the speculative diagnosis that men are handing out like candy—borderline personality disorder.

About as frequently as women claim to have dated an “avoidant attached” man, men will claim to have dated a “BPD” woman. And, similar to how women discuss tips for avoiding the avoidant-attached, men warn each other to steer clear of women who appear that they might have BPD.

There’s just one problem—a lot of these women don’t have BPD.

When men discuss “BPD women” online, they are often warning each other to stay away from such women, who are stereotyped as being chaotic (if not also sexy) but ultimately too crazy to date. They are, essentially, the “danger zone” part of the infamous “Crazy Hot matrix” which, suspiciously, asserts that half the female population is a “no-go” based on physical appearance alone, and that the only women worth marrying are an 8/10 or higher.

Then there are other men who seek out BPD women (or women they believe to have BPD) for this reason. Pickup artists have given advice for how to better manipulate BPD women with “dread game” given that they are more susceptible to fears of abandonment. Another guy detailed his experience with a “BPD woman,” marked mostly by the fact that she was hot and wanted sex a lot, but was “crazy,” without any explanation of what the craziness entailed.

However, according to a 2024 scientific comprehensive review of the disorder, the upper estimate for the prevalence for BPD is about 2.7%. You might have heard that those diagnosed with. BPD are overwhelmingly women, and it’s true that within inpatient settings, BPD skews female (72% vs. 28%.) But as it turns out, this is an artifact of who is most likely to seek inpatient treatment. In the general population done via a community sample, the rates of BPD were very similar between men and women—3% for women and 2.4% for men. In a tweet that felt nearly self-aware, one guy proclaimed that despite being only 2% of the population, somehow BPD women made up a third of his exes.

This is almost identical to the issue with “avoidant attachment disorder.” When attachment styles were evaluated in a sample of people, avoidants leaned slightly male, but not as significantly as the Reddit posts would have you believe—and true attachment disorders are relatively uncommon.

Granted, if 2% of the population has a disease, and you date 20 women over a ten-year period, there is about a 33% chance that at least one of these women will have BPD for real. However, I suppose I’m less credulous because these men seem to be assigning completely unrelated characteristics to the supposed BPD diagnoses. For example, without a doubt, their “BPD ex” was always hot and really good in bed. Perhaps being hot allows women to get away with having a difficult personality, BPD or not, or perhaps some men have simply coded “hot and difficult” with “BPD.” BPD stops being a mental illness and becomes a shorthand descriptor for “my hot ex-girlfriend was crazy.” Statistically, their “crazy” ex-girlfriends—provided anything was medically wrong with them in the first place—were more likely to have just had anxiety or depression. And isn’t it curious that bipolar disorder—about as common as BPD in the general population—is not as commonly cited in dating stories?

But BPD is incredibly easy to self-diagnose because the symptoms are incredibly vague to a layman. Most people will not diagnose themselves with BPD, because it’s not a disease anyone “wants” to have, but they will jump to diagnose the difficult women in their lives—often ex-girlfriends, but occasionally mothers, mothers-in-law, and other nefarious and troublesome shrews and banshees.

Some examples of BPD symptoms include impulsive behavior, difficulty controlling anger, and intense but unstable relationships. While I’m sure these things are accurate, the Barnum effect is clearly at play here. The Barnum effect is a psychological phenomenon that describes people’s eagerness to believe that vague descriptions, from a psychic or generic personality test eerily describe themselves or someone else. While the Barnum effect enables people to self-diagnose as autistic because they have hobbies and interests, it also enables people to diagnose others around them. Go to Reddit for five minutes and see how many people claim to have a family full of clinical narcissists, and then to top it off, their in-laws, ex-roommate and ex-boyfriends are also, miraculously, narcissists. And narcissistic personality disorder is—you guessed it—not that common. People with NPD make up just 1-2% of the population.

Almost anyone, when looking back on an ex-relationship, will describe aspects of it as “intense” but ultimately unstable (because, well, it ended.) And if they had a big fight during this relationship, or multiple fights, they can easily diagnose their ex as exhibiting “difficulty controlling anger.” Boom-you have a “BPD ex-girlfriend.” Doesn’t matter what triggered the anger, doesn’t matter what the fight was about. The fact that the fight happened and the fact that she got angry during it means she had BPD, is irredeemable, and the entire relationship was a mistake based entirely on her being an unlovable and untouchable person. A woman who was the victim of infidelity—if she acted as most people would in response—could easily be recast as “BPD.”

The idea that people with BPD are fundamentally broken is all over the Internet. A Reddit post urges everyone to steer clear of anyone with BPD, including, I assume, BPD that you merely suspect. While I’m sure there are some people out there whose problems are currently too severe for them to be in a functioning relationship, people with BPD can be treated, especially if trauma is the underlying cause. But the way people talk about them online, you’d think that women with BPD were shapeshifting selkies, malevolently waiting to lure unsuspecting sailors to their doom.

But similarly to how it’s easy to tell yourself that your mother-in-law is a flaming narcissist and therefore, every argument you’ve ever had has been settled retroactively in your favor, diagnosing an ex-girlfriend with BPD makes the entire relationship easier to dismiss. If your ex-girlfriend had BPD, she wasn’t just crazy but irredeemably crazy in a way that women with anxiety or depression apparently aren’t. Therefore, you did nothing wrong. No matter what she was angry about, no matter what she “went crazy” over, it was all just her having BPD and you were blameless. The only lesson learned from such a relationship is, essentially, “bitches be crazy.”

When I was younger and around lots of single people, I noticed that every guy claimed to have a “crazy” ex-girlfriend, and often, men would declare that “all my ex-girlfriends were crazy.” One of my own ex-boyfriends regaled me with stories of his “crazy” ex-girlfriend, and I’m confident that he later went on to describe me that way. The BPD hysteria is merely a new version of that, updated to 2020s therapy culture. And even before men were assigning the BPD label to these women, I always had my doubts that the ex-girlfriends were really all that crazy. Who amongst us has not been driven to madness in a particularly bad situation or relationship? The same way a man is not necessarily “avoidant” because he doesn’t want to talk about marriage after just a few dates, a woman is not necessarily “crazy” because she got worried and upset when you returned home drunk at 3 AM after ignoring her texts all night. Nearly every person can be driven crazy under certain circumstances, and if you are driving every woman crazy (or, for that matter, driving all men away into “avoidant” behavior) perhaps there is a lowest common denominator at play.
