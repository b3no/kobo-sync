What Colonial Williamsburg can teach us about the trades revival

Everyone is vulnerable except for the mantua-maker.

Halina Bennet
Jun 5
∙
Paid
 
READ IN APP
 

On a recent Sunday afternoon in Colonial Williamsburg, a journeyman wigmaker held up two locks of hair — one horse, one human — to explain why 18th-century barbers and their clients preferred one over the other. As she worked the hair through a hackle and into a silk net, she described the trade network that allowed colonial wigmakers to import the nets from London. But, she said, when boycotts on British goods began, it was fortunate that the shop’s apprentices already knew how to weave the cauls themselves.

Down the road, an apprentice joiner shaved down a piece of oak destined for a cabinet, the late-May breeze carrying through the open window and ruffling her skirts, which had been made by the tailor next door. It was all quite romantic.

And it made me think about today’s trades revival — not because I was thinking about the actual tradespeople who lived and worked in Revolutionary America, but because I was thinking about the tradespeople who work in Colonial Williamsburg today and their unusual insulation from both market vulnerability and the A.I. anxiety that has come to animate so much of the current conversation about work.

The trades revival of 2026 is real — it is responding to real conditions. But it’s missing the most important lesson that Colonial Williamsburg, of all places, has to teach.

The case for trades is rational

Trade school enrollment is up sharply: According to the National Student Clearinghouse Research Center, undergraduate certificate programs have grown 28.3 percent since 2021. Those figures are almost entirely driven by community colleges focused on vocational training, which saw enrollment surge 11.7 percent in spring 2025 alone. Meanwhile, four-year bachelor’s programs grew just 0.9 percent in the same period. Nicknamed the “toolbelt generation” by the Wall Street Journal, Gen Z has increasingly turned toward trades as the cost of a bachelor’s degree has risen and concerns about A.I.’s impact on white-collar work have grown.

Those concerns are grounded. The World Economic Forum’s Future of Jobs 2025 report claims that 40 percent of employers plan workforce reductions attributable to A.I. within five years, with the most at-risk jobs concentrated at the entry level in positions such as basic legal research, routine coding, and content moderation. Geoffrey Hinton, the Nobel Prize-winning computer scientist who helped build the foundations of modern A.I., said in a 2025 podcast interview that physical manipulation would remain hard to automate for a long time.

“A good bet,” he offered, “would be to be a plumber.”

Wages reinforce the signal. According to U.S. Department of Education estimates, for every five workers who retire from construction, manufacturing, and skilled trades sectors, only two replacements enter the workforce. The shortage is devastating; the pay has improved accordingly, and the credential-to-income ratio compares favorably with many four-year degrees. The trades revival is a rational response to very real conditions.

Rational isn’t the same as stable

The case for trades as an A.I. refuge rests on the observation that trade work is physical, variable, contextual, and hard to standardize. All of this makes blue-collar work difficult for current A.I. systems to replicate.

And that argument is accurate. But a job that is hard to automate is not necessarily a job that is economically secure.

Trade workers remain subject to economic cycles, to the cost of tools and licensing, to the same structural precarity that has always made working-class labor vulnerable. The middle-class families who are newly nervous about their children’s prospects in a disrupted white-collar economy are, in many cases, the same families whose guidance counselors and parenting choices led to credential inflation that systematically devalued the trades through the 2000s on the theory that a four-year degree was the universal ticket to stability. Now that the professional class feels vulnerable, the trades have acquired a new rhetorical respectability.

The strongest version of the A.I.-resistant argument, in any case, doesn’t point toward the plumber. It points toward Colonial Williamsburg.

The wigmaker has real job security. The plumber has a bet

Colonial Williamsburg’s Historic Trades and Skills program employs nearly 100 masters, journeymen, apprentices, and interpreters who use historical research and archaeological study to practice nearly two dozen 18th-century trades. Some of these craftspeople are the world’s sole practitioners of their techniques. The Colonial Williamsburg Foundation’s own description notes that some trades “survive only because of the foundation’s long-term dedication to preserving ephemeral human skills.”

Though they practice skills not unrecognizable today, the wigmaker and the blacksmith and the joiner at Colonial Williamsburg have something the contemporary plumber does not: their employment is insulated from market forces by institutional decision. They are not competing for customers, under pressure to optimize their work, or exposed to economic cycles or automation risk. They are subsidized by a nonprofit foundation and by the $35.76 I spent on my admission ticket. Their labor is preserved fundamentally because someone decided that it mattered, even though it lacks much instrumental utility.

This model makes for, by some distance, the most economically secure position a skilled tradesperson can occupy in America in 2026. It has nothing to do with the market logic driving the trades revival. It is the result of a deliberate, costly, sustained institutional commitment, begun by John D. Rockefeller Jr. in the 1930s as a deliberate act of deciding what the American past should look like, undertaken during a period of national pain.

Ironically enough, the tradespeople at Colonial Williamsburg are not, for the most part, workers who skipped college. They are scholars approaching craft as an intellectual project, treating trade knowledge as worthy of scholarly attention. The apprentice brickmaker has an anthropology degree from James Madison University, the cooper has a master’s in history, the tailor is an adjunct professor at William & Mary. They are preserving the same skills that a construction worker or an HVAC technician deploys every day, but only the former are treated as doing something intellectually rigorous because of the historical scholarship baked into the work.

Colonial Williamsburg doesn’t resolve that contradiction by elevating the trades. Rather, it exposes the contradiction by revealing that our culture only grants trade knowledge full legitimacy when it’s been laundered through an institution and a credential.

What the Industrial Revolution did

The tradespeople at Colonial Williamsburg I spoke with emphasized that the Industrial Revolution didn’t just mechanize production. It fractured a centuries-old system of knowledge transmission within a single generation as factories replaced workshops and standardized processes replaced individual craft judgement. Much of what Colonial Williamsburg preserves was lost not to time, but to a sudden economic transition.

The Historic Trades program explicitly “sustains the method for transferring skill and knowledge, passed down only through practice, from aging generations to new ones by way of the master-and-apprentice relationship.” That is serious institutional work that costs serious money, requires decisions about the things worth preserving, and depends on a commitment divorced almost entirely from market incentives. (Though, it is obviously the case that funding for most organizations — especially for museums and cultural projects — is varied and unstable.)

So what happens when the next disruption comes? For tradespeople, that disruption is unlikely to be an immediate, wholesale replacement. But A.I. and robotics are transforming the specific techniques that define skilled work in 2026 in the same way that industrialization transformed the specific techniques of 1776. The HVAC technician who knows how to read a pressure chart will one day be like the Colonial Williamsburg joiner: a custodian of knowledge that the market no longer requires but that future generations might wish had been preserved.

The contemporary trades are already being transformed by software that would be unrecognizable to a tradesperson from 1990. Getting more teenagers into construction solves a shortage and provides a pathway to make a living. This is good. But today’s version of those jobs will disappear sooner than we expect — and unless someone decides that particular knowledge is worth protecting, so will the skilled workers who hold it.

When the market moves on

The trades revival of 2026 is driven by labor markets, A.I. anxiety, and the recognition that overindexing on college was a class choice presented as meritocracy. Those forces are pushing us to immediately make decisions about how we fund vocational training, how we structure apprenticeships, and how we talk to high school students about their options.

But a market bet puts the burden on individual workers to correctly anticipate which skills will be valued next, make the necessary investments, and absorb the cost if they lose their bet. In the colonies, skilled tradespeople commanded premium wages precisely because labor was scarce. When industrialization arrived, those wages and those skills collapsed within a lifetime.

The wigmaker has job security because an institution decided, as a matter of principle, that her skill should persist regardless of market conditions.

As the United States marks its 250th anniversary, Colonial Williamsburg sits at the center of the commemoration. This is fitting, since it is both a place where the argument for American independence was refined and a reminder that national memory is always a construction. The decision to preserve colonial craft, to treat the cooper and the blacksmith and the mantua-maker as worthy of scholarly attention and institutional investment, was not inevitable. It reflected a judgement that this knowledge mattered and was worth preserving, prior to any question of whether it was useful.

Maybe future generations will decide that very little trade knowledge from this century is worth preserving. Regardless, the march of time and technology goes on, and, with the exception of Colonial Williamsburg tradespeople, no one is exempt.
