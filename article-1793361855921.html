Yes, I Have Cankles

Thought you knew of every body insecurity? How about "tree trunk ankles?"

Cartoons Hate Her
Oct 30
∙

READ IN APP
 

I’m no stranger to the world of bizarre and specific insecurities that can be foisted upon women. I’m fully aware that our boobs can be too small, too big, too saggy, too asymmetrical (I have yet to see a woman whose natural boobs are “too perky” but I’m sure if I peruse Reddit, there’s someone humblebragging about it.) And as the insecurities became more and more obscure, propagated by social media algorithms—everything from the lack of a thigh gap to the presence of hip dips—I present to you, the thing that is probably the most objectively wrong with my body: my cankles.

If you are blessedly unfamiliar with this concept, some people just happen to have shapeless ankles which blend into their calves. The Daily Mail is fully aware of this epidemic, as they declare: Even being beautiful, thin and rich won’t save you from ankles that merge into your calves. Apparently I am in good company with Lady Victoria Harvey, Helen Mirren, and Donatella Versace. (They also added a nine-month-pregnant Kim Kardashian in there, but I regret to inform Kim K that she is doing cankle stolen valor—everyone has cankles at nine months pregnant. Only some of us are blessed with them full time.)

I inherited my cankles from my mom, although it’s possible some becankled woman on my dad’s side of the family passed them down too, leaving me with turbocankles. My maternal grandfather had gone to art school and enjoyed drawing the human form in all its genders, sizes and poses. He had a habit of remarking, unprompted, on the “physiques” of both men and women in a purely neutral, non-sexual way (which I’m sure was not perceived that way, especially when it was directed towards strangers, but I digress.) And when it came to my mom’s legs—which would eventually be my own legs—he had one singular diagnosis: they “fell apart after the knees.”

In my case, there is literally nothing I can do about it. My cankles did not appear with age—they were present even when I was a gangly teenager. In fact, back then, they were even more evident because I was so skinny otherwise. At their thinnest, they looked like Flats the Flounder from Spongebob, where they could pass for narrow if I was facing forward, but if I turned to the side:

I promise you that I am not imagining this because I’m a neurotic woman who hates her body. I don’t hate my body at all. But my cankles are simply not a matter of opinion, nor am I the only person to have noticed them. I cannot fit into any socks from Aritzia or Skims, both of which leave painful, itchy red bands around my ankles, so I guarantee I am not imagining this.

I first noticed my cankles when I was in a theater camp production of HMS Pinafore when I was about thirteen. We had to wear these low-heeled Mary Jane style dance shoes for the performance, and one day I was fooling around with my black and white Kodak camera and decided to take a retro-looking artsy photo of my feet in the dance shoes. When I got the photos developed with my mom, I was aghast at what I saw. The “retro-looking” feet looked as though they belonged to a fifty-five-year-old British choir teacher, not a teenage girl.

My mom explained to me that we shared the same body type and that there was nothing I could do to change my ankles. I could lose weight—not that I should—and they’d still be there. She pointed out that ankles like ours aren’t uncommon among pear-shaped bodies who gain all their weight below the waist, and I should simply learn to dress for them instead of worrying about changing their existence.

And dress for them I did. I quickly realized a few things were out of the question. For one, wide-leg cropped pants, or anything in the culotte milieu. I don’t know who decided these were flattering, but while they look kind of shitty on everyone, they look especially bad on People Experiencing Cankledom. Occasionally, of course, I still dip my enormous ankles into unflattering silhouettes when I visualize my body differently in my head.

Eventually, I realized that low-heel or flat shoes in general were problematic, but mostly if paired with mid-length skirts and dresses. Flats with any kind of lace-up apparatus were especially ugly on my cankles, giving off the impression of sausages wrapped in wire. I did buy one pair of flats like these, and they were extremely cute on the ninety-pound, skinny-ankled lady in the advertisement but just as awkward on me as I suspected they’d be. Still, I can wear them with full-length, straight-leg jeans.

Modern-day fashion is, frankly, antithetical to the accommodation of cankles. I loved the late 2010s trend of chunky “dad sneakers” or the early 2010s trend of platform high heels, because my cankles look better paired with blocky, hefty elephant hoof shoes. You know what looks terrible with them? Delicate kitten heels. They actually look worse than flats. My ankles begin to look like walruses balancing on butter knives.

Other bad shoes for cankles: flat Oxfords, any flat shoe paired with a midi skirt and socks aaaaahhh…it’s so bad. This article (also the source for the below image) is full of examples of svelte and non-cankled women wearing loafers with socks and skirts, which is a cankled woman’s worst nightmare.

You may still be tempted to think I’m being too hard on myself, that this is something only I notice, and if I want to wear stuff that doesn’t flatter my cankles, nobody else will ever be able to tell. But you’d be wrong! When I don’t dress to conceal my cankles (and sometimes, I don’t, because certain outfits are just too fun to pass up) the denizens of Twitter are not afraid to share this feedback!

This person was not the only one to mention them. I have also been informed by some random guy on Twitter that he’s in a group chat with upwards of ten women who repeatedly share photos of my cankles. So there’s that. Nothing like the Internet to assure you that, actually, it’s not all in your head.

But anyway, now that I’ve hopefully convinced you all that my cankles are real and not some imagined flaw from the mind of a madwoman, I should cover what can look good with cankles, or at least…fine. Maxi dresses are great. Short skirts can be fine, but ideally if the ankles are covered via some kind of boot. Flare-leg jeans are obviously a winner. I also happen to have short legs (not uncommon among the CankleX community) so a wide-leg or flared high waisted pant is one of the most flattering things I can wear.

Although I put a decent amount of effort into dressing for my cankles, I have to admit they don’t bother me that much. If a fairy came to me and told me that with one wish, I could change anything appearance-related about my body, with no plastic surgery, no risks, and no money spent, I still would not choose my cankles.

So why don’t my cankles bother me that much? Even from a young age, perhaps correctly, I surmised that “her ankles are too wide,” was the kind of critique that would only ever be leveled by people whose only goal was to be rude. I did not anticipate that I would ever lose dating opportunities over it, and I don’t think I have. Honestly, the same could probably be said for many “body flaws” which would really only be a problem if you were trying to get a modeling job. Most of the body flaws that are completely outside our control are, thankfully, rarely life-altering.

My cankles did not affect dating, but that’s not because men don’t notice. I’m positive men have noticed because I know for a fact that my husband noticed. Right around the time we first started dating, he surprised me by showing up at my dorm room. Not only was I probably unshowered and wearing no makeup in the midst of an acne breakout, but I was wearing a pair of slightly cropped, low rise, dark purple terrycloth sweatpants which put my cankles on full display. It was as if I was dressing for someone with a cankle fetish. I knew how unflattering these pants were, and never planned to wear them outside my dorm room. I did not, in a million years, expect that the extremely hot guy I had a crush on would randomly show up there and see my ankles in their worst-ever state. Before that moment, he had only seen me in flare leg jeans. As I opened the door, I thought Okay, hopefully he is not looking at my cankles.

But he was! He didn’t say anything then, because of course not, but once we became serious and I jokingly mentioned my cankles, making it clear they were not a real insecurity, he immediately re-told the story of how he showed up at my dorm room to discover my cankles for the first time, and specifically cited the horrendous terrycloth pants, ordering me to “please dispose of them” as soon as I could. On another occasion, he warned me against wearing a particularly slim pair of sneakers because it looked like they were about to be “devoured by my calves.” And they say romance is dead! (Don’t worry, none of this actually offends me. In fact, I also tease him for his chicken legs—that’s right, I married a guy with genetically tiny ankles. I can only hope our kids inherit normal ankles.)

But overall, I am happy to be at peace with my cankles. I reached the “acceptance” phase long ago, and I could not possibly imagine shelling out the money for some kind of bizarre ankle-reduction surgery, which I’m sure exists somewhere in South America or Turkey or South Korea. I have discovered a myriad of ways to flatter or at least reduce their appearance—but am not so intent on hiding them that I shy away from a cool pair of capri pants (these ones are vintage ‘90s) with flats (Bobbies, in case you’re wondering.) While dressing for your body type is a worthwhile endeavor and one I continue to do, I refuse to let my freaky ankles prevent me from wearing something I really love—even if I run the risk of being photographed and ridiculed by The Daily Mail.
