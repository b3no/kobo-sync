Life of the Party

In Xita Rubert’s unsettling novel, “The Key Biscayne Affair,” a daughter struggles to disentangle love from neglect.

Ron Charles
Nov 10

The Tuesday Review

It’s a shock to see a penis in the opening line of The Key Biscayne Affair. The narrator, who was 12 at the time, isn’t being evasive. Looking back as an adult, she confesses, “I don’t know what happened in Key Biscayne.”

How did this Spanish girl find herself in that situation on an island off the coast of Miami?

Of course, she could look at old photographs and talk to family members, but she doesn’t really want to remember what happened. “What I want,” she tells us, “is to change it.”

Such is the prologue to Xita Rubert’s new novel.

When it appeared in Spain, The Key Biscayne Affair attracted a wave of critical attention, which is hard to fathom in our comparatively parched book-review ecosystem. And it won the 2024 Herralde Prize, previously given to such international luminaries as Roberto Bolaño, Javier Marías, and Mariana Enríquez. It arrives in the United States today in a lithe translation by Julia Sanches.

This is a strange, slim novel that forces humor, tenderness, and dread to sit shoulder to shoulder at the same table in uncomfortable camaraderie. The narrator, who is never named, describes events that took place over several months when she and her brother lived with their 71-year-old father, a professor of philosophy once as renowned for his scholarship as for his charisma.

We meet him at the Boston airport in December 2009 when he and the children appear to be fleeing to Miami. “We forgot our suitcases,” he explains to the skeptical ticket agent, “which left us no choice but to put everything in trash bags.” It’s a laughable explanation, of course, but entirely characteristic of their father, a Don Quixote figure who’d started a yearlong appointment at Harvard University just a few months earlier.

“Life with him was like this,” the narrator remembers, “his goals always front and center, however absurd or laudable, with no regard for the rules that govern mere mortals.” Why stay the whole year in Cambridge? It’s snowing, for goodness’ sake! “In Miami it’ll be less cold and we’ll have more fun,” he assures the children.

He’s working on a paper titled “In Praise of Incompetence.”

What actually happened to their father’s contract with Harvard? How was he able to secure another teaching post at the University of Miami? And how can he so blithely disregard the custody agreement with his ex-wife, who agreed he could take the children to Boston for one year to learn English?

We will never learn the answers to these questions and many others. We’ll remain, for the duration of this novel, in the walled garden of our narrator’s 12-year-old vision of her father during that year when their intense relationship “started to fray.”

“My self-assuredness was a mirror of his, and I slept in that illusion, swallowed that illusion,” she says. “In a way, the reason I’m summoning the memory of Key Biscayne now is that part of me longs for the lie. For faith. What we believe protects us. What we know sets us adrift.”

That seductive defense of self-deception isn’t the last time this novel will make you squirm.

Rubert spent a few months in Key Biscayne when she was about 12, and her father was the Spanish philosopher Xavier Rubert de Ventós, who died less than two years before The Key Biscayne Affair was published. But she insists this is a work of fiction, and I’m not sure much is gained by doubting her. Rather than illuminating her life, the novel offers us insight into the unwieldy play of memory, love, and personal history. The scenes she presents are like puzzle pieces of our own lives, glued together where they don’t quite fit, forming a jagged mosaic that can only approximate how things might have looked.

There’s an Auntie Mame quality to this father that’s irresistible. He makes the world bend to his will. “He had an artist’s temperament,” the narrator says, “and there isn’t much difference between an artist and an emperor.” On Key Biscayne, he’s feted as a celebrity; parties are thrown in his honor; guests hang on his outrageous stories. The local consul lends him his Cadillac!

And what an improbable island this is — “at once glitzy and rundown.” The narrator describes it as looking “like a crime scene frozen in time.” No one lives here, she claims, “unless they have something to hide.” Her father’s friends — are they friends? — seem like they might be other academics, political attachés, or tawdry gangsters from some worn-out Gatsby reenactment.

The narrator remembers feeling both dazzled by and protective of her father, even as she sensed the dimensions of his outrageous neglect and irresponsibility. “If Key Biscayne was lawless,” she writes, “then our apartment was the seat of anarchy.” Together she and her father conspire to ignore those increasingly panicked messages from her mother.

But peril wafts into this zany world like the smell of burnt hair. The narrator’s classmates — “the army of mannequin girls,” some already boasting breast implants — slither through an eroticized world of flirtation and pornography. There’s no excuse for her father giving her unprescribed sleeping pills. And any adolescent blacking out this often should see a doctor.

Our minds, naturally, scuttle to the worst, grossest offense. A shelf of trauma novels has primed us to expect some dredged-up horror that will neatly explain everything. But Rubert denies us that grim satisfaction. She’s interested in a subtler kind of abuse. “My father was obsessed with everything he gave his children, but I don’t know if he ever thought of all that he slowly took from us.”

The Key Biscayne Affair is both a love letter to a wildly charismatic father and an account of the damage he inflicts. How did you find a way to hold those two truths together?

Being cast as her needy father’s prime audience is both a thrill and a burden. “I was stranded in childhood,” she writes, “stuffed into my father’s pocket.” How delicately she describes the process of emerging from that pouch, trailing strings of affection for the man she loved so much.

In the best possible way, this is a deeply unsatisfying tale, purposely “mis-narrated,” as the narrator confesses, “to distance us from the story it really was.” I’m left feeling impressed but troubled, almost ashamed of enjoying its charmingly batty scenes, its witty asides. Rubert’s deft narrative, so much more highly crafted than it claims, frustrates our craving for boundaries, for cutting off toxic parents, for knowing, finally, what happened.

“Parts of our relationship were a lie,” she writes, “and parts of it were true.” Parts of it were carnival and parts were carnal.

The best thing she can say she got from her father is a sense of how to write fiction.◆

The Key Biscayne Affair, by Xita Rubert. Translated from Spanish by Julia Sanches (Ecco, 208 pp., $28)
