A radical idea for breaking the cycle of public anger

What if we actually focused on the cost of living?

Matthew Yglesias
Apr 28
∙
Paid
 
READ IN APP
 

“Just wait for the opposition to screw up enough” is not the world’s greatest political strategy, but it’s often a good enough political strategy.

Last week, Fox News released a poll showing Democrats are more trusted on the economy for the first time since 2010. I’m on the record as saying that Hakeem Jeffries’s skills as an opposition leader are widely underrated, but this is pretty clearly not because Democrats pulled off some incredible transformation of their public image.

What happened is that Donald Trump has made a hash of things.

He won office largely because of mass public outrage over high prices. He explicitly promised to make prices go down, setting himself up for trouble since this is not an achievable promise.

But he hasn’t even delivered on the pretty banal goal of “make the inflation rate go back down to the 2 percent target level.” Mortgage interest rates drifted downward very slightly across the course of 2025, but didn’t come close to returning to their pre-2022 level. Then they went back up again. And Trump has taken two easily avoidable high-profile actions that have a marked tendency to push prices up: tariffs and this war with Iran.

This is leaving Democrats in decent shape for 2026 and with high hopes for 2028. But it also, of course, raises the question of how they’ll respond to public anger about prices if they get a chance to govern.

So I want to propose an idea here that’s so radical nobody seems to be considering it. Something that if he’s smart, Trump will consider before it’s too late. Something the Biden administration never did. And if Trump doesn’t do it, then whichever Democrat wins in 2028 should.

My idea: Actually try to bring inflation and interest rates down through very banal orthodox technocratic economic policy.

A riddle and something boring

I believe that clear thinking about this slightly boring issue has been hampered by the emergence of a more interesting but fundamentally less relevant question: Why are Americans so miserable?

Because while the United States of America in 2026 is definitely a country with economic problems, the expressed views of the American people are much darker than seems justified by the statistics.

And the question lends itself to two kinds of answers. One is the answer that Brian Beutler and I basically converged on in last week’s Politix, namely that this is mostly a mediagenic phenomenon. People are bombarded with more negativity now. It’s making things feel worse than they are, and this is a reality politicians need to deal with in their communication strategies.

The other is the answer that a lot of economic policy shops seem to be going with, namely that because public expressions of discontent are out of line with conventional statistics, we need bespoke statistics and innovative new solutions to a purported “affordability crisis.”

But I think it’s worth actually looking squarely at the problems that do exist in the conventional statistics.

Because while inflation is not super-high, it absolutely is running above the target level and has been for multiple years. What’s more, interest rates — which are not considered part of inflation for statistical purposes — remain quite a bit higher than they were in the recent past.

Neither the genuine inflation overshoot nor the genuine rise in interest rates seem nearly large enough to statistically explain the scale of public discontent. But they are legitimate problems. The fact that people seem irrationally annoyed by the problems doesn’t make them fake. And even if you think the electorate is making a mountain out of a molehill, it’s not the worst idea to do something about the moles.

These are solvable problems

Making interest rates go down is, on one level, easy enough. The Federal Reserve can more or less pull all kinds of interest rates down throughout the economy.

The problem is that with inflation already running a little bit hot, further interest-rate cuts risk further overheating.

It is, however, totally possible to exert downward pressure on inflation and interest rates simultaneously through deficit reduction. Balancing the American budget or addressing the long-term fiscal imbalances in Social Security and Medicare is a challenging problem. But making some progress would be pretty easy if someone wanted to try. On the revenue side, two pieces of low-hanging fruit are 1) reversing Trump’s gutting of Internal Revenue Service tax-enforcement staff and 2) backing away from the creation of gimmicky tax exclusions like “no tax on tips,” which are costly measures that don’t really improve incentives to work, save, or invest at the margin.

On the spending side, Dr. Oz’s team at the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services released a pretty aggressive plan back in January to crack down on scammy upcoding in Medicare Advantage plans. This is far from a complete solution to Medicare issues or even the subset of issues related to Medicare Advantage. But it’s a good idea and a good start.

Unfortunately, Trump backed off the good changes (the Medicare Advantage lobbyists got wind of them, presumably), and is now poised to drive up overpayments by making it easier than ever for Medicare Advantage plans to score high-quality ratings.

The point is not that fiscal issues are easy to deal with.

But there is, in fact, this low-hanging fruit. If you’re tough-minded about it and ask “What tax measures have minimal costs to economic growth?” and “What spending has minimal benefits for the needy?” you can come up with some things that would genuinely make it easier to get low inflation and interest rates.

After that, it’s all about productivity. In an economy with high productivity growth, rising nominal spending leads to more real G.D.P. and less inflation. How do you make productivity growth higher? Well, it’s hard! But you can go down the list and try to get rid of policies like the Jones Act or rules against selling cars directly to consumers.

Theoretically, you actually could get falling nominal prices without a dangerous deflationary spiral purely through productivity improvements. Realistically, that’s unlikely. But an administration that genuinely made this a high priority could certainly make progress.

Give the people what they want

This is all mildly challenging stuff. But if you focused on it, you could make headway. As with anything else in politics, you would generate opposition and negative press and alienate some people. I can understand why the Biden administration didn’t want to pivot to this agenda, and I can understand why the Trump administration doesn’t want to, and I can understand why the next Democratic administration also probably won’t want to.

The actual holdup here isn’t that administrations are fundamentally opposed to spending down political capital in order to pursue important goals — all administrations spend their political capital on something. The issue is that both partisan coalitions are organized around their ideological agendas and insist that political capital be hoarded for those projects.

And that, to some extent, is the whole problem.

Political elites have decided what they think is important, and elected officials have decided to blow off what the public thinks is important in order to pursue those goals.

When the policy community hears that “affordability” and “the cost of living” are key voter concerns, they don’t decide to actually pivot their policy goals in order to address those things. What they do is reframe everything. So progressive ideas that 10 years ago were sold as creating jobs are now lightly refreshed as ideas to advance affordability.

But they’re still the same basic ideas aimed at advancing the same basic goals — reducing carbon dioxide emissions and expanding public funding of child care services — that progressives have been pushing since at least Barack Obama’s second term.

It might be a good idea to instead try to give the people what they actually want.
