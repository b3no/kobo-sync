Supernatural NIMBYs

When magic spooks progress

Halina Bennet
Oct 31
∙
Paid
 
READ IN APP
 

On a wide, windy beach, a young wizard-in-training holds his friend — a free elf named Dobby — as the feeble creature takes his last breaths. Looking out over the swell, Dobby remarks to Harry Potter that it is “such a beautiful place to be with friends.”

That scene, filmed at Freshwater West Beach in Pembrokeshire, Wales, is where Harry says he wants to bury his friend “properly. Without magic.” It is also where fans of the films paid tribute to the elf after his tragic death in the 2010 movie “Harry Potter and the Deathly Hallows: Part 1,” by erecting a stone memorial, atop which there stands a crude sign, “Here lies Dobby, a free elf.”

The beach was also, in 2019, directly in the path of a multimillion-dollar power line project. Greenlink planned to run a cable beneath the sand, connecting the power grids of Britain and Ireland. After project manager Simon Ludlam appeared on BBC Wales from the beach to announce the proposal, fans started to call. There were many of them, both adults and children, pleading with Ludlam to leave Dobby alone.

Ludlam told the New York Times earlier this year that his first reaction, as he told colleagues, was that the whole thing was fake. And, indeed, Dobby isn’t real. Even in the book, he dies in Cornwall, not Wales. But Ludlam said he came around quickly, realizing that for some members of the fandom, their love for this fictional character was real and the situation was serious.

The cable was rerouted (for Dobby, and for environmental and archaeological reasons, according to the company) using horizontal drilling, which avoids digging trenches. Greenlink said the revised plan didn’t disturb the beach or the grave. The cable has carried power since the beginning of 2025, the Times reports.

When the Times covered the saga in August, the piece made its way around the internet as a joke to some, a triumph to others, and an example of frivolous NIMBYism to the abundance-minded. But Claire Moses, the Times reporter behind the story, wrote in a comment that one person who oversaw the project told her that the episode forced him to rethink his checklist before breaking ground: “Are there going to be any trolls or werewolves or deep sea monsters that live in the present memory of many people?”

Subconscious belief is enough to move mountains. Or boulders.

While Dobby’s grave is the most recent viral case of the supernatural getting in the way of progress, it is far from the first.

In 1999, a county in western Ireland was planning a bypass road around the town of Newmarket-on-Fergus. Standing in its path was a single hawthorn bush. In Celtic and Irish folklore, the hawthorn that stands alone is a sacred fairy tree that marks the threshold between the mortal world and the Otherworld, symbolizing love and protection. A folklorist and poet named Eddie Lenihan warned the engineers that if they bulldozed the bush, they, the road, and everyone who travels on it would incur the wrath of the fairies, who would curse the road with failing brakes and crashes for those who drove it.

The road was rerouted, and the fairy tree is intact today, standing strong beside the road once meant to go straight through it.

What is striking about this particular case — and other cases of similar infrastructure modifications in Ireland — is that almost nobody involved in it claimed to believe the lore. Lenihan told the New York Times in 1999 that Irish people tend to laugh off the old stories about fairies and leprechauns but that there’s a reason why the supernatural tends to win these fights: “Subconsciously, they believe.”

A folklore archivist at University College in Dublin described this kind of belief as “passive” — and common even among the young and well-educated.

“There is a reluctance to interfere in things which have an association with the fairies or with the other world,” she told the Times. “We would all rather be safe than sorry. People are not taking unnecessary chances. Life is complicated enough.”

The outlet reported at the time that the Irish were more concerned about radiation from cell phones and nuclear waste and, in some cases, aliens from outer space than they were about fairies.

In Iceland, on the other hand, elven creatures called huldufolk (“hidden people”) are more widely considered real, and the elf lobby has a history of blocking construction projects with their advocacy. The Guardian reports that Iceland’s government even has a standard response to questions about elves, explaining that construction has sometimes been delayed to give elves time to move elsewhere. In 2013, the Gálgahraun road was proposed to connect Álftanes and Garðabær near Reykjavík and was met with protest from Hraunavinir (“Lava Friends”), a group made up of people concerned about both the impact to the environment and to elf culture. Hraunavinir was concerned about the road’s disruption to a lava field that was the site of a boulder of significance referred to as an “elf church.”

In 2015, the 50-ton boulder was relocated, and the road was built.

Nobody thought Dobby was going to curse the cable, and the hidden people demanded respect instead of issuing threats, but each case is part of a pattern of otherworldly, intangible forces with enough cultural or spiritual capital to move an infrastructure project. And in these cases, the projects were ultimately built without too much additional cost.

The sacred is not the supernatural

Not every claim of this kind is cheap to honor. All over the world and as far back as the history of progress goes, there have been cases of the supernatural or intangible figures getting in the way of progress. Most commonly, those cases deal with religious or cultural sites or deities, which are often linked to populations with legal protections.

Projects that have infringed upon sacred Hindu sites in India have often been stalled or canceled. The Sethusamudram Shipping Canal has been stalled since 2007 because it would have dredged through Adam’s Bridge, a stretch of natural limestone shoals and sandbanks that many Hindus identify as the Ram Setu from the epic “Ramayana.”

A 2002 transit project in New Zealand faced a significant delay when the Ngāti Naho, a Māori sub-tribe, warned that a section of the planned multimillion-dollar Waikato Expressway cut through the swamp lair of a taniwha — a supernatural water spirit in Māori mythology. Work in that area ground to a halt, and the transit authority rerouted the highway to bypass the habitat. Similarly, plans to construct a prison faced years of opposition and legal challenges from the Ngāpuhi tribe, who argued that the site would block another taniwha’s underground passage. The prison was eventually built.

The Treaty of Waitangi, New Zealand’s founding document, includes provisions recognizing the rights and interests of Māori, which have often been used to sway environmental decision-making.

In the United States, there are strict land protections for Native American sites, and you frequently hear about “Indian burial grounds” as a trope that explains supernatural happenings. In reality, some sacred resting places for Indigenous people are legally protected, as are sacred and funerary objects by the Native American Graves Protection and Repatriation Act of 1990.

But not all that is sacred is taken seriously, as with a recent case in Arizona, where this spring the U.S. Forest Service transferred control over Oak Flat, a site the San Carlos Apache and other tribes hold sacred, to a copper company.

Deference to an Indigenous group whose rights have special legal protection is, as I see it, a different story from a community’s eccentric interest in fairies or elves. In the latter cases, the stories had a happy ending because cheap solutions were available. The sovereign legal rights of Indigenous communities carry more moral weight, but addressing these concerns frequently turns into a costly, burdensome ordeal.

When the ghost pays the rent

For other communities, association with the supernatural or otherworldly is a source of economic opportunity.

In 1947, in Roswell, N.M., the U.S. Army Air Forces announced they possessed a “flying disc” after recovering debris from a nearby ranch. The military quickly retracted its statement, but the incident heightened interest in aliens and unidentified flying objects (U.F.O.s) that, as Gary Thrine of the Roswell Chamber of Commerce told me, “put us on the map.”

The alleged crash site is on a private ranch about 40 miles outside of Roswell, surrounded by nothing but desert. Thrine says the rural town that’s three hours from Lubbock, Texas, three hours from Albuquerque, and four hours from Santa Fe welcomes tens of thousands of tourists to the U.F.O. festival every July. The International U.F.O. Museum and Research Center anchors an alien-themed downtown. Thrine spent much of our conversation telling me about all Roswell has to offer: a cheese plant, an aircraft refurbisher, a new Amazon distribution center, solar projects, the National Championship Air Races, and — he hopes — eventually a pecan festival.

“Not everything is U.F.O.,” he told me.

But, he clarified, nearly everything is because of U.F.O.

Without extraterrestrial-themed tourism, there would not be enough business passing through Roswell to keep it afloat.

Salem, Mass. — known for its 17th-century witch trials — has experienced a similar mystical boon to its economy.

Jeff Swartz, executive director of the Salem Chamber of Commerce, dates Haunted Happenings to the early 1980s, when the festival was a one-day event started by a few of the town’s oldest witch attractions, including the Salem Witch Museum and the Witch Dungeon Museum. Today, the festival lasts over a month. Events once held in October have migrated to late September and the festivities continue through Halloween. By Swartz’s count, more than 100,000 people can fill downtown on a weekend day in October, contributing to his estimate of about 1.5 million visitors across the month.

Like Thrine, Swartz — an effective messenger for the chamber of commerce — was quick to tell me that witches aren’t “the only game in town.” Salem was once a county seat until the dissolution of county governments in Massachusetts in 1999. It is home to a state university, plenty of businesses and nonprofits, and about 45,000 people.

Still, he said, without the witches, the city wouldn’t be nearly as prosperous as it is today. Most of the money stays local, and Swartz says the downtown economy is “almost entirely small-business driven” and tourism-focused. Tour guides, he said, pay mortgages and put kids through college on tour guide salaries and tips. He put himself through school that way. Like any city built around tourism, Salem’s interest is getting visitors to stay and spend money in town.

Popularity comes at a cost. There are crowds and a housing shortage, which Swartz attributes to demand outrunning supply. I asked if part of that squeeze could be due to strict historic preservation guidelines keeping the city from developing its spookiest or most historic areas. He said that historic districts rarely constrain new housing, since those neighborhoods are mostly zoned for single-family homes anyway.

In that case, if preservation isn’t the binding constraint, zoning is. I asked whether preservation is an economic asset that far outweighs razing the quaint parts of Salem in exchange for high rises.

“They’re not mutually exclusive,” Swartz told me.

His proposed compromise is to build upward on former industrial corridors and bypass roads, construct accessory dwelling units, and convert old churches and academic buildings as a continuation of the adaptive reuse of old buildings he said already happens often in Salem. He contrasts Salem with Boston’s West End, a neighborhood about the size of downtown Salem that was, in his words, “completely leveled” for urban renewal in the 1950s. Salem lost part of its core, too, but where progress flattened the West End, in Salem, the quirk that survived became the asset that has allowed the city, as Swartz tells it, to thrive. It was tourism that helped Salem bounce back more quickly than comparable cities after Covid.

“As an economic element, it’s absolutely essential.”

If the supernatural is an anchor for Salem and Roswell, it is a lifeboat for Forks, Wash.

In Forks, there was no magic intrinsic to the town’s identity for much of its history. Until the early aughts, it was a declining timber town sandwiched between the Pacific Ocean and Olympic National Park, trying to revive itself through an unsuccessful eco-tourism pivot.

Then, in 2005, Stephenie Meyer wrote “Twilight.”

She had never visited Forks but chose it as her setting because it came up in an internet search of the rainiest and cloudiest isolated places in America. The book sparked vampire and werewolf hysteria and gave the struggling town of 3,300 a new life, through no effort of its own.

In 2009, in anticipation of the film version of the sequel, Maclean’s reported roughly 69,000 overnight visitors to Forks that year, versus about 6,000 B.T. (before “Twilight”).

“Twilight has been an absolute blessing,” then-Mayor Nedra Reed told Maclean’s. “It’s kept the last person from turning out the lights, and as a matter of fact, lights are going on in abandoned buildings.”

I visited Forks last spring. That trip, while unrelated to “Twilight,” was completely “Twilight” themed. Airbnbs and inns mention the franchise in their listings, the diner in town has a “Bella Burger,” and the heroine’s red truck is parked outside the chamber of commerce and Timber Museum.

The town is by no means wealthy, and research from the Journal of Rural Social Sciences found that the economic benefits from the supernatural have been uneven among demographics. But if the town were to attempt to progress differently, to modernize in such a way that detracts from the quaint, albeit decrepit, elements that made it, to the author of “Twilight,” the perfect place for vampires to hunt and werewolves to lurk, it would lose the thing that saved it.

Sometimes the thing we can’t prove is convincing enough

The supernatural has, in countless cases throughout history and the world, had a significant effect on progress: how and whether it moves.

Sometimes, progress bulldozes straight through the magic, especially in cases where it isn’t simple to work around it.

But in many, the supernatural acts as a powerful barrier to progress. It dictates human choice, even when it is not out of fear of vengeful fairies or spiteful elves, because it appeals to the part of our humanity that believes whether subconsciously or consciously that magic is real — or at least wants to preserve the real manifestations of the fictitiously fantastical.

So, to echo the Greenlink project manager who let Dobby rest, it seems worth asking whether any trolls, werewolves, or deep sea monsters live in the present memory of many people. And whether those supernatural beings and their stories might not cost much — or might pay off in spades — to honor.
