In Maggie O’Farrell’s haunted Ireland, the land remembers everything

Set after the Great Hunger, “Land” follows one Irish family through grief, migration, and mysteries buried beneath the soil.

Ron Charles
May 26
 
READ IN APP
 

The Tuesday Review

I thought I understood the Irish Potato Famine that killed more than a million people and sent millions more fleeing abroad. But a few years ago, during a tour of Trinity College Dublin, our guide paused to mention the Great Hunger.

He couldn’t have been more than 30 years old, but the fury in his voice had not cooled since the 1850s. The cruelty of the British government, its catastrophic inaction in the face of mass starvation — that wasn’t history to this young man. It felt as raw and immediate as the loss of his own siblings.

Maggie O’Farrell, who was born in Northern Ireland, opens her new novel, Land, in 1865, during the stunned aftermath of the Great Hunger, on the famine-scarred west coast of Ireland. Towns have shriveled. Villages have emptied. Rows of empty cottages have succumbed to rot.

It’s here on a damp, windswept promontory that we find a pair of Irishmen, Tomás and his dutiful 10-year-old son, Liam. They’re working for the British army, part of the enormous Ordnance Survey to map — and further subjugate — Ireland. Despite deeply conflicted feelings about his employer, Tomás is tireless and taciturn. Having survived the famine that took every member of his family, he knows the importance of steady work, the necessity of submission.

“He is the only one of their division,” O’Farrell writes, “who can measure and calculate, draw detailed draft maps in ink for the engravers to copy, and also converse with the people about where the boundaries lie, who owns which field, what this valley or that bluff is called and why, where might the ruins of this building be.”

As she demonstrated in Hamnet, her magnificent novel about the death of Shakespeare’s only son, O’Farrell once again opens up the past like she’s cracking a geode. Suddenly, the undulating fields are sparkling in the moist air, redolent with the incongruous scents of peat and political oppression.

Stomping into a copse to retrieve Liam’s lost boot, Tomás stumbles upon a small coin of water surrounded by curling ferns. Is it a spring or a faerie well, a vernal pool or a portal through time? Such are the mysteries of O’Farrell’s fiction, which periodically wanders into thin places between this world and another.

Whatever Tomás saw in that ancient grove of trees, he emerges “a wild-haired man-creature,” barely recognizable to his frightened son. The stony surveyor once willing to do the redcoats’ bidding is now whooping and cackling with a crown of woven leaves around his head. “I have so much to tell you, so much, you will never believe,” he shouts in Irish, risking a beating from the British soldiers.

“Everything is different,” he tells Liam. “I have been everywhere and yet nowhere. I have scaled the heights, I have run the depths, I have been abroad and yet have stayed within.”

Readers of Land will experience that same mystical transportation. Like a skylark periodically wheeling from its path, this story sometimes flies into other timelines, past and future, here and around the world. (In fact, for several pages deep in the novel, O’Farrell switches to a bird’s perspective.) And in the most historically distant section, we see the lives of ancient people who sacrificed their loved ones, seeding this land with passions that still seep up through the boggy soil. There’s more than a touch here of Daniel Mason’s 2023 novel, North Woods, which observed a plot of western Massachusetts for 400 years.

Tomás seems to have been infected by the animus of some ancient spirit lingering in the ground, some hydromancy the villagers have gossiped about for generations. Having drunk that dark water from a granite bowl in the copse, he peremptorily moves his family from their home in town to a dilapidated cottage near the pool that set their lives “off-course, or maybe even on-course, because who is to say that the six of them were not destined to end up exactly where they did.” His alarmed wife, Phina, can’t fathom what’s come over her once stolid husband — this bizarre declaration that henceforth he’ll only draw maps of the real Ireland. But we know from a flashback to their harrowing and astonishingly romantic courtship that she’ll follow him anywhere.

This is a rich, irresistible story, as intoxicating as the water Tomás drank in that “unquiet place” amid the trees. With the propulsive energy her fans know well, O’Farrell tunnels into the lives of Tomás and Phina’s four children, colleens and lads born into the rupture, the great silence after the Hunger. Drawn to music, the church, the labor, and the land, they’re as different as the points on a compass that will someday take them away. This is the Irish diaspora writ small but powerfully, the dispersion of a single family in the most unlikely ways. Only the ghosts remain, with “their phantasmic sighing, their wistful longing, their grief at their inexplicable and eternal incorporeality.”

O’Farrell is not just telling a 19th-century story, she’s tilling the fields of those great Victorian novelists who understood that the only thing that redeems the contrivance of an unlikely coincidence is the pleasurable shock it gives us. When Jane Eyre collapses near the home of her cousins or when Tess slips her confession under Angel’s carpet, incredulity is instantly swallowed up in the relief or despair that readers of Land will experience at a chance meeting or — no, no, no — a missed one. It’s no wonder Liza Marshall, the producer of Hamnet, has already secured the film rights.

This, after all, is the novelist’s mapping, what Liam later thinks of as the “peculiar mix of science and storytelling, mathematics and artistry.” By the end of Land, no matter what your ancestry, these are your people. “When we die, we surrender our bodies to the earth and we become earth,” Tomás says. “It is the end of one story but the beginning of another.” O’Farrell has tenderly gathered them up.◆

Land, by Maggie O’Farrell (Knopf, 385 pp., $32)
