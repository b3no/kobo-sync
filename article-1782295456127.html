I ran the numbers. Trump cost us 274,000 jobs.

The bipartisan return to protectionism has been a costly mistake

Milan Singh
Jun 24
∙
Paid
 
READ IN APP
 

Over the last decade, protectionism has come back into vogue in the United States. Starting in 2018, both Republican and Democratic administrations have imposed new tariffs on U.S. imports; some trading partners have responded with tariffs on U.S. exports. The stated justification for this return to protectionism is to bolster manufacturing employment.

In theory, tariffs could increase domestic employment in protected industries if consumers substituted away from imported goods and toward domestically produced substitutes. In a September 2024 piece in The Atlantic, Oren Cass, the founder of right-populist think tank American Compass, made just this argument, claiming that critics of Trump’s tariff proposals “ignore even the possibility that higher import prices might encourage investment in domestic production” — production that “anchors local economies in a way that personal services cannot.”

But Cass’ implicit model of the economy assumes a fairly simple supply chain: Some products are made wholly in America, others are made wholly outside America, and consumers choose between the two.

That may have been common in the past, but it is increasingly less so today. These days, supply chains are multinational. The cars made by General Motors and Ford in Detroit include components shipped between Michigan and Ontario as many as four times before they are finished. So, in a world of complex supply chains, imposing new tariffs on (say) Canada would raise production costs for American automakers and reduce American employment.

That’s exactly what happened. From 2018 to 2024, import tariffs had a negative, statistically significant effect on manufacturing employment. Specifically, I estimate that tariffs reduced manufacturing employment by approximately 274,000 jobs over this period.

The bipartisan return to protectionism

In 2016, Donald Trump and Bernie Sanders ran for president as opponents of the North American Free Trade Agreement (NAFTA). In the general election, Hillary Clinton reversed her support for the Trans-Pacific Partnership (TPP), a proposed free trade agreement between the United States and 11 Pacific-adjacent countries, which she had helped negotiate as secretary of state.

In his first term as president, Trump formally withdrew the United States from the TPP in January of 2017 and renegotiated NAFTA (though the updated trade agreement, the United States-Mexico-Canada Agreement, made few substantive changes). Starting in January of 2018, President Trump invoked Section 232 of the Trade Expansion Act of 1962 and Sections 201 and 301 of the Trade Act of 1974 to impose tariffs on imported solar panels, washing machines, steel, aluminum, and select Chinese imports. In remarks at a signing ceremony for the tariffs, President Trump argued that they would bring “a lot of great jobs … back into our country.”

These tariffs represented a major shift in American trade policy and were met with retaliatory tariffs from the Chinese government.

As a candidate for president in 2019, Joe Biden opposed Trump’s tariffs on China, writing on Twitter that “Trump doesn’t get the basics” and that “any freshman econ student could tell you that the American people are paying [the cost of] his tariffs.”

However, as president, Biden did not deviate much from his predecessor’s trade policy. In the spring of 2024, Biden invoked Section 301 to impose 25% tariffs on Chinese steel, aluminum, and electric vehicle batteries; 50% tariffs on Chinese semiconductors and solar panels; and 100% tariffs on Chinese electric vehicles.

In his remarks announcing the tariffs, President Biden made a similar argument about employment, saying that his new tariffs “ensure that our workers are not held back by unfair trade practices.”

Indeed, when we look at applied tariff rates¹ on U.S. imports and exports, we see that tariffs — especially on goods imported from China — rose during Trump’s first term and stayed elevated during Biden’s term.

As discussed above, the stated justification for these tariffs was that they would create (or “reshore”) jobs, particularly in the manufacturing sector. Did they?

Follow the (dismal) science

To provide some context for readers, I want to briefly walk through some of the existing literature on trade and employment.

Several papers have found that trade liberalization with China reduced U.S. manufacturing employment. A 2013 paper found that increased trade with China led to lower manufacturing employment in areas where industries competed heavily with Chinese imports.²

A 2016 paper also found that trade liberalization with China reduced U.S. manufacturing employment, this time because there was less uncertainty about trade policy. When China gained Permanent Normal Trade Relations status in 2000, firms could be confident that tariffs on Chinese imports would not suddenly increase, and they could therefore feel confident in offshoring parts of their supply chains.

So if increasing Chinese imports reduced U.S. manufacturing employment, do tariffs reverse the job losses?³ No.

According to a 2024 study, Trump’s 2018 tariffs on Chinese imports had no statistically significant effects on employment in newly protected sectors, while retaliatory tariffs on U.S. exports had negative effects on employment in agriculture. Worse still, those 2018 tariffs led to higher prices for consumers, according to several researchers.⁴

Of course, it would be weird if this didn’t happen. The entire point of protectionism is to raise consumer prices so that consumers will instead choose to buy American. There’s no way for tariffs to redirect consumption away from imports and toward domestic products without making imports more expensive, and people wouldn’t be buying imports instead of domestic products if the imports were not cheaper or higher quality or both.

There are two channels through which tariffs could theoretically affect manufacturing employment.

The first is investment. Building factories is expensive and takes time. Firms are more likely to make costly investments when they can be certain that tax, regulatory, or trade policies will not suddenly change. But the bipartisan return to protectionism has been almost entirely done by executive order (or Truth Social post), not by legislation. So the 2018 trade war might have made firms less willing to invest in retooling their supply chains due to uncertainty about how tariff policy might change in the near future.

A 2020 paper measured trade policy uncertainty using newspaper coverage, earnings calls, and actual tariff rates. The researchers found that uncertainty shot up in 2018 to levels not seen in recorded data. This increased uncertainty lowered aggregate U.S. investment by 1%.⁵

The second channel is input costs. If the U.S. raises tariffs on imported steel, that’s good for domestic steel producers, since they now face weaker competition. The problem is that many domestic firms use steel as an input, so their costs go up. Moreover, if a trade war leads to retaliatory tariffs, that is harmful to American exporters.

A 2024 paper by Aaron Flaaen and Justin Pierce found that from 2018 to 2019, tariffs reduced U.S. manufacturing employment, with any positive effects from import protection being more than offset by negative effects from rising input costs and retaliatory tariffs imposed by trading partners.

My senior thesis — from which this piece is adapted — followed the same basic methodology as the Flaaen and Pierce paper, but used some slightly different data and measures and extended the window of study from 2018 to 2024. But I reached the same conclusion: Tariffs hurt manufacturing employment.

Tariffs cost America 274,000 manufacturing jobs

I looked at how manufacturing employment, industrial production, and prices changed across different industries as tariffs changed. The method I used stripped out the quirks of each industry and also broad swings hitting the whole economy, so what’s left is closer to what the tariffs actually did (for the wonks, more info can be found in my paper, which you can read here).

These estimates imply that tariffs reduced manufacturing employment 2.31% between January 2018 and December 2024. That translates to a loss of approximately 274,000 manufacturing jobs, relative to the baseline of 11.87 million workers employed in manufacturing in early 2018.

This sort of back-of-the-envelope scaling-up technique is relatively easy to do, but some downsides are worth touching on briefly.

Here’s one way to think about it. The numbers in the table above identify the relative effects of tariffs: that is, how manufacturing employment or producer prices changed in industries that got hit hard by tariffs, compared to industries that didn’t. What it can’t tell you about is the baseline everyone shares. Tariffs might have dragged down every industry, even the unexposed ones; a comparison between industries would miss that, because it only sees the difference between them and not the common drop.

This is called the “missing intercept” problem; conceptually, it means that my estimates of total job losses could be either too high or too low. But there are good reasons to think that these estimated job losses are, if anything, too low.

First, we know that the 2018 tariffs were mostly passed on to consumers in the form of higher prices. That means that the tariffs lowered aggregate demand (particularly for imports and their substitutes) by depressing people’s purchasing power, since each dollar consumers spent bought a little bit less.

Lower demand for manufactured goods is straightforwardly bad for the workers who produce those goods.

Second, if domestic industries were benefiting from a broader protective effect created by substitution away from imports, we would expect to see gains in employment in “purely protected” industries — meaning industries that were not subject to input or retaliatory tariffs, but whose foreign competitors were subject to import tariffs.

As you can see in the chart below, changes in employment for purely protected industries are statistically indistinguishable from zero. In other words, there is no evidence of any sort of protection benefit for employment, even among the most favorably situated industries.

Protectionism fails on its own terms

Besides reshoring manufacturing jobs, three other justifications are typically offered for protectionism: forcing exporters to reduce their before-tariff prices,⁶ winning votes, and improving national security.

The logic behind protectionism as a vote-getter is that tariffs provide concentrated benefits (to workers employed in manufacturing) and impose diffuse costs (everyone pays marginally more for goods).

There’s some competing evidence on this point: One 2019 paper found that Trump’s tariffs reduced support for Republicans in the 2018 midterms. But another paper suggested that it helped Republicans, albeit with a lag: Despite being economically costly, the researchers found import tariffs increased support for the Republican Party in 2020.

We also know that during the 2024 campaign, Donald Trump’s protectionist policies were popular: A 10% tariff on all imports polled at +23 net support, and a 60% tariff on all Chinese imports polled at +20 net support.

But most of that likely reflects a latent anti-China sentiment rather than a principled anti-free trade sentiment within the electorate. For the last 14 years, a majority of the American public has viewed foreign trade as more of an “opportunity for economic growth” than a “threat to the economy,” per Gallup’s time series polling. The share of voters viewing trade in positive terms increased from 61% of adults at the end of the Biden presidency to 82% in 2026. Other polling data showed that a large majority of the public disapproves of Trump’s second-term trade policy and that evaluations of Trump’s handling of trade fell sharply after the “Liberation Day” tariff announcements.

To the extent that the public prefers protectionism, that preference is weak. Voters — particularly those from places that lost employment due to import competition from China — may appreciate the symbolism of tariffs, but they do not appreciate having to pay higher costs for that symbolism.

On national security, what people are mainly talking about is China.

Importing cheap Chinese electric vehicles and solar panels would lower energy costs for American consumers and lower U.S. greenhouse gas emissions. Nonetheless, the Biden administration imposed new tariffs on imports of Chinese electric vehicles and solar panels. The underlying logic was that allowing Chinese firms to dominate the market for clean energy technologies would undermine America’s national security and clean energy industry; similarly, Biden-era restrictions on selling advanced computer chips to Chinese firms were aimed at protecting America’s edge in artificial intelligence.

The main point I want to make is that tariffs are economically costly. They don’t increase manufacturing employment. They’re also not popular.

It is possible to defend select, targeted tariffs on national security grounds. They do generate tax revenue, which the federal government is in desperate need of (although the tariffs Trump has imposed in his second term are probably unconstitutional).

But tariffs have not delivered the return to the idealized manufacturing economy that was promised. On its own terms, our return to protectionism has been an enormous failure. The next president should abandon this misguided experiment.
