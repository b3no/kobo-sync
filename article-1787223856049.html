No one is buying the right-wing theory of the elite

What Bernie Sanders gets right about billionaires

Jerusalem Demsas
Aug 20
 
READ IN APP
 

After 75 years of conservative attacks on college professors, journalists, and bureaucrats, hardly anyone — including Trump voters — calls those groups elite.

Way back in 1951, conservative William F. Buckley Jr. wrote God and Man at Yale, a book that tarred his alma mater as a hotbed of left-wing radicalism: “The institution that derives its moral and financial support from Christian individualists … persuad[es] the sons of these supporters to be atheistic socialists.”

Spiro Agnew, President Richard Nixon’s vice president, called network news leaders a “small and unelected elite” for being hostile to the president’s message. Irving Kristol, the father of neoconservatism, defined an elite “new class” including teachers, journalists, social workers, foundation staff, and bureaucrats.

Today, the standard-bearer of the left-wing-elite theory is surely the Manhattan Institute’s Christopher Rufo, who has turned his ire on “a small number of people with influence in the knowledge-based institutions” who are acting “in their own interest and against the interest of the vast majority of the American people.”

Rufo has compared his project to Nixon’s to “tame the national bureaucracy” and dismantle “radical organizations.”

But despite decades of effort on this front, according to The Argument‘s most recent poll, elite basically means billionaire or celebrity to the majority of American voters — including Trump 2024 voters:

It’s clear from this data that the Buckley-Nixon-Rufo project has had some effect on Republican voters: Trump 2024 voters are 11 percentage points more likely to view journalists as part of the elite than Harris 2024 voters, 5 points more likely to view government employees as part of the elite, and 13 points more likely to view college professors as part of the elite.

Still, none of these categories crack 20% for Trump voters.

When I was designing this poll with our director of political data, Lakshya Jain, I was particularly interested in going deeper. Not just “Who are the elite?” but “What are the public’s perceptions of elites?”

We discovered a strong correlation between elites and “people who hold positions I don’t like.” “Elite” is not just a socioeconomic category; it carries negative baggage.

Trump voters overwhelmingly imagined elites to be more liberal than the country, while Harris voters overwhelmingly imagined them to be more conservative. Forty-nine percent of Trump voters placed elites to the country’s left, compared with just 21% who placed them to its right. Among Harris voters, the pattern reversed: 68% said elites were more conservative, while only 10% said they were more liberal.

A partisan mirror image
“Generally speaking, how do you think the political views of elites compare to those of the country as a whole?”

As a result, the “elite worldview” produced by our poll results was ideologically incoherent, a grab bag of positions from both sides.

Sixty-six percent of voters support government-provided health insurance for all Americans, yet 59% think elites oppose it.

Eighty-one percent of voters think men and women are equally suited to leadership, yet 50% of voters think elites disagree with that statement.

Just 11% of voters support government ownership of most businesses, but 28% attribute that position to elites.

The public’s picture of elite opinion, then, looks like a map of beliefs that ordinary voters find objectionable.

While this is ideologically incoherent, it is consistent. Elites believe what ordinary people reject; therefore, since ordinary people are ideologically incoherent, so too are elites.

I also don’t want to overstate the incoherence here — voters are remarkably consistent — across party, class, race, age, and every other demographic we polled — about the category of person that qualifies as elite: billionaires and celebrities yes, but to a lesser extent politicians and businessmen.

The strangest part of all this: In such an environment, how does a billionaire, celebrity, politician, businessman — the poster child for what is considered elite — become president not once, but twice?

Notably, Trump is incredibly unpopular, so I wouldn’t call what he’s doing a strategy for success. But it does call into question how much our poll is really capturing about anti-elite politics.

Americans really don't like Donald Trump

Many people viewed 2016 as an election where the public overthrew the liberal elite — personified in Yale alumna and former Secretary of State Hillary Clinton. Trump himself explicitly presented the election as ordinary Americans reclaiming power from a corrupt establishment.

There are many ways to interpret these findings all together, but my interpretation is that hostility to elites only explains a small part of electoral outcomes. Particularly in 2016, the sense was that Trump was aligned with many of the relevant swing voters on certain issues like immigration and trade.¹

After we dropped this poll, my co-host Matt Yglesias was excited to talk about it on the podcast. During our conversation, he argued that elite status should be defined by consequential decision-making power. We also debated the most interesting part of the poll (to me): what the celebrity findings mean for political and cultural change.
