What I’ve learned after a year covering housing

Abundance is mainstream, but it is fragile.

Halina Bennet
Sep 12
∙
Paid
 
READ IN APP
 

I’ve been writing a weekly housing update for about a year now.

Over those 12 months, I’ve watched the housing abundance agenda go from being championed by a handful of already-YIMBY-pilled leaders to becoming the heart of the single most significant piece of housing legislation in a generation. Prior to this job, I had never written about housing and I knew effectively nothing about housing policy or development.

Here’s what this year has taught me.

State-level wins, local backlashes

Taken literally, Yes in My Backyard suggests a movement of people excited about new development near to where they live. But in practice, the most consequential housing wins of the past year came from states preempting local control, not from localized enthusiasm for new building. The order of operations matters; the people who’ve led building efforts without state encouragement have often found trouble at the ballot box.

California’s Senate Bill 79, which was authored by State Senator Scott Wiener, passed the legislature last September, was signed by Governor Gavin Newsom the following month, and took effect in incorporated cities on July 1, 2026. The law legalizes dense housing near transit stops in transit-rich cities across the state, introducing a tiered system that allows for the greatest heights and densities near the busiest rail and bus corridors. Wiener had been fighting for this kind of zoning reform since long before it became popular. Senate Bill 79 was Wiener’s third attempt to advance statewide legislation that upzones land near public transportation, after similar bills failed in 2018 and 2020.

In July, Newsom signed Assembly Bill 179, which aims to reform California’s affordable-housing finance system and reduce local development fees on state-funded affordable-housing projects. California’s statewide housing push has also given cities a clear framework to build on, with local governments adapting their own rules to fit the new state standards. Cities such as Santa Monica, Oakland, and San Francisco are adjusting their rules.

Of course, there are some cities that have been early movers on housing policy reform. In August, I spoke with San Diego Mayor Todd Gloria, a longtime housing advocate. The city’s Accessory Dwelling Unit Bonus Program, which he fought for, was launched in 2021 to let property owners add unrestricted market-rate A.D.U.s for every deed-restricted affordable unit they build. In transit priority areas, there is no cap if half of the units are income-restricted. That legislation, which passed years before the statewide push, has produced hundreds of affordable, deed-restricted A.D.U.s at little to no cost to the city. The state took San Diego’s model and has presented it as an option for other municipalities.

Another blue state on the opposite coast had its own statewide success when New York went after its NEPA-equivalent — the State Environmental Quality Review Act (SEQRA) — and passed major environmental-review exemptions. Governor Kathy Hochul in 2023 had tried to gut SEQRA’s power to block housing as part of her “Housing Compact,” but the effort stalled in the legislature.

A version of Hochul’s bill only passed this year when, riding the housing wave, Mayor Zohran Mamdani announced his plans to build 200,000 new affordable, rent-stabilized homes over the next decade while preserving and stabilizing an additional 200,000 existing units. Hochul’s state-level exemption, which passed in May, applies to housing on previously disturbed, utility-connected sites with up to 300 units in urbanized areas and 100 units in rural ones. It exempts these qualifying projects from SEQRA review and imposes new deadlines on remaining reviews.

Washington state and Idaho both passed their own supply-side housing packages. Washington followed up on its existing “Year of Housing” from 2025, which, among other reforms, created a rent-increase cap for older buildings, capped parking minimums, and encouraged denser development, especially near transit. Senate Bill 6026, which passed in March, generally requires cities of 30,000 or more to allow housing in commercial and mixed-use zones. Idaho focused on the most basic minimum-lot-size reform and A.D.U. legislation, allowing lot sizes as small as 1,400 square feet under laws passed this summer that were written specifically to boost starter homes.

In local government, it continues to be the case that building booms are often met with backlash.

Ann Arbor, Mich., Mayor Christopher Taylor spent 12 years in office championing efforts to build housing in the increasingly expensive college town. He shepherded a unanimous city council vote in March 2026 for a new comprehensive land-use plan that would legalize duplexes and triplexes citywide and allow high-rises downtown. In August, Democratic Socialist Yousef Rabhi beat him handily, 55 percent to 45 percent, having explicitly built his campaign against the density push, framing it as serving “out-of-town corporate interests.” Taylor’s downballot allies mostly survived, including two of his endorsed council candidates. In a progressive city during a D.S.A.-dominated election cycle, Taylor had odds to overcome beyond his YIMBYism, but he also became the face of development that some residents opposed.

In Jersey City, former Mayor Steven Fulop, a YIMBY champion, was replaced by a more orthodox progressive, James Solomon, who ran on a promise to “take control over the City’s growth back from the developers that have bought our politicians.”

New theoretical understandings of NIMBYism

The classic academic analysis of NIMBY sentiment dates back to Dartmouth economist William Fischel’s “homevoter hypothesis,” which posits that anti-development sentiment is primarily an effort by homeowners to defend the financial value of their investments.

Newer academic research has called into question the centrality of this mechanism. Michael Hankinson’s study from 2018, for example, found that in San Francisco, both homeowners and renters who supported new housing citywide were significantly less supportive of it in their own neighborhood.

A study by David Broockman, Christopher Elmendorf, and Josh Kalla published in January of this year argues that anti-development sentiment is less about self-interest and more about what they term “symbolic politics.”

The researchers note that owners and renters have similar views on housing issues but that attitudes toward new building are highly correlated with attitudes toward developers, investors, government, and new residents.

The same researchers published a paper in November 2025 that determined that aesthetics are also a primary driver of NIMBY attitudes. People who live in dense neighborhoods generally like them and are supportive of additional density in general. But people who don’t live in dense neighborhoods tend to dislike the idea of putting apartments in single-family neighborhoods whether or not they live there — they simply object to development in places where it does not fit the neighborhood’s vibe or visual aesthetic.

In addition, research from Elmendorf, Clayton Nall, and Stan Oklobdzija indicates that many voters are “supply skeptics” who attribute high costs to developers and landlords rather than to scarcity.

Financial constraints bind

In the places where zoning reform has happened, there are still ongoing struggles to actually get housing built. Denver, for example, legalized A.D.U.s and created incentives for residents to build them. But now the city is bumping up on budgetary barriers to meeting its targets.

A similar saga already played out in Minnesota. As part of implementing its 2040 Plan, Minneapolis legalized duplexes and triplexes citywide in 2020. By 2024, according to the Minneapolis Fed, 87 new duplex, triplex, and fourplex buildings were permitted, totaling 225 new housing units. At the same time, the city added 11,503 units in multifamily apartment buildings. Through 2023, it added roughly 1,006 units in larger projects, which, unlike duplexes and triplexes, actually penciled out for developers, according to an analysis of building permits by the Metropolitan Abundance Project.

But more broadly, the macroeconomic environment has shifted against homebuilding, with national multifamily construction volumes falling due to high interest rates and construction costs.

Apartment construction starts were at their lowest quarterly level since 2011 in the first quarter of this year, a 73 percent drop from their 2022 peak. The national multifamily pipeline, also known as units under construction, decreased more than 50 percent from its 2023 peak during the first quarter of 2026 to about 579,000 units. Census data from May showed that multifamily housing starts fell 41.6 percent month-over-month, from 486,000 to 284,000 annualized units, while single-family starts fell only 1.9 percent.

I’ve seen projects across the country, over and over again, hit financial roadblocks that make building impossible. Density can still be hard to achieve, even when everyone is on board.
