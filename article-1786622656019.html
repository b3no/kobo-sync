Most Women Aren't That Curvy

Forget weight and size--we have an unrealistic idea of what the average healthy young woman is shaped like.

The other day on Twitter, I stumbled into an argument about how a well-known female sex symbol was actually not curvy and was just thin, if a bit unremarkable and basic-looking. Want to guess who it was?

You’re wrong: it was Marilyn Monroe.

This was not an isolated incident. I’ve seen multiple sex symbols once known for their curves revised as “average.” Two others who come to mind: Jennifer Lopez and Halle Berry. Once curvy hourglasses, today according to the denizens of the Internet: “meh, she looks great, but that’s what any woman would look like if she took care of herself.”

The problem here is that society (especially online society) has developed Instagram Goggles, or to put it another way, BBL blindness—and because it’s unrelated to dangerous eating patterns, many people ignore it as an unrealistic standard, or even cheer it on. Our understanding of the typical waist-hip ratio (the degree of curvature between waist and hips) in a healthy-weight young woman has gotten wildly out of whack based on what we see online. Allow me to illustrate, and please refer back to this image when you’re confused about the numbers I’m about to benchmark. The lower the waist-hip ratio, the curvier the shape of the woman, irrelevant to overall size (although obesity can increase waist-hip ratio.)

Now, is a lower waist-hip ratio more conventionally attractive? Yes, much in the way tall men with broad shoulders are attractive. But you would also be deluded to think your average man, or even your slightly above average man, is 6’3” with the shoulders of Dwight Howard. But today, people think your average healthy-weight woman has the body of Halle Berry in the nineties—or Marilyn Monroe in the fifties for that matter. We have come to see a 0.7 waist-hip ratio (what used to be considered the ultimate desirable ratio) as the “meh” default for a slim woman, and a 0.6 (or lower) waist-hip ratio (see: Kardashians) as the new ideal.

But your average woman (yes, even your average woman of color—I’ll get to that in a minute) does not have anything near a 0.6 waist-hip ratio. You might be surprised to learn she probably doesn’t even have a 0.7 ratio. Yes, even if she’s healthy. Yes, even if she is at an ideal weight. Overexposure to doctored Instagram photos and plastic surgery has completely distorted the shape we think most women are capable of attaining just by being “healthy.” You might be surprised to learn how your average young, slim, healthy woman is actually shaped.

Whenever this argument comes up, people tell me that actually, I’m just being Eurocentric because women of color do typically have 0.6 waist-hip ratios and it’s only white women who are “built like fridges.” After all, was Kim Kardashian not just copying Black women when she embarked on her plastic surgery journey?

While Kim Kardashian may have been copying the ideal body shape for Black women, she was not emulating the shape of the average or even above average Black woman. Studies have been done across multiple cultures around the world to evaluate average waist-hip ratios across ethnic groups. In this one, which covered multiple traditional societies on different continents, waist-hip ratio increased both with number of children and with age, but even among young women who had not yet had children, a 0.7 waist-hip ratio, let alone a 0.6 waist-hip ratio, was not the average or anywhere close to it. Yes, even within Africa. In fact, the three African groups evaluated in that study had average waist-hip ratios ranging from about 0.77-0.83, even while limiting only to nulliparous women (the ones who hadn’t had kids yet). The lowest waist-hip ratio in the entire dataset was 0.61, but that’s one woman out of many—the same way if you measured the heights of many men, you might see one guy who was 6’8”.

(I couldn’t find data specifically on African-American women which allowed for filtering by age and parity, only American women in general.)

Another study done in Brazil (a country famous for small waists and big butts) limited only to adults in their twenties, showed a mean waist-hip ratio for women at 0.75. Now, that’s a fairly curvy waist-hip ratio, but it’s nowhere near a Kardashian or even Halle Berry, and a lot of Internet dwellers would still consider it inadequately feminine. A cohort of young Brazilian women still do not have waists curvy enough for today’s Internet standards.

The same pattern holds true across many different cultures and regions. There is no studied population in the world where a 0.6-0.7 waist hip ratio is common, even for young nulliparous women. According to a study done by the CDC, a waist-hip ratio of 0.74 is in the lowest 5%, even for American women age 20-24 (most of whom are nulliparous) all races combined. (Worth noting that unlike the other studies mentioned which measure waist at the narrowest point, the CDC measures waist a few inches below the natural point, so the actual smallest 5% is likely a bit lower than 0.74—maybe in the range of 0.68-0.7. But still—we’re talking about the lowest 5%, not the average.) Meanwhile, the average waist-hip ratio for that cohort is 0.83 (probably more like 0.77 if we account for the different measurement location.)

I mocked up this image, leveraging typical measurements for a young American woman already at an “ideal” slim weight—around a 0.77 waist-hip ratio. Marilyn Monroe is not, in fact, the “average” body for a young slim woman (you can play around with the visualizer here.)

Besides, if women of color were all built like Kim Kardashian (a spurious claim to begin with) they would not have made up a significant portion of patients seeking BBLs to reduce their waist size while increasing the size of their butt and hips. If anything, the ridiculous expectation that women of color all look like Kim Kardashian is placing undue pressure on these women to conform to a beauty standard that was never natural for anyone. In the music video for Baby Got Back, the dancers—attractive women selected specifically for their curves—still don’t look like Kim Kardashian.

In many ways, the culprit is boring and evergreen: social media. If you go on r/InstagramReality, you will notice that Instagram is full of women of all ethnicities leveraging poses, camera angles, and most of all FaceTune or Photoshop to alter their bodies—specifically to make their waists smaller and their hips and butts bigger. There is a reason that, across multiple countries and major cities, I generally do not see women who look like this in real life. I see women with curves, I see fit women, I see women with hourglass waists, but I have basically never seen someone who looked as outrageous as a typical Instagram influencer in real life.

In fact, there is a popular TikTok influencer (becamichie,) who has been accused of dramatically editing their waist to physically impossible proportions, amassing a huge following of adoring teenage girls clamoring for diet and exercise tips. (I’m using they/them pronouns because they are nonbinary.) Their ab workout videos—which are honestly pretty basic and contain no “secrets” to such a shape—are incredibly popular. But becamichie is also a professional model, so people are privy to images of them on the catwalk where they look slim, fit, perhaps curvy, but not built like a video game character as they are on TikTok and Instagram. The teenage girls, however, aren’t so discerning. They follow becamichie in the hopes of cinching their waist to a 21” measurement, which the influencer claims to have naturally at 5’9” (they have denied editing their photos and videos every time this has come up—and it’s come up many times.)

This has even leaked beyond the world of teen girls and into corporate advertising, as Gymshark was forced to delete this advertisement (leveraging influencer Lauren Johnson) for such an extreme (and likely edited or strategically posed, although she also denies editing) waist-hip ratio.

But the distortion is not just about what we see, but what we don’t see. Most women don’t look anything like these influencers even with a healthy lifestyle and slim weight, and those women are less inclined to post photos of themselves, knowing they could be ridiculed or insulted. Average-shaped women (who may erroniously believe themselves to be far boxier than average) may post photos of themselves, but they will likely be wearing clothes, and those photos won’t go nearly as viral. As a result, if most naked or semi-naked women you see exist on social media, you will really believe that a 0.6 ratio is ideal but still attainable for a sizeable chunk of the population if only they ate well and did squats, and 0.7 is average for a slim and healthy woman.

And as a result, women who do not have an extreme waist-hip ratio are dismissed as “boyish,” “masculine” or “built like a fridge.” Sydney Sweeney has been on the receiving end of this body-shaming before, by both men and women, in part because most of the time that she’s on the red carpet, she’s wearing a corset. Therefore, when people see her real, undoctored and uncinched waist, they joke about her being a “rectangle.” But Sydney Sweeney is actually not exceptionally boxy, at least compared to the average American woman of her size. According to the many studies on average waist-hip ratio across age cohorts, she has a very typical waist shape for a young nulliparous woman! In fact, as recently as the 2000s, she’d have been lauded in the left-side photo for amazing abs and a flat tummy, with nary a comment made about too-narrow hips. Our Instagram goggles have gotten us completely confused, and it doesn’t help that photos like the right side (where she’s wearing a corset) are circulated all over social media without any context.

One frustrating thing about the Instagram goggles phenomenon is that women are often just as likely to engage in this form of body-shaming as men. It makes sense, because for a long time, the word “curvy” was used in opposition to “skinny.” Women felt that by pitting curves against skin and bones, they could put an end to “heroin chic” and celebrate women in their natural state. But this didn’t really make sense, because skinny is about size and curvy is about shape, and curves are not inherently more attainable than bones. These are two completely different variables. You can, in fact, be both skinny and curvy, depending on your bone structure and genetics. You can also be chubby and not curvy! But somehow, we got it in our minds that unrealistic beauty standards involve wanting women to look like malnourished little boys, and healthy beauty ideals celebrate “curves.” Ergo: curves are good, skinny is bad, let’s celebrate curves (even though these two things exist on completely different spectrums and aren’t mutually exclusive, and extreme curves are even more unattainable than extreme thinness.)

I’ve seen countless posts discouraging women from using Ozempic or engaging in crash diets by showcasing a photo of a “thick” model with an exceptional waist-hip ratio almost never found in nature. “Why would any woman want to be skinny when she could look like this?” they ask, as if every woman is an extreme hourglass hiding behind the wrong size. “Women think men are attracted to THIS (insert photo of skinny model) when men are actually attracted to THIS (insert photo of thicker model with proportions unattainable to 99.999% of people”

“Curves” are mostly out of our control (sans surgery) and largely unrelated to whether you’re eating enough. Anorexic women can still have pronounced waist-hip ratios. I am randomly served a lot of eating disorder Twitter content on my feed, and a great deal of the thinspo revolves not around looking boyish, but about having a dramatically smaller waist in comparison to hips while being scary-skinny. Thinspo which features more straight-shaped women is not nearly as popular as thinspo featuring dramatically snatched waists. We should probably be honest with ourselves about how a focus on “curves” does nothing to vanquish unrealistic body standards—especially when the curves in question are especially unrealistic!

Now, I won’t fault men for finding a 0.6 waist-hip ratio attractive. I also won’t fault women for doing what they can (it’s not much, outside of surgery) to acquire such a shape. Perhaps I’ll write about this at some point, but I spent a great deal of my twenties on a DIY looksmaxxing journey, obsessed with a 0.6 waist hip ratio that I was hoping I could attain without surgery, so trust me, I get it.

But instead of demonizing a beauty ideal (not my endgame, beauty ideals are ideals) we should just be honest about what the “average” shape really is, the same way we would argue against a belief that the “average” man was 6’2”, while still acknowledging it’s fine for women to be attracted to tall men. It’s one thing to think tall men are hot, and it’s another thing to declare any man under 6’2” is short and effeminate…or that Marilyn Monroe was built like a fridge.
