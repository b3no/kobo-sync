"I Can't Cook For Myself" Is The New "I Can't Start a Family"

Just as you don't need to have a six-figure wedding, your Tuesday lunch does not need to be DoorDash sushi.

Seemingly every month or so, Twitter is overrun by “DoorDash discourse,” which nearly always goes the same way: the majority of people believe that DoorDash (or any other on-demand food delivery app) is a nice treat to have, but not a fundamental human right. Then, a smaller but very loud cohort of people insist that these apps are simply the only option for a significant number of people, and even if one could theoretically cook, it’s an indictment of America, capitalism, or the elites, that every single person in the country cannot afford a daily private chauffeur for a hoagie.

The excuses are endless for why restaurant delivery is not a luxury and is in fact a pressing necessity for the proletariat: yes, DoorDash is expensive but “groceries are too,” so what does it matter? Lots of people can’t cook because they “can’t afford all the right spices and don’t know the right recipes.” (All this screen time and yet, they are incapable of looking up a recipe.) Lots of people can’t cook because debilitating ADHD prevents them from cleaning up afterwards. Lots of people are allergic to food that was previously frozen or even refrigerated (thank goodness restaurants never use anything that was previously chilled!) Lots of people can’t cook because they work multiple shifts, have no mouth, and suffer from long hantavirus.

This conversation comes off the heels of businessman Kevin O’Leary admonishing middle class young people for regularly spending $28 on lunch, to which leftist Twitter personality Sean McCarthy retorted, “Lunch just costs $28 now.” This conversation dragged on around Twitter for several days. For the pro-DoorDash folks, any suggestion to cook your own food or bring your own sandwich to work, was the equivalent of being commanded to eat gruel on the floor of the Victorian workhouse.

Eventually, we came full circle to the point where the pro-DoorDash contingent were genuinely agreeing with the sarcastic idea that under a utopian socialist government where people were paid fairly (except, I imagine, for delivery workers and restaurant workers), everyone would have a Ferrari. (Go ahead and be a socialist if you want to, but I should remind you that socialism doesn’t mean everyone would be rich.)

On the one hand, it’s easy to laugh at these people. Anyone with a basic understanding of addition knows that cooking your own food, provided you are not making lobster truffle mac and cheese topped with ossetra caviar, is significantly less expensive than ordering out. And while a very small minority of people might be too severely disabled to prepare their own food, DoorDash has only been around for thirteen years. People survived cooking their own food, heating up frozen food, and maybe ordering the occasional pizza for most of modern history—and Gen Z spends the smallest proportion of their food budget on groceries compared to all previous generations, with a much higher percentage spent on restaurants and delivery.

A lot of the indignant rage over DoorDash being “too expensive” seemed to come from people who believed the baseline for a Tuesday lunch at work should be a seared steak salad with avocado and fresh pomegranate seeds, not a chicken sandwich. And when it comes to home cooking, anything short of something that would appear on the cover of a magazine displayed at the microlooting station—sorry, I mean the Whole Foods checkout—is Soylent Green dystopian slop.

You will never catch me blanket-condemning treats, and maybe I’m just saying this because I’m hungry right now but I would love a seared steak salad. But this is not how most people eat throughout the week, or even on the weekends. Perhaps due to constant mainlining of social media, some young people have been conditioned into believing that the baseline expectation of a meal—whether you’re cooking or ordering out—should be one of indulgence. Perhaps we should blame all those “day in the life” TikTok videos which purport to be showing what it’s like to work at a tech company, but are instead highlight reels of all the various expensive liquids and solids consumed by a woman with vocal fry.

(Before anyone yells at me for being “out of touch,” because I want the masses to eat dust sandwiches, I can confirm that my weekday lunches are far from luxurious—I usually make my own Greek yogurt cucumber soup or gazpacho which costs about $7 per serving, and basically never order out for lunch, nor did I for the thirteen years when I worked in an office. Back when I worked office jobs, I usually brought my lunch from home—usually pasta or a salad—and occasionally I’d go to Chipotle.)

Our expectation of restaurant lunches (or gourmet home cooking) is not unlike our perception of something else that has been built up to unattainable, ridiculous heights on social media to the point where people just don’t think they can: marriage and parenting. Our extremely modern high standards for what marriage and parenting look like makes people believe they can’t do it, can’t afford it, and that any compromises in this area are akin to eating a singular boiled hotdog for dinner.

It’s impossible to write about budgeting without getting one thing out of the way, which is that housing is genuinely very expensive. In 2026, 65% of US households could not afford a new median-priced home. Housing prices have significantly outpaced income for the past several decades, and the housing crisis is palpable in any metropolitan area. Yes, it’s not financially savvy to buy $28 lunches when you’re on a budget, but many of these young people understandably do not see their delivery habit as a threat to their ability to buy a house, if only because they don’t believe they’d ever be able to buy a house anyway. This was the same reason the notorious “avocado toast” jab aimed at millennials (which implied we would be able to buy homes if we stopped eating avocado toast) was silly—not that it was smart or reasonable to constantly buy overpriced avocado toast (I maintain making it actually isn’t that expensive, but I digress) but rather that avocado toast wasn’t expensive enough to be the reason that someone couldn’t afford a house.

Housing costs, of course, factor into how we plan for marriage and kids. A year ago, I ran a survey of over 1,000 people to see which obstacles lay between their ideal number of children and their actual number of children (More than half of respondents did not have children at all, but many of those people wanted them someday.) 51% of respondents cited housing costs as an obstacle to having their ideal number of children, and 37% said that they would have to move to another home if they discovered that they were going to have a baby within nine months (this number jumped to 52% for people who did not have any children yet.) Unsurprisingly, people in urban environments were more likely than anyone else to say that housing quality was an obstacle to having children.

I don’t blame these people. I would have answered the same way when I was twenty-four and living in a rented apartment infested with black mold, about which my landlord continually gaslit me. Ideally, if you had a child, your child would have his or her own room and adequate space to play. And we live in a culture where multigenerational housing is not an aspirational setup. But in most places around the world, it’s not unusual for multiple generations of families to live together, for children to share rooms, or even for children to share beds or sleep in bed with their parents. I don’t blame anyone for not wanting to raise children in such a way, but when we put the birth rate in the context of history, our current expectations—even the “reasonable” ones—present a massive departure from the way humans have lived for a very long time. What we now see as the bare minimum used to be the standard. And while housing has gotten more proportionately expensive, when the oldest boomers first became parents, they were actually more likely to be renters than homeowners. (per Lyman Stone.) Now, with homes more expensive than ever, the majority of aspiring homeowners believe owning a home is a precondition to major life events such as marriage or parenthood. I kind of understand the parenthood thing—but marriage? Is it impossible to maintain a wife in a rented apartment? Must she be free to roam around a backyard off-leash to be adequately stimulated?

It’s not that people are wrong to want housing to be more affordable, or that people should never aspire to anything. It’s that our modern-day standards for what one must do before getting married or having children are higher than they’ve ever been. And I wouldn’t even mind it—I have some fairly high expectations myself—except that people seem hellbent on insisting this isn’t happening, people are worse off than ever, and everyone is merely scrounging around for scraps like dogs. Oh, but those scraps better not be previously-frozen!

As tempting as it is to blame the declining birth rate on your favorite pet issue (in this case, late-stage capitalism and Amerikkka) the birth rate is declining everywhere, even in countries with strong social safety nets. Although, to be fair, none of these countries have provided free DoorDash, so who’s to say.

I reject the trad notion that your only goal in life should be having as many children as possible, as young as possible, all else be damned (some of these people have even started to glorify teen pregnancy). But what many Americans in 2026 believe is the bare minimum to have children or get married would be considered unbelievable luxury to people all over the world and many people in recent American history—just like a restaurant-made seared salmon salad every day for lunch. And even during the time (the 1950s and ‘60s) when we imagine that your average American was far more prosperous than we are today in the midst of our hellish era of “late stage capitalism,” people were dining out for dinner far less than they do today, estimated at just once or twice a month on average. And to think that the majority of Americans were surviving just fine without this essential human right!

Finances aren’t even the full picture of the higher-than-ever standards we have for marriage and children. Some of these standards cannot really be bent without the police getting involved. Childcare is a serious expense, and it’s no longer acceptable (or frankly, legal) to just let your kids fend for themselves while you work. It used to be socially acceptable for “latchkey kids” to be left home alone after school until their parents returned from work, even when they were in elementary school. Today, in some states, it’s illegal to leave eleven-year-olds home alone for any amount of time, and up until very recently, in some states, it was illegal to leave a thirteen-year-old home alone. And even without the police getting involved, people just expect to be more present, involved parents today than in decades past, and their expected time spent with each child directly impacts how many children they decide to have. Perhaps this is a vicious cycle: with more cousins and siblings, less interaction with parents is genuinely needed, but today’s children being asked to “go out and play” might not have any playmates.

Even if you don’t want kids, marriage timelines are affected by these rising standards. Technically, it costs very little to get married provided you don’t have a wedding-wedding (you could just throw a party in your back yard if you really wanted to—or go to the courthouse.) And yet, the average wedding cost in 2026 is upwards of $30,000, with many couples spending more than $100,000. What we consider “necessary” for a wedding has now extended to professional photographers, inordinately expensive floral arrangements, and attire that often cost more than $1,000. Bachelor and bachelorette parties are no longer a fun night out, but an entire three-day weekend event in Nashville.

But weddings in the 1950s were frequently only a few hundred dollars, not only because things were cheaper back then but because the weddings were just genuinely much simpler. When someone says they “can’t afford to get married,” it’s not true. They can afford to get married. They just can’t afford to get married the way people get married all over Instagram.

And yes, social media, today’s omnivillain, is once again to blame. Yes, things are more expensive (sometimes disproportionately) but expenses aside, how many of our expectations—for lunch, for marriage, for parenting—are completely misaligned with the way people have been doing things up until very recently? One of my most popular articles last year was about how TikTok and Instagram consumerist trends effectively gaslit a generation of mothers (and a few gullible husbands) into spending ridiculous amounts of money on curated “experiences”—prom-posals, sleepovers, and more—as if having a professional coordinator for your child’s sleepover was now a baseline expectation and not something that would be used to motivate new ISIS recruits into hating America with more fervor. I joked that a comfortably upper middle class friend of mine felt inadequate after looking at images of the absurd birthday party Kylie Jenner threw for her then-toddler daughter. Obviously, that’s an extreme example, and I think my friend knew it was silly, but my point remains that social media has completely distorted even reasonable people’s ideas of what our baseline expectations for life should be.

It’s easy to strawman my argument as “everyone should just eat discarded fish gills for lunch” (oops—I actually did that when I was making $20K in San Francisco) or that nobody should ever aspire to greatness. And by all means, if it helps motivate you to imagine a world where you can buy sushi for lunch every day and owns Ferrari, go for it! But this is not the reality for the vast majority of people—even for all those out of touch boomers, who are cooking at home more than anyone. Big dreams are fine, but they shouldn’t be seen as the baseline expectation, and it’s best if they aren’t inspired by Instagram and TikTok.
