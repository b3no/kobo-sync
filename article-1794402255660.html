The First Time I Got Fired

Every company has one employee who is the source of all bad things. I was that employee.

Cartoons Hate Her
Nov 11
 
READ IN APP
 

The following is a true story from none other than ME, famously known for getting fired a lot. But there’s a first time for everything, and this is the first time I got fired. I previously published this story at the very beginning of my Substack career and very few people saw it, so in the spirit of the Wednesday tradition, I’m publishing a polished version for free.

“This is going to be one of those unfortunate conversations we’d both rather not have,” Ana said. She shut the conference room door behind her. Granted, a conference room at Articley looked more like a Ronald McDonald Playplace than anything office-related. I hadn’t even had time to sit down on the giant hand-shaped, lime green chair before I blurted out “Wait, am I being fired?”

“Kind of,” she said, her face showing just as much discomfort as I’m sure mine was . I could tell she had either never done this before, or specifically dreaded firing me because since day one at Articley, over a year ago, I had been routinely asking her if I was in danger of getting fired. “It’s not your performance, I wouldn’t even call it firing. We just need to eliminate one person from our entertainment team because we’re putting more focus into our gaming articles.”

Despite the fact that I had always feared this moment, I found myself in an unfamiliar state of mind: wanting to get information quickly and fix whatever I could without too much emotion.

“Do I get severance?” I asked.

“No.” 

It seemed a bit cruel to me that contractors who were paid so little and given no benefits would also not get severance. If anything we should have gotten more severance in the event that we were let go, as a consolation prize for not receiving benefits or a livable salary. But that wasn’t Ana’s fault, so I wasn’t going to press her on it.

“Can I leave early to tell my boyfriend?” My boyfriend at the time (now husband) Nick, was working on his own startup. Despite the fact that I barely made enough money to survive in San Francsico, Nick somehow made less. The two of us were living in the spare bedroom of a fifty-year-old alcoholic yoga teacher named Van who was born without a sense of smell. Van was almost never home, although occasionally we found him passed out on the floor in the hallway. (I know I’m not lavishing praise on Van here, but he was actually a great guy--certainly better than Nick’s previous roommate, who had a framed photo of George W. Bush on his wall and accused Nick of selling drugs because he once heard him “flushing the toilet suspiciously often.”) Technically Nick and I weren’t even supposed to live with Van. Every few weeks Van’s religious landlady would come around and make Van promise that he wasn’t renting out his spare room or keeping any “unmarried women” there. I had to keep all my clothes in a suitcase in the closet to avoid detection.

“Of course you can go see Nick,” Ana said. She was pretty familiar with Nick, not because she knew him, but because given that he was my best and possibly only friend in the city where I had only been living for a year, I talked about him constantly, both because I loved him and because it was a knee-jerk reaction to avoid giving any other men the impression that I was available.

“So should I pack up all my stuff now?” I asked. 

“You can work here for two more weeks,” Ana said. “We thought we’d give you some time to look for something else.”

Another concern popped up in my mind--one that I didn’t quite know how to articulate because I knew it could be awkward, but I would be kicking myself if this particular issue came up and I hadn’t said anything. “Just another thing,” I said, as I opened the conference room door to leave. “I know that when Kat left, everyone threw her a going-away party with a cake. I don’t know if I get a going-away party because I’m a contractor and I’m not really quitting, and I’m not requesting one at all, but just in case anyone tries to throw me one, can you let them know I have a gluten intolerance and I don’t eat cake so it would be better if they did, like, a cheese board?”

Just a year and a half earlier, I graduated college and immediately moved to San Francisco to live with Nick, who I started dating in college. Nick graduated before me, so he already had a job where he could bring his entrepreneurial spirit and passion for technology to the table, even if it didn’t pay much. I, on the other hand, had no solid career plans other than an extremely misguided dream of one day being a famous novelist (I did not yet realize that most “famous novelists” also scrape by as middle school English teachers during the day. My plan was to simply write the best book ever, hope people liked it, and become insanely rich.)

This was during the Great Recession, so although I had lofty dreams, it was starting to dawn on em that my ideal job wasn’t in the cards. Before I graduated, I went to the career planning office at my school and told them that although I wanted to be a novelist, I would also graciously be open to a job as a copywriter or other kind of professional writer. I would, potentially, open my options up to becoming a creative director at some kind of swanky ad agency if I absolutely had to.

The career counselor told me, sympathetically, that I’d need to go to grad school for all of that that. I hated college as it was. It was boring and lonely, with Nick so far away from me, and nothing to do but peruse I Can Haz Cheeseburger memes while drunk. (Okay, so maybe I didn’t do a great job of making friends, and yes, it was entirely my fault for having abysmal social skills, but so what?) The idea of doing that again for even another year terrified me.

This was when I decided my next job would simply be the first job available to me that did not involve backbreaking physical labor or selling photos of my feet. This meant I briefly took a PR internship role at a fitness company when I arrived in San Francisco. The women who ran the company told me up front that they didn’t have any money to pay me, so I’d have to just “find PR opportunities” for them as part of this unpaid job. No explanation of what opportunities would suffice, or even what PR meant. I was a bit afraid to ask. Since they weren’t paying me, I decided not to work, so I went into their office space every day at 10 AM after trying on $150 bandage dresses at Bebe that I couldn’t afford, read trashy articles for a while, and left. I’m not sure what my plan was, but I think I just wanted to say I had a job. Of course, I continued my job search in hopes of finding a job that actually paid me, figuring I could at least list this sweet gig on LinkedIn so that I wouldn’t look unemployed.

An alum from my college worked at Articley, a media company with which I was vaguely familiar. He invited me into the office for an interview. Although I thought this would be some kind of rigorous test, unbeknownst to me, the interview was pretty much a formality to make sure I was a living, breathing human being. My job would pay $15/hour and it would involve churning out various pieces of short-form content about TV shows, movies and other entertainment franchises. On my first day of work, my boss, a floppy-haired Pixar dadlike character named Ralph, told me that I didn’t even need to come up with my own content. Most of the stuff I would be assigned to write would entail paraphrasing other articles and press releases. Occasionally, he said, I could bill more hours for articles I wrote about topics of my choosing. He also let me know that I didn’t need to come into the office or even work full time. I could pretty much clock on and off whenever I wanted as long as I didn’t falsify my time sheets. This may have sounded good to someone else, someone whose parents were paying their rent, but considering this job barely enabled Nick and me to survive in Van’s spare room, I knew I couldn’t waste any hours. Besides, I hoped that this position would be a stepping stone to a full-time salaried role at Articley.

Ralph reported to Ana, which I thought was odd because Ana was only twenty-eight–at least ten years younger than Ralph–and dressed more like a tomboy teenager than an adult woman, with a makeup-free face, Converse sneakers, and shoulder-length, Amish-colored hair that had probably never seen hairspray or dye.  I could tell she was pretty, and perhaps she knew she was, but she didn’t want anyone else to know she knew, if that makes sense. Despite the fact that I had almost nothing in common with her, Ana gave me hope that perhaps in six years I could be like her--managing people, managing older men no less! My girlboss icon!

It took me about three hours into my first day to be reprimanded for something, which I thought was odd because I started the day terrified of getting in trouble and imagining all the ways it might happen. I had never been fired before and had no reason to believe I would be, but for the past few years, I kept reading terrifying articles about how “there are no jobs for millennials,” and I thought if I lost my job at Articley I’d be thrown into a deep pit of despair and unemployment, culminating in Nick and me both moving back in with our parents and being long distance again. Although Ralph had gone home early that day, he sent me an IM letting me know that someone emailed him to tell him that I had been, in his words, “doing some shoppy shoppy” during work hours. This was completely true and given that I billed hourly, a totally reasonable complaint, but my defense was that I hadn’t been given anything to do, which was also true, and I was afraid to ask anyone for more work. Ralph told me that if I found myself with downtime in the future, that I should “shadow” other people, which I knew was good advice but I was also extremely angry that anyone cared enough to tattle on me. I immediately hated anyone who sat within five feet of me, or whose everyday routes around the office included walking past my desk. It was barely fifteen minutes of shoppy shoppy.

I figured I would start off on a better foot the next day. Unfortunately, Ana pulled me aside within an hour of my arrival at the office to speak to me about my clothing. I wore a long racerback tank top with some kind of embroidered pattern on it, along with black moto leggings and boots. Given Ana’s attire--and the fact that other women in the office didn’t seem to be following any real dress code--I figured this was okay. I mean, if hoodies and jeans were OK, why would leather paneled leggings be a problem?

“I don’t want to make you feel uncomfortable,” Ana said. “I know you’re just wearing what you enjoy. But we had a woman who worked here a little while back named Peyton and she wore, like, hot pants and booty shorts all the time and everyone absolutely hated her. Nobody took her seriously. And eventually, she was fired.”

I had questions. But also, this felt weirdly threatening and I didn’t want to argue with her.

“She wasn’t fired because of her clothing,” Ana said, knowing exactly where my mind was. “She was just...I guess it was her personality. She was a real attention-seeker and she was very unprofessional. I’m not saying you’re like that at all, I’m just saying it’s about perception and when you’re a young woman who works here, you want people to know you as the smart girl, not the pretty girl.”

I could tell Ana thought I was offended, but nothing could have been further from the truth. I was ecstatic that she thought I was in danger of being known as “the pretty girl.” Me? Not being taken seriously on account of my looks? Inject that shit right into my veins!

“So would it be better if I wore, like, a sweater or something?” I asked.

“I was actually thinking of the leggings,” Ana said. “I have this thing where I really just don’t think leggings are pants.”

Fair enough. No more leggings. This would be difficult for me, I realized, because almost all my clothing was of the “going out” variety--bandage dresses I got on clearance, various spandex numbers from Chinese eBay sellers, shiny corset style tops from Charlotte Russe and Wet Seal, black dresses with giant mesh holes in the chest area. I barely even owned jeans, let alone “work pants.” Now I was supposed to buy a work wardrobe on $15/hour? Technically I did have some pants suits my mom bought me from H&M back when I started my job search, but I had a feeling showing up at the office dressed like Hillary Clinton in a sea of hoodies and jeans might have provoked more negative attention than hot pants. At that point, I decided I would dress more like Ana and just wear my lounge clothes to work, because at the very least, Ana couldn’t take issue with it and I wouldn’t be fired for it.

On my third day in the office, Ana told me that everyone was going for a “team building activity” around the city. Not to spoil what happened on this team building event, but I don’t remember a whole lot of it because I spent most of it in a drunken stupor. I do recall that it involved separating the company into color-based teams and then prancing around the city in search of some flag that one of the teams was hiding. I vaguely recall at one point scampering across the street into traffic, gripping said flag and emitting a cackle that I’m assuming included an involuntary snort. But whatever this day entailed, my memory came back to me around five PM, when everyone was at the bar to celebrate. 

First, I made sure to go up to the guy who got me the interview at Articley and thank him for helping me land my “dream job.” He then pointed to a placard on the bar that said “This seat reserved for Chocolate Dick” and to this day I still have no idea what that was about.

At some point I wound up in a conversation with Hank Maslowe, the VP of Articley. Hank was in his early forties, single, with long blonde hair and a tan, like a surfer. I wasn’t sure how long I had been talking to Hank, or what we had been talking about, but the conversation came to an abrupt halt when Ana pulled me aside, like my perpetually present, annoying fairy godmother.

“You need to watch out for Hank.”

Oh, come on. What now?! I was dressed appropriately, wasn’t I? 

“He’s just being nice.”

“Yes,” she said, with a hushed tone. “But Hank has had issues in the past with...other young women at the company who said he made them feel uncomfortable. HR has been involved.”

“But Hank isn’t making me feel uncomfortable.”

“Are you sure? Because if he is, you can tell me and I’ll make sure he doesn’t bother you. Also you don’t want people to think something’s going on between you. It’s all about perception.”

“No, we’re just talking. He’s actually really nice.”

Ana shrugged. Hank and I started going shot for shot, and my only real concern was making sure that Nick knew I was coming home and that he didn’t think it was suspicious. I didn’t particularly care what anyone at the company thought, partially because I was too drunk to worry about everyone’s opinions and also because I figured that if everyone else in the room was allowed to get shitfaced together, I should have the same right, whether I was drinking with Hank or someone else.

Hank actually lived near Van’s place, so he offered to share a cab and pay for it. Of course I wasn’t going to say no to this for two reasons--first of all, I had no money and was otherwise planning on walking home in the middle of the night through a city I barely knew. Also, I thought that a fifteen-minute cab ride with Hank could give me some great one-on-one face time to really solidify my position at Articley. By the time I got into the cab, I was seeing double. I vaguely recall looking at Hank as he faded, his head resting against the car window. I said “Wow, I can’t believe I’m in a cab with you. You’re like, one of the most powerful people at Articley.” Hank didn’t respond.

Despite the fact that this story sounds like it’s about to culminate in some horrible sexual assault, nothing happened. The cab dropped me off at my place where I stumbled into bed with Nick after bragging that I had totally nailed this whole career woman thing because I secured some quality facetime with the VP.

The next day in the office, Ana predictably asked me what had happened the night before with Hank and asked if I was okay. I assured her it was fine and nothing happened. I realized that perhaps other people weren’t as shitfaced as I had assumed, because everyone seemed to be looking at me strangely. Surely, they all thought that I hooked up with Hank. More realistically, they thought Hank assaulted me because apparently this was just a weird quirk of his that everyone knew, but of course nobody did anything about it.

I continued to churn out my articles--new movie to be released in 2012, write an article based on the press release, find some royalty-free images of the stars, post. I was able to do this very quickly, but of course I wanted to work my full hours so I had to extend my hours with acceptable downtime, like eating heaps of raw almonds from the free snack supply in the morning for breakfast, and making way too many cups of tea. At one point, Ana informed me that I would need to stop eating raw almonds at my desk so much because someone else in the office had a nut allergy and apparently broke into hives because of the amount of almonds I kept there. I was really starting to feel like there was no action I could take at work that wouldn’t somehow provoke Ana into letting me know, gently but firmly, that I was wrong.  A few weeks later I ate an apple, left the core on my desk for a few minutes while I went to use the bathroom, and returned to a note written in bubbly popular girl handwriting that said “Do you want fruit flies? THIS is how you get fruit flies!” 

I grew up unpopular and excluded, so I wasn’t sure if my feelings of being judged were just residual anxiety from a typical awkward middle school experience or if people at Articley actually didn’t like me.  At one point we had to do anonymous peer reviews for each other and compare our self-ratings to the ones our peers gave us. I rated myself a 4 out of 5 for most of the fields (technical abilities, work ethic, etc.) and discovered that my anonymous coworkers had ranked me a 2-3. Because nobody was ever nasty to my face, I was in a constant battle with my own mind over whether I was imagining the judgment or not. The peer reviews sent a clear message: I may have been paranoid, but also, I wasn’t imagining it.

Although I didn’t complain to Ana about this--I certainly wouldn’t have wanted to give her any new reasons to pull me aside and dispense advice--she did at one point tell me that if I wanted to get closer to my coworkers, I should join them for lunch.  Don’t even ask, she advised me, just insert yourself into a group during the lunch hour and go to a nearby restaurant with them. I did this once, and I wound up at an artisanal grilled cheese restaurant with six people who didn’t speak to me, and had to pay about $13 for a tiny and unfulfilling gruyere sandwich. It seemed like a sick joke that if I wanted to fit in at a place where I wasn’t paid very much, the “best way” to do so would be spending an hour’s worth of my salary to eat with them. 

I decided to bring in my own lunch more often, simply because I couldn’t afford to eat out, but this came with its own obstacles. I didn’t really own many pieces of Tupperware--at least, none that didn’t actually belong to Van--so I would buy groceries on my lunch hour and bring them into the office once a week, so I could make my lunch in the micro kitchen. My lunch consisted of cold cuts, whole wheat bread, chipotle mayo, and if I was feeling luxurious, avocado. This system worked out really well for me until Hank of all people pulled me into his office and told me he had received numerous complaints about me taking up too much space in the communal fridge with the groceries. 

“Why do you bring so many groceries here anyway?” he asked, more confused than angry.

“I can’t afford to eat out with everyone and it’s easier to just make fresh sandwiches here.”

“And the oatmeal?”

“Well, I like to make oatmeal in the mornings because I used to have almonds in the mornings but then Sal had that allergic reaction.”

It was starting to occur to me that my reputation at Articley had more or less been solidified as a “problem person.” Sure, some of the things I did were genuinely disruptive and strange, but I imagined that if it were someone else eating huge amounts of almonds at their desk--Ana, or Ralph maybe--there wouldn’t be such a big fuss about it. I started wondering which action led to this permanent perception- was it the tank top? The leggings? The shoppy shoppy? It wasn’t my work, that was for sure. Just a week ago, Hank had told me that I saved Articley a tremendous amount of money by churning out articles so quickly. I wanted to believe that all you needed to do in order to get ahead in your career was to perform well. Perhaps this illusion came from playing Sims too often. Apparently there was this whole other game I had to play--one that I was terrible at playing.

Then there was the computer issue. My computer was old and had a big three-inch wide stripe down the center that didn’t show anything (it almost looked like a bright, seizure-inducing barcode.) Given that I was a contractor I wasn’t entitled to receive a work computer so I had to do my work on this piece of junk--which was fine (as was my old iPhone 4) because I cared less about having the right technology and more about avoiding unnecessary trips to the Apple or AT&T stores. Also, I didn’t exactly have the purchasing power to just buy a new computer, although if I held out long enough I could maybe ask my parents for one for Christmas.

Ana noticed the blinking chode of a barcode across my computer screen and offered to get me a loaner for work purposes, which actually turned out to be a great computer--whatever the latest Mac model was at the time.  When I took it home (as I was allowed to do, because I could work from home) Nick saw it and asked if he could borrow it because it was so great.

For some reason, I figured this would be fine. After all, even with the blinking stripe on my old computer, I was able to get my work done. I had never even requested a loaner, Ana pushed it on me. And Nick liked the new computer so much more than I did.  I showed up at a team meeting the next day with my same old blinking stripe computer.

“Didn’t we get you a loaner?” Ana asked.

“Oh yeah,” I said. “I still have it. But I let Nick borrow it. He thinks it’s really cool.”

Ana looked at me incredulously. The entire room looked at me like I had just shit my pants. “That’s a work device. You don’t loan work computers to your boyfriend.” You know what? Fair! But did she consider that Nick really liked it?

Even if I had behaved like a model employee, devoid of raw almonds and leggings, I realized that some cultural differences might have prevented me from truly fitting in. Articley was full of self-styled “geeks” in graphic tees, who owned bobbleheads of various comic characters and who apparently learned nothing from their years of being bullied in high school. I made the mistake of admitting I had never seen Star Wars. A couple of my coworkers faux-fainted and gasped “Oh my God, I feel so old!” I clarified that just because I was the youngest person in the company didn’t mean I hadn’t seen it because I was too young to know what it was--my parents just didn’t let me watch a lot of TV growing up.  Worse yet, I had been operating for almost a year believing that “Boba Fett” was a nickname for a guy who worked in our office named Bob Fetterman.  

Bob Fetterman was not without his own mishap-related story. His girlfriend, Willa, also worked at Articley on the design team. They looked exactly like what you would imagine your average couple of Reddit users to look like, the type of people who have a Harry Potter themed wedding and cut the cake with a broom before having an extremely kinky afterparty with their five-person polycule. Bob was geeky (I mean, who at Articley wasn’t, except for maybe Hank) but sociable and outgoing. Willa, on the other hand, was quiet and unassuming, and she dressed like a retro librarian. I thought they made a pretty cute couple, but I did not think about either of them very often.

Sometimes I would go to the gym before work (a grimy 24 hour fitness, where I once found literal poop on an exercise mat). One day, I was finishing my shower and exiting a sheath full of approximately 329 strains of foot HPV when I walked across the locker room and saw none other than Willa, also fresh out of the shower. We were both in our respective towels. I wasn’t going to say anything, because we weren’t that friendly, but Willa clearly saw me and I thought it would be awkward not to say anything. I said hi to her (from the looks of her face, you’d think I dropped my towel and flung a ping-pong ball out of my anus) and she abruptly walked away.

The next day at work I told Bob that I saw Willa at the gym, wondering if perhaps the weird encounter was all in my head-maybe she didn’t recognize me with wet hair, maybe it wasn’t even really her, I didn’t know. 

“Yeah, she did see you,” Bob said, with a knowing smile.

“Oh, okay, I thought that was her but she didn’t say anything when I said hi.”

Bob scratched the back of his neck and chuckled. “Well, you kind of left out the obvious reason that she said nothing.”

It wasn’t in my head! “Because I was wearing a towel?”

“Uh...yeah? You think?”

What kind of grown woman was so terrified of another woman in a towel? Surely she knew this was a possibility when showering at a crowded gym? I wanted to tell him how stupid this was. But at this point I was genuinely wondering if everything I did was inappropriate and perhaps not only everyone at Articley, but everyone I had ever met found every interaction with me deeply disturbing.

I started to feel especially discouraged when another woman who had the same role as me got promoted to a full-time content specialist, which was the path Ana took to eventually becoming a manager. When I first started at Articley, this would have been encouraging, but Ana squashed that hope at our next team meeting by saying “I know some of you may think that because Julie got promoted, it’s in the cards for you. But it’s not. This is a contractor position. This is not a position that puts you on a path for career development at Articley. It’s meant to build your resume.” It wasn’t surprising that Julie of all people would be promoted even if it wasn’t supposed to happen. Julie was Ana’s favorite, and they spoke to each other in a bizarre falsetto voice, ping-ponging phrases like “ROFLcopter” and “totes adorbs” all day. But moreover, this was the first I had heard of this supposed rule about contractors. After all, didn’t Ana once have my job? Was this not even a rule, but a temporary guideline specifically to prevent me from staying around for too long?

We had a companywide team-building hackathon later that week where they brought in donuts and pizza--we didn’t usually have free lunch, just free snacks, so it was a big deal for me. Given that Nick and I were living off lentils, Chinatown kohlrabi and bulk chicken from the $1.99/lb sale at the supermarket which I transported home in a giant rolling suitcase, I treated free food like a chipmunk harvesting for winter. After loading my plate with donuts, I sat down at my desk. I was a few minutes late, although given that I wasn’t falsifying my timesheets I didn’t think it mattered. After all, it was my choice as to whether I came into the office or not. Within a few minutes, I got a predictable IM, not from Ana but from Julie: just so you know, it doesn’t look very good to come here late and get food before logging in. 

Given that I was told to, quite literally, give up hope on ever getting promoted, I stopped caring quite so much about being liked, or even being considered a good performer. As they told me, the quality of my actual work probably had nothing to do with my trajectory at Articley, so these types of things hardly seemed to matter.

At one point after work (I was staying around late, probably because I got sidetracked listening to parodies of Rebecca Black’s “Friday”) Hank and the CEO, Earl, asked if I wanted to share a cigar with them on the fire escape. Earl was in his fifties, bald and paunchy, married with kids (it was pretty rare for anyone at Articley to have children, although not unheard of.) The fact that they invited me to smoke with them was, of course, extremely fucking weird (although many people had already gone home for the day, there were plenty of other colleagues to share a cigar with who weren’t the twenty-two-year-old contractor) but I saw this as a potential chance to get that promotion that seemed all but impossible to get. Maybe I didn’t need to get in with Ana, I just needed to get in with the head honchos. 

I joined them and took a lusty, confident inhale of the cigar. It should be noted that I had never smoked a cigar or even a cigarette before, although I spent quite a great amount of time smoking weed with Nick after work, using our second hand vaporizer machine with the huge plastic bag attached. I smoked this cigar the way I inhaled from the plastic bag. Apparently this wasn’t what I was supposed to do. Of course, I started hacking, and Hank and Earl laughed at me. But this laughter felt truly endearing and friendly--not like the kind of laughter I’d face if I admitted I didn’t know anything about Flash Gordon.

I forget what we talked about--probably nothing of consequence. When the cigar was done, I packed up my backpack and went home.

A few days later, I got an email from Earl forwarding an invite to a startup thought-sharing summit out in the suburbs. At first I assumed this must be a mistake, but then I realized he had specifically addressed me in a message, asking me to come with him. I couldn’t believe it. Whatever I said to him during our cigar session must have really made an impact. Perhaps all the aggression at this company was completely in my mind. Or maybe it wasn’t, but none of it mattered because I was best friends with the CEO.

I forwarded Earl’s message to Ralph and Ana and told them “Just FYI, I’ll be leaving work a few minutes early today to accompany Earl to this summit.”  That would show Ana for constantly scolding me; I was so above her level. Within five minutes, Ralph had pulled me aside into the micro kitchen, an extremely concerned look on his face.

“You don’t have to go to this,” he said.

“Why wouldn’t I want to go?”

“I just think it might be a little inappropriate that you were invited,” he said.

Ana appeared. Because of course.

“Earl should not be inviting you to this,” she said.

“Maybe I’m mistaken,” I said. “But I thought Earl invited me because he’s happy with the quality of my work?”

Ralph sighed. “I should clarify. Your work is good. But Earl doesn’t know that and he hasn’t been told. Neither has Hank. That’s not why he invited you.”

“Well, why else would he invite me? It’s not like we have anything in common.”

Ana and Ralph shared an all-knowing look. Oh, right, I was the Monica Lewinsky of the office. Everyone thought I boned Hank in a cab. And now everyone thought Earl was going to abduct me and molest me over pigs in a blanket and wine in plastic cups at this startup summit, which of course would be my fault. I paused for a second, wondering if they were right. If they were right, it just seemed so outrageous. Earl knew I had a boyfriend. I knew Earl was married. Earl was more than old enough to be my dad. Earl was taking me to a public summit where people he and his wife knew were likely to attend. What exactly was his endgame?

I had already told Earl I was going, so I couldn’t get out of it. I had a pit in my stomach, as I tried to figure out if there was some benevolent reason for the invitation. I left work with him that day as we embarked on an hour-long drive to the summit. I figured if there was any ulterior motive, I would put an end to it right away by telling him more about Nick. 

“My boyfriend loves startup stuff. I think I told you about him. His name is Nick. He has a really early stage startup near our office.”

“You really love that boyfriend, huh?” Earl put his polarized sunglasses on to block the sun glaring through the windshield. I didn’t have any with me.

“Well obviously, we live together and I hope we get married someday. Aren’t you married? How long have you been married?”

“A while.”

“So, any good career advice? You’ve been working in this business so much longer than I have and I’m sure you have a lot to tell me.”

Earl paused for a while. “I have some advice for you,” he said. “Just don’t give a fuck.”

This would have been good advice, given that I was always worried about people not liking me, but would it have done me any good at Articley? If anything, I suffered from somehow caring too little despite caring all the time. It was always the seemingly banal stuff that got me in trouble, stuff I never would have been able to foresee (okay, except the computer thing, but hey, I just wanted to be a good girlfriend.) If I truly stopped giving a fuck, did whatever my version of “inviting the twenty-two-year-old office floozie to a business summit for no reason” was- perhaps making out with Willa next time I saw her at the gym- would anything materially change? 

We spent the rest of the drive in silence. When we arrived at the summit, I expected a fun cocktail hour with appetizers, perhaps an opportunity to meet friends. But my face dropped when I saw a room full of people the same age as Earl, mostly men, moseying around in a sixty-six-degree white conference room with absolutely no food. I was starving. Then, before anyone even had time to have a real conversation, we were separated into “breakout groups” to discuss things that were so boring and over my head that I can’t even vaguely recall what they were. Earl and I weren’t even in the same session, so I was left to my own devices to discuss some gray, unknown topic with five other Earl lookalikes who must have wondered why I was there, and why I was wearing a sweatshirt with sentient, one-toothed, smiling hotdogs on it. The night was never-ending and I was too far away from the city to leave on my own. This was also before Uber. I had to wait for Earl to drive me home, and the drive home was somehow even more awkward than the drive there. I didn’t think to ask him, “So, what the fuck did you bring me here?” or find out if his intention wasn’t to have an affair with me, what exactly his plan was--certainly, if his plan was to have an affair, there would have been sexier, more discreet places to take me. 

The next day, Earl resigned from Articley. He truly didn’t give a fuck.

When I finally left, it obviously wasn’t my own decision, but it wasn’t as traumatic as I always assumed it would be. With no hope of ever getting promoted, coworkers who thought I was routinely being Eiffel Towered between Hank and Earl, and a boss constantly policing my food and clothing like a horrifying boyfriend in a Reddit post, I wasn’t sure I even wanted to stay there. But I managed to screw up one more time before leaving. Always an open book, I told the few coworkers I liked that I was leaving and it wasn’t my choice. Perplexed, they asked me if I was being fired. 

“No,” I said. “They just needed to get rid of someone on the team. Downsizing, I guess.”

To be clear, I didn’t know what downsizing meant. I thought it was an umbrella term for anyone who was terminated without having actually done anything wrong (although in my case I suppose that was debatable.)  On my last day, Ana pulled me aside one more time to inform me that I should stop telling people we were downsizing because I had started a panic among employees that they were about to be laid off. 

And later that day, the problem was gone.
