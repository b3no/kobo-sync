On "Hot Autism"

Even with autism, hotness is still a social asset, not a liability.

Cartoons Hate Her
Jul 27
∙
Paid
 
READ IN APP
 

Autism is a hot topic on the Internet (or perhaps just my corner of it). Questions abound: are people pretending to be autistic “for clout?” How can you tell if someone is autistic or just really annoying? Is there an autism accent? Is the “autistic Barbie” offensive to children with autism too severe to play with Barbies? Is it “gentrifying autism” for people with level one or milder autism to frame autism as a superpower when it can be a disability for many others? Are autistic women “women?”

But I’m here to talk about a different question: the question of “Hot Autism.”

Obviously, any person with autism has the capacity to be hot, but in particular, there seem to be a lot of conversations about hot women with autism—more specifically, the idea that the combination of being hot and having autism makes the social consequences of autism worse because people can’t reconcile hotness with social weirdness. Some people have postulated that beauty becomes a curse when combined with autism, or that people viscerally hate hot autistic women in a way they don’t hate other autistic people.

Other folks respond to this content by accusing the creators of lying about having autism, or insinuating that if someone is not behaving exactly like a character from Love on the Spectrum, that their autism is being faked for attention. I disagree with this. I believe that these women have autism, and I understand that they probably face some degree of skepticism from people who have a particular stereotype of autistic people in their head which requires conventional unattractiveness.

What I don’t believe is that being hot makes their social problems any worse than those of non-hot autistic people.

Before I get into it, a land acknowledgement of sorts: I don’t know if I have autism. I have yet to be formally evaluated for it. Lots of people on the Internet think I have it, to the point that when I’m annoying, total strangers defend me with, “It’s not her fault she’s autistic!” Most of these people tend to be under the age of fifty, because every time I’ve broached this topic with a boomer, they say something along the lines of, “No, you’re just annoying.” Many such cases.

But anyway, my only confirmed diagnoses are OCD and ADHD, both diagnosed in my teen years. I also have “sensory difficulties” with noises and textures which I always assumed were part of the OCD/ADHD combo. I have never had an easy time reading social cues. Up until very recently, I mistakenly believed that I had been evaluated for autism and that it had been ruled out because I had seen many psychologists as a child for developmental disorders, until I learned from the Christianity On The Spectrum podcast that ADHD (diagnosed as “ADD” in the nineties) was considered mutually exclusive with autism, so if you were diagnosed with ADD, people did not consider autism a possibility. This error was made among many girls in the nineties, which could explain the wave of late-diagnosed autistic millennial women, many of whom report special interests in American Girl Dolls, historical fashion, and the Sims. (Autistic or not, this was me.)

But anytime a large segment of millennial women start doing something, people find it insufferable. For example, certain autoimmune diseases are most common in women, not because women are hypochondriacs but because we just have different immune systems which make us more susceptible (see: lupus patients trending 80-90% female). Bu invariably these diseases eventually get branded as fake, attention-seeking and “trendy.” So while I know it’s easy to ignore the late-diagnosed millennial autistic woman as a histrionic clout-chaser, there are genuine reasons why a lot of mildly autistic girls in the nineties went undiagnosed (perhaps including myself. TBD.)

Another thing about mildly autistic or otherwise neurodivergent people: they can be off-putting! Therefore, it’s entirely unsurprising that content about autism or other forms of neurodivergence can come off as annoying. Being annoying is…part of it.

Take, for example, this video where a woman explains what it’s like to have autism as a woman, and half of the examples of things people say to her are, “But you’re so hot.” Oh please. Immediately, I am put off. It is unbecoming for anyone—hot or not, male or female—to talk about how everyone thinks they’re hot, even though she’s clearly pretty enough that I’m sure it’s happened. But, need I remind myself, being unaware of how off-putting this can be is a symptom of autism! It should not invalidate her diagnosis, only strengthen our belief in it. (I’m also aware you can be annoying without being autistic, but if someone has an autism diagnosis and behaves in a socially inept way, I don’t think it’s that much of a mystery as to why they’re being annoying.)

I found myself in this trap a while ago. I went on the Search Engine podcast several months ago after PJ Vogt saw my article about struggling to get good turnout at my parties. Essentially, the episode was a really fun deep dive into why people don’t like me. Unfortunately, we had to condense about ten years worth of social issues into an hour-long episode, which meant some things were cut for time and some timelines were unintentionally obfuscated. For example, there was some hilarious material about me forcing people to listen to my musical impressions at parties (which paired nicely with the Trump bit) but a lot of people who listened did not pick up on the fact that I stopped doing this among real-life people more than ten years ago. (I’m aware it’s giving, “Made out with a hotdog? That was one time!”)

But perhaps the biggest mistake I made occurred after the episode. I read the Reddit thread discussing it (apparently, contrary to human nature, I was not supposed to be curious about hundreds of strangers discussing my personal defects) and a couple people brought up my appearance (at least what’s known of it, namely my body) as a potential reason I might have trouble connecting with women. Just my luck, the Reddit comment most explicitly making this declaration has since been deleted, which really makes me look insane, but the comments in response to it make it clear what it was saying, and it was one of several. This same topic came up on Substack too, more frequently, as an explanation for why I’ve struggled to make female friends.

So naturally, taking this at face value, I decided to address this issue in my followup post. I actually specifically pointed out that I do not see myself as a “hot girl,” but even if I were, I don’t think it’s been a social detriment for me. But apparently, even acknowledging that some people said that a headless version of me was hot was a huge social faux pas! Sure enough, a new thread popped up about the episode on Reddit, where a highly-upvoted comment noted my post, disagreed that “anyone” ever said I was hot, and accused me of lying and being delusional. Another person said that such behavior confirmed I was a “walking red flag” and explains why nobody likes me. It’s moments like these that I’m glad I’m banned from posting on Reddit, because I would have pulled up the receipts and crashed the fuck out, fully humiliating myself in the process no matter how correct I technically was.

But it doesn’t matter if you actually are hot or not, or if people actually ever said it or not. People do not want you to admit it’s true, or even admit that some people think it’s true. The only socially acceptable thing for me to do would be to not address this topic at all, no matter who brought it up. This doesn’t make a whole lot of sense, but it’s accurate. And despite this story illustrating my own lack of social aptitude, I find myself put off by people describing themselves as hot.

It’s therefore unsurprising that when autistic women make videos blaming their hotness for their social ills (or implying that being autistic but not hot would make things easier for them) that the behavior comes off not as autistic, but as annoying. Unfortunately, making annoying content without realizing how annoying it is….well, that’s something an autistic person would do. That doesn’t mean I agree with their statements (being hot has absolutely made life easier for them, they just don’t realize it) or that it’s good content, only that it doesn’t make me think they don’t have autism.

Charitably to these women, perhaps they are correct that their hotness, in some people’s eyes, could “override” the autism and their behavior would simply be branded as “insufferable.” If nobody believes they’re autistic on account of their hotness, nobody will accept autism as an excuse for social ineptitude. What might come off as “autistic person who can’t do two-way conversation” could be reframed as “narcissistic diva who needs to be the center of attention.” Many such cases. However, clearly the main issue at play is the autism, not the hotness, and they are probably still accommodated and excused quite a bit more than unattractive people with autism.

A hot autistic woman could be accepted socially at first because of her appearance, only to be expelled back into social isolation once people discover, via weird behavior, that she’s autistic. But this is, sadly, a bit of a “check your privilege” situation. Not that you can’t be annoyed about it, but that in the grand scheme of autistic people, being a hot, mildly autistic woman is still a significantly easier thing to be than any other type of autistic person. Yes, you will face social struggles, but at least your looks earn you a foot in the door. You know what’s worse than hearing, “But you’re so hot!” when you tell someone you’re autistic? Hearing, “Uhh…yeah, we can tell.”

Men notoriously accept quirks from hot women more than from unattractive or even average-looking women. Half of the time a video of “cute” female behavior goes viral, it’s a hot woman doing something that would be branded “cringe” if she weren’t hot. A hot woman being goofy is expressing “childlike whimsy,” and an ugly woman being goofy is just gross and annoying.

But what about women? Surely, it’s the women who exclude hot girls because they’re jealous or threatened, right? I’m sure it happens. There are some petty and jealous women out there. But for the most part, hot women have more friends, not fewer. For every woman who is threatened, there are three more who are drawn to your beauty or who assume positive things about you because of it, otherwise known as the Halo Effect.

For women who are autistic, socially awkward or neurodivergent in some other way, they run up against a consistent problem which can reinforce the belief that their hotness is the problem, which I’ve covered before. It goes a bit like this:

  • Because they are pretty, or even just normie-looking, women are initially warm towards them.

  • Quickly, the women—who are very good at noticing small social errors—pick up that something is “off” and start excluding them.

  • Men are also drawn to them for their looks, but are not as good at noticing that something is “off,” or perhaps don’t care. Even when men aren’t looking for sex, they just enjoy being around attractive women.

  • The woman will perceive this experience as “Women don’t like me because I’m hot” or “Women can’t handle me being hot and weird.” Their evidence is that men don’t have a problem with them. The reality is that their looks were actually their foot in their door with both men and women, but their weirdness prevented them from getting further with women despite having little to no effect on their interactions with men.

It’s easy to see how hot women who don’t easily understand social cues could experience this, time and time again, and conclude that people would be more accepting of them if they were plain. I’ll concede that, at the very least, people would be more willing to accept that they were autistic. But I do not believe things would be any easier for them socially. Go ask a plain or ugly autistic person how many people are trying to be their friend, or better yet, trying to date them, and see what you find.

Another unfortunate habit associated with “hot autism” is that many of these women claim to have a form of autism that actually, paradoxically, makes them really good at social skills, to the point that they are so good at masking (ie: concealing autism) that they are incapable of “unmasking.” I have even seen content attempting to guide “hot autistic girls” through an “unmasking” journey. If it’s that hard to “unmask,” when masking itself is usually a major challenge for autistic people which they learn over years, I will admit I have questions! And yet, I am conflicted. I’m not saying these people don’t have autism, but…it’s kind of like complaining about how nobody believes you have OCD because your obsession is “not having obsessions.” My most charitable read is that they do have autism, and saying “I can’t stop masking because my special interest is making everyone like me” is a very socially inept and annoying thing to say, furthering evidence that they are at least neurodivergent in some way.

It’s hard to talk about this issue without resorting to the Oppression Olympics. Hot women with a special interest in socializing are in a privileged position in the autism world, but are still dealing with a developmental disorder with other symptoms and issues, and they’re allowed to talk about it. A socially awkward guy with level one autism whose special interest is 1930s architecture is still more “privileged” than someone who is nonverbal. This is not a game I care to play and I don’t think anyone else should play it either. But at the same time, I refuse to buy that being a hot, autistic woman who is “too good at masking” is, within the autism world, a uniquely difficult experience. People do not dislike her because she’s hot. They dislike her because she’s autistic, and her hotness is one of the only things forcing people to tolerate her when they might otherwise not.

But, if I may: blaming one’s hotness and great social skills for a uniquely difficult autism experience is such a socially inept thing to do that it should quell any doubt that the person in question has autism.
