The real reason nobody feels good about technological progress

The internet is a small miracle that in practice we mostly use to watch short-form video.

Jordan Dworkin’s micro-site Ordinary Abundance has created a surprising stir on the internet. The site walks viewers through a modest contemporary apartment and reveals it to be full of contrivances that would strike our ancestors as almost impossibly delightful. Some of the initial reaction was over-the-top enthusiastic, which in turn prompted a level of negativity that took me aback.

The haters’ reactions did make me take another look, though, and on closer inspection something struck me about the apartment: It doesn’t contain a television.

There’s a phone on the coffee table, but it’s discussed as a communications device. Dworkin quotes “The Story of the Telegraph” from 1858, which says that “of all the marvellous achievements of modern science, the Electric Telegraph is transcendently the greatest and most serviceable to mankind. It is a perpetual miracle, which no familiarity can render commonplace.” And it is of course true not only that the telegraph was received as a game-changing miracle in the 19th century but that the ability to text your friends or family or sources at zero marginal cost is far superior to early telegraphy.

Dworkin also mentions the minor miracles of photography, art prints, and streaming music.

The music one is striking.

In 1888, Edward Bellamy’s book “Looking Backward” was a huge hit and was especially influential among socialist intellectuals. It’s a science fiction book about a utopian society that arose by the year 2000, featuring a universal retirement age of 45, equal incomes, and a shorter workweek for the people who do dirty, dangerous, or unpleasant jobs.

One aspect of the utopian society he describes is an apparatus that can make a phone call to one of several channels, each of which features a band or orchestra playing music. He describes this on-demand music as “the limit of human felicity.” That is not, mechanically speaking, how FM radio works, but radio did basically give us the capability he was discussing long before the year 2000. And then by the 2010s we had Spotify and its competitors, providing a form of music on demand that is far superior to what Bellamy regarded as the limit.

In terms of revealed preferences, though, music on the radio is absolutely felicity-mogged by video.

And while in the 21st century, television as a literal technology is under significant competitive pressure from the internet, that’s mostly because it turns out you can use the internet to watch lots of videos. But few would argue that we exceeded Bellamy’s utopia because today we can while away hours at home alone watching Instagram Reels.

Scrolling alone

When Jerusalem Demsas and I read Robert Putnam’s classic “Bowling Alone” for our podcast, I was struck by the central role television plays in his causal argument despite it being relatively peripheral to the analysis.

But as he documents, the lion’s share of the time Americans stopped spending on the communal activities that he believes build valuable social capital was instead spent watching television. He also shows that there are powerful television-related cohort effects. The people who were already adults in the late 1940s bought televisions in large numbers and enjoyed them but did not have the same pattern of habitual television-watching as the Baby Boom and Generation X cohorts did. He also notes that at around the time he was writing, middle-class households were starting to own multiple televisions — transforming TV watching from a family activity into a solitary pursuit.

Putnam says all this but doesn’t focus on it in his prescriptive work.

To speculate, I think that’s in part because complaining about television’s impact on society, though widespread in the second half of the 20th century, was also a bit of a snobbish crank thing. It would have made the book much less original if it simply converged on the snob/crank diagnosis that really everyone ought to watch less TV.

On the other hand, if you really did think that people watching too much television was a major public-policy problem, the good news is that there are more straightforward policy solutions to that problem than the vague ideas he offers for bolstering social capital — you could use tax policy to make television-watching more expensive, as we do with cigarettes. Of course, if people watched television less, they could just sit at home reading books. But Putnam’s evidence suggests that, in practice, if people watched less television, they would spend considerably more time visiting with friends and joining clubs.

That being said, I think Dworkin’s site primes the intuition that there’s actually something special about television.

Imagine a world with all the modern-day contrivances but no TV or streaming video. It’s true, per Putnam, that people would surely reallocate much of their current video-watching time to activities that build social capital. But if you imagine a guy who’s not really one for clubs and likes to spend his evenings relaxing in his tasteful modernist chair and reading a book off his well-appointed shelf while enjoying Bellamy’s ultimate felicity of in-home music, that sounds pretty good. This guy is a bit of an introvert and maybe an intellectual, and there’s nothing wrong with that.

What you worry about is the guy who dropped out of the bowling league — or more realistically never thought to join any league of any kind — because all he really wants to do with his downtime is watch YouTube Shorts.

Life in the information age

I appreciate that I cannot really prove “books and music good, television bad” with a level of rigor that would work for a philosophy term paper. I’m intrigued by the Dworkin piece because I think that rather than make an explicit argument, it taps into a tacit understanding. If you want to get people to appreciate the sublime aspects of banal life in the contemporary developed world, you really do not want to draw attention to how much we all, in practice, enjoy watching streaming video content.

This family living in a slum neighborhood in contemporary Nairobi is enjoying a level of entertainment abundance that would have struck people of Bellamy’s day as genuinely unimaginable. But a description of life here would not have made for a very good utopian novel.

Americans, of course, have much higher living standards than Kenyans do. Our ability to consume streaming video on our smartphones is pretty similar, but we have vastly higher rates of access to things like clean water, large dwellings, and abundant food.

But if you think about economic changes since Putnam’s book came out, you understand they’ve been concentrated specifically in the “computers and internet” space.

At the time the book was published, many were incredibly optimistic about the impact of the net on society and daily life. Putnam is skeptical of these claims and, in a lot of ways, that’s the aspect of the book that holds up best. In terms of the course of my own life, I will say that as an enthusiastic early adopter of the internet, it initially struck me that one of the very best things about it was its tendency to displace television. People were on message boards. They were writing blogs. They were sending emails. They were reading and writing and communicating instead of just watching.

One of the most overlooked aspects of recent American history is the very genuine idealism that accompanied the early days of the internet: Al Gore talking about an “information superhighway” and Google’s mission “to organize the world’s information and make it universally accessible and useful” and Mark Zuckerberg’s desire to “connect the world” weren’t bullshit.

Lots of people really thought this stuff was going to dramatically change the world for the better.

And in some very real ways it has. But those ways also turn out to be pretty limited. The dominant business model for internet companies is to serve streaming video to people and/or to sell the devices that they stream video on.

A few months ago I saw a Meta ad about people back in their hometown for Thanksgiving using Facebook to organize a spontaneous meetup with friends at a local bar. It’s really been a very long time since keeping in touch with friends was the main use case for that app. But it’s a reminder that in some sense the company wants to be something much more wholesome than one of several vendors of algorithmically distributed short-form video.

And yet that primarily is what people are doing on the internet.

As amazing as all the wonders Dworkin recounts are, the really big technological progress over the past generation has been in video watching. Video went from low-definition to high-definition to 4K. It went from broadcast to cable to streaming to short form. It went from a family television in the living room to a television in everyone’s pocket.

By revealed preference, people really, really enjoy this. But it doesn’t make for a very appealing vignette.

Too much felicity

Obviously we’re not going to un-invent the smartphone or streaming video or televisions.

But I really think people ought to take seriously the idea that we’ve been in a 75-year rolling moral panic about the influence of television on society and the critics have more or less been correct all along.

To some extent it is, in fact, good that people have obtained ever greater ability to watch videos that they enjoy. People really do enjoy it! And it’s genuinely great that things like being on an airplane or recovering from illness have become less tedious and boring. But every parent I know wrestles in different ways with the fact that today’s iPads with their bottomless well of games and streaming content produce a disturbingly large amount of felicity, such that left to their own devices, kids would essentially never get bored of plugging in. Responsible adults are seeking technological solutions to lock themselves out of omnipresent streaming.

And I don’t want to exempt myself from this critique at all. I am, obviously, a Twitter addict. But worse than that, I’m someone who last Sunday at 10 p.m. was tired and ready to go to bed, but I clicked on a funny video that someone sent me and then scrolled around to some other stuff, and, lo and behold, I didn’t head to bed until 11:30. When it was time to wake up on Monday, I was sluggish and tired.

This is not the end of the world, but it is the reality that a large share of 21st-century technological progress has gone into these compulsive content engines that do not make for appealing vignettes about modern life, even though (by definition) we really enjoy them in the moment.

Share

People who deactivate Facebook and Instagram in an experimental setting end up happier. Students say you’d have to pay them a fair amount to get them to quit TikTok, but they would be willing to pay out of pocket in order to get themselves and all their friends off the app.

We’re obviously not going to make it illegal to watch short-form videos.

But in a world where the government collects taxes, I think it would make a lot of sense to look at ideas like taxing digital ad revenue (subscription models encourage more mindful consumption) and other parts of the compulsive video industry and shift the burden off other things we currently tax.

I was reading a story in the local paper about raising money to support a candlepin bowling operation in Ellsworth, Maine. We should be trying, on the margin, to make it cheaper and easier and more lucrative to be involved in bowling or going to a restaurant or playing ping-pong or mini-golf or exercising and to make it more expensive and less convenient and less lucrative to be in the “glowing screen, home alone” business.

The precise details of these policies obviously matter, but I think the details can be worked out if we acknowledge as a society that there’s an actual problem of too large a share of recent progress being channeled into activities that we don’t feel good about spending more and more time on.
