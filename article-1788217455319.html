The many ways to build more housing

A judge blocks HUD and California stacks up YIMBY wins. Commercial conversions and A.I. show different paths to more housing, while rent control gets in the way.

Last week, a federal judge blocked the U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development’s July plan to overhaul the Fair Housing Initiatives Program. The overhaul would have redirected most of fiscal year 2025’s funding (about $56 million) away from more than 100 fair-housing nonprofits and toward just five grants for groups aligned with the Trump administration.

In his ruling, Judge Myong Joun expressed skepticism of the HUD plan, concluding that the proposed changes did not accurately address HUD’s stated reasons for making them, which included modernizing the department to broaden participation and funding organizations that align with the administration’s priorities.

“This pithy description falls woefully short of the mark in both form and substance,” Joun wrote in response to HUD’s argument, according to reporting from the New York Times.

The judge’s ruling comes just weeks after another federal judge in Rhode Island blocked HUD’s attempt to change rules that relate to homelessness grants, suggesting a recent pattern of courts pushing back on hasty overhauls by the department.

Two new California housing bills move to the governor’s desk

The California state legislature on Sunday unanimously passed State Senator Scott Wiener’s latest bill, SB 677, which closes off two loopholes that are commonly used to delay already-approved housing development.

Under the current rules, even after a housing project is approved, there are often additional steps that can be appealed or challenged, resulting in stalled construction, cost increases, or loss of financing. Developers often draw up formal maps dividing a property into lots or units, and Wiener’s bill limits the public’s opportunity to use that map-drawing process as a post-approval chance to challenge the project, curtailing third-party appeals of subdivision maps for infill housing, California YIMBY writes. The bill also attempts to prevent delays related to federal financing for affordable housing by giving the state treasurer the authority to grant approval for federally funded projects.

SB 677 now heads to Governor Gavin Newsom alongside another major housing measure from Assemblymember Matt Haney.

Haney’s bill requires California’s seven largest transit-rich cities — Los Angeles, San Diego, San Jose, San Francisco, Sacramento, Oakland, and Long Beach — to designate transit districts where standardized density requirements would apply, including a 150-foot height baseline, with buildings of 450 feet or more allowed in at least a quarter of each district, California YIMBY writes.

The bill, AB 2074, also asks the California Housing Finance Agency to evaluate construction loan programs to help determine how to realize the projects eventually proposed under the bill.

Meanwhile, existing housing bills are being tested in California

California’s existing density incentives are already shaping projects on the ground, but some are running into pushback.

In San Francisco, plans for an apartment tower that was proposed earlier this year on the site of a former movie theater have expanded from 64 to 172 units and nine to 12 stories following two state laws that streamline approval and Mayor Daniel Lurie’s “Family Zoning” plan to allow denser development in the area, the San Francisco Chronicle reports. The new proposal includes 7,000 square feet of retail space and 89 parking spots — an increase from the originally proposed 28 spots. Local leaders such as Supervisor Myrna Melgar, who supports the project, are bracing for a response.

“People are going to lose their minds,” she told the Chronicle. “This is going to be my Marina Safeway.”

Down the coast, in San Diego, the proposed Turquoise Tower (Project Vela) in Pacific Beach envisions 23 stories and 214 units but is still under review. It would be taller than surrounding buildings in the neighborhood and conflict with the city’s longstanding 30-foot coastal height guideline. U.S. Representative Scott Peters, who typically supports housing development, has come out strongly against the project in a letter to San Diego Mayor Todd Gloria. He argues that the tower will create real traffic, safety, and infrastructure concerns and that state development incentives are being unreasonably activated to produce a far overbuilt result that will put neighbors on the defensive against any future proposed projects.

The YIMBY Democrats of San Diego County — which Representative Peters helped found with Mayor Gloria in 2018 — have submitted their own letter to Gloria and the city council arguing that the city ought to approve the project because the state’s Density Bonus Law allows qualifying affordable-housing projects to exceed the 30-foot coastal height limit, according to a copy of the letter provided to Slow Boring. The letter urges the city to stand firm against neighborhood opposition over neighborhood character or the development’s height rather than use those objections to deny or stall a project that state law requires the city to process ministerially.

Meanwhile, one Bay Area housing project that was approved two decades ago is standing unbuilt rather than as the 158 condos it was meant to become. The San Francisco Chronicle writes about how neighborhood pushback and financial complications led to the unfinished eyesore.

How two different housing markets are approaching commercial conversions

A report from the New York Times found that New York City is increasingly relying on office-to-apartment conversions to address its housing shortage. The Times found that in 2026, the city permitted 74 conversion projects with the potential to contribute a cumulative 10,100 homes — about one-third of all newly permitted housing. The lackluster post-Covid return-to-office combined with a tax abatement that gives developers who broke ground by the end of June a 35-year break is driving the surge. One product of the surge was a July episode in which the former Pfizer building began to collapse while undergoing conversion.

The city is investigating the Pfizer incident and has slowed down or temporarily restricted work on some other projects. Meanwhile, its data suggests that conversions are not experiencing more violations than comparable new construction, the Times reports. City officials maintain that this kind of housing is important to pursue if New York is serious about closing its housing gap.

Since 2023, the city has permitted nearly 20,000 units to be created through conversion.

While New York is repurposing its empty buildings, another city is reinvigorating vacant land and buildings to spur economic development and generate incomes needed to support its housing needs. Cleveland’s Site Readiness for Good Jobs Fund, which converts unused industrial real estate into productive use, has raised $80 million to clean up and rehabilitate abandoned land, with an end goal of facilitating more than 25,000 jobs, Forbes reports. Cleveland hopes the fund will promote new housing development and sustainably and effectively bring dollars back to a city that has faced deindustrialization, job loss, and divestment. The broader effort includes rezoning, lower permit fees, tax incentives, and public-private partnerships. One project on the horizon is a modular-home manufacturing facility, which is slated to create more than 150 jobs while producing pre-approved designs.

The broader effort is to create neighborhoods where residents can walk from their homes to work and necessary services.

Artificial intelligence makes an early impact on permitting speeds

A new $200 million per year federal Innovation Fund that was created as part of the 21st Century ROAD to Housing Act to motivate local efforts to speed up permitting, combined with $3 million in HUD grants for localities to adopt automated permitting systems for building codes, could help expand the use of artificial intelligence tools that are already making the permit process faster and more efficient in some cities, Stateline reports.

Rather than using A.I. to replace city employees, Stateline writes that cities such as Honolulu, Seattle, Denver, and Louisville are using automated tools to review permit applications and check for missing information and errors before they go to a human for review. Early results suggest that the new process is reducing review and correction times, since applications are less likely to be sent back multiple times, which can add weeks or months to projects.

Rent stabilization in Maryland has resulted in decades of underbuilding

Maryland’s oldest rent stabilization law is under review by the Takoma Park City Council after a study by the consulting group RSG Inc. found that though it preserves racial and income diversity and reduces rent growth and turnover, the law has resulted in more code violations and lower housing quality, as well as stalled multifamily construction after the late 1970s.

A landlord group cited by the Baltimore Banner called the policy an “abject failure,” while tenant advocates, including former Councilmember Marc Elrich, warn against editing the law too much lest it become utterly useless.

“You have tools to deal with the problem that you’re trying to identify,” Elrich said at a hearing in June, the Banner reports. “I would ask you not to sacrifice the other stuff in order to get there.”

Options for addressing the policy’s negative effects include streamlining the landlord petition process, extending tax exemptions for new buildings from five years to 10 or more, and replacing the tie to the Consumer Price Index for annual rent increases on stabilized properties with minimum and maximum allowable increases within which landlords could adjust rents.
