You can’t get a masters in publishing in Canada anymore

A guest SHuSH on the unwinding of Simon Fraser’s MPub

Editor’s note: Universities have been deprioritizing the humanities for some time, and the Canadian book publishing scene has problems of its own, so it’s perhaps no surprise that Canada’s only master of publishing program at Simon Fraser University is winding down. Its tragic, nonetheless. We still have undergraduate programs at York and Toronto Metropolitan universities, as well as Centennial and Humber colleges, but SFU’s high levels of practical skill development and research leadership will be sorely missed. Rowland Lorimer, one of the program’s founders, has graciously shared his recollections of how the program started, how it operated, and what it accomplished. kw

On July 6, 2026, in a press release, Simon Fraser University (SFU) announced that the fall 2026 intake to the Master of Publishing program (MPub) was to be the final group to join approximately 400 former graduates and other attendees of the program.

The shutdown came as no surprise. The program had ceased to attract a solid majority of Canadian would-be entrants to Canadian book and magazine publishing. It had also morphed into a short but fairly standard academic master’s degree program that set aside the founding professional tenets of the program.

Regrettable though the closure may be, the MPub deserves to be noted and celebrated. Its first twenty years were an outstanding success story. This short piece is my attempt to give a sense of that success.

Simon Fraser University’s leadership in publishing research and education was spearheaded by Ann Cowan and me. Ann came to publishing through her master’s thesis at Carleton University, where she focused on The Canadian Forum, a notable, modernist-oriented, Keynesian-leaning cultural magazine founded in 1920; it underpinned the Canadian Commonwealth Federation (CCF) and hence the NDP. Shortly after her 1977 arrival at SFU, Ann launched a series of well-received, writing- and publishing-oriented, industry-taught, professional development courses.

My background and focus on publishing complemented Ann’s. In the early 1970s, I was one of eight faculty members who formed a communication department. There, my teaching and research focused on book publishing and mass communication. In publishing, I assessed the adequacy of elementary school textbooks as socialization tools for Canadian children, given that the production model, for the most part, consisted of adaptations of US melting-pot-inspired textbooks published by Canadian branch plants of American companies. My teaching focused on introducing book publishing as a cultural industry and government policies.

The MPub emerged amid a cultural awakening and a post-war baby boom. SFU itself opened its doors in 1965 with curricula that encompassed various forms of arts and culture. Outside universities, by the late sixties, adventurous, self-confident authors and other entrepreneurs were establishing independent book and magazine publishing firms. Their aim was to give status to the writings of their friends and colleagues, and to other leading lights of their generation.

In recognition of the era’s changing social realities, the Canada Council established a grant program in the early 1970s for Canadian-owned book publishers to foster the development of a writing community of Canadian authors and their reports and imaginings in written form. Next came an Ontario Royal Commission on Book Publishing, which reported in 1972 and 1973. The Commission’s report emphasized the need to protect and foster Canadian culture by securing the economic survival of Canadian-owned publishing houses. The federal government was slower to act. At first, it provided some tentative support measures that the industry dubbed “welfare grants” because, while they kept firms from bankruptcy, they were insufficient to encourage business development.

The emerging independent book publishers were confident, insistent, and assertive. In 1980, the pressure was palpable. The Association of Canadian Publishers published Canadian Publishing: An Industrial Strategy for the Preservation and Development in the Eighties. Lobbying was intense. In 1985, thanks to lobbying and a prescient minister, Marcel Masse, the Mulroney government brought forward the Baie-Comeau Agreement. “Baie-Comeau,” as the publishers called it, was a far more generous publishing support program, funded with an initial $75 million. The agreement also closed off foreign investment in book-oriented businesses, and, with great courage, called for the patriation of foreign-owned subsidiaries. Later, in 1989, a further $110 million was added to the pot. It would be safe to call this a national initiative.

At the same time, both Ann Cowan and I were busy; she expanded her course offerings taught by industry members and made international contacts, and I worked on textbooks. In selecting industry members for her many courses, Ann showed impressive insight in identifying outstanding professionals, which led to her becoming a trusted advocate for writing and publishing in BC, Canada, and internationally.

Following a brief sojourn in late 1981 in Australia, where I extended my research into Australian schoolbooks, and based at the Canadian Learning Materials Centre at Dalhousie University in Halifax, I undertook a national research project that resulted in The Nation in the Schools: Wanted a Canadian Education (OISE, 1984). The book painted a national picture of school curricula across Canada that woefully neglected Canadian realities throughout the provincial curricula.

By 1987, Ann and I were working together exploring the opportunity to create a national centre for publishing research and training. With informal industry and government support, and with colleagues, we formed a research centre, the Canadian Centre for Studies in Publishing (CCSP). Later our “centre” became an “institute.” Our confidence came from our close working relationship with industry. Our constant contact with dynamic book and magazine professionals was key. In British Columbia we were in constant contact with Jim Douglas, Karl Siegler, Alma Lee, Scott McIntyre, Rob Sanders, Celia Duthie, Stephen Osborne, Margaret Reynolds, Basil Stuart Stubbs, Mary Schendlinger, and Howie White. Nationally, our industry consultants included Doug Gibson, Cynthia Good, Pierre Berton, and Jim Lorimer. Our meetings with Ontario-based Maclean Hunter editors and managers, then a major Canadian magazine force with their broad portfolio of consumer and trade magazines, were also important.

Given our activities, the CCSP/CISP received funding from the federal department of communication to analyze training and education oriented to book and magazine publishing in Canada. The results of the study were published in 1984 under the title The Canadian Centre for Studies in Publishing: A Report of a Feasibility Study. Two years later, we published The Canadian Centre for Studies in Publishing: A Prospectus. One year after the prospectus, we circulated a business plan and a supplement within SFU and among members of the publishing industry.

In 1989, with major funding from SSHRC, Ann and our colleagues, Carol Gerson, and Laura Millar, began the creation of the Canadian Publishers’ Records Database. (CPRD). https://digital.lib.sfu.ca/canadian-publishers-records The CPRD stimulated publishers to organize their archives and make them publicly accessible, an impressive historical resource that describes 1,400 archival collections.

A central element of our reports and plans was establishing a professional master’s program. Reflecting its graduate-degree status, our proposed MPub embraced education beyond training, focusing on the role of publishing in history and society, emphasizing past practice and impact, current social, cultural and business realities, emerging trends, policy, and likely futures, especially through technological innovation. Including an internship semester and an internship project report, we intended our graduates to be of immediate value to publishing firms.

The feasibility study, its federal funding, our teaching, the formation of an advisory board, and our academic publications, consolidated our recognition in various publishers’ offices, their associations, and in government circles across Canada. Indeed, by 1989 we were commissioned by the British Columbia government to undertake a review of book publishing in BC and make recommendations for provincial support. In 1990, Canada’s Secretary of State commissioned us to evaluate the effectiveness of their Print Learning Materials Development Program. In 1992, the federal Department of Communication hired us to evaluate their “English-Language Titles Analysis Project.”

Throughout the 1990s, with the continuing extensive participation of BC-based publishing professionals, we were on a roll, “churning out” fifteen to twenty graduates for firms across Canada and, occasionally, international venues. As well, in 1993, we took on scholarly journal publishing. In 1994, working with a Communication colleague, Richard Smith, and his computing science students, the CJC became the first established journal in the world to make its back issues freely available online. Later, in 2002, Richard partnered with us to form the Advanced Publishing Research Lab (APuRL). Federal funding led to the development of the Publishers Extensible Online Database (PExOD), software designed to create a single centralized source of bibliographic data. PExOD paved the way in the years following for book publishers to transition to the evolving international standard for bibliographic data, ONIX. We also formed our own press in 2004 staffed by faculty and students. In the end, CCSP Press (later CISP Press) published seven titles.

In short, the MPub and CCSP/CISP established SFU as a national resource. Our annual placement of 100 percent of our interns in four-month positions; our placement of graduates in publishing jobs (about 80 percent); the continued participation of noteworthy industry professionals in our courses; and our continuing funded R&D initiatives told the story.

You’d be forgiven for thinking along the lines of Julius Caesar’s famous arrival-and-consequences speech. Wrong. It actually took ten years of concentrated effort and lobbying to gain approval for the MPub.

Within the MPub itself, the proposed structure and associated content served us well as a starting point and earned the approval of all the necessary committees. The realities of delivering the program were much more complex.

To give the industry confidence that we could deliver worthwhile training, the university had to embrace hiring outstanding professionals at salaries that reflected their professional expertise, who often lacked “normal academic credentials.” Given that unfamiliarity, we could have run up against reluctance to move our faculty nominations forward. The dean of arts left us alone to succeed or fail.

As we moved from our on-paper proposal to reality, we continued to work closely with various publishers. To join me in teaching, we hired Valerie Frith, a respected editor with a history PhD; Roberto Dosil, the pre-eminent, multi-award-winning book designer in Vancouver; Ron Woodward, the former director of a college publishing program and associate of the Banff Book Publishing Workshops (a two-week intensive, industry-run program); and retiring Reader’s Digest Canada’s CEO Ralph Hancox with his Neiman Fellowship in Journalism and exposure to management at the Harvard Program for Management Development.

A few years later, we hired one of our grads, John Maxwell, who had technology expertise as well as educational television experience. A few years after that, we hired editor extraordinaire Mary Shendlinger, co-founder of Geist Magazine, along with writer Stephen Osborne, both of whom turned out to be superb instructors. SFU’s flexibility of formal qualifications was unusual and tremendously important.

In the hiring process, I emphasized that the program required a full-time office presence and interactive participation with students five full working days a week. Incoming students were told to treat the program requirements as a full-time job that would, at certain times, demand their availability on evenings and weekends. Attendance in all courses—there were no electives—was compulsory. Each year we watched as the cohort came together and, with Ron Woodward’s facilitation, bonded, some for life.

Our approved overall curriculum plan called for three “academic courses” covering history, current realities, and technology; three professional courses covering editing, design, and business and marketing; and three project courses focused on books, magazines, and emerging technological forms. Each project simulated industry realities—launching a season of book titles, founding a new magazine, implementing a technological innovation. Soon after the program’s inauguration, we came to realize that our courses worked best when they married course fundamentals with direct relevance to the demands of the projects. As we pursued direct relevance, the projects improved tremendously in their scope and quality.

In general, course delivery also featured a continuous parade of participating Industry professionals, some providing guidance for project development, others evaluating the strengths and weaknesses of the guided student projects. The project presentations gave both us and the industry a sense of how well the program was performing. The presentations also gave publishers early exposure to students they might want to select as interns and employees, as well as new ideas that came to them while reviewing the students’ work.

The value of the requirement of students to work productively, together, and to deadlines, where responsibilities such as editorial, design, marketing, and sales were divided amongst group members, cannot be overestimated. Superimposed on their work together was evaluation, predominantly based on the quality of the overall product. It was a high-pressure affair.

My day-to-day responsibilities included nurturing cooperation and respect, an academic ideal that felt distant from the competitive individualism that I experienced in my first twenty years as an academic. With respect as a foundation, we saw ourselves as modelling the productive interactions we expected of students.

In part, our good relations stemmed from the program’s success. Our students were smart and talented. The projects that emerged were extremely credible and innovative. Their cleverness and their talents shone brightly. Our internship placement success and their subsequent hiring were outstanding, as were the research and professional achievements of faculty members.

The MPub provided a foundation for greater diversity, mainly of women, from varied socio-economic and racial backgrounds, many with bachelor’s degrees in literature. Our admission policies favoured students with a “life-long” engagement with books and magazines and publishing work experience. It also demanded significant prerequisite technical and accounting familiarity. In general, our students were brilliant and adaptable to group work. Many distinguished themselves.

Not long after graduation, two MPubbers, Jesse Finkelstein and Trena White, were both given joint managerial control of BC’s largest trade publisher, Douglas and McIntyre. D&M was preparing itself for sale. Once D&M was sold, they co-founded their own firm, Page Two Books. A look at the Page Two website shows twenty-nine staff members, six partners, and a backlist of over 300 titles.

Another MPubber, Anna Comfort O’Keefe, worked her way up within Harbour Publishing and became managing editor and then publisher of the new D&M. Two other MPubbers, Lara Smith and Heidi Waechtler, are principals in Figure1 Publishing, a company that specializes in an established D&M line: art and museum publishing. Another graduate, Tori Elliot, became publisher of Touchwood Editions, a firm with quite a long BC history. Each of these is also a sizable firm with an impressive backlist and awards.

Yet another graduate, Xiaoyan Huang, founded her own children’s publishing firm, Everafter Books, and was named the world’s leading publisher of children’s books for 2025 by the Bologna Prize. Chris Hill has served as editor-in-chief of Destinasian magazine for some years. Paschal Ssemaganda came to the MPub from Uganda and subsequently worked at the World Bank for fifteen years on digital information and product work. Others still lead publishing initiatives around the world. Samuel Smith Esseh returned to Kumasi, Ghana, with both an MPub and a PhD to teach in the publishing program at his university. Katie Shamash has worked with Digital Science, a world-renowned research-oriented organization owned by the Holtzbrinck Publishing Group. Katie also worked with the British not-for-profit Jisc, a key organization that provides network and IT services and digital resources throughout the UK to support further and higher education and research.

Another track led to government employment. After working in the writing and publishing section, Sarah Dingle and David Schimpkey have moved into senior administrative positions in the Canada Council. In Canadian Heritage, Sarah Mayes is manager of the support for organizations program within the Canada Book Fund, while Mounir Khoury is a senior policy analyst in policy and programs for the Canada Book Fund.

Yet another track included consultancies and agencies. Monique Sherrett created a marketing consultancy called Boxcar Marketing. For a time, everywhere I looked, there was Monique and Boxcar consulting on marketing plans, it seemed, for everybody. A real highlight was her work with Raincoast on all the online marketing for the Harry Potter series. Currently, she is consulting on AI with the Department of Canadian Heritage. Turner-Riggs is another key consultancy. Working for the Association of Canadian Publishers, Craig Riggs and Kiley Turner created and ran a book marketing site to promote Canadian titles called 49thShelf.com. Later they founded ReaderBound with Doug Plant, a programmer/developer who was also the technical backbone of PExOD. They have created over twenty-one online commerce sites for publishers.

The program even graduated two PhDs, Nancy Duxbury and Amada Lastoria, and two literary agents, Ron Eckel and Claire Cavanagh. Several MPub grads, including Alina Cerminara, also launched their own successful magazine titles, some of which, such as Alina’s Folklife, continue to this day. Anicka Quin has worked in senior positions with the magazine Western Living and currently is editor-in-chief. Laura Byspalko returned from an internship in India to promote the Jaipur Literary Festival, followed by her and her husband Sirish Rao founding the yearly Indian Summer Arts Festival in Vancouver. She is now the chief operating officer of an organization sensitizing kids to architecture.

All this deserves to be celebrated! Hence this backgrounder. The force may be weakened, but it lives on in the hearts and minds, and our publishing realities of those of us who participated in its first twenty years.

—Rowland Lorimer
