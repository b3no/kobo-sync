One big reason nobody’s walking to school anymore

How to end the dreaded carpool line

In 1995, downtown Durham was struggling: North Carolina’s tobacco industry was struggling, and the city had barely any shops or restaurants. But its school district took a risk on the blighted area, transforming its 73-year-old downtown high school into a new magnet school, now called Durham School of the Arts.

Over the following three decades, the school’s successes — it has expanded to serve around 1,800 students and maintained a high level of diversity — paralleled those of downtown Durham itself, which added thousands of new homes, new space for research labs, and other attractive amenities. While the school’s magnet status and special classes have been key to its success, so has its physical location: The roughly 17-acre downtown campus is integrated into the surrounding neighborhoods and easily accessible by the many students who walk or bike to class. It’s just a few blocks from the city’s main bus station, and dozens of shops and restaurants are within spitting distance.

All that will come to an end in the coming years. Instead of renovating the aging campus, the school district has chosen to move it to a new, previously forested, nearly 60-acre site north of downtown. The new facility will be surrounded by a moat of surface parking, inaccessible to the city’s bus network, and farther from most students’ homes.

Share

Though Durham’s school board made the call in part to save money, it was also responding to a tangle of official state policies and societal norms that have resulted in schools across the country being pushed to isolated suburban fringes.

After investigating trends in school siting, we found that stories like Durham’s are more common than not. Before 1950, the median American high school in our sample sat on a 17-acre site. The typical high school built since then sits on nearly 40 acres. Similar changes have occurred among elementary schools, too, in tandem with a well-documented collapse in the share of students who walk or bike to school. In some places, it’s led to parents and kids sitting in comically long drop-off and pickup lines that contribute to traffic congestion on nearby streets.

No single rule explains how so many schools got so big and far-flung. Societal trends like rising incomes, urban sprawl, increased car use, and parental preferences have all contributed. But obscure state policies promoting minimum acreage requirements, parking rules, and drop-off lanes have made it harder to build compact, neighborhood-oriented schools. In some cases, states have all but outlawed them.

There’s an opportunity for change, though, and some states and localities are trying to reverse course — and get students back onto their own two feet.

The postwar school size boom

Our sample¹ of nearly 400 U.S. schools shows that elementary school parcel sizes have increased substantially since the World War II period. Prior to 1950, the typical elementary school was set on about a five-acre site, gradually increasing to about 10 acres in the 1950s and 1960s, and reaching a peak of more than 15 acres in the 1990s. We observe a sharp decline back to a median of about 10 acres for elementary school sites in the 2000s.

For high schools, we don’t see the same 21st-century turn toward smaller schools that we did for elementary schools. These facilities’ sites more than doubled in size after World War II — and have continued to grow in the period since.

Some of the increase in school site size reflects growing enrollment. Yet, even when adjusted for counts of students, we find clear evidence for ballooning school facility sizes.

These larger facilities have been positioned in areas with poor access to their surroundings. Looking at the median Walk Score for our full sample of schools by decade, we found that walkability reflects the elementary school parcel size trend, bottoming out in the 1990s and recovering somewhat more recently.

Why does school siting matter?

The way that kids get to school each day has changed drastically over time. When the U.S. first started collecting this data, just over 15% of kids got to school in a private car. By 2022, a majority of kids were getting to school that way instead of walking, biking, or taking the bus. The trend shows no sign of abating.

Mode of transportation to school over time
School drop-offs have dramatically surpassed kids walking or biking to school.

This shift has several causes, including rising incomes that allow families to own multiple cars, low-density development patterns that discourage walking and biking, and busy schedules of extracurricular activities that may be incompatible with a rigid bus schedule.

But another key explanation for changing travel patterns is the design of schools themselves.

School districts seek large sites for athletic facilities and parking, while enabling schools to isolate themselves from potential outside threats. Large sites, in turn, require schools to be placed along large roads at the edge of town, making them difficult to access by public transportation, walking, or biking. As such, parents often have poor choices other than to drive their kids to school.

How kids get to school shapes families’ daily lives. Students who switch from active to passive modes of transportation to school get less exercise in general. Kids’ loss of unsupervised time with friends at the bus stop or on the walk to school may negatively impact their mental health. Parental quality of life, too, can be diminished by the need to shuttle their kids to school each day, particularly when it means sitting in long drop-off and pickup lines. Car dependence increases both pollution and transportation costs, since families must spend more money on gas.

As the share of kids taking the bus declines, school districts reduce their transportation service, sometimes leading to a death spiral where decreased service and diminished ridership reinforce each other and more students lose access each year. School districts have to build on larger sites to accommodate more parking and in turn have to spend more money on land. And sites positioned at communities’ outskirts force localities to invest in larger roadways. This may limit their ability to co-plan for community spaces, requiring duplication of playgrounds, auditoriums, and other types of public facilities, whereas schools built closer to existing parks and libraries may be able to rely on facilities open to the public.

These steep costs have admittedly come with an important benefit: The rate of kids killed while walking or biking has fallen 90% since the 1960s, albeit from an already-low risk. But this progress has been won largely by new limits on kids’ freedom, rather than by improving the safety of their environment.

The policy choices resulting in school sprawl

Nineteenth-century schools were often built on very small lots by modern standards: two acres or less. In the early 20th century, as localities were just beginning to implement minimum lot size requirements for development on private land, education reformers also began advocating for minimum lot size requirements for schools.

The view that school sites should be larger gained influence in both the education and planning fields, with a group called the Council of Educational Facility Planners, International (CEFPI), playing a particularly important role. (The group, which changed its position and began recommending compact, walkable sites about 20 years ago, is now called the Association for Learning Environments.) CEFPI and others argued that larger sites would allow for separation from the surrounding streets and neighborhoods, reducing the perceived nuisance some may feel as a result of adjacency to children.

As of 2003, 27 states had mandates or guidelines for minimum acreage of school sites based on grade level and student body size. Many states repealed these policies over the past two decades, but minimum acreage mandates or guidelines are in effect in 18 states today.

Beyond these acreage rules, other policies governing parking site design have contributed to increasing school size. Given the space required for parking and associated circulation, a high school’s parking needs alone could be 5.5 acres, equivalent to four football fields.

Similarly, state rules often require or suggest on-site loading space and multiple driveways, both intended to ameliorate traffic jams caused by drop-off lines that extend onto streets adjacent to schools. Though South Carolina repealed its minimum acreage requirements in 2003, it still recommends three to four driveway entrances for high schools, each separated by at least 600 feet and linked to widened surrounding streets. If two entrances each were placed on two sides of a typical high school site, this detail alone entails a 32-acre site. While this is a recommendation and not a requirement, state capital funding is often tied to approval of site plans. This leads school districts to err on the side of following state guidelines.

The Durham school board’s decision to transfer the city’s School of the Arts to a greenfield site was motivated, in part, by fears of traffic congestion. The board’s justification for the new facility included the claim that the state department of transportation recommended car queueing areas internal to the campus, which could not fit on the downtown site. Of course, the new site’s location will encourage more parents and students to drive.

Large schools may set off a vicious cycle where kids tend to arrive in private cars, leading to demand for more parking and circulation space at future schools, which leads to even larger sites that are more dangerous and less pleasant to walk to, which leads to even fewer kids getting to school independently.

Turning the school siting ship around

Our data is descriptive; we know that newer schools sit on larger, less walkable parcels, aligning with policies that encouraged this outcome. But we don’t know how much of the shift toward these sites stems from official rules. Indeed, research doesn’t provide clear evidence that specific state-level reforms are making a difference. One study examining outcomes in South Carolina following legislators’ repeal of acreage requirements found that the average acreage of middle and high schools built after repeal decreased, while average acreage of elementary schools increased. Neither of these changes was statistically significant.

Another study on school site sizes compared outcomes in Maryland, which eliminated its state site size mandates in the 1970s, and Virginia, which has state guidelines for school site size but no mandates. In spite of localities’ freedom to determine school acreage, the study finds that school boards had either adopted their own requirements or had chosen to rely on CEFPI recommendations issued prior to the organization’s move to encourage flexible site sizes. School planners interviewed in that research said that they chose large sites in part to meet parents’ expectations for drop-offs, parking, and athletic facilities.

But momentum may be shifting. The U.S. Environmental Protection Agency has encouraged localities to avoid locating schools in remote locations where students and staff have little choice but to drive.

At the state level, Vermont emphasizes that schools should be built as complements to local growth plans, such as siting schools so that they may be able to share athletic facilities with public parks. New Mexico recommends leveraging schools as “focal point[s]” where joint-use facilities are feasible. New Hampshire has joined Vermont in encouraging school districts to select sites that encourage pedestrian, bike, and transit access to help reduce transportation costs.

Washington state takes a similar approach in participating counties, giving priority to schools in already-developed areas. It uses a point system to reward school districts receiving state funding. Schools receive extra points if they are located in infill areas, use multistory designs, are near transit, provide sidewalk and bike infrastructure, and that have leveraged parking reduction strategies. The new Vancouver Innovation, Technology and Arts Elementary School near downtown Vancouver, Washington, which sits on approximately three acres along a bus rapid transit line, is co-located with a public park, and is adjacent to a community arts center, exemplifies the new approach. So does the new Salish Middle School in Lynnwood, which will be connected via pedestrian paths to neighborhoods on three sides.

In Portland, Oregon, the school district recently reconstructed its 1,700-student downtown high school, Lincoln, on its existing 11-acre site, which includes a football field and other sports facilities. To do so, the district built a six-story building and provided car parking only for staff, but added sheltered spaces for 120 bikes. The school is located two blocks from multiple light rail stations, and all Portland Public School high schoolers have free transit passes embedded into their ID cards that they can use to ride public buses and trains rather than school buses.

Large-scale school sites positioned at the urban edge are definitely less expensive on a per-acre basis relative to infill locations. Nonetheless, they are associated with substantial negative externalities on children’s lives, parents’ transportation costs, and urban service provision, none of which school districts typically account for in their decision-making.

Picking a site for a new school inevitably requires trade-offs. But state and local policymakers should move beyond the postwar model of large school sites optimized for car access that impose large transportation costs on families and strip kids of the opportunity for some independence. School boards should get creative with multistory schools, more compact sites, and sharing facilities like athletic fields across schools of different levels or with public parks to improve the feasibility of infill construction. Students, parents, and the broader public will all benefit.
