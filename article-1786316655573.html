When the majority is wrong.

The Verdict, by John Thomason

Americans, we keep learning, are obsessed with the price of groceries. At the same time, many of them seem very uncomfortable with one of the ways that meat producers make groceries cheap: by cramming livestock so close together that they can’t even turn around.

When you pose this problem to voters a kind of commonsense decency can sometimes win out: Healthy majorities in Florida, Arizona, Massachusetts, and California have voted in favor of raising confinement standards over the last 25 years.

You might notice that none of those are major pork-producing states.

But because the initiatives in California and Massachusetts limited not just what their farmers could do, but also what sorts of animal products could be sold within their borders at all, the animal welfare implications were immense, amounting to perhaps the most substantial protections for farmed animals in U.S. history. The 2018 passage of California’s Proposition 12 meant that, if hog farmers in Iowa want to sell whole cuts of pork to Californians (which they very much do), they have to abide by Golden State standards.

All of that was almost thrown out over the course of this spring and summer.

The Farm Bill is a sprawling document that covers everything from food stamps to irrigation programs — an ideal vehicle for a policy change to escape public notice. That seems to be at least in part what the proponents of the Save Our Bacon Act were hoping for. The provision, championed by members of Iowa’s congressional delegation, would have preempted state-level reforms like California’s and Massachusetts’s, making it impossible for states to bar the sale of pork from pigs whose mothers couldn’t turn around or extend their limbs during pregnancy (so long as those farms’ states allow it).

Farm-state Republicans lined up behind the provision, while food safety groups, animal rights groups, and most of the Democratic Senate caucus were against it. (The pork industry is actually split between those who have not yet complied with strict standards and those who have already invested in compliant practices.) The latter appeared to be winning out in June, when the Senate decided not to include the provision, apparently over the controversy it had engendered. But that doesn’t mean Save Our Bacon is dead. The Farm Bill stalled in the Senate Agriculture Committee on Thursday, over a dispute on an entirely different issue, with the committee now in recess until the fall. The Save Our Bacon Act is likely to get a second wind at that time.

If we’re being honest, the vast majority of the nation didn’t just miss this drama — they probably wouldn’t have cared all that much if they’d known.

It’s common to hear political junkies talk about “high salience” and “low salience” issues. The cost of pork is high salience — something voters will definitely notice and express their dissatisfaction about at the polls — while the welfare of the pigs who suffered to keep prices down is low salience, the purview largely of those who in some sense have made a choice to care about it.

With high salience issues, the will of the voters is clear enough and the cost of ignoring it is losing elections. But on low salience issues, democracies count on elite political actors reaching some sort of reasonable agreement that is in the best interests of the public.

The conflict over Save Our Bacon unsettles the idea that low and high salience issues are cleanly separable.

First of all, which “will of the voters” should be honored in this situation? That of the median 2024 voter, angry about the price of groceries, or those of the actual majorities in multiple states who voted for farm animal protections? (Even Iowa voters appear to reject the Save Our Bacon Act, though the poll in question was commissioned by an advocacy organization.)

Still, asking people to improve pigs’ lives is one thing; asking them to pay a lot more for bacon, let alone give it up entirely, is another. Ending farm animal suffering would require the latter. If animal rights become high salience, animal rights groups lose. If it stays low salience, then they never even get a shot at winning. Such is the fate of those with unpopular convictions in a democratic society.

No country for moral fanatics?

Four years ago, a very small dog wandered into my life, and it changed my politics forever.

I had always been a little judgy about people who dote on their pets, yet there I was, realizing that I might find myself in tears if this ornery creature had so much as a hangnail. It no longer made sense to me to care so much about the welfare of this one animal without caring about others who seemed just like him.

Most market hogs are mutilated without anesthesia, spend their lives on bare slats above their own manure, and never go outdoors until slaughter. If we did any of this to dogs we’d call it torture. None of this was exactly news to me, I’m ashamed to say, but for the first time I actually felt that it might be impossible to live with myself if I knew that, while I doted on this dog, creatures just like him were being put through hell on earth for me.

In a political climate where we’re constantly being told that the moral stakes are higher than ever — that, as former President Obama lamented in 2015, “those who disagree with us are not merely wrong but bad” — contemplating the fate of livestock induces a kind of vertiginous loneliness, precisely because it’s not a matter of public controversy. Quite the opposite: Per-capita meat consumption has never been higher, and all either party can talk about is making that consumption cheaper. Meanwhile, more than 80 billion farm animals are slaughtered worldwide every year after the sort of lives I described above.

But different people have dramatically different moral red lines. Many of my fellow citizens think that we’re living through a genocide of unborn children. Others think that we should strip private property rights from anyone with one too many 0’s in their net worth. I don’t agree with either of them, and most of them don’t agree with me. We have to live together anyway. A certain amount of moral loneliness — the kind that could keep you up at night, aghast at the evil that people will tolerate — is the price that every zealot pays to live in a democratic society. At least I know they won’t hang me for it.

It’s uncomfortable to realize you’re part of a moral minority. What options do you have, besides the despair of accepting majority will, or the tireless fanaticism that characterizes groups like PETA?

It’s here that I take some solace in the weird indeterminacy of representative democracy. If you put the actual question I care about before voters — would you give up meat as you know it to end farm animal suffering? — it would lose. If you put the question purely to elite power brokers, it would also lose.

In a majoritarian society, moral minorities will never be entirely satisfied. Ninety-nine percent of the time this brings me great relief: I don’t want anti-abortion zealots dictating health policy by fiat. Yet I too believe that egregious moral crimes are being committed daily and, yes, I would impose my preference for stopping these crimes on everyone else if I could. If only I could.

But of course I don’t want to be imposed upon myself. For all its backsliding, the American system still does a pretty good job of ensuring that. And the very fragmentation that helps shore up this freedom — the separation of powers, the strategic veto points, the uneasy coalitions — also gives radicals of every stripe avenues to pursue their goals. It’s not such a lonely place for a fanatic after all.

What’s News with The Argument

The Argument recommends, by Milan Singh

Both Editor-in-Chief Jerusalem Demsas and Staff Writer Kelsey Piper recommend you pick up A Trade of Blood, the recently released third book in Robert Jackson Bennett’s fantasy series.

“For those who haven’t read it, the first book, The Tainted Cup, is a fantastic book about the murder of an imperial officer that is part Sherlockian mystery and part meditation on the running of an empire,” Jerusalem explained.

“I think the murder mystery format is weirdly well suited to the meditations on empire,” added Kelsey. “If you read British mystery novelists (Doyle, Christie, Heyer, le Carré) you can kind of watch the empire exhaust its case for itself and collapse in the background, and Bennett is clearly wrestling with American empire — notwithstanding the extremely wild biopunk sci-fi setting.”

Senior Editor John Thomason has been listening to Don DeLillo’s White Noise while commuting into the office: “Something about the folksy narration — from veteran stage actor Michael Prichard, who grew up on a farm himself and absolutely nails the chapter on the most photographed barn in America — is making me see DeLillo’s novel less as the landmark of postmodern lit that I remember from college and instead as surprisingly faithful to the 19th-century realist tradition, or maybe a kind of pastoral.”

Gender and culture writing fellow Maibritt Henkel watched the millennial classic 13 Going on 30: “While it is a very silly movie, I would say two things in my defense: (1) it belongs to Mark Ruffalo’s complete works and should therefore be considered part of the cinematic canon, and (2) considering your life and your morals from the perspective of your 13-year-old self is a pretty profound exercise.”

Editor Eli Richman has been going back and forth between reading the Dungeon Crawler Carl books and watching the new season of House of the Dragon. “I worry [House of the Dragon] is catching The Boys syndrome, where the need to have things go badly for the protagonists overwhelms the need for the story to make sense,” he said. “Dungeon Crawler Carl is unironically fantastic, ping-ponging between absurdly silly and very real without missing a beat.”

Finally, I personally do not recommend being hit by a car. The adrenaline rush is something else, but it hurts quite a bit.
