You Don't Have Trouble Making Female Friends Because You're Too Hot

Take it from me, someone who used to have trouble making female friends: women are not all jealous of you.

Since starting this Substack and writing about my difficulties making friends, especially as a child but also into young adulthood, I’ve bumped up against one theory over and over again: what if women have disliked me for no good reason at all, and they’re simply jealous of me for being hot?

Your words, not mine! Many of you have suggested that my social ills are a product of being too hot for my dorky personality (I concede my social skills were bad for most of my life, but my hotness, if it exists, is irrelevant.) Without getting into the nitty-gritty of whether I’m actually that hot, because obviously a lot of this is subjective and the only opinion that matters is my husband’s, you’re just going to have to take my word that especially in my teens and twenties, I was what one would consider “reasonably attractive”—not a model, but cute and in very good shape—and I struggled to make female friends.

Does that attractiveness explain why women typically haven’t liked me? Is this why, when I worked at a big tech company, I was the most hated woman in the office? The clues are all there: I’ve never struggled to make friends with men. My husband’s friends have all liked me. Women have, at times, spread baseless sexual rumors about me (shoutout to the girl who told my husband, right after he met me, that I was a “huge slut.” I would never be so forward as to directly ask him to rail my brains out, but you really helped us move things along.)

But I don’t think that’s it, and I don’t think this is the answer for many other decent-looking, yet friendless women. But I get it. It’s extremely tempting to assume it is.

When things aren’t going well for you, it’s comforting to tell yourself it’s just because you’re too good for everyone, everyone is threatened, and everyone is jealous. How many frustratedly single women have declared that “men are just threatened by their ambition” despite the fact that, in a worst case scenario, men don’t have much of an opinion at all on women’s jobs, and in a best-case scenario, they find ambitious women attractive? Declaring that everyone is jealous or threatened is an easy “out” of any real self-reflection. And yes, some people are petty, small and vindictive and will hate you for being hot, rich, or for having a more complete Calico Critter collection. But if the problem persists with almost every person you meet, your enviable traits are almost definitely not the reason things aren’t working out.

Of course, men can be delusional too. There are men who believe women don’t like them because they’re all on the birth control pill, which is paradoxically making them repulsed by alpha males and perversely attracted to effete losers. Sure, Jane!

But when you start to believe these things, even just to make yourself feel better, your mindset that you are superior to everyone else (even if admittedly inadequate in other ways) and that people hate you for it is probably the culprit for why people don’t like you. Or at the very least, initially the culprit was something else, but now you exude arrogance on top of whatever else is wrong with you. I don’t say this to be rude. I say this because I’ve been there.

When I was systematically excluded on my company orientation retreat (my most popular personal essay of all time, I strongly recommend reading it) I thought I was doing everything right. I tried my hardest to be socially capable and yet, the other women on the retreat wanted nothing to do with me. I wasn’t super chummy with the men either (mostly because it felt inappropriate, as a married woman, to exclusively be friends with my male coworkers) but the men did not seem to dislike me so actively. If I sat next to them at lunch, they’d talk to me. They would not leave the table when I sat down or systematically exclude me.

This has been a constant theme for me throughout my life. It’s not that I’m “just one of the guys” because I can’t understand stupid girly stuff and I’m so simple and down-to-earth. I am an extremely girly woman and I love feminine things. I am remarkably out of touch, prissy and high maintenance—an “indoor cat,” as several people have put it. I am exactly “like other girls,” at least on paper. In fact, I’m worse. I’m the caricature of the “other girls” that these memes claim to satirize. Yes, I can enjoy a good Borat impression and I like to eat pub food, but I refuse to believe those things make me “one of the guys” unless your idea of women comes exclusively from pre-1970 Disney movies.

When I was racking my brain for reasons my female coworkers didn’t like me, I eventually settled on the idea that maybe they were envious of my body. I may not be an Instagram e-girl, but I’m in good shape and I generally wear form-fitting clothing so they’d have probably noticed. This was, by far, the most comforting explanation because it made me feel good about myself and required no changes of me. I was on the phone with a close family member one night in my hotel room while all the other women hosted a Bachelorette viewing party that included every woman on the orientation except me, plus the one gay. Frustrated and hurt, I mused, “I hate to be that person but…maybe they’re jealous of me. I am in really good shape, after all.” My family member politely changed the topic.

I still don’t fully understand why those women specifically hated me so much after just having met me, especially when I was deliberately trying to be socially skilled. I made sure to ask lots of questions, not to talk about myself too much, and give lots of compliments. Perhaps they picked up on the fact that I was still learning social cues and none of the small talk came easily to me. I’m sure I sounded a little scripted and maybe set off their autism alert. But that still feels like a silly reason to literally avoid being in the same room as someone, which…actually happened. One of these women was so opposed to being alone with me—God knows what she thought I was going to do, kill her?—that when I invited her to my party, she oddly was the first person to arrive, but refused to come inside until her group of girlfriends showed up, even when that meant standing outside my apartment in the literal rain while I encouraged her, in vain, to come inside. (Eventually, her group of friends arrived and they ignored me at my own party.) While this was the most egregious case of women excluding or ghosting me, I’m used to it. It’s been happening my whole life. I have always had some female friends, and we usually fall for each other really hard and really fast without much of a need for warmup. But everyone else? The people who don’t adore me at first sight, who need a little small talk and warmup? Up until recently, it’s been a struggle!

It’s been years, but I think I have figured out why women have specifically been so hard to crack (and why I’ve had a much easier time making friends with men.) And I can assure you that it has absolutely nothing to do with my ass. The simple explanation is that poor social skills will harm you more with women than with men, and you will notice this contrast more easily if you’re good-looking. But there is a real reason why.

In Jonathan Haidt’s book, The Anxious Generation, he touched on a topic that can be traced back to the 1960s-era work of psychologist David Bakan: boys and men are more agentic, while girls and women are more communal. Put simply, belonging to a group and adhering to the rules of a group is more important to girls than to boys. This is why, as Haidt points out in his book, bullying directed at girls seeks to isolate them from their groups of friends in a way that bullying directed at boys does not.

Haidt did not write about this part, but I also believe women just have a stronger “ick” than men do, whether for dating or friends. Perhaps a holdover from a time when women needed to be on the lookout for potential predators, women have a very hypersensitive alert system for when someone just seems “off.” This alert system is not always accurate, of course. A socially awkward person is probably not any real danger to you, but I fundamentally believe this is a lizard brain thing and not something women are consciously doing.

It doesn’t matter whether these traits are biological or influenced by society. I’m sure, as is Haidt, that it’s a bit of both. But what does matter is that these tendencies fundamentally affect the way a socially inept woman can expect to befriend men and women. Women will pick up on minor social deficits much more easily, especially if you are trying to befriend a group of women as opposed to one specific woman. Groups of women will have a harder time accepting new members than rejecting new members. Meanwhile, men are less group-oriented and require less conformity within groups. If you also happen to be a good-looking woman, men will be more amenable to including you in their group, even if they’re partnered, even if you’re partnered, even if they have no intention of sleeping with you.

Unsurprisingly, good-looking but socially inept women will find that this gap between their ease making female versus male friends amplified. It will appear as though “women are so catty and standoffish, but men are so chill!” It’s easy to see this happen and assume it’s because the women are jealous and men are devoid of bias. But in fact, good looks are associated with the halo effect, wherein people (even same-sex platonic connections) will be more likely to assume other positive things about you based on your looks. You will be seen as kinder, more trustworthy, and generally a better person. (Cue: this is how you age when you’re unproblematic!) It was easy to tell myself that I was simply too hot to make female friends, except for the fact that all around me, women far better-looking had plenty of female friends! Southern sororities exist, and basically all of them are better-looking than I am, and those girls are swimming in a sea of friends.

Instead, something else is happening. A good-looking woman with poor social skills will immediately find herself struggling to break into female friend groups due to the fact that these groups are inherently very communal, and she is probably breaking a bunch of their unspoken rules and preferences without realizing it. Even if she has done nothing “wrong,” they can usually pick up that she’s different in some way, and they’d feel this way even if she was plain. Her beauty is not a detriment; it just doesn’t help her that much because they surmise something is “off” about her, and because these are straight women, they don’t care if she’s pretty.

Men, being the less communal sex, will not as easily pick up on her weirdness. Men will not get the platonic “ick” quite so easily. That’s not to say men never notice social ineptitude, but the bar to clear is a lot lower. And on top of that, men just really like hot women even if they know sex isn’t on the table. I’ve never fully understood this tendency, but it happens. I had a coworker—a sort of dowdy, frumpy, below-average-looking guy—who surrounded himself with a bevy of extremely attractive (but taken!) female friends. He didn’t seem to be making moves on them, and never attempted to break up their relationships, but enjoyed just…being near them. One time, he reported that he invited his female friend and her boyfriend to stay over with him when they were traveling in his city, and the couple had sex on his couch all night. Look, I can’t claim to know exactly what was going on with this guy, but he clearly enjoyed being in close proximity to attractive women who were obviously never going to date him.

I may not have loads of single male friends at this point in my life. But in college, I was an honorary member of a fraternity. I even had my portrait on their composite in the yearbook. This was a particularly geeky fraternity, full of future doctors, scientists and engineers, although at the time they were mostly just a bunch of constantly-drunk nerds.

When I first entered college, my goal was to make friends with girls—fun, party-loving, fashion-loving girls like myself. We would all get ready, prance around to Rihanna music, and say things like, “Love you, BITCH!” But that didn’t happen. Unsurprisingly, those girls wanted nothing to do with me—as I had been so inured over the years, they immediately surmised I was…I don’t know, something else. An alien in a preppy college girl skin suit. I attempted to party with them one time and some girl immediately spread a rumor that she caught me humping an inflatable palm tree, which felt really unnecessary. Fun fact: this girl later got expelled from our college for punching a police officer after she was caught giving a blowjob in public to a real, non-inflatable man. Ah well, nevertheless.

I moved onto a sorority—yes, I know, I don’t seem like the type, but it was one of those “everyone gets to be in it, and we do charity work” sororities. I attended a few events and some of the senior girls pulled me aside to let me know I was the “favorite.” You can imagine my surprise! Maybe I was good at girl-groups after all! But of course, this didn’t last long. I broke a rule which now feels obvious, but didn’t at the time. I was interested in a boy, and I heard through the grapevine that he used to hook up with one of the senior members. (We went to a really small school, so everyone had hooked up with everyone.) I didn’t want to pursue anything with him unless I knew it was okay, so I did something that felt both polite and direct: I asked her point-blank if she had a problem with us dating, and promised that if she did have a problem with it, I’d stop speaking to him. She laughed and said they only hooked up once, years ago, and she didn’t care about him. I took her at her word.

But—and I’ll keep this part devoid of details—he sexually assaulted me shortly after. It was one of those classic gray-area college situations where escalating it to police (even campus police) felt like an overreaction. I didn’t report it or do anything about it, but for a while I just felt weird and scared, and I didn’t want to go anywhere. At one point, I mustered up the enthusiasm to attend one of the sorority events. One of the sorority sisters noticed I seemed off, and asked me if I was okay. I broke another rule: I told her I wasn’t, and I told her why. I didn’t realize that the correct answer, regardless of how I really felt was, “Yeah, sorry, just have a lot going on! How are you?” Instead I did what the teens now call “trauma dumping.” A few other girls overheard and came over to comfort me. I didn’t really want them to know, or to do anything, but what was initially a two-person conversation had become a ten-person conversation. In the moment, they all appeared to be on my side.

But immediately after, I stopped being invited to their events. In particular, I recall seeing one of them stuffing pumpkin-carving party invites into everyone’s mailboxes, seeing me, and briskly walking away. Even the girls who had effusively hugged me just a few days ago were avoiding eye contact. One of them—the girl who had previously hooked up with him—was actually overtly mean to me in a way that felt like she was playing an over-the-top middle school bully on a Disney show. I only remained friends with one of them, who told me that my exclusion actually wasn’t about the guy at all—it was about other members knowing “too much about me” too soon.

Once I realized that a big group of female friends wasn’t in my future, everything changed for me in the most unexpected way. One night at a random dorm room party, where I knew nobody and had just wandered in because we used to do things like that, a senior guy pulled me aside and asked me if I wanted him to take me home and make me cum. I was like “Hm, no, that’s weird.” His response, “What, you don’t like cumming?”

At this point, a few of his fraternity brothers—the aforementioned geeky frat guys—overheard and decided to step in, taunting him by calling him, “Orgasm Guy.” I laughed the whole thing off because it was genuinely very funny. We all got talking, and they introduced me to other members of their fraternity. For the next few months, I was over at their suite basically every night, watching movies, eating junk food, and pretending to do my reading. We all played pranks on each other, including a prank to convince one guy after a night of blackout drinking that he had charged the college diner demanding free food (the diner staff cooperated with the prank, and upon revelation of the ruse, awarded him free food for the rest of the year for being a good sport.) All of their fraternity traditions were opened up to me. I attended events meant only for men. And in response, I added feminine touches that they were missing. I organized their Christmas party and bought them a mini tree and ornaments. I cooked a few times. Some people might have called this “pick me” behavior, but to me it just looked like friendship.

Frequently, I would worry that I wasn’t socializing “right,” and that this kind of fraternization, no pun intended, was unbecoming of a woman. I knew what people probably thought about me—that I was sleeping with all of them, that they were passing me around, or at the very least, that I had taken Orgasm Guy up on his offer. In fact, I was sleeping with none of them! Eventually, one of the fraternity brothers did become my long-term, monogamous boyfriend, but only him, and I remained friends with the rest of them after we broke up. This paled in comparison to the lascivious fan fiction other people cooked up about me.

Sometimes, I wonder if those friendships were just about a bunch of geeky guys wanting proximity to a girl, even if she was taken (although, to their credit, many of them had girlfriends.) Maybe I’ll never truly know why they liked me. Maybe it was a combination of things. But I do know that they forgave my social defects in a way that girls—who, just to be clear, were my first choice—did not. And the more I hung out with those guys, the less any girls wanted to hang out with me at all, so my comfort around other women didn’t improve.

When I asked my husband about this phenomenon, he pointed out that I might be undercounting the fact that plenty of women actually have disliked me for a stupid, superficial reason—the way I dress (or used to dress.) This, I believe, along with any other behavior that reads “attention-seeking,” (ie: my entire personality) is probably going to harm your odds with women more than simply being hot. Perhaps it will not be a problem for a group of CERTIFIED BADDIES, but I have yet to attempt to break into such a group. Basically, from ages twelve to about thirty-five, I dressed in an extremely provocative way, which I wrote about here. Okay, someone from Miami might not consider my wardrobe that provocative at all, but I spent most of my life in the preppy Northeast and then later in geeky San Francisco, where deliberate cleavage or midriff-baring was not normal attire. At a party in San Francisco, my husband overheard a woman I had never met talking about me and saying, “She fucks everyone,” despite the fact that I had been in the same serious live-in relationship since before I arrived in San Francisco. This kind of thing happened all the time. Once a particularly influential woman decides to make something like that up, she can very easily poison the entire group against you. What does a woman have to gain by standing up to the alpha of the group by insisting that actually, that woman wearing the push-up bra and bebe bandage dress to the dive bar really is incredibly sweet, especially if they don’t know you at all? And even if nobody makes anything up about you, women can see behavior and attire that reads “I am obsessed with male attention,” whether you are or not, and decide you’re more trouble than you’re worth before they get to know you.

Another reason that it was so much easier for me to break into a literal fraternity than a group of girlfriends was that groups of men generally do not have a bunch of unspoken rules. This is not a value judgment on men or women, but while I’ve gotten to the point in my life that I can very astutely read social cues from an individual, I find navigating group dynamics to be borderline impossible. I joke about how I have “no friends,” but I’ve usually had at least one close female friend—usually someone similar to me (my longest-term best friend also has my subtype of OCD and loves fashion, my local best friend enjoys sewing for hours on end.) But I am so oblivious to unspoken group dynamics that in seventh grade, I was briefly part of a five-person friend group and I was oblivious for months while they all secretly hated me, discussed hating me in private, and ultimately voted me out of their group in a public ceremony. Having coffee with a new female friend is now something I do regularly, but I do not have a “friend group” and I don’t know if I ever will.

It’s not that women are blinded by silly social rules and men are more noble and unbiased. Sometimes I wonder if any of those frat guys would have been my friend if I were, truly, unbelievably, irredeemably chopped. Even though our friendship wasn’t sexual or romantic, I have to say…probably not! Men, even ones with no romantic intentions, will let attractive women get away with a lot of social weirdness. That doesn’t mean they’re more “chill,” although it can feel that way. It also doesn’t mean that the women who find you annoying are jealous of you. The problem is that these women are not attracted to you, and as a result they won’t put up with your shit.
