How to misread a blue wave

No, the midterms did not validate economic populism

Jerusalem Demsas
Nov 18
 
READ IN APP
 

The American people do not want to pay for your political project.

You might think to yourself, she doesn’t understand that in the long run my political project will make everyone happier, healthier, more prosperous, beautiful, and filled with that je ne sais quoi that exudes from runway models and serial killers. But you would be wrong, reader. Because today’s voters don’t seem to want to pay for much of anything.

The 2024 and 2026 electorates spoke as clearly as possible: They are mad about the price of consumer goods and services. They want something that probably no one can promise to deliver: for a burger and fries at a diner to cost $10. But they do really want it, and there’s no use pretending they secretly want something else.

Yet for the past two weeks since the Democrats’ decisive victory in the midterms, all I keep hearing and reading about is how finally the voters have given the party a mandate to do “economic populism.” Pundits and politicians argue that voters are clamoring for the new Congress to tackle “corporate greed” and enact a $20 minimum wage. Seven months before the midterms, the left-populists at the Open Markets Institute somehow already knew that it would be a mandate to confront “concentrated corporate power.” (It does kind of give the game away if you claim to know the will of the voters before any of them have had a chance to vote.)

What is economic populism? Is it, as the vast majority of Americans support, expanded healthcare access and higher taxes on the wealthy and corporations? If so, that would mean famed technocrats Hillary Clinton and Barack Obama were economic populists. Hell, it would even mean Bill Clinton was an economic populist.

This is obviously not what is being meant by this term in the capital-D Discourse — in which populism appears to be a rejection of standard economic theories. Unfortunately, rarely does any self-described economic populist offer a comprehensive definition. Some refer to it as mere rhetoric that honors the working class and attacks economic elites; others just blithely say it’s about “economic power.”

In practice, economic populism appears to be the belief that greed is what makes things cost too much, that the fix is for governments to overrule markets and cap prices, levy tariffs, and shield jobs from competition. It is, roughly, the list of things economists tell you not to do.

This isn’t just my read. Populist economist Isabella Weber recently wrote that “economic populism also means dethroning economics.”

Barry Lynn, founder of the Open Markets Institute, said economics “rewards those who do the best job of excluding reality.” Matt Stoller calls economists’ work “fake science.” This is all a mirror image of the aftermath of the 2024 election, when MAGA supporters treated Donald Trump’s victory as a rejection of wokeness and “transgender ideology,” as well as a mandate for “counterrevolution.”

Remarkable how fast the electorate changes its mind. Or, perhaps, it has had the same overriding priorities the entire time.

It’s not cost of living and something else

Perhaps it’s because everyone is in thrall to the rule of threes — or because they fear the shame of being branded a monomaniac — but people can’t help but add additional causal factors to the simple truth that people really want things to be cheap. It happens in discussions of why Kamala Harris lost the 2024 election and why Republicans lost the Senate and House two weeks ago.

For a year, The Argument has surveyed thousands of American voters every single month. Every month, among other questions, we have asked what voters’ top two most important issues are. Cost of living consistently emerged as the most common priority selected — and it wasn’t particularly close. (More on healthcare, which consistently placed in a distant second, later.)

To the extent voters have one common concern, it really is cost of living and nothing else.

Some on the left have read voters’ anger over prices as evidence that the people have finally turned against free enterprise, but there’s little evidence that voters have turned their frustration over high prices into a broad rejection of successful companies. In The Argument‘s May survey, 63% of Americans viewed Amazon favorably, while only 19% viewed the company unfavorably. Majorities also viewed Google and Microsoft favorably.

Even Sen.-elect James Talarico, who criticized billionaires’ political power on the campaign trail, has drawn a clean distinction between a critique of the wealthy and a critique of free enterprise. Like our survey respondents, he seems quite favorable to the latter, calling himself a “big fan of SpaceX, of Tesla,” and praising Elon Musk as an innovator. (This despite the fact that Musk spent over $100 million to try to defeat him!)

In short, there seems to be little evidence that voters want politicians to wage war on the companies whose products and services they enjoy.

Many on the left want “affordability” to be about protecting jobs rather than lowering prices. The problem is that protecting jobs from competition makes the goods produced by those jobs more expensive.

Tariffs are the cleanest example of this: A tax on imported goods only preserves a job stateside if it eliminates the foreign good’s cost advantage. But the broader dynamic was on full display during President Joe Biden’s presidency: Low-income wage growth took off, and so did steep price increases. As a result, Biden left office with a 40% approval rating as voters decided that a second round with another historically unpopular president was preferable.

Earlier this year, we asked voters whether they would pick lower prices or lower unemployment if they could only have one. They decidedly chose the former.

We already know how to make things cheaper

The funny thing about left-populists trying to rescue economic populism from right-populists is that the single best thing Trump could have done to improve Republicans’ election results is simply see the light of neoclassical economics. This is mostly on trade, yes, but also on the inadvisability of spiking gas prices by launching a war in the Middle East.

Standard trade models show that tariffs mostly end up getting paid for by the people who buy the tariffed goods. In 2024, a panel of 44 economists was at 95% agreement that consumers in the tariff-imposing country bear a substantial share of the costs. This wasn’t just an educated guess: Researchers had already looked at Trump’s first-term tariffs and found that the costs had passed through entirely to American consumers.

When it comes to gasoline, who even needs a model? One-fifth of the world’s oil trade passes through the Strait of Hormuz! Gas prices averaged $2.98 per gallon before the attack on Iran according to AAA; by mid-October, prices had been $4 or more for 165 days.

The fact is, no matter how annoyed it makes everyone, most every lasting price decline Americans have enjoyed has come in one of two ways: forcing companies to compete — with each other and with foreigners — to make things cheaper, or building more.

In 1901, the average American family spent 42.5% of its budget on food and another 14% on clothes. Fast forward 100 years, and those shares were 13.1% and 4.2%. Economists at the Dallas Fed once worked out what things used to cost in hours of work rather than just dollars: A three-pound chicken cost 2 hours and 37 minutes of labor in 1919 and just 14 minutes in 1997. And since 2000, the prices of things like TVs, washing machines, and laptops have fallen by roughly 25%.

None of that happened because of tariffs or price controls or a coordinated campaign against corporate greed. (I still don’t know what anyone really means by this, though I agree greed is bad.) It happened because companies had to compete for customers and we invited the world in to compete with domestic firms too. (Trade with China alone was worth about $1,500 a year to the average American household in the 2000s.)

Every alternative on offer has been tried, and none of them made anything cheaper: Trump’s washing machine tariffs raised prices, and San Francisco’s rent-control expansion shrank supply by 15%, pushing up rents. When Hungary capped prices on groceries in 2022, retailers made up the difference on non-capped goods and the right-populist government of Viktor Orban ushered in the highest food inflation in the EU.

One of the most important places we haven’t seen declining prices over the past century? Housing. Because we have made it illegal or impossible to actually meet demand. This is the central critique argued by the YIMBY movement which has spent decades pointing out that exclusionary zoning laws, permitting rules, environmental regulations, and historic preservation statutes were all conspiring to choke the supply of housing, thereby raising the cost.

In the San Francisco area, land use rules alone add more than $400,000 to the price of a quarter-acre lot. In Manhattan, barriers to building accounted for roughly half of what an apartment cost.

There are harder economic problems than making consumer goods cheap

Once you’ve graduated from the “increased competition generally provides cheaper goods” part of economics, you learn that there are some harder problems.

Healthcare has not seen cost declines, for instance, and unlike with housing almost no economist believes the answer is a freer market for healthcare. The first problem is the so-called cost disease that plagues labor-intensive sectors: When, for example, factories get more productive, wages rise in other fields because those fields have to pay more to lure people away from the productive industries.

But unlike a factory where wages can rise in tandem with productivity, a doctor can’t see that many more patients an hour without compromising care. As a result, healthcare (along with daycare and elder care) gets more expensive relative to other fields.

There are many other structural reasons why healthcare doesn’t function as a normal market where competition can lower prices, including the unique difficulty of comparison shopping for care.

This is why — even during the heyday of what we call “neoliberalism” — even centrist Democrats were champions of using the government to expand healthcare access. This remains the case in the party today: While the left is still divided on what “Medicare for All” should really look like, or whether abolishing private insurance is desirable, everyone agrees that healthcare is a market where the government has to do more than simply encourage competition. Basically nobody on the Democratic side disagrees with restoring the ACA subsidies, expanding Medicaid, and even creating some greater role for government-run healthcare.

The fact that Democrats have achieved a rough consensus on the second most important issue to voters is bad news for those who would like to use this election’s results to dethrone economists, enact a tariff regime (but left-ishly), and set prices for everything from airfare to rents to beer at Yankee Stadium.

No progressives in a tax revolt

Unfortunately for those of us who see the need for higher taxes in order to expand healthcare or other important social services, voters appear to be in full-on revolt against both new and existing taxes. Even the progressive standard-bearer in the Senate leadership race — Sen. Chris Van Hollen — has proposed a tax cut for the middle class.

Van Hollen at least proposes to offset this tax cut with higher taxes on millionaires. But across the country, politicians from both parties are promising property tax cuts even as their coffers scream for funds. In Florida, voters approved expanded property tax breaks for homeowners.

In California, a state where the case for taxing the rich should be especially strong, we got a test with the billionaire wealth tax that was supposed to provide funding for healthcare — and it lost with almost 53% of voters opposing it.¹

The harder lesson is that even a popular purpose like healthcare may not overcome voters’ resistance to higher taxes. Even Democratic Senate candidates who won with the support of the populist commentariat — Abdul El-Sayed, James Talarico, and Troy Jackson — have signaled that they are unwilling to push the government to raise new revenue.

Railing against rich people is undoubtedly good politics. Whether it was Barack Obama successfully painting Mitt Romney as an evil agent of capital or FDR declaring that the forces of “organized money” were “unanimous in their hate for me—and I welcome their hatred,” showing that your opponent is an out-of-touch elite is a time-worn recipe for political success.

The problem comes when you try to translate rhetoric that targets billionaires into policies whose only purpose is to attack free enterprise, competition, and low prices. This is the dilemma that populists seem unwilling to confront: Where they propose limiting competition to take on “corporate power,” they are set to raise prices. And where they are just expressing standard liberal commitments to expanded social services, they are going to have to raise taxes, an idea their elected champions seem hostile to.

All of us who want to do more with more have a hard road ahead. Doing more costs money, and voters keep telling us that they do not want to pay. But it starts by taking voters at their word. It starts with removing the ideological gloss that elites paint onto election results.

It starts, in short, by actually being a populist and, finally, putting low prices first.
