“You Can See Everything” is Critic Bait of the Most Sophisticated Variety

it’s a fun, interesting diversion being wildly overpraised by the class of people it was made to pander to

I will be doing a talk and signing about my brand new book All In Your Head (which is releasing next Tuesday, yikes!) at RJ Julia in Middletown, CT, on Monday November 2nd at 6PM. I’m afraid I haven’t done a very good job promoting it, so please come out if you’re in the area. Lots of All In Your Head pub. Here’s a (rave) New York Times review, here’s a piece in Fortune, here’s a review in Christianity Today, here’s my related piece for The Boston Globe. Grab yourself a copy if you haven’t already.

Nobody, it seems, can say exactly what happens in You Can See Everything. Sometimes this is presented as a way to preserve the experience for the viewer; before discussing the subject or the running time, critic after critic opens their review of Nathan Fielder and Lance Oppenheim’s Elizabeth Holmes documentary with a very same-y kind of throat-clearing: I don’t want to spoil it, it’s impossible to describe, you’ll never believe who turns up!, go in cold. The movie premiered at Telluride in a surprise, secret, phone-free screening, and the critics who were there have want to make sure you know how cool it was to be among the elect, ever since. Since the movie’s opening, the film has driven discourse to a degree never before achieved by a film documentary, farming thousands of positive reviews and an an immense amount of awards buzz. The result is a perfect Tomatometer rating, a Metacritic score in the high 90s, and a level of reverence that’s usually reserved for films made by our most celebrated auteurs. And, indeed, I think from here on out both Fielder and Oppenheim will find themselves in that category.

I… enjoyed it. There’s a lot of weird, interesting, provocative stuff in it. The first stretch of the movie, in which Fielder more or less moves into the Del Mar rental with Holmes and her partner Billy Evans as she spends her last free weeks before jail, is funny and provocative in the way Fielder is always funny and provocative. He lurks, simultaneously hiding and attracting others toward him, like one of those weird deep sea fish with a bioluminescent lure on their heads. He asks questions nobody else would ask Holmes - though, to be clear, this is true both because other people would be too polite but because other people would also find them vulgar to ask of a woman who genuinely risked people’s lives. There’s a lot of prodding at the documentary’s subject, feeling out her various forms of weirdness. Fielder plays Holmes the Abbot & Costello “Who’s on First?” bit to see whether she laughs; the point, of course, is provoking the ensuing awkwardness, which has been Fielder’s stock in trade for a long time. Then, about ninety minutes in, Holmes reports to prison, and it’s here where the movie loses me both on an ethical level but also in terms of pure enjoyment. The movie turns into something else entirely, a series of reenactments, re-examinations of footage we’ve already just watched, and [yes yes spoilers spoilers blah blah] Amanda Seyfried reprising her role from The Dropout and getting into it with Evans over wine. This pivot is definitely clever. Fielder is never not clever. But that turning point is also where the film starts repetitively mistaking cleverness for depth, and the critics have followed it right over the waterfall. What we’re left with is a movie that (like everything Fielder does) is ultimately all about him and a critical reception that’s derived from his expert manipulation of the class of people who write about movies...
