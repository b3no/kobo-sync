The “No New People” Epidemic

Many people aren’t “lonely.” They just refuse to interact with anyone they don’t already know, and it’s ruining socialization for everyone else.

Oct 2
∙

About a year ago, I wrote A Full Investigation of Why Nobody Comes to My Parties, which was exactly what you might imagine. For my entire life, I’ve struggled to get good party attendance—I’m talking fewer than 50% of invitees actually showing, often far fewer than that— and I wanted to figure out why. This article eventually caught the attention of one of my favorite podcasters, PJ Vogt of Search Engine, who turned it into an entire investigative journalism project and of course, a really funny podcast episode where I got some of my friends and families to share my most humiliating party blunders.

When I wrote the original article, and throughout the episode, I had the feeling that the issue was simply that I was, how you say, annoying. The episode essentially landed on this conclusion too (no offense taken). But—and I don’t say this to deny having an incredibly poor understanding of social cues for most of my life, which I’ve consciously improved—I don’t think it’s only me and my personality, because this is happening to many other people too, especially parents. In fact, one of the baffling parts of the podcast investigation was that my party attendance actually got worse after I learned social skills, which some commenters took as a sign that I was “unconvincingly masking.” But this drop in party turnout also happened after becoming a parent, and throwing parties for parents.

I tweeted about this issue, and many parents chimed in with their own stories about nobody showing up to parties or events they were hosting—sometimes even children’s birthday parties. And a few people were bluntly honest about why they never respond to invitations or attend parent or family events: they don’t want to meet anyone new. They don’t see the point of going to anything where they don’t already know everyone. Some people, bafflingly, declared that all other parents are boring (I’m either assuming these people aren’t actually parents, or are some of the least self-aware parents alive.)

So what the hell is going on? As we hear more and more about the “loneliness epidemic,” and how parents are so “isolated,” I’m wondering why so many people are rejecting opportunities to make friends. Moreover, I’m getting the sense that these flakey parents do have friends—probably friends from an earlier era in their life—and are simply resistant to any event where they might run the risk of encountering people they don’t already know. At some point, they had to have met those people, but presumably after a certain age or number of friends, we’re supposed to just shut that down.

So perhaps, for many parents, it’s not so much a “loneliness epidemic” but a “refusal to meet anyone new” epidemic. As I did for my own party predicament, I want to investigate why—because there’s no way every parent with an empty Paperless Post attendee tab is as annoying as I am.

My older child recently started going to a new school. I’m in the process of throwing a free, siblings-included, parents-included Halloween party at our house on a weekend, for my son’s class. It seemed like a fun thing to do, and we are a big Halloween family, so why not? I’ve sent two reminders to RSVP already. Want to guess what percentage of the class is coming? 25%. A couple people RSVP’d no, and then the rest are just…not responding at all. None of these people really know me yet, so this is not a repudiation of my personality, but may in fact be a repudiation of the entire concept of interacting with new people.

This issue is not unique to me. I was happy to see that a father of another child in the class was hosting a class picnic and playdate at the park. Despite having a good amount of friends at this point in our lives (we see friends at least once a week now) my husband and I are always open to making more, so we went. Besides, it wasn’t just about us—we were happy to give our kids an opportunity to socialize too.

We showed up and we were the only other parents there. Okay, after about an hour, one other set of parents showed. But still. That’s out of almost thirty families. The parents who hosted the picnic told us that they had been active in the school community for a while and attempted to host many of these events, but they always went the same way: the vast majority of parents don’t RSVP at all, and the same 10% of families show up, if that. They expressed the same confusion I had so many times before: if we are living in a loneliness epidemic where parents are “more isolated than ever” why are they apparently so complicit in their isolation?

Sadly, I have not enlisted PJ Vogt to help me sort this one out (the offer is open, PJ) so I’ll have to rely on anecdata and speculation like any good Substack writer.

First, the low-hanging fruit: people are “busy.” To some degree, this is true. People work (often both parents) and especially if you are a middle to upper middle class parent, people enroll their kids in absurd amounts of activities, leaving them with very little time for family bonding, let alone bonding with other families. Travel sports genuinely seem to take up more time than some actual jobs I’ve had, and even if a kid isn’t in travel sports, they’re probably involved in other scheduled activities that rule out most weekdays and perhaps some weekends. Just one example: I met a family whose three-year-old attends a private school ninety minutes away from home (so that she’s locked in through high school, of course) and she’s on a school bus for three hours a day, presumably returning home at around the same time as a corporate lawyer with a tiny Elmo briefcase. We can quibble about how many of these activities and long blocks of travel are “worth it,” but we can agree that they exist, and they obviously get in the way of attending social events.

Second, some social events genuinely are inaccessible for parents, and maddeningly, this applies to many events designed “for parents.” This has not been true of events I’ve thrown. I’ve always made sure that the entire family (including siblings) would be invited. I’ve never asked people to pay, unlike this one dad from my son’s preschool class who asked for a $20 “donation” per family towards a few Domino’s pizzas he brought to the park (he went to the Soe Noire school of business.) But the lack of accessibility and affordability has been a problem with many other events. A great deal of the parent events to which I’ve been invited (often school-oriented) were parents-only and took place at night. Wonderful concept, because sometimes adults just want to have drinks together, but for many parents, it’s not that easy to “find a sitter.” If we can get procure free babysitting from our in-laws on a weekend when they’re available, we will probably prioritize a romantic date night as opposed to a parent happy hour at a dive bar, especially given that we don’t really drink. If we had a sitter we could hire on demand, any weekend, then the availability wouldn’t be a problem but then we would be paying $100 in babysitting fees to attend a parent bingo night which also costs $20 per person, which also might suck. At that point, you could just go to a fancy restaurant and have a much better time!

And sometimes, we really want to go to things, and we just can’t find a sitter! This happened recently when we were invited to an adults-only potluck and couldn’t find anyone to watch the kids. We figured, oh well, it’s just a potluck, probably not that fun, and we even joked about how ludicrous it was to bar kids from something as casual as a potluck. Later, we found out it was a cocktail attire potluck where people were dancing in their finery all night, and other parents described it as the “party of the year.” Well, fuck me.

So yes, there are some understandable reasons why parents might just bail on things, even if they want to go. But come on. If it’s a weekend event with no cover charge and the entire family is invited, there is no good reason that 70-80% of the attendees would be too busy to even respond to the invitation at all. If it’s hosted by a person, and not a committee or a school, it’s even weirder not to RSVP or to attempt to go. I have a sneaking suspicion that most people who flake on these invites have no real reason other than “not wanting to.” In fact, when I ran a survey on this topic, 68% of respondents admitted they had declined a party invitation (not a school-sponsored event, but a party) because they just didn’t feel like it. Apparently, this now extends to weddings, and if you Google “nobody came to my baby shower” you will find a plethora of stories on Reddit, Babycenter, and other mom forums about baby showers with quite literally zero attendees. At a certain point, “busy” can’t be the reason.

This was more or less confirmed in the social media response to my Search Engine episode. Multiple people smugly pointed out that nobody—especially not a parent—would want to take the risk of going to an event where they didn’t already know everyone, especially if it required any modicum of effort in dressing. They floated some horrific worst-case scenarios, such as “being bored” or “things getting awkward.” And when I tweeted about this topic, plenty of people echoed the same sentiment: “Why would I go to any event hosted by someone who was not already my close friend?”

This is tricky for me, because I don’t believe I (or anyone) is entitled to anyone else’s attention or friendship. For a long time, I’ve said that you can’t be morally outraged when people decide not to be friends with you or date you based on a first impression, even if their reasons are shallow and silly. If you make no attempt to learn social cues or social skills, or behaving weirdly, and people think you’re off-putting, they aren’t being “mean” by rejecting you. So, in the same vein, if you throw an party which is unappealing, nobody is obligated to show up. (I do think that the laws of etiquette require them to RSVP, but no, they don’t have to want to go.)

And yet, surely there is some middle ground between “attending every event that crosses my path because it’s my duty as an American” and “bailing on any event that isn’t solely hosted and attended by my best friends, also I am literally forty years old.” I am troubled by so many people being completely uninterested in (or perhaps, scared of) meeting new people or attending events that aren’t thrown by a small, pre-approved group of besties. While I’ve seen many different types of people profess their phobia for new friends, parents seem especially guilty of this, especially because they “already have friends,” typically people they’ve known since college or earlier. And while I know I can’t guilt or shame these people into being more extroverted and open-minded, it does worry me that this is happening en masse.

Think about what this means for a family who moves to a new city. They’ll probably want to make friends, as would anyone in that scenario. But what if most people there have decided they’re “full” because they already have four friends from high school? It’s going to be much harder for the new family to meet anyone! What if the new family figures the best way to make friends is to join a school community, but quickly discovers nobody attends those events either? Presumably, they could try to suss out the other newcomers, but what if there aren’t many? And what if those people are also “busy” and “tired” and can’t be asked? Many people do not live in the town where they went to college, and will have moments where they need to make friends as an adult. This is a much smoother process when most other adults are at least open to meeting new people.

The same phenomenon is happening at bars. This is something I’ve witnessed but also heard about from singles who have tried to meet other singles at bars. My husband and I were really involved in nightlife in the 2010s, and we often met new friends at bars (some of which actually stuck) and our single friends were always having spontaneous meet-cutes with mysterious strangers. But in 2026, people mostly go to bars with an existing group of people and have no intention of meeting anyone new. If you go to a bar, you’ll feel like you walked into a middle school homecoming dance, where every clique is sticking together and avoiding each other. People complain about the lack of “third spaces,” but first of all, we already have third spaces, and second of all, even in the spaces explicitly meant for socialization (bars, ticketed events, etc.) most people aren’t actually interacting with anyone new! Something tells me that if someone invented the perfect library-cat cafe-park third space combo, people would still just show up there with their existing groups of friends and then refuse to speak to anyone else.

For all the talk about Gen Z and their pathological anxiety about socialization and in-person interactions, millennials aren’t much better. Almost all the parents bailing on event invitations are doing so strictly because they’re uncomfortable doing anything outside of their usual routine and just can’t be bothered. Okay, perhaps they’re not literally shaking and having a panic attack at the prospect of attending a disco-themed happy hour, but the prospect of mild discomfort (boredom, awkwardness, not knowing anyone) is keeping them home when it really shouldn’t.

Derek Thompson recently wrote about the decline in hosting and having friends over for dinner. One thing he pointed out is that all this time when people claim they’re “busy” is actually just spent at home. He mostly focused on people who aren’t hosting at all, not people who are just declining invites, but I’m sure there’s quite a bit of overlap between those groups…and as the data shows, these people are mostly just at home, not gallivanting around town with their existing friends. Now, maybe they are doing something tremendously important at home. Maybe they’re cracking AGI alignment from their couches as we speak. But I have a feeling that this time looks more like “mainlining low-budget true crime documentaries made entirely out of body camera footage.”

I can forgive people being too busy to host parties. I’m fine hosting every time, and I do not expect everyone to reciprocate! But the least you can do is participate in society and show up now and again. At the very least, reply. And if that’s too much for you, I fear you are no better then the zoomers who can’t make a phone call.
