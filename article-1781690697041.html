The experts are wrong about 2026

The Argument's modeling breaks with Cook and Sabato on dozens of races

This cycle, I’ve been baffled at how expert ratings seem to be forecasting a neutral-to-good year for Republicans in the House. As of this moment, both The Cook Political Report and Sabato’s Crystal Ball both show more seats leaning Republican than Democratic.¹

I think this is completely wrong. In fact, all of the modeling we’re doing suggests that this isn’t even close. The House leans Democratic, and extremely strongly so. That’s why our midterms model finds that Democrats have an 86% chance of winning the lower chamber, even after all of the mid-decade redistricting takes place, with a median outcome of 229 Democrats to 206 Republicans.

On a seat-by-seat level, it looks even more grim for Republicans. The Texas gerrymander that the party created to bolster its majority is likely to net the party just two new seats instead of the five they originally envisioned.² Meanwhile, Democrats are cleaning up in the heavily Hispanic seats that had recently begun to slide away from them.

On a seat-by-seat basis, it also means that districts like the redrawn 2nd Congressional District in Alabama — which Cook rates as solid Republican — are actually toss-ups in a year that is this blue, while Trump +10 Hispanic seats like Texas’ 35th Congressional District are arguably tilting Democratic.

How is this possible? Simply put, experts aren’t pricing in how blue the year actually is. Cook’s ratings imply a neutral year. But our work finds that this is shaping up to be a very, very blue year. We currently forecast a November margin of D+8 for Democrats, which would make this the bluest congressional environment since 2008.

That’s why we disagree so strongly with the expert ratings on a lot of these districts. This set of outcomes is what our model would expect in a neutral year, rather than a blue wave. Our model has major divergences with Cook and Crystal Ball in 25 districts — and in each case, we think the outcome is substantially more Democratic than experts are forecasting right now.

I have a lot of respect for people at rating agencies. It’s a difficult job that requires considering a lot of public and private evidence. Experts tend to wait for an overwhelming amount of evidence before shifting races, and in many cases, I think this approach makes sense, especially as waves tend to break late toward the winning party.

But in this case, I think such an approach is wrong. If an election were held today, our polling suggests that Democrats would comfortably win many districts currently rated at “toss-up” by both Cook and Crystal Ball.³ It’s not correct to say that Texas’ 35th is “likely Republican” at the moment, or that Alabama’s 2nd is “solid Republican,” especially when the balance of the evidence suggests that those seats are already competitive today.

The Argument is not alone in forecasting a blue tsunami. Look at the generic ballot aggregation from Silver Bulletin or from VoteHub, which both find Democrats with a healthy and growing lead on the generic ballot right now. Or consider the May poll from The New York Times/Siena College, which found Democrats with an 11-point advantage among registered voters in May.

This wave is comparable to (or even exceeds) the 2018 blue wave, but the seat-by-seat picture looks quite different because the electoral map has changed. Our model currently projects Democrats to win just 229 seats, which would be six fewer than they won in 2018, despite winning the national vote by more.

Why? Redistricting. Through mid-decade gerrymanders, Republicans have strengthened their vulnerable incumbents and created new pickup opportunities for themselves. Our model expects Republicans to flip 10 seats this cycle, and all but one of them — Maine’s 2nd Congressional District, a Trump +9 seat — are due to redistricting-related gains. Conversely, of the 23 seats our model has flipping to Democrats, just six (across California and Utah) are due to redistricting-related gains.

The Republican redistricting win, however, simply limits the ceiling for Democrats. Even after accounting for redistricting-related gains and losses on both sides, Democrats need to flip a net of just six seats to win control of the chamber back. And they’re in prime position to do just that, as the 10 most vulnerable non-redistricted seats in today’s map are all held by Republicans.

Perhaps surprisingly, 2026 is shaping up to be so blue that the Senate is actually a toss-up. Democrats currently have a 44% chance of flipping the upper chamber, despite needing to win at least two seats that Trump won by 10 points or more.

Democrats are actually favored to flip North Carolina and Ohio, while Alaska is rated as a toss-up. Meanwhile, Trump +2 Georgia is at “safe Democratic,” thanks to incumbent Democrat Jon Ossoff’s candidate strength, while the sheer magnitude of the anti-Republican sentiment across the country currently means Graham Platner — who has been plagued by scandal — might still be a slight favorite to defeat Susan Collins in Maine.

For those of you used to getting your forecasts from expert qualitative raters, which have a long track record of accuracy, a prediction like this may come as a shock.⁴ But it’s one that we stand by. The House is not competitive, and the Senate is a toss-up.

Methodology (very wonky):

First, we constructed a generic ballot average of registered voters, which is weighted for sample size, recency, and pollster quality (according to VoteHub’s pollster scorecard). Next, we calibrated the registered voter microdata from all of The Argument/Verasight’s monthly polls in 2026 to this baseline average and then applied a likely voter screen to the survey data.

After doing this, we calculated the swing from the presidential margins at a state, regional, and national level, for each demographic — with each geography’s swing weighted for sample size in the composite aggregate. We then constructed a likely electorate at the district and state level alike and applied this demographic-level swing to that electorate, before adjusting each estimate for incumbency and prior candidate overperformance (using Split Ticket’s Wins Above Replacement scores).

This was then blended with two other components: a statistical model trained on the last four election cycles and polling averages internally computed for each House and Senate race. Finally, we conducted a Monte Carlo simulation, with correlated error terms on demographic, state, and national levels.

Polling microdata is taken from monthly The Argument/Verasight surveys. All other polls were aggregated via VoteHub’s polling API. Likely voter screens are created through a blended model of the data from the Catalist voter file and self-reported voter enthusiasm.
