Nobody likes a party crasher

Trump has ruined America’s 250th anniversary. I’m still celebrating.

Halina Bennet
Jul 1
∙
Paid
 
READ IN APP
 

I don’t feel like celebrating America’s 250th anniversary, and the fact that I don’t is pissing me off.

The semiquincentennial is the anniversary of a country that, whatever else is true of it, has functioned for a quarter millennium as a bet that people who show up with nothing can build something. Donald Trump and Trumpism have spent the past year and a half turning the anniversary into a branding exercise to further the inherently unpatriotic MAGA project.

You’d be forgiven for not following the inside baseball of the country’s 250th, because a nonpartisan anniversary should not be something to keep an eye on.

Congress set up the U.S. Semiquincentennial Commission — the body behind America250 — way back in 2016. It included 16 private citizens, four senators, and four House members, with the congressional seats split evenly by party; George W. Bush and Barack Obama later signed on as honorary co-chairs. Then, nine days into his second term, Trump signed an executive order creating a second, competing anniversary program. He installed himself as chair and directed the Interior Department to treat Freedom 250 as the “primary branding” for the whole affair, redirecting at least $10 million in funds Congress had appropriated for the nonpartisan commission.

Unlike America250, Freedom 250 doesn’t disclose its donors online and has been selling access by the tier. Half a million dollars buys V.I.P. seating at every event, $1 million buys a private reception with the president and what the group calls a “historic photo opportunity,” while $2.5 million buys a speaking slot at a July 4 event in D.C.

When several of the musical acts booked for the marquee concert pulled out rather than be associated with it, the president threatened to cancel the concerts instead of recruit new performers. He said he would hold “a giant MAKE AMERICA GREAT AGAIN RALLY” and dismissed the lineup as “overpriced singers, who nobody wants to hear.” He has made himself the headliner.

All this is par for the course from a president who has time and time again put himself before the country he is meant to serve. And it isn’t really his bummer celebration that is getting to me; I’m mostly sad that Americans’ attitude toward America has soured under the president’s shameful leadership.

Surveys from major polling organizations have found that national pride is falling while pessimism is rising, and young Americans especially are rejecting displays of patriotism. Gallup found that more than three quarters of Americans believe that the nation’s founders would be disappointed by the country today. The Public Religion Research Institute found that Americans are both less patriotic and less sure of what patriotism means.

The argument that it feels improper to celebrate America right now is not a fringe take, especially on the left. But this is still a country worth celebrating, and surrendering patriotism to Trump’s version of it lets him and his movement define what the nation stands for. Once Americans do that, we give up on this country, and any hope of restoring the project of independence and freedom is dead. These are ideals I believe are worth fighting for.

My grandmother and her parents came to the United States after the Holocaust destroyed their lives in Poland. This was the country that took them in and allowed them to rebuild. That opportunity made them, and now me, patriots.

My family’s story is not unique; it is the most common story in this country. I grew up in Colorado, where more than 80,000 refugees have resettled and rebuilt since 1980, becoming homeowners, small-business owners, taxpayers, voters, and neighbors. This possibility is the result of a promise made by this country.

That promise is being broken right now, and it is up to those of us who believe in it to hold this administration accountable. Colorado Public Radio reported in March on a 30-year-old Afghan man who mapped satellite data for the U.S.-backed government in Kabul before the Taliban retook the country in 2021. After the takeover, he fled to Iran, found a Denver nonprofit through a cold LinkedIn message, and was eventually granted humanitarian parole into Colorado in June 2023, where he built a small business detailing cars. He has done everything by the book, but his pathway to citizenship is no clearer.

In December 2025, after Trump paused asylum processing, he got a notice to appear at an ICE check-in. He was allowed to leave after the appointment, but his case is still unresolved and could take years. He’s one of roughly 5,000 people in Colorado who are currently stuck in that kind of limbo, Colorado Public Radio reports.

Eighty years separate my family’s welcome from his delayed one, but both stories unfold under the same flag. His treatment, which is owned entirely by this administration, is not a verdict on the country that made the original promise.

I understand the argument that it is naive, and perhaps dishonest, to wave a flag or call yourself a patriot when the people running the government have made patriotism feel synonymous with loyalty to them. I understand, too, the feeling that it is impossible to celebrate a country that produced these current conditions. Watching the anniversary get taken over and monetized by the man breaking each of the country’s promises makes the idea of flying a flag feel like agreeing with him.

But ceding the flag, the word “patriot,” and this anniversary to Trump and the movement built around him is a forfeit. It only gives him the one thing he actually wants, which is sole custody of what it means to love this country.

Patriotism and nationalism aren’t synonyms. One is loyalty to a place and to the standard it set for itself, including the duty to say where it’s fallen short. The other is loyalty to a brand, an in-group, and a leader who just happens to be holding the flag. The administration usurping “America’s birthday” does not have a stronger claim to loving this country than the people walking away from it do. But those people who walk away are the ones who will be at fault for handing the country over to the thing they claim to hate.

There is plenty to be ashamed of in American history. There was slavery and the violent displacement of Native nations, the incarceration of roughly 120,000 Japanese Americans without trial during World War II, and the fact that we didn’t federally guarantee Black Americans the vote nationwide until the Voting Rights Act of 1965. That history is something to remember and remark on, but this country also has a Constitution flexible enough to be amended 27 times, including amendments that corrected some of its worst sins; it has won civil rights fights at the ballot box and in court, not only in the streets; and it still naturalizes hundreds of thousands of new citizens almost every year — people who, by most measures, describe themselves as more patriotic than those born here. One new citizen in D.C. this winter told National Public Radio that even before she naturalized, she had become an American “in my mind and my heart.”

So I am sad that 250 years — the only major milestone that I can count on being around for — is one I am not in the mood to celebrate. This is a country worth keeping, and the people who actually believe in the experiment and its unfinished promise are the ones who can save it from a movement that has never shown much interest in any of that. I’m proud to believe in the democratic experiment and grateful to have been born in a country that has given me educational, social, and career opportunities that seem like a distant dream to people all over the world. And I want those opportunities not only to survive but to expand during the country’s 250th year and every year after Trump has become a fading stain on our democracy.

Walking away does not punish Trump. It just leaves him holding the flag in victory, which is the one outcome he can’t achieve on his own. I’m not giving him mine, even though I’m angry that he crashed the party.
