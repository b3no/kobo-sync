Landlords are inevitable

State control doesn’t make tradeoffs go away.

Matthew Yglesias
Jul 23
∙
Paid
 
READ IN APP
 

I’ve had three different landlords as an adult. One of them did a pretty good job but was obstinate about trying to charge more rent than the place was worth. Another was a total catastrophe in almost every respect. The third was basically fine, except he decided he wanted to get out of the landlording business and sell the house I was renting, so I had to leave.

I don’t know if my experience is typical, but I do think it’s indicative of the kinds of problems people have with landlords. They can be shitty. They can be good but charge a premium for not being shitty. Or they can be good and charge reasonable rent and then realize that it’s not necessarily worth the trouble.

This is one reason I think the idea of large institutional landlords is underrated.

A big player can try to do what Walmart or Chipotle or Hilton Hotels does and actually build a reputation for providing reasonable service at reasonable prices and benefit from scale. Mom and pop can’t.

Regardless, though, I understand why people often find landlords frustrating. And I get the appeal of this line in the new Democratic Socialists of America platform about how under socialism “you don’t pay a mortgage or have a landlord, because comfortable housing is a human right.”

But this doesn’t make sense. If you live in a home you don’t own, then you have a landlord. It’s just that the landlord will be a public sector entity rather than a private one.

It’s worth thinking about, say, public school: Kids have a right to a free public education in the United States, but that doesn’t mean schools don’t have rules.

It does mean, in practice, that it’s harder to get kicked out of public school than private school. But from many families’ perspectives, that’s actually a downside of public school. One child’s right to his place in public school is another child’s need to put up with disorderly behavior.

In other regards, the dynamic flips — a public school can be tougher with grades because it has less need to please the customers.

The point, though, is that while a public school can be managed in many ways, the mere fact that it’s public doesn’t answer the question of how it should be managed or guarantee that it’ll be managed well. And the exact same thing is true of a public housing project. You might not call the person in charge of it “the landlord,” but someone is making decisions about who can live where and under what terms and what happens if the elevator breaks.

Real scarcities matter

The D.S.A. view is that housing, along with food, energy, medicine, and transportation, is one of several consumption goods that are so necessary for life as to be too important to leave up to the whims of the market.

Note, though, that this is more or less already how we think about food.

We have more than our share of problems in this country, but thanks to the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program (SNAP) and America’s food banks, we don’t have people starving to death. State action to support the poor is one important reason for that. But part of why SNAP works as well as it does is that groceries are objectively abundant in America. Of course, on any given day, people looking to feed their families may feel the squeeze. But that’s because Americans looking to feed their families are generally aiming for something more than basic caloric sustenance. If you’re willing to shop frugally, food is pretty cheap. As a result, a public sector program to give people money to buy food can successfully feed them without breaking the bank.

We have a comparable program, Section 8 vouchers, for housing.

The narrow problem with Section 8 is that unlike SNAP, it’s not funded in a way that ensures all eligible families get a voucher. About three-quarters of the people who theoretically qualify do not receive housing assistance.

The deeper problem, though, is that there aren’t enough homes for everyone. If I give you some money, point you at a supermarket, and tell you to go buy a cheap meal, you’ll easily walk out with a cheap meal. Housing is not like that at all.

Medical care and energy are in less acutely short supply, but they still suffer from real scarcities. There aren’t a lot of unemployed doctors wandering the streets of America. The provision (or not) of health insurance and the financing of health care services are important topics. But if some people are forgoing large amounts of useful health care, that’s at least in part a supply-side issue.

And the United Kingdom has shown us over the past five or 10 years that if you starve a totally public system of resources, you can still have acute supply shortages.

Here in the United States, mass transit systems are all publicly owned. But that doesn’t guarantee that service will be frequent or convenient. To make that happen, cities need to actually build trains or buy buses and hire drivers and otherwise do the work of managing complicated systems. The appeal of the SNAP model is precisely that it lets you skip all that. Walmart figures out how to run a store and the government focuses on making sure poor people have income support.

In terms of energy, the United States has all kinds of utility models. There are tons of publicly owned electrical utilities in this country. Most of them are very small, but there’s also the Tennessee Valley Authority. Lots of people are customers of private regulated utilities. Some are vertically integrated (i.e., the utility owns the power plants), while others are restructured (i.e., the utility buys power on a market).

There are pros and cons to all of these models, but none of them have made electricity free or eliminated pollution or done any of the other good things you might want to accomplish. That’s because making clean energy abundant requires all this object-level work — you need to build the solar farms and the batteries and the transmission lines and the nuclear reactors.

You can’t just say, “well, it’s a common good now, problem solved” when you haven’t actually solved anything.

Location, location, location

This is particularly true for housing, where even though it’s a commodity that’s bought and sold for profit, the status quo is that there’s no free market in building.

You could completely eliminate the capitalistic aspects of this system and all the problems would recur.

Say you’ve been living somewhere for 10 years and now suddenly, for whatever reason, way more people are interested in living in that location. Is the fair system to re-run a new lottery for this suddenly in-demand location, in which case you and other incumbent residents might be displaced? Or is it fairer to say whoever was there first gets the prime location forever? What if it’s a three-bedroom unit and your kids are all grown up and there are young couples with babies who need larger dwellings? If we stick with continuity, we’re going to have a situation where none of the kids who grew up in this neighborhood are able to live there as adults — where will they go? Do we turn farms into new housing developments or are those beloved aspects of the landscape?

It seems to me that if a given location is in high demand — in the socialist utopia, this would be oversubscribed lotteries rather than high prices — the most reasonable step is to build more there.

But who decides this? Is it at the discretion of the housing authority or is it decided mechanically as a result of the lottery? Does the local community get a say in disruptive changes? Aren’t politicians going to be inclined to listen to the local community and not build, whatever the formal process is?

Having won the lottery to secure a good house wouldn’t have financial value in this system — you couldn’t sell the right to live there. Of course, people would try, and there would have to be Sublet Cops trying to stop them. But the good house would still be valuable, and NIMBYs would still try to protect that value. And none of the fundamentals of land use and planning will have changed because we’re already talking about a centrally planned system. And just as a public school needs to decide on rules of discipline, so would a public landlord — what would the terms of occupancy be? What would happen if you violated them?

I don’t deny that abolishing all private homeownership in the United States would be a big deal, because obviously it would be a huge deal. But it would be a big deal that paradoxically wouldn’t change any of the main pain points.

The ultimate “tenant’s right” is exit

One big upside of a free market is that if you’re not happy with the person on the other side of the transaction, you can just leave. I went to a restaurant the other day, looked at the menu, and ordered something that struck me as a little overpriced but that I was hoping would be good. But when the food came, it wasn’t very good. So I’m not going to do that again.

When a landlord screws you over, it’s harder to just pick up and move because the logistics are disruptive and the change can’t be made instantly.

Still, the fundamentals of a healthy housing market should be that if people aren’t happy with the quality of service provided by their landlord, they can leave. All the strong tenant protection laws in the world can’t come close to matching the power of the ability to exit a bad situation.

Indeed, tenant protections can be a double-edged sword. In D.C., at one point they made it so landlords couldn’t evict violent tenants, which is a “pro-tenant” policy only if you take a very dim view of the behavior of the average renter. The right that people need is the right to move, ideally without moving across the country or necessarily even across the city. That requires an abundant supply of housing in the locations where people want to live.

The risk of government-run housing is eliminating all possibility of exit. There’s just one landlord, and if they’re doing a bad job, you’re stuck. You haven’t eliminated landlords — you’ve created a universal landlording monopoly.

Subscribed

Don’t get me wrong — the status quo housing situation facing young people is genuinely dire. But we need solutions that work. Less-constrained zoning, more access to credit, and even really boring stuff like making the unemployment insurance system work better for construction workers will make things better by giving people more exit options. Trying to ban landlords won’t, because at the end of the day the landlord is inevitable and unavoidable. Competition is the only remedy.
