The housing market is way out of whack

With mortgage rates above 7 percent and stocks over-performing, some Americans are rethinking their investments. Meanwhile, Portland is having a rent mismatch.

Halina Bennet
Sep 28
∙
Paid
 
READ IN APP
 

Mortgage rates rose above 7 percent for the first time since the beginning of 2025, with the average rate on a 30-year home mortgage hitting 7.03 percent on Thursday, according to data from Freddie Mac.

Home loan rates are pegged to the 10-year Treasury bond yield, which hit a near two-decade high of just under 5.3 percent last week — an increase driven by inflation and bolstered by the war in Iran. Inflation rested at an annual pace of 3.4 percent in August, causing the Federal Reserve to raise interest rates earlier this month.

Back in February, before the Iran war began, the average 30-year mortgage rate briefly dropped below 6 percent, giving people cause to believe that this might be the year the housing market would bounce back from the dip that followed the Covid-era boom. Instead, the spring and summer homebuying seasons were a bust, with August seeing the lowest rate of sales in 14 months. The shock caused by rates crossing the 7 percent psychological barrier is likely to make buyers and sellers further slow their activity, Bloomberg reports.

Another factor contributing to out-of-whack costs is A.I.-driven capital spending, which is transforming the economy. While the boom has clear economic upsides — the S&P 500 is on fire, posting double-digit annual gains four years in a row — it’s also pushing up the cost of capital and the price of energy and other resources.

These factors, Fortune reports, are making a stock investment much more attractive than a home investment to young Americans. Shut out of the housing market, young people are electing to rent and put their money into stocks rather than save toward a down payment on a home they then may not be able to afford. Economists Michael Luca and Raymond Fisman wrote in an op-ed for the Wall Street Journal that the mismatch is, indeed, altering the way we should think about investment. Owning a house, they argue, is a nice thing that comes with many upsides. But it is not, at this point, a good investment when the S&P 500 rose 235 percent over 10 years, compared with 87 percent for home prices over the same period.

“Even after recognizing other financial benefits of ownership, such as a tax code that puts a thumb on the scale toward buying, opportunity cost makes the returns to homeownership considerably less spectacular,” Luca and Fisman write.

Even if homeownership isn’t in the cards, housing affordability is top of mind for Americans, and the issue has become central to key campaigns this midterm cycle. Candidates like New Hampshire Senate candidate Chris Pappas — a Democrat — are increasingly tying private investors to rental affordability, the Financial Times reports. Pappas is advocating for stricter guardrails on where private capital can be invested. According to analysis by the Financial Times, more than two dozen congressional candidates have pledged to support similar restrictions on private-equity and hedge-fund housing purchases.

The risk of overbuilding and of poorly calibrated subsidies

Portland’s rental market has reached a tipping point where market-rate and subsidized affordable housing are comparably priced and competing for the same renters, the Wall Street Journal reports. A glut of apartments and a weak local economy have resulted in flat market-rate rents and narrowed the gap between the two types of housing. Government-set rents for income-restricted units, by contrast, have continued to rise.

As a result, about 40 percent of the city’s market-rate housing now rents for within 15 percent of the price of subsidized units. This makes market-rate apartments, which have amenities and far fewer administrative and bureaucratic hurdles, more attractive to some low-income renters. As a result, affordable-housing providers are stuck with empty apartments. About 2,200 income-restricted units in the city are vacant.

The same dynamic — to a lesser degree — is playing out in Austin, where rapid multifamily construction pushed market rents down and brought market-rate units into competition with income-restricted ones, and in Seattle, where the city has acknowledged that increases in area median income can push income-restricted rents higher as market-rate rents stay flat. Denver is also seeing an imbalance in vacancies, where they are elevated in units reserved for those earning between 60 percent and 80 percent of area median income.

These cities, with Portland being the most extreme example, may be early warnings of an emerging national mismatch in which cities have plenty of housing for households earning moderately less than area median income but too little for the poorest renters.

California’s creative housing blocks

A subsidized housing project meets a segregation argument
Residents of Mill Valley, Calif., the Bay Area’s whitest town, sued to block a unanimously approved 45-unit affordable-housing project on city-owned land, arguing that it would worsen segregation, the San Francisco Chronicle reports. Supporters of the project say it would help diversify the community as well as meet state housing requirements, while opponents argue that affordable housing is already concentrated in the affluent, 80 percent non-Hispanic white town’s less affluent east side (where the project is located) and should be distributed more widely. The group of neighbors sued on the basis that the site violates the state’s “affirmatively furthering fair housing” directive because the location is lower-income and less homogenous than the rest of town.

Mill Valley is in Marin County, just north of San Francisco. The county has struggled to meet state housing targets amid high home prices, limited and expensive construction, and longstanding patterns of housing segregation.

Whose habitat?
Further south, a plan to build 398 homes at a country club in Los Angeles stalled in August after city planners determined that the golf course qualifies as a mountain lion habitat, forcing the developer to undergo the city’s more arduous approval process rather than use a state law designed to fast-track housing on commercial land, the Los Angeles Times reports.

The proposed development includes 175 for-sale homes, 223 rental apartments — 34 of which are rent restricted — and more than 55,900 square feet of open space. City Councilmember Bob Blumenfield supported the determination, citing ecological research and fire risk in the area, while the developer disputes the finding on the basis that experts concluded the site is not a protected wildlife habitat.

This week, Jon Rawlings, a chapter leader with YIMBY Los Angeles, shared a pointed interpretation of the dispute in the Orange County Register. He argues that the mountain lion designation is the latest in a series of efforts by the city and neighborhood groups to block the housing project, including past attempts to challenge its eligibility for the state’s expedited approval process. Rawlings writes that opponents rejected an offer to discuss a lower-density compromise. He frames this particular dispute as a case study in a broader conflict playing out in Los Angeles among neighborhood opposition, environmental and fire-safety concerns, and the city’s need to build more housing.

Scott Wiener’s housing record in question
State Senator Scott Wiener has made housing the defining issue of his more-than-10-year tenure in California’s legislature, and he continues to center it in his campaign for the U.S. House of Representatives. He has spent his time in office pushing for laws designed to increase construction, streamline approvals, and encourage density. One of his earliest measures, Senate Bill 35, has facilitated the construction of more than 18,000 housing units, according to a U.C. Berkeley analysis cited by the San Francisco Chronicle.

But the Chronicle asks: “Has Wiener’s avalanche of housing bills actually led to more homes?”

The Chronicle writes that many of Wiener’s initiatives have produced little housing as the state’s affordability crisis worsens. SB 79 — the highly visible bill permitting higher-density housing near transit — took effect in July and has yet to yield significant housing gains. The same is true for his other bills. Implementation and construction take time, and it is too early to measure impact in most cases.

So, then, to answer the Chronicle’s question, his bills have facilitated the construction of at least 18,000 units, and it is — as the paper itself acknowledges — too early to know what the rest of the bills will produce. Wiener’s record is under a microscope as he runs to succeed U.S. Representative Nancy Pelosi, with tenant, labor, and environmental groups criticizing his supply-focused housing bills over concerns about displacement, development, and worker conditions.

Further reading

Meta’s Louisiana data center is driving a housing boom
Meta’s planned $50 billion data center in rural Richland Parish, La., is already reshaping the local housing market as thousands of construction workers arrive in an area that has limited rental inventory, Business Insider reports. With as many as 7,500 workers expected in a town of 20,000 for several years during peak construction, new mobile-home and R.V. parks are popping up, rents are spiking, and workforce housing is all the rage — for operators and residents alike.

One local operator leased every one of its four-bedroom homes to Meta-related workers for $3,000 per month with utilities included. The area’s rents before the data-center project were roughly $1,200 to $1,500 for a similar home.

The boom is benefiting related local businesses and contractors, giving residents new employment options in a town with few other opportunities. Developers are uncertain about how demand will change once construction is finished, illustrating the instability created when a moment of economic opportunity meets the longer-term housing challenges that large data-center projects can create in rural communities.

Oregon moves to open its affordable-housing books
Oregon gave developers an unprecedented $1.4 billion, and the cost of building apartments has doubled. But a carve-out in state records law keeps the financial details secret, a ProPublica investigation from June revealed. After the outlet’s reporting was published, State Representative Pam Marsh, who chairs Oregon’s House Committee on Housing and Homelessness, said she plans to introduce a bill next year to repeal the exemption. State Senator Khanh Pham, her counterpart in the Senate, said she wants to tackle it, too, ProPublica reports.

A teenager takes record keeping into his own hands
Neil Rao, a 17-year-old high school junior in San Mateo, Calif., has mapped San Francisco’s rent-controlled housing units. In analyzing approximately 463,000 San Francisco Rent Board records, he found that they are not concentrated in poor neighborhoods. Rent-control protections track building age (whether it was built before or after 1979), not income, so wealthier areas, which in San Francisco tend to be the historic neighborhoods like Pacific Heights, have the densest concentrations of rent-controlled units. The city, itself, has no official inventory of them.

Read more about Rao’s work filling in the city’s records from the San Francisco Standard.
