Big words, small change

The federal government budgets for decline in the book sector

I downloaded the federal budget last week and started to read it cover to cover, as one does.

Well, maybe I didn’t read all 511 pages. No human does that. But I gave it a good look. I scanned the entire table of contents—that’s five pages right there. And I plunged ahead into the cover letter, jointly authored by the finance minister and the prime minister. It’s highly unusual for a prime minister to co-sign a budget cover letter. It made me think this budget isn’t just another budget … maybe something of a new Liberal platform. I read the whole damn cover letter—another seven pages.

I was thrilled by what I read. “In an increasingly turbulent world, Canada is determining our future…. Budget 2026 is the next step of our ambitious, pragmatic, generational plan to make Canadians maîtres chez nous, building our sovereignty and giving us the freedom to live our lives as we choose.” Hurrah.

There was a lot of stuff about investing to make Canada stronger, along with the usual boasting about everything the government is already doing for Canadians. In terms of new initiatives, the budget promised action against consumer-gouging oligopolies in banking and telecommunications. It promised action on homelessness and affordable housing. And the next item up—something that never gets mentioned this high on a budget priority list—culture! Ahead of national defence! Ahead of disaster preparedness and government efficiency!

Being masters of our destiny will allow Canadian culture in all its diversity and creativity to flourish. Budget 2026 provides unprecedented support to Canadian culture and recreation—more than five times last budget’s record levels…. We’re empowering creativity to tell great Canadian stories in our own voices, in our own ways.

That sounded fantastic, so I zipped through the chapter on Canada’s investment supercycle, and the one on making Canada more competitive and connected, and the one on affordability, and arrived finally on page 223, the start of chapter four: “A strong and united Canada.” This was the culture chapter:

Canada was an unlikely country, born of complexity, necessity, and a collective will to unite in the face of adversity. Over time, we have grown to be a strong and successful nation, one greater than the sum of its parts. 2027 will be a special year of commemoration, when we will celebrate the 160th anniversary of the nation we call home. It is a moment not only to celebrate how far we have come, but also to shape the country we want to become. As Canada celebrates its 160th anniversary, Canada’s new government is investing in the stories, places, and experiences that connect Canadians to their country and to one another. Our culture lives in the books we read to our children, the music we know by heart, and the rinks, libraries, and festivals where neighbours come together… As more of what we watch, read, and listen to is coming from beyond our borders, Canadians deserve to see themselves in their own stories. Our cultural sovereignty depends on Canadian artists and creators being able to tell those stories, express our perspectives, and ensure Canadians continue to see themselves reflected in the culture they experience.

Rather refreshing to read that after a decade of federal governance devoted to picking at and apologizing for Canada’s failures, as though failures were all we’d left on the historical and cultural records.

I was eager to see exactly how this government was “investing in the cultural institutions and the creatives that tell our stories.”

Inevitably, there was porkbarreling: politicians can’t resist using an occasion like Canada’s upcoming 160th to sprinkle money into their ridings, upgrading arenas, supporting festivals, etc. There were other initiatives that seem totally out of place in a section on cultural reinvention: “investing in our oceans” and “conserving our oceans.” In my exultant mood, I brushed these quibbles aside.

I came upon a genuinely welcome initiative: the Canada Creative Credit, which is proposed tax relief for the first $45,000 of annual income derived from creative works. This could be a boon to Canadian authors, who on average are earning $9,380 annually, although the details on who qualifies won’t be available until spring. There doesn’t appear to have been much thought at all given to the program yet, so I have a suggestion. Given that the whole point of this exercise is to reinforce Canadian sovereignty and that the government’s stated priority is a Canadian-owned cultural sector, let’s have the tax break available to creatives who work with Canadian-owned publishing companies rather than foreign-owned multinationals.

I read on through how the government will again provide free or discounted access to parks and museums next summer, and finally arrived at what I was most looking forward to: the explanation of how all this reinvestment would directly hit the book sector:

Canadian culture finds its fullest expression in the rich stories, experiences, and perspectives we share. Seeing those reflected in the media we watch, listen to, or read strengthens our shared identity and gives Canadian creators more opportunities to flourish. Canadian stories help us understand our country and one another. When a young reader in Iqaluit, Halifax, Saskatoon, or Trois-Rivières picks up a book by a Canadian author, they see their country, and themselves, more clearly. Building on existing support for the audiovisual sector, local journalism, publishing, libraries, and digital access, these investments will help Canadian creators keep making the music, films, shows, books, and news that reflect who we are, and make that content easier to find wherever Canadians watch, listen, and read.

I thought, shit, they finally get it. Over the last several decades, the federal government has through its policies and grant programs taken a leading position in the book industry only to see the Canadian-owned part of the book industry—the part it is pledged to grow—shrink from about 27 percent of that industry to about 5 percent. Now they’re going to address the damage and help us turn things around. Great!

Then came the details.

Introducing the Canadian Planner 2027!

We noticed that virtually every planner available in the Canadian market has generic or American holidays and content, so we made a new one that debuted last autumn. This year it’s back with new colours—navy and red—and new content.
This elegant, thoughtfully designed planner is an indispensable companion for staying on top of your busy life and celebrating where you’re from. With two days per page, a clean layout, and generous space for appointments, notes, and to-dos, it keeps you focused and productive. Sprinkled throughout are:
* key national observances
* public holidays and civic observances
* famous birthdays and historical notes
* major arts, culture, and sports events
* natural and astronomical occasions, weather highs and lows, and even bird migration and fruiting seasons.
Beautifully packaged in cloth binding with gold/silver foil accents and a matching ribbon marker, the Canadian Planner is the perfect way to stay organized and connected to all things Canadian.
It’s 6x9 inches, 224 pages, comes in navy and red, and sells for just $19.95. Order one here!

1) $32.2 million over four years, starting in 2027-28, and $9.5 million in future years, to make it easier for Canadians to discover and borrow books by Canadian authors by establishing a free national digital collection. Launching with at least 200 titles in 2027-28, the collection will grow to more than 2,000 titles, with capacity for up to 2 million loans annually by 2031-32. Funding will support licensing, an online lending platform, promotion, and outreach to help Canadian stories reach more readers.

2) Budget 2026 also proposes to provide $20 million over four years, starting in 2027-28, and $5 million ongoing to renew the Canada Book Fund so Canadian publishers can bring more Canadian-authored books to readers at home and abroad and give authors, illustrators, translators, and other creators more opportunities to build lasting careers.

I looked at that first program for a long time, trying to figure out what I thought of it and who would benefit. I searched online and couldn’t find anyone in the publishing world who was asking for it. I still can’t make sense of it. Canada already has one of the best funded and best used library systems in the world. How would building a parallel library system—finding another way to lend more Canadian books for free—benefit struggling writers and publishers? It’s true our public libraries are woefully light on Canadian content. Last I looked, Canadian-authored books were only about 10 percent of public library collections and about 10 percent of circulation. But that’s a choice by the libraries. They could spend two or three times as much on Canadian-authored books if they wanted to. Why not just give them this new money on condition that they buy and shelve a minimum of 25 percent Canadian content?

I looked at the second program and thought, great, $20 million for the Canada Book Fund. Of all the granting programs in Canada, the Canada Book Fund is the best. It provides $40 million (2026-27) in support for publishers based on their level of sales. It doesn’t try to influence what you publish; it rewards companies for releasing books that find audiences; and it is staffed with genuinely helpful people. I was glad to see it singled out for reinvestment.

Then I thought about the $20 million. It’s over four years. Five million a year sounds like a healthy increase. But the five million replaces a top-up of $3.3 million a year which is set to expire. So it’s only about $1.7 million in new money, or an increase of 4 percent, which is likely to be substantially less than the increase in the cost of living over four years.

Totes back in stock on time for the holidays!

A new batch of the Sutherland House tote bag has arrived. It’s made of fine heavy canvas, and it’s got a bottle holder inside. Dimensions are 39 x 36 x 12 cm. It sells to SHuSH readers for $15. To order, email Timo@sutherlandhousebooks.com.

That’s it. That’s what your government is giving—below a cost-of-living increase—to support the “fullest expression” of Canadian culture. That means the value of the Canada Book Fund is set to erode for the foreseeable future, just as it has eroded since the base funding was set in 2001. The Canada Book Fund has not had an increase in funding apart from these temporary top-ups and near-cost-of-living increases in a quarter century, leaving it worth about 40 percent less than it was 2001. The government has the temerity to call this “renewal.”

It’s not like the government is short of cash for culture. The budget gave $300 million to the CBC, bringing its parliamentary appropriation to over $1.5 billion. Museums and other institutions got $783 million. The National Gallery got $60 million. It gave $134 million to local journalism. It’s spending half a billion on a journalism labour tax credit. It’s allocating $393 million to promote the Francophonie Summit. It recently announced $600 million in immediate support for Canada’s audio and audiovisual sectors. There were many millions more spent on culture. But the books sector, the foundational expression of Canadian culture, couldn’t get a cost-of-living increase after seeing the value of its funding dwindle year after year for a quarter century.

I like this government. I think it’s sincere in its commitment to Canadian culture. I’m encouraged that it recognizes that Canada needs a cultural reset and that culture has something to contribute in this strange geopolitical moment. But I think this government is absolutely clueless about what’s happening on the ground in the book sector. It has no idea of the enormous gap between its commitments and its rhetoric. That’s partly on the government, because it asked for a leading role in our industry and needs to do a better job, but it’s also on the industry for failing to make itself heard in Ottawa.

The government gets away with this shit, year after year, because nobody in the publishing world—publishers, authors, booksellers—is really raising a voice in Ottawa. We’re all too polite.

That is as far as I got in the budget document.

Sutherland Quarterly is pleased to announce its latest edition, Rob Csernyik’s A Losing Hand: The Human Costs of Canada’s Gambling Epidemic:

Recent decades have seen an explosion of government-approved casinos, sports betting, and online gaming opportunities. The politicians and officials behind them meticulously track the revenue they receive, but turn a blind eye to its often devastating human costs.

Through gripping personal stories and a comprehensive investigation, journalist Rob Csernyik shows how public officials evade accountability for lives shattered by gambling addiction―for instance, rarely recording gambling as a risk factor in suicide. From the rise of video lottery terminals to the explosion of smartphone betting, A Losing Hand shows how modern gambling has become more accessible, more immersive, and more dangerous than ever. The result is a crisis hiding in plain sight, one that policymakers have ignored for decades.

A powerful work of investigative journalism, this book forces a reckoning with the true cost of gambling in Canada.
