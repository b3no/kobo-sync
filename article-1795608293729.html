Against the war on WAR

There’s a telling scene in the movie Moneyball, where Billy Beane, the general manager of the Oakland Athletics, is attempting to build a winning baseball team on a shoestring budget by relying on statistics rather than conventional scouting wisdom.

Exasperated at inefficient player evaluations, where scouts judged potential additions on their jawlines, looks, swing style, and even girlfriends, Beane excoriates his team for their outdated methods.

“You guys are sitting around talking the same old ‘good body’ nonsense, like we’re selling jeans.”

Much of our elections discourse feels similarly stuck in the pre-Moneyball era.

After all, how do you judge whether a candidate is good at winning voters? The answer, for many, is “vibes.” You’d look at how the candidate looks, or how they speak, or how they command a room and the sizes of the crowds at their rallies. The problem, however, is that our intuitions about what makes a candidate appealing are not necessarily good guides to what actually wins votes.

Beane’s approach of gaining an advantage over “vibes” in baseball lay in statistics, and his teams were so successful that front offices across the league began copying them with time. Today, an analytical metric like Wins-Above-Replacement (WAR), which is an estimation of how many extra wins a player nets his team relative to a replacement-level player, is common parlance all across baseball.

And though politics is not sports, I do think there’s still value in using statistics to quantify things that people tend to judge by vibes.

Over the years, we at Split Ticket developed an electoral version of Wins-Above-Replacement to answer a similar question: In any given contested race, did the Democrat or Republican win more votes than expected?

A better-than-expected WAR score can mean that you’re a very good candidate or that you have a very weak opponent. WAR measures the performance of that matchup; it cannot fully separate the two candidates’ contributions.

Since the release of Split Ticket’s 2026 Wins-Above-Replacement (WAR) model, I’ve gotten a ton of attention and questions from people all over the political spectrum. Many of these queries have improved our historical modeling and yielded fruitful discussions about future threads of research.

But other critics have questioned whether WAR is useful at all. Any metric with real-world stakes should be rigorously evaluated, and WAR is no different. So I want to walk through some of the most prominent criticisms of the model — in particular those outlined by the progressive elections analyst G. Elliott Morris. I’m going to show you that these critiques miss the mark: WAR is predictive, it’s informative, and it’s got a persistence that’s on par with other highly regarded sports metrics despite the small sample size inherent in elections.

And that means that, just as those sports metrics help teams win championships, so too can WAR help candidates win elections. It would be a mistake to claim that WAR is a perfect model¹, or that it is maximally informative about the world, but we have never claimed that it is. WAR is simply an imperfect but solid and important explanatory metric that fills a void of information.

Is WAR predictive?

In his article, Morris says that WAR scores are “not particularly useful for predicting future elections.” By this he means that they are not predictive of anything after controlling for incumbency.²

Incumbents have advantages over challengers, so if the WAR is just telling us that incumbents do better than challengers, then that would indeed be a pretty weak metric! The problem for Morris’ argument is that it’s not true.

The simplest definition of a strong candidate electorally is one who wins voters who otherwise vote for the other party. Split Ticket’s WAR scores are unquestionably useful in helping predict when candidates overperform in this manner. The cleanest way to measure this is by looking at how much a candidate overperforms others from their party on the same ballot.

To test WAR’s predictive power, we looked at four election cycles: 2020, 2022, 2024, and 2026. Using only information from previous elections, we tried to predict how much better or worse a House candidate would perform than the top of the ticket did in their district. In presidential years, this was the presidential nominee; in midterm years, it was their party’s Senate or gubernatorial nominee.

The chart below shows that even after controlling for incumbency, WAR adds a lot of value in predicting a candidate’s future overperformance against the top of the ticket, reducing overall error by 14.4% across all four cycles. For incumbents, this was even greater, reducing error by 16.1%.

Across all four cycles combined, adding WAR to an incumbency model actually yields a statistically significant improvement in forecasting overperformance, both over an incumbency-only model by itself and over a model that adds prior raw House overperformance to that incumbency-only model.

So it’s clear that this metric has, at least, shown some real value in predicting external overperformance. And it’s a lot better than the alternative of “we can’t quantify anything, because elections are too weird.”

Is WAR too noisy to use?

A strong election result can reflect lasting strengths (like charisma) or circumstances that won’t recur (like a particularly scandal-plagued opponent). The thing we are trying to figure out is how much a candidate’s performance in one election tells us about their performance in the next.

Baseball analysts have the luxury of watching players perform over 162 games a season. Congressional elections happen just once every two years. So it obviously makes sense that baseball WAR would be less noisy than election WAR, since we have far fewer opportunities to distinguish lasting talent from temporary luck.

What may surprise you, though, is how persistent election WAR actually is despite this small sample. Don’t take it from me — take it from one of the most prominent critics of election WAR: G. Elliott Morris. Among the House members Morris tracked, nearly half of the top WAR scores in 2026 came from candidates who had the top WAR scores in 2024. Meanwhile, 41% of the worst WAR scores in this cycle came from candidates who had the worst scores in 2024.

If the rankings were completely randomly assigned, you would expect that one in five members of the original top fifth would remain there. In reality, nearly half did. This is how you know WAR scores are providing substantially more information than a random ranking would.

Morris made his own chart that demonstrates this:

I want to highlight that this is considerably better than random chance, and shows real signal in the metric. If WAR were truly random, with little carryover from one year to the next, then scores for 2026 should have minimal correlation with the scores for 2024. As Morris demonstrates, this is obviously not the case here.

Here’s something funnier: Election WAR has similar consistency from one election to the next as some well-established baseball statistics have from one season to the next. For example, election analyst Lau Sze Yui shows how Split Ticket’s WAR correlations look remarkably similar to those from one of the most widely regarded statistics in baseball: a pitcher’s walk rate (how frequently they give a batter a free trip to first base).

Similarly, Joel Wertheimer points out that even more advanced “expected value” statistics like Fielding-Independent Pitching — a statistic designed to evaluate pitchers independently of the quality of the defense behind them — only have a year-over-year correlation of 0.62. That’s surprisingly close to Split Ticket’s year-to-year correlation of 0.56 between 2014 and 2026.

A correlation of 0 means last cycle’s score tells you nothing about following one while a correlation of 1 means the rankings repeat exactly. At 0.56, candidates who substantially outperform expectations in one election are likely to do so again (even though we shouldn’t expect them to replicate their previous performance exactly).

Given that election WAR is a two-way metric, evaluated as the gap between the Republican and the Democrat rather than the individual strength of either candidate, and considering that one half of the ticket usually changes between the years (an incumbent rarely faces the same person twice in a row), we think it is simply unreasonable to expect much more of a correlation than this. After all, if half the matchup changes, part of the WAR will too!

But given that elections happen far more infrequently than baseball events do, and given the changes that are inherent to any political landscape, it is extremely encouraging that our explanatory metric both delivers some predictive value and has reasonable year-over-year correlation, especially compared to other sports metrics that are based on much larger sample sizes.

Is WAR useful?

WAR is best understood as an approximation of relative candidate strength between two candidates. And though we have shown the utility of this tool in a predictive and analytical context, we are under no illusions that it is determinative or definitive.

To be clear, there are real, acknowledged limitations to this noisy tool. For instance, while it can tell you that the Texas Senate race was far more Democratic than a baseline matchup between the two parties would imply, it doesn’t tell you how much of this was because of James Talarico’s strengths and how much of it was because of Ken Paxton’s weaknesses.

Modeling candidate strength is an exercise in isolating signal from a rapidly shifting world full of noise, and electoral WAR is meant as just one explanatory tool to be leveraged in understanding it. The persistence and predictive power suggest that this is a metric with real signal, and one that provides insights no other public tool has yielded to this day.

After all, WAR is not meant to be blindly trusted and followed. But it does tell you that there is a signal in the noise of elections, and it can tell us who’s likelier to be a good — or bad — candidate.

A candidate who loses a difficult race while repeatedly doing better than expected might be worth recruiting again, like Rebecca Cooke was in Wisconsin. A comfortable winner who consistently falls short of their party’s expected performance may deserve closer scrutiny, as Ann Wagner discovered in Missouri. WAR can help identify those cases for further investigation.

So, is WAR useful?

To answer this, I think it’s useful to phrase this question in a different way: Is electoral strength quantifiable and persistent, like Split Ticket claims it is, and does their WAR metric do a good job of quantifying it?

The answer to both of those questions is yes.
