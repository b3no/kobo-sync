No, AI safety is not a threat to GDP

The economy will survive an AI slowdown

This year, about one-fifth of all U.S. GDP growth has been tied to investment in the AI industry. At the same time, safety concerns are taking center stage: Figures as disparate as Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei, Elon Musk, and Sen. Bernie Sanders agree that we should “pace” or pause frontier AI development. Meanwhile, tech industry boosters — particularly those connected to President Donald Trump, like venture capitalist David Sacks — argue that prioritizing AI safety means forgoing economic growth.

The boosters are wrong. Their worries about undercutting the economy stem from a basic misunderstanding: If we create oversight measures that slow down the pace of new model releases, that does not mean we are slowing down all AI-driven innovation.

The central issue is that the anti-AI-safety crowd appears to assume that all AI innovation comes from the so-called frontier development of the most powerful models at companies like OpenAI and Anthropic. But frontier AI is not all AI.

Policymakers need to be able to distinguish between the lofty technological promises made by frontier AI companies and the potentially very large innovation-driven growth that could happen just through the widespread adoption of current models. If they don’t, they will be overly reluctant to tap the brakes even if frontier AI poses real safety risks — as it already has in multiple instances of autonomous hacking “swarms.” They will, like Senate Majority Leader John Thune, insist upon a “light touch.” They will view safety and growth as a trade-off, when it mostly isn’t.

Yes, hyperscalers like Alphabet and Microsoft need their investments in data centers to pay off. But that revenue could come from people and businesses using any model — new or old, proprietary or even open models, which are free to download and modify but still need to run on chips somewhere. As long as more and more people and businesses are running new queries, then AI can continue to drive meaningful economic growth. And if those queries make those people and businesses more efficient at whatever economic activity they’re doing, that will drive even more growth.

The central requirement is just that there is enough demand for AI in the future to make use of the build-out. That primarily depends on all of society finding more and more uses for the technology. It depends, largely, on diffusion.

AI diffusion is just starting

“Diffusion” is a word that makes sense to economists, but it’s quite hard to get a picture of in your mind. It’s not like Thomas Edison inventing the lightbulb. Instead, it’s a wide variety of people and businesses spending years figuring out how to use a technology in new ways. It may only become clear in retrospect.

In 1900, only 5% of industrial mechanical power was supplied by electricity, rather than by older sources like a steam engine, according to Brian Potter, senior infrastructure fellow at the Institute for Progress. It didn’t become a majority source of power for industry until the 1920s, at which point it enabled other industrial innovations.

In factories before electricity, “work operations [had] to be laid out in linear paths connected to the overhead belts,” according to Christopher Jones’ account in Routes of Power. But electricity was much more flexible. It could be “delivered anywhere in whatever amounts were desired, freeing operators to organize production according to the flow of parts, not the transmission of power.”

Electricity didn’t only change some abstract cost structure of business processes, and it didn’t only supply machines where they didn’t exist before. It literally altered the physical flow of space. A business literally reorganized around the new technology, and then other businesses followed until whole industries changed.

The fantastic efficiency of the modern assembly line was made possible by electricity, yes. But it also only became possible after the entire production process was redesigned around electricity. We might expect something similar with AI: Its efficiency gains can’t be unlocked in a given industry until a period of trial and error leads to entire processes being reconfigured with the new technology in mind.

This is what diffusion is, and it takes time basically because you need many people to try stuff with the technology. Then, you need people to copy those people.

Finally, you need businesses like Anthropic or OpenAI (and all of the smaller labs, software businesses, inference providers, and others in the AI ecosystem) to design another round of software products atop the models that businesses can then use for these new processes.

Diffusion is slow because it’s not one person inventing something, like Edison. It’s millions of people making small tweaks, responding to each other, and nudging progress forward. It’s a coordination exercise. When you add it all up, it equals demand for a technology. And that’s the thing that will justify the massive investment in data centers that’s currently underway. It doesn’t necessarily depend on the ability of Anthropic and OpenAI to build models with godlike powers.

You can see AI diffusion happening right now

To understand the mechanics of diffusion, it helps to differentiate AI models — things like Anthropic’s Fable 5 and OpenAI’s GPT-6 Astra — from the software products you use to access them. The model might tell the surrounding software to search the internet or edit a spreadsheet, and when the webpage comes back, or the cells are read, the model ingests it, comes up with an answer, and tells the software what to do next.

The software product is something like “Claude for Small Business” or “Claude for Legal.” It is the user interface that helps people use the AI in the most effective ways. It structures and suggests use cases to nontechnical users, which is what helps people across society find new uses. A small business software product, for instance, would be well-designed for linking expense sheets to accounting software, while a legal AI would be good at searching cases based on relevant ideas, scraping the PDFs, and giving you a list of citations.

The same model — say, Claude Opus 5.5 — is underneath Claude for Legal, Claude for Small Business, and the Claude you talk to on claude.ai. It just has different surrounding tools and instructions and buttons for you to press.

New model releases get headlines, but when you look at the business strategies of OpenAI and Anthropic, you can see that promoting software products is actually a major opportunity for growth right now. When those companies look out over the economy, they seem to think there is a lot of room for businesses to do more adoption — they agree, in effect, that diffusion is just starting.

How do I know? You can see it in who they hire and what they release.

Start with the roles that each lab has been hiring for. In March, the nonprofit research group Epoch AI did an analysis of job postings at OpenAI and Anthropic. It found big spikes in “go-to-market” roles, or the positions in a tech company responsible for integrating sales, technical, and marketing teams to get new products out to customers. Numbers rose from 17% of all open roles at Anthropic to 31%; they rose from 18% to 28% at OpenAI. This was the first sign that new products to help people use AI would be central to their overall business plans. This preceded a spate of releases, like Claude for Legal, to help people deploy the technology.

We are still very clearly on the learning curve of how to integrate this technology into useful processes. The IT company Gartner has projected that 40% of “agentic AI” projects in businesses will be scrapped by the end of 2027.

Right now, a high share of workers and firms use AI without much productivity to show for it. A nationally representative survey of U.S. workers in May found that nearly 63% of workers and 45% of firms use generative AI. But only 6% of work hours involve AI use on average, and that use only creates the equivalent time savings of 2% of work hours. Businesses are still finding the productive uses, while AI companies are still altering the products to make it easier.

But Anthropic, OpenAI, and the other major labs are not the only players in the game. Wide diffusion will also happen as businesses adopt open models to build on top of their own software — models that are free to download and customize for a specific use case.

Take the company Thomson Reuters, which has massive libraries of legal, news, tax, and accounting information for professionals, with records that go back 175 years. In August, it announced that it had spent two years using all of this material to customize its own model to the specific kinds of tasks that its customers need.

“The frontier is measured on general capability, but our customers are judged on something narrower,” the company wrote in an explainer of the technology, “whether a citation holds up, whether an answer is complete, whether the reasoning survives a partner’s review.”

In other words, while Anthropic is advancing the overall capability frontier and also pushing Claude for Legal on the side, companies inside that industry, like Thomson Reuters, are using their own corpora of data to solve specific problems in their own domain.

This is diffusion in action, and you can expect it to keep happening behind the scenes, creating demand for AI, while we read headlines about all the new things frontier models can do.

What about the economy in 2027?

Of course, policymakers may agree that long-term growth is still possible but are simply worried about a short-term crash. It’s possible investors funding all this infrastructure will get cold feet when they hear about a pause. Indeed, tech stocks fell the Monday after Amodei called for pacing AI.

In the short run, infrastructure spending is showing up in current GDP. That means GDP could keep growing in two ways if a pause or pacing plan goes into effect in the next year:

  1. If the build-out slows because investors get spooked, productive capacity could move elsewhere in the economy.

  2. If investors maintain confidence in the build-out and keep spending, despite the pacing of frontier development.

The Argument contributor Matt Yglesias recently pointed out why No. 1 would be the case. Even if the data center build-out stalls, redirected investment can still keep the economy afloat: “What’s happening is that right now, A.I.-related activities are the highest-reward use of financial capital, so tons of investment funds are flowing into them,” he wrote.

But that same capital could simply flow to the next-highest-reward activity — maybe it’s building a house or investing in a wind farm — leading to similar overall expenditures. Or, if investors are less willing to borrow and the economy does start to slow, interest rates could go lower, leading to productive investment in other areas.

The main reason to think this would happen is that the economy isn’t facing weak demand, like after the Great Recession; it’s running hot, and there’s room for interest rates to drop.

But it’s also important to recognize that this scenario is compatible with No. 2 also happening — maybe a slowdown means investors pull the marginal dollar from the AI build-out, but the vast majority of what makes these investments pay off later is long-run demand, which could stay high even if businesses “only” get to build on top of Astra or Fable or their current open-model competitors.

Even if the investors don’t agree, then productive capacity will move elsewhere in the short run until they are proven wrong over time — when diffusion actually does start to pay off, and economic growth is built atop the businesses that figured things out.

The political economy of slowing down

When I spoke to Nate Soares, the co-author of If Anyone Builds It, Everyone Dies, I asked him what level of growth was consistent with his plans to pause new AI development.

“Everyone who’s saying ‘what about the growth?’ has not actually grappled with the real situation,” Soares told me, emphasizing the need to argue for a slowdown in principle rather than transactionally. “The way we see lawmakers shift on whether they support bills like this and whether we open the sort of international coordination channels is more about whether they realize the actual problem in time.”

Advocates like Soares tend to think that lawmakers need to buy the case for a slowdown in principle in order to come around. These advocates have a tough time reckoning with the fact that policymakers have interests that compete with what advocates consider existential risks to humanity. Once you persuade them of those risks on the merits, you still need to show them why taking action won’t swamp their other priorities (like whether or not their constituents have construction jobs next quarter).

In other words, pause advocates need to deal with the political economy of pausing. They need to make this case that we are not sacrificing growth for safety.

An apt comparison is climate policy: Early attempts to legislate framed reducing carbon emissions as a cost we should be willing to bear, often in the form of taxes, in order to save the planet. Even when Obama had 59 Democratic seats in the Senate and Republicans participating in negotiations, the deal fell apart.

A decade later, the Biden administration turned to clean energy subsidies. This was a workable policy and a more politically palatable one, too. It reframed combating climate change from something costly into a win-win policy.

The same thing is likely to be true with regulating AI: If we associate all safety policies with massive costs, like forgone economic growth, it will make a legislative coalition much harder to put together. But if we understand that slower progress at the frontier is consistent with major economic growth, then we may have a chance to do the right thing before the next swarm does its damage.
