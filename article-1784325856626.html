Vibes are winning, whether you like it or not

Politics is about a different kind of platform, and no one is around to preserve the truth.

Halina Bennet
Jul 17

Less than 30 years ago, if you wanted to learn how to fillet a fish, you needed a cookbook, a class, or a family member or friend to teach you. Today, you just need a phone. The same is true of squats, sourdough, watercolor painting, wallpapering, or even language skills. As smartphones and social media became ubiquitous, millions of people stopped looking to institutions to teach them skills and began relying on creators instead. For many subjects, this shift has mostly been good. And when creator advice is bad, the consequences — a disappointing dinner or a backache from improper exercise — are immediate and usually easy to correct. And fortunately, the stakes are generally low.

Elections have made the same shift to social media as everything else, but they don’t provide that same feedback loop — and the stakes are significantly higher. A campaign’s social performance has become a leading predictor of political success and, increasingly, a proxy for political viability. The 2024 U.S. presidential race is the clearest case, dubbed by strategists across the spectrum the first “influencer election.” One campaign built relationships with podcasters and streamers — Joe Rogan, Theo Von, Adin Ross — while the other leaned more heavily on short clips, trendy videos, and creator collaborations. Both treated partnerships with online personalities, rather than press conferences or position papers, as the center of their campaign strategy, devoting resources to working with dozens of influencers who had the capacity to reach millions of Americans online. In 2026, that approach is ubiquitous, with candidates in both parties rebuilding their communications operations around creators instead of the press. Mayor Zohran Mamdani last year proved the efficacy of social media when he went from having no name recognition to ousting the establishment candidate and becoming one of the most famous politicians in America. Now, everyone wants to follow his blueprint.

Time and again, candidates who operate with the perhaps naïve view that their policy positions and television ads will communicate their qualifications to voters are bested by those with social media momentum, whether or not they have a coherent policy platform. And as it happens, the anti-establishment wave washing across the country is helped by the fact that the more politically established candidates are the ones who haven’t yet wised up to the reality of how internet engagement works. In other words, it’s hard to teach an old dog — not necessarily in terms of candidate age so much as familiarity with older modes of campaigning — new tricks.

The metric that replaced the editor

For most of the last century, a claim passed through an editor, a wire service, or a producer before it reached the public. That layer was slow and occasionally self-interested, but its fundamental purpose was to ensure accuracy before virality. The perception was that publishing incorrect information would do serious damage to an institution’s reputation, or perhaps just that it would be morally wrong.

Social media reversed that order. People who used to get their opinions — however imperfectly — from newspapers, evening news broadcasts, and conversations with their communities now get them from influencers in the form of infographics and vertical videos designed to capture attention and sow outrage.

Algorithmic distribution boosts the content with the best engagement metrics, and a campaign fluent in that fact can win the metric without ever having to worry too much about accuracy. Loud, simple, emotional messaging beats accurate, complicated messaging in a system that cannot tell the two apart. Campaigns have given in, and voters don’t expect or value nuance or tedious realities.

Most of the time, the content reaches millions of people before anyone checks its accuracy, and by the time someone corrects the record, the truth is buried under a cacophony of reposts and replies. The best-case scenario is that a candidate has both kinds of platform — political and social — but if you are going to choose one, it had better be the latter.

Politics as a personal brand

Social media is, at the end of the day, a stage. People use it to create and maintain a version of themselves for an audience, and politics has been folded in as just another piece of a well-rounded profile — functioning as an aesthetic choice as much as a conviction. Politics is, in many respects, just another thing to signal alongside other signals for the sake of signaling.

The audience enforces that mechanism, as political speech online runs increasingly on public shame. The threat of getting screenshotted, unfollowed, or dragged for holding unpopular views (or for staying silent) pressures people to post and to make sure it’s the correct post for whichever crowd is watching — regardless of how firm the underlying belief actually is. In my own experience on Instagram, this pressure to post began in 2020 with Black Lives Matter, and echoes of it continue through infographics and reposts about Gaza, ICE, public health, or whatever is gaining momentum on the algorithm that week. Staying quiet is either complicity or evasiveness, so it is safer to post fast and in line with your followers.

Politics, now a form of self-expression, is marketable, and it is slotted in with makeup, clothing, and beverage companies as promotable content. Influencers who have the trust of their audience now get paid directly or through collaborations to promote candidates alongside their normal content. Followers absorb a political endorsement by someone they already trust to influence their taste in every other commodifiable way, and the best thing a person can be is on trend — both with their vibe and their politics.

While the algorithm removes the check on accuracy, the performance layer removes the connection to belief in the first place. A claim does not need to be true to win the feed, and an influencer does not need to hold a belief to promote it. Both push politics toward optimizing for how it looks moving through a timeline rather than how it works when enacted.

The standard defense for all this is that bad information still inevitably gets corrected through a fact-check, and candidates who perform online but underdeliver in office eventually lose support. This has always been true for politicians who fail on their campaign promises.

But the correction in this case arrives after the vote, which is backward from how the previous check systems used to stop the damage from happening in the first place. Unlike a bad recipe, a bad political belief has consequences that land on everyone living under the policy it helped bring about, whether or not they ever saw the post that caused it. And the role of journalism — the gatekeeper — is not just to correct falsehoods. It is to make policy differences between candidates clear and to hold them accountable for their behavior, their spending, and their consistency. Now, as journalism is threatened by the same collapse-by-algorithm as politics, newspapers, too, reward not quality content, but the stories that produce clicks.

The net is gone and the facts are in free fall

Local newsrooms, which once acted as the institutional layer that held the mandate to ensure accuracy and fairness, are rapidly disappearing. Since 2005, nearly 3,500 U.S. newspapers have closed, including 136 in 2025, more than two closures a week. Roughly 50 million Americans now live in a news desert or in a county with only one remaining local news source. Into that vacuum have stepped younger, less credentialed information providers who are structurally rewarded for engagement rather than accuracy. Twenty-one percent of U.S. adults and 38 percent of adults under 30 now regularly get their news from “news influencers,” 77 percent of whom have no affiliation with a news organization at all.

One partial attempt at mitigating the factual free-for-all is crowdsourced verification such as X’s Community Notes. In one study, medical evaluators rated 97.5 percent of a sample of vaccine-related notes entirely accurate. A separate large-scale study found that, once displayed, notes were effective at cutting the spread of flagged posts roughly in half. But research also found the median note took more than 18 hours to appear, while false claims on the same platform typically reached half their eventual audience faster than that.

Voters are not inherently gullible, and social media is not uniquely corrupting. The bigger issue here is timing. The mechanisms built to replace the gatekeepers are activated only after the moment arrives that they were supposed to prevent. The gatekeepers of yesterday were at times slow and imperfect, but they served the public by helping people make informed political decisions. By the time the new ones arrive, they have much more difficulty making a difference.

It is embarrassing to serve a bad meal that was cooked up following an inaccurate TikTok recipe, but it is much worse to build a political system based on algorithmic optimization and vibes instead of policy. The fastest, most reactionary, and most clickable claims will keep drowning out what’s accurate. Until something closes the gap between timing and truth, campaigns will keep optimizing for the feed instead of the fact, because that’s where the most votes are won.
