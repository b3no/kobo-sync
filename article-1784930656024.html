Thank you, Christopher Nolan

— Homer, probably

Halina Bennet
Jul 24
∙
Paid
 
READ IN APP
 

I read “The Odyssey” as a sophomore in high school — read is probably a strong word for what was realistically a skim. Regardless, once Odysseus returned home, I closed my Fagles and never looked back. My young mind’s attention span and underbaked comprehension skills were no match for Homer, so I spent the following decade caring very little about the war, the horse, the voyage, or the suitors.

None of that came up during my four years in college, despite my English major. Nobody wants to reread the poem they slogged through as a teenager. And, besides, everyone knows about the cyclops, the sirens, and the Trojan horse — punchlines that few, in my experience, actually understand. My lack of interest in Homer meant that I skipped the modern retellings and myth-inspired books meant for teenagers like “Percy Jackson” and “The Song of Achilles.” I wrote off his epic and its universe completely.

But over the weekend, I saw Christopher Nolan’s adaptation out of FOMO since the whole world was talking about it. Now, I feel like I wasted a decade that could have been filled with an interest in the story, had I had an easier time accessing it.

Maybe it has something to do with being 10 years older than when I was assigned it in high school, but the movie made me far more curious about the story and far more interested in reading Homer than I ever was before. I want to read “The Odyssey” now not because it is considered by many to be a foundational Western text, but because I was so taken with the adaptation that I’d like to live in the world for a little while longer — to know more about Penelope and her suitors, Circe’s island, and how other translators handle the parts that Nolan compressed.

I think this value supersedes any discourse about Nolan’s casting, his handling of female characters, or the fact that he skipped “Nobody” — I’ll read about that in Fagles or Wilson. The real triumph of the adaptation is its ability to bring audiences in, to give them access to the stories that seem otherwise out of reach.

Criticisms of “The Odyssey” run in a few different directions. Nolan cast Lupita Nyong’o as Helen of Troy and Clytemnestra, Elliot Page as the warrior Sinon (the bravest man Odysseus had ever met), and put Travis Scott in as a bard. He wrote much of the dialogue in plain modern English (including Telemachus calling his father “Dad”), leaning on Emily Wilson’s well-regarded but arguably “woke” 2017 translation instead of a more traditional one — a translation that happens to be both accessible and rigorous, which undercuts the idea that populist and scholarly goals are opposed. He also used a replica Viking longship, displaced several centuries from the appropriate time.

Critics on the right have called the casting a political statement and accused Nolan of diverging too much from the source material. Other critics have focused on the director’s neglect of female characters or whether he let important parts of the text slip through the cracks. (Milan Singh keeps telling me that it should’ve been an hour longer to accommodate missing details.) Nolan’s response to all this has basically been that he made the film he believed was the strongest, most sincere version of the story he could.

I don’t care at all whether Helen is a blonde, perhaps because I have spent a lot of time studying live theatre where actors very often come in genders, races, shapes, and sizes that don’t always match what the playwright might’ve envisioned. All of Shakespeare’s women were originally played by men, after all. But both sides of the argument share an assumption that the point of adapting a 2,700-year-old poem is to protect it, that getting the adaptation wrong is a kind of vandalism of the original.

To that I say: If you care so much about the text, you should celebrate an adaptation that makes people like me interested in it.

If I were teaching middle or high school English, I wouldn’t get the students into Shakespeare by mainlining “Othello.” I’d start the unit with Kenneth Branagh’s 1993 “Much Ado About Nothing” or even whet their appetites with “10 Things I Hate About You.” If I were teaching Jane Austen, you’d better believe the introduction would include the 2005 “Pride & Prejudice” film. I’d let the teenagers fall in love with the scaffolding of the story before asking them to wade through unfamiliar, heightened text to get it. The adaptation is not by any means a substitute for the original, but it is a perhaps even more valuable thing because it is an entry point for far more people.

The fidelity debate assumes that the value of a classic is self-evident and fixed and that the job of an adaptation is to not damage it. But canonical status can only exist if it is earned by every generation of curious readers, and in a generation less interested in reading than any before it, “The Odyssey” truthers owe a debt of gratitude to Nolan.

Accessibility, then, isn’t the debased version of the real thing. Rather, it is the mechanism that gives the real thing a fighting chance at survival, and a blockbuster that takes some creative liberties but that people actually see is the best thing that’s happened to Homer’s readership in recent memory.

The loudest critics of the movie are furious that Nolan built it around a modern, widely praised translation and then cast a diverse set of actors. They take it as evidence that wokeism is rearing its ugly head to threaten tradition. Fine. But the result is that people who don’t share those critics’ views and maybe just really like Zendaya are going to the cinema and gaining an appreciation for the epic poem that conservatives believe is so important. That seems to me like a win for the right. And on the other side, if you, like many classicists, think the Wilson translation is good, you should be thrilled that a bunch of people who are mad about “wokeness” are spending significant energy driving traffic to it.

The movie could have served as a gateway to the story without any controversy; it certainly did for me. But the backlash is happening, and its effect is to make the poem more famous, not less. With Nolan’s adaptation, as with many that are done well, everybody wins — especially Homer. And if you are just upset that Nolan had to trim some parts of the poem for the sake of his film, then stick to the text (and learn ancient Greek while you’re at it).
