One policy that unites liberals and conservatives

Greater welfare spending

Lakshya Jain
Sep 18
∙
Paid
 
READ IN APP
 

Across three surveys conducted between February and April 2026, The Argument asked Americans to place themselves on five-point scales measuring social and economic ideology. On average, respondents described themselves as moderate on social issues and slightly conservative on economics.

Their answers to specific policy questions complicate the picture, however.

Large majorities favor more government spending on Social Security, healthcare, and education, with substantial support even among self-described economic conservatives. On social issues, the pattern is more mixed. The issue polling suggests that a successful electoral strategy could involve increasing the salience of popular government spending programs but treading with more caution on polarized fights over ideology and culture.

Americans say they’re not liberal, but they want many liberal policies

Our respondents’ self-description doesn’t neatly mesh with the policies that voters actually claim to want. For instance, Americans overwhelmingly believe that the federal government should be spending more on Social Security rather than making cuts to the program. That even includes a whopping 56% of economic conservatives.

Americans want the government to spend more on social security
When it comes to the following programs, do you think that the government is spending too much, too little, or about the right amount? - Social Security

That isn’t the only issue where a large group of self-identified conservative voters wants to spend more money. When it comes to healthcare, just about every ideological group thinks the government spends too little, including a plurality (41%) of economic conservatives.

Americans want the government to spend more on healthcare
When it comes to the following programs, do you think that the government is spending too much, too little, or about the right amount? - Healthcare

And it’s a similar (if somewhat less pronounced) story for education, where 62% of voters want to see the country spend more, including 37% of economic conservatives. Only 21% of those conservatives say the government spends too much.

Americans want the government to spend more on education
When it comes to the following programs, do you think that the government is spending too much, too little, or about the right amount? - Education

It’s not that everyone just wants the government to spend money indiscriminately on progressive causes. For instance, when it comes to welfare benefits for ex-felons, 47% of respondents think that ex-felons should be subjected to some period of disqualification, compared with just 36% who feel that ex-felons should never be disqualified.

Americans want limits on welfare for ex-felons
How do Americans feel about welfare eligibility for ex-felons after they have served their prison sentence?

On the whole, our poll certainly doesn’t suggest that the public is economically conservative, at least in the way that the Republican Party has traditionally defined the term. If anything, the results point to a popular case for the kind of economic liberalism that the Democrats have coalesced around, with everyone from Sen. Elizabeth Warren to Sen. Chuck Schumer in broad support.

It could also help explain why Mitt Romney got torched in 2012: The constituency that thinks we spend too much on healthcare is roughly the size of the constituency that thinks America could use a king. Indeed, dropping the Republican stances on health care and Social Security (rhetorically, at least) was a key part of why Donald Trump surged in the Midwest in 2016.¹

An agenda promoting popular welfare spending, centered around heavy government investment in areas such as healthcare and education, could serve the political left very well. In fact, this is perhaps the single best poll finding I have seen for the progressive movement in over a year of surveys we’ve conducted at The Argument.

Americans are pretty heterodox on social issues

The average voter identifies as a social moderate, but what that means to them is not entirely clear. Some traditionally socially liberal stances, such as support for marriage equality, are now accepted by large swaths of social conservatives, while others, such as allowing transgender athletes to compete as the gender with which they identify, are opposed by many who consider themselves socially moderate or even liberal.

When it comes to marriage equality, even 41% of social conservatives align with the socially liberal view.

Americans support marriage equality
For each of the following statements, please indicate whether you agree or disagree. - Same-sex marriages should receive the same legal rights and protections as traditional marriages.

But it’s a very different story on trans issues, where voters cluster much more decisively against the progressive view. A supermajority of socially moderate voters, including 31% of self-identified social liberals, support requiring trans children to participate in sports leagues corresponding to their biological sex.

Read that again: Nearly one-third of people who self-identify as socially liberal don’t agree with the progressive movement’s stance on trans sports. For comparison, just 5% of social liberals don’t support marriage equality.

Americans are much more conservative on trans sports
For each of the following policies, please indicate whether you support or oppose national laws that enact them. - Require transgender athletes in K-12 to compete with the gender matching their biological sex.

The splits remain pretty unfriendly toward progressives on almost every other tested topic related to trans individuals, ranging from bathroom bills to puberty blockers for minors. For instance, majorities of socially moderate and conservative voters favored laws requiring trans people to use the restroom corresponding to their biological sex.

Americans are much more conservative on public restroom usage for trans people
For each of the following policies, please indicate whether you support or oppose national laws that enact them. - Require transgender people to use public restrooms corresponding with their biological sex, rather than the one corresponding with the gender they identify as.

Opposition to trans-inclusive policies isn’t always a narrow disagreement about fairness in sports; for many voters, it accompanies a moral objection to gender transition itself. A plurality of voters said it was morally wrong for someone to change their gender, including a plurality of self-described social moderates.

How Americans view trans identity with respect to morality
For each of the following statements, please indicate whether you agree or disagree. - Saying you are a gender that is different than the one you were born as is morally wrong.

Based on their revealed preferences on specific policies, it’s easy to argue that Americans are much more economically liberal than they claim. But I can’t do that for social liberalism.

So how do voters with contrasting self-reported ideologies actually vote? Perhaps unsurprisingly, it’s pretty similar across both the social and economic axes: In both cases, Democrats already win 95% of self-identified liberals. Democrats are also more reliant on winning conservatives than Republicans are on winning liberals — this, of course, is a necessity to stay competitive, because conservatives outnumber liberals in America.

But in 2024, Kamala Harris actually lost socially moderate voters to Trump, whereas in 2026, Democrats are winning them by double digits. In fact, the largest Democratic gains come from socially and economically moderate voters, who appear to be outright rebelling against Trump.

Where are Democrats gaining the most between 2024 and 2026?
Comparing Kamala Harris' vote share to Democratic vote share in 2026 shows Democrats gained the most with moderates

It might be tempting to look at that finding and conclude that Americans really want a stridently moderate Democratic Party that will avoid rocking the boat on either social or economic issues. While I think that the revealed preferences of voters certainly provide evidence for moderating on social issues, I’m less convinced that they give Democrats any reason to moderate on economic ones. In our polling, Republicans won 32% of voters who said we spend too little on healthcare and 30% of those who said we spend too little on education.

How do voters who want more economic spending plan to vote?
How do voters who claim to want more economic spending plan to vote?

Those are strikingly high numbers that Republicans continue to pull. If Democrats can elevate the importance of spending money at home while decreasing the salience of social and cultural issues, there is no reason to believe that this must continue to hold.

Trump was lifted to office on the “America First” message that gave swing voters critical reason to believe he was different on policy from other Republicans. Ten years after his first election, voters view his tenure in office as a failed presidency, and this gives Democrats a rare opportunity to recapture the high ground on economic populism.

Whether they can take it, of course, is another question altogether. But it’s telling that Democrats have already seen a few of these campaigns emerge in the laboratory of 2026. It’s what drove Graham Platner’s insurgent movement (before his scandals destroyed his candidacy). And it’s part of the reason Sen. Jon Ossoff is getting so much buzz for the 2028 nomination in Democratic circles.

It’s far too early to say that any of this will work out for Democrats in the way that polls suggest it could. But the polling shows that there is a clear path forward for them.
