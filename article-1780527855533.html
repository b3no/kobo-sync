The housing regulator is now also the spymaster

Plus a $6.8 billion bet on homebuilding, $70,000 in Chicago grants, and what sex toy data tells us about the affordability crisis

Jun 3

President Donald Trump announced on Tuesday that he was appointing Federal Housing Finance Agency (F.H.F.A.) Director Bill Pulte to be acting director of national intelligence following Tulsi Gabbard’s resignation. Pulte is set to retain his existing roles overseeing the F.H.F.A. and as chairman of Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac.

Pulte, who has no known prior intelligence experience (but does have a history of abusing his power to target Trump’s political enemies), will now oversee the entire U.S. intelligence community, which includes the C.I.A. and N.S.A.

The appointment drew bipartisan criticism. Senator Elizabeth Warren said Pulte had spent his time at F.H.F.A. “abusing his authority to attack President Trump’s perceived political enemies,” prompting a congressional watchdog investigation. Senate Majority Leader John Thune also raised doubts, saying, “We don’t need a weaponized D.N.I., we need professionals there.” What Pulte’s new post means practically for housing policy is unclear. While he is expected to maintain his F.H.F.A. post, his bandwidth and attention will now be split across two of the most sensitive jobs in government.

HUD’s focus on homelessness

On a more conventional note, two major Department of Housing and Urban Development actions on homelessness bookended the weekend. On Friday, May 29, HUD released its 2025 Annual Homelessness Assessment Report, finding 745,652 people experiencing homelessness on a single night in January 2025. The number marks a 3 percent decrease from 2024, but it is still a 27 percent increase since 2013.

Secretary Scott Turner framed the findings as an indictment of Housing First policies, attributing the decrease to reductions in “Sanctuary Cities.”

Then on Monday, HUD released its Continuum of Care Notice of Funding Opportunity for the fiscal year 2026. HUD announced a $1.3 billion investment in new projects, focusing most of its attention on transitional housing projects and supportive services programs — a clear departure from the permanent housing model that the department had already been moving away from.

Monday’s announcement marks HUD’s second attempt during the second Trump administration at redirecting Continuum of Care funds. Its first effort, which occurred in November, was blocked by the courts after advocates and several states sued. Congress also rejected HUD’s approach in the appropriations law for this fiscal year; the administration has simultaneously proposed eliminating Continuum of Care entirely in its budget for the 2027 fiscal year.

Abundant Albany

The most consequential state-level housing news of the week came from New York. On May 27, Governor Kathy Hochul signed what her office describes as the “most significant reforms” to the State Environmental Quality Review Act since its passage in 1975, as part of the new state budget under the “Let Them Build” banner.

The new legislation is designed to free small- and mid-sized housing projects from lengthy environmental-review processes that often keep them stuck for years. According to the state, housing projects in New York can take up to 56 percent longer to reach construction compared with similar projects in other states because of its review processes, adding roughly $82,000 per unit in costs for projects in New York City. Full environmental reviews will still be required for New York City projects of more than 250 units in most areas, and more than 500 units in medium- and high-density zones. Rural, undeveloped, and agricultural land will remain subject to more stringent environmental review requirements under the State Environmental Quality Review Act.

Berkshire Hathaway’s bet on development

In the biggest private-sector housing news of the week, Berkshire Hathaway announced it will acquire homebuilder Taylor Morrison Home Corp. in a $6.8 billion cash deal. This is the conglomerate’s first major acquisition under C.E.O. Greg Abel, who took over from the legendary Warren Buffett at the start of 2026. The offer of $72.50 per share represents a 24 percent premium to Taylor Morrison’s closing price on May 29. According to the developer, the deal is expected to close in the second half of 2026.

Abel said Berkshire plans “to unify our site-built homebuilding operations into a combined platform enabling us to deliver the dream of homeownership to more Americans,” with the deal reflecting Berkshire’s “long-standing commitment to housing.”

Warren Buffett, now 95, praised Abel for making the deal, which lands against a difficult backdrop for buyers and sellers. Single-family starts fell 9 percent in April, homebuilder sentiment remained subdued in May, and mortgage rates — which are near a one-year high — are expected to weigh on demand through the rest of 2026.

Abel’s move, then, is best understood as a long-cycle bet on housing recovery rather than a call on the near-term market. Taylor Morrison C.E.O. Sheryl Palmer said that “homebuilding runs in five-, seven-, 10-year cycles” and Berkshire “thinks in probably seven-, 10-[year], and longer cycles.”

Purchase assistance in Chicago

Mayor Brandon Johnson and the Chicago Department of Housing on Monday announced a program set to launch June 8 that gives low- and moderate-income Chicagoans up to $70,000 in financial assistance toward purchasing a home. The HomeGrown Purchase Assistance Grant is aimed at families who are priced out of homeownership — the median sale price for a Chicago home in April was $388,918, up more than 5 percent year over year, the Chicago Sun-Times reports.

The $21 million program is funded by Johnson’s $1.25 billion Housing and Economic Development Bond, which was approved by the city council in 2024. The grant is expected to aid between 300 and 400 buyers, according to the Sun-Times. Eligibility requirements include completing HUD-certified homebuyer education and purchasing a one- or two-unit home to occupy for at least five years. Household income cannot exceed 150 percent of the area median income, which is roughly $182,250 for a family of four.

The program is divided into two zones: Zone A covers neighborhoods with significant home price increases (like Lincoln Park and Lake View), offering up to $70,000; Zone B covers low-income census tracts (like Englewood and Roseland), with grants up to $50,000.

Minnesota’s school conversion

In a nice example of adaptive reuse, a long-abandoned school in northern Minnesota has been converted into 32 rental units through a $17 million project, now called the Bookmatch, that addresses a workforce housing shortage in the area, the Minnesota Star Tribune reports. The building is evidence of the success of one underutilized tool in the housing toolkit as rural communities struggle to attract and retain workers.

San Francisco buyers scramble before an even bigger boom

San Francisco’s housing crisis is only deepening, with more than half of all properties now selling for over asking price, according to Compass, a brokerage company. This is the highest ratio in the country by far, with Chicago a distant second at 30 percent and the national rate at just over 16 percent, the San Francisco Standard reports. The city’s median price of $1.6 million across all property types is nearly five times the national figure.

Agents say some buyers — those who refuse to flee the city for cheaper suburban living — are doubling down on purchasing a home, willing to temper their expectations and raise their budgets to close on a home before A.I.-company I.P.O.s push prices even higher.

“People feel like in the next couple of years, with all these upcoming I.P.O.s for these A.I. companies, how can prices not go up?” Michelle Pender of the real estate agency Engel & Volkers told the Standard.

The single-family median in May was above $2 million, with homes regularly selling at a sales-price-to-list-price percentage north of 120 percent, the San Francisco Business Times reports.

Mortgage rates are up — and so are listings

Just a week after mortgage refinance demand dropped 18 percent as rates hit their highest level since August, a new Oxford Economics report revealed that the firm’s Housing Affordability Index stood at 77.9 in the first quarter of 2026, meaning a median-income household earns only about 78 percent of the income needed to afford a median-priced home. The report’s most likely forecast scenario has the index staying below 80 for the next 10 years. By that estimate, homes will not be generally considered affordable again until the 2030s at the earliest, Real Estate News reports.

The Realtor.com May 2026 Monthly Housing Report released Wednesday found that median list prices fell 2.4 percent year over year in the steepest decline in the site’s data since 2017. At the same time, pending sales rose for a sixth consecutive month and new listings hit their highest May level since 2022, continuing what Realtor.com describes as “the most active spring market in four years.”

The report identifies two trends to watch in June: contract cancellations and/or delistings (which spiked in May and June 2025 amid tariff uncertainty but have so far remained below recent-year levels in 2026), and whether the supply surge in the Northeast and Midwest sustains after new listings reversed declines.

The best argument for affordable housing? The birth rate.

My favorite housing-related story of the week comes from Mashable, which reported on “Economics of Orgasm” data from sex toy brand LELO that found “an undeniable correlation between a lack of residential independence, intimacy barriers, and the psychological strain of feeling like you’ve failed at adulting.” In LELO’s words, the current economic state of affairs has resulted in “erotic inequity.”

Not only are young people failing to create intimate relationships that would lead to child-rearing, but apparently an orgasm recession driven by housing costs is bad for productivity and, thus, the G.D.P.

In full transparency, I have not determined whether LELO’s survey is to be trusted. But I like the take, regardless.
