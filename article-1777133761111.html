Stop trying to make me buy a house

Senator Warren and Senator Moreno revive the bipartisan case against renters.

Jerusalem Demsas
Mar 30
∙
Paid
 
READ IN APP
 

Imagine if, at 18 years old, your only options were to remain in your parents’ home or to buy a home of your own.

I think most young people would be extremely put off by the idea that the only way to forge your own path in the world would be to both have enough money to purchase your own property and to have enough certainty to commit to a specific location (and therefore job market and dating market).

But, you know, people are different!

Roughly half a million 18- to 23-year-olds are homeowners, and while that’s only 2.3% of all people in that age group, it just goes to show that there are a lot of people in this country living lots of different kinds of lives.¹ And that’s okay.

The few, the mighty, the 18-23 year old homeowners
Estimated number of 18 to 23-year-olds heading — or living with a spouse or partner who heads — an owner-occupied household.

As you can see in that graph, low mortgage rates in 2020 caused a big jump in young homeowners, which indicates that there is unmet demand for homeownership among even very young adults. But still, I think it’s safe to say that the majority of young adults would probably not choose to buy a house even if they had the financial means to do so.

At its best, homeownership is stability, but that stability comes at a price: Freedom.

Fresh out of college earning $48,000 a year, I rented a room for $900 on a month-to-month lease in Prince George’s County, Maryland. There were some problems with the house, but when the campaign I was working on ended and I got a new gig in South Carolina, it was trivially easy to move without incurring a bunch of costs of either breaking the lease or — god forbid — selling a house.

People make pro-homeownership arguments all the time so I won’t repeat them here, but rarely do people hear about the downsides. The costs of moving for homeowners are high, though. Either they have to sell their house or become landlords (or the very wealthy can afford to hold multiple properties).

As Yale Law professor David Schleicher explained in his great paper, “Stuck! The Law and Economics of Residential Stagnation,” homeowners often don’t move even if they’re offered better opportunities elsewhere. Worse, if the economy is doing badly, homeowners who may otherwise have braved the difficulties of a sale have an extra incentive not to sell because they smartly want to hold out for when the market recovers. It’s not exactly clear what the mechanisms are, but, “homeownership rates correlate with unemployment and lower interstate labor mobility.”

All this to say, there are real reasons why someone would prefer the freedom of instability to the freedom of stability.

So far this is one massive subtweet, so I’ll just make the subtext, text:

Last week, the debate in Congress continued over a provision in the ROAD to Housing Act that would significantly hamper institutional investors from building single family homes for the purpose of renting them out.

Putting aside the benefits or costs of such policies, I want to draw out a latent belief held by conservatives on the left and right: That all renters are temporarily embarrassed homeowners.

Republican Sen. Bernie Moreno argued that “it’s absolutely correct that we should be building homes to buy and not to rent. And I urge my colleagues in the House to vote for the bill as is, without modifying it, and get that bill passed.” The quote was hyped on Twitter by a senior Warren staffer, the president of The Century Foundation (a former Elizabeth Warren staffer), and two leaders of the monomaniacally-obsessed-with-corporate-power ideological sect: Matt Stoller and Nidhi Hegde.

This came after Warren’s own remarks that while it was fine for institutional investors to build “as many apartment houses, as many condo complexes, as many triplexes as they want,” that “homes should be for families, not for giant corporations.”

Apparently, the apartments I lived in as a child were not real homes.

This type of anti-renter, anti-multi-family housing sentiment has deep roots in American policymaking. For much of the country’s history, property ownership was a prerequisite for voting—built on the theory that men who have no property are basically women or children with respect to their “good judgement” and independence of mind². In the infamous 1926 Supreme Court opinion, Village of Euclid v. Ambler, Justice George Sutherland memorably called apartment buildings “mere parasite[s].”

The small-c conservative deference to property owners is one of those widely unchallenged presumptions across the political spectrum. Yes, very few would say we should restrict voting to people who own homes, but the sense that it is obviously better to own housing (and anyone who rents has either been failed by the market or public policy) is endemic.

I’ve written before that I think the homeownership society is overrated. A large number of people are in a large number of situations where homeownership would make life strictly worse, and public policy incentivizing them to become homeowners serves to actually hurt their financial interests. For instance, between 2017 and 2020, the Department of Housing and Urban Development offloaded foreclosed homes in flood zones to first-time homeowners.

After NPR uncovered this pattern, an agency spokesperson doubled down, revealing just how distorting homeownership brain can be: “Locking low-income homebuyers out of a great number of affordable homeownership opportunities because the homes are located in communities within flood zones not only runs contrary to the Administration's objectives of achieving greater equity in homeownership, but prohibiting sales based on income would be comparable to sanctioning a resurgence of redlining."

I think some people have a hard time believing that others value flexibility in their economic arrangements. Perhaps this is because they feel so far away from the 18-year-old version of themselves that did so or because people who work in policy are a highly selected bunch who are disproportionately willing to commit to things like college and grad school.

Whatever the reason, there is ample evidence that some people have different preferences than you.

First, while it’s more common to own than rent once you hit middle age, there are still roughly 45 million renter-occupied households in the country.

Renting isn't just for the young
Number of owner- and renter-occupied households by age of the householder. Counts reflect housing units, not individuals which underrates the number of renters there really are.

When you look at renting vs homeownership by income level, it’s clear that being richer correlates with homeownership. Whether people are making that choice because the homes they want are only available for homeowners or to take advantage of tax breaks is not totally clear. But I think it’s safe to say that even if you were to get rid of the mortgage interest deduction and increase the supply of top-end single family home rentals, more high-income people would choose to own than rent.

There are roughly 10 million renter households earning over $100,000 a year
Number of owner- and renter-occupied households by annual household income. Each household counts once regardless of size.

But again, “more” does not mean “all.” This country has lots of different people, and even at the top end of the income distribution, millions are choosing to rent. Are they all morons who don’t know about the tax benefits? Or, perhaps, are there many people who could reasonably decide that, for their situation and their preference architecture, they would simply rather rent.

As I’ve written before, the selection issues in the messenger class — journalists, policymakers, tech workers, and other Main Characters in The Discourse — often blind those messengers to ways that their experiences and preferences set them apart from other people.

Now, I am similarly blinded, so it’s good to look at self-reported experiences. When asked why they rent, the most common answer (from 68% of respondents) was because they can’t afford a downpayment. But 58% of renters (respondents were allowed to select more than one reason) said it was because it’s “more convenient or flexible to rent,” while 47% said “renting is less financially risky” and 39% said simply that they “prefer to rent.”

Many people just want to rent
Not all renters are boxed out of homeownership.

These people are basically invisible in the discourse because high-socioeconomic status politicians cannot imagine that someone could simply disagree with them on this question and not be a moron.

The American Housing Survey (AHS) asks householders to rate their homes every two years. The specific question is: “On a scale of 1 to 10, how would you rate this housing unit as a place to live, with 1 being the worst and 10 being the best?” On average, homeowners give an 8.74, while renters give a 7.72.

This is a real gap, but even the raw data indicates that renters are not a massively dissatisfied bunch.

Using Claude Code, I ran a simple OLS regression to determine how much of this gap could be explained by things like housing characteristics, income, and region. Income and region explain basically nothing. But the characteristics of the house explain a full third of the gap between homeowners and renters.³

These include details like the year the home was built; whether it’s a single-family detached home or a small, medium, or large multifamily; the number of bedrooms; and the size of the home, as well as the AHS’s own composite flag for whether the unit is adequate (constructed from reported problems with plumbing, heating, electrical, and upkeep).

Real economists have done similar exercises.

In Canada, the dwelling satisfaction gap nearly disappears once you control for the number of bedrooms, issues like mold or pests, and whether it’s a single family home.⁴ Simply put, renters aren’t dissatisfied because they rent, they’re dissatistifed because they get worse housing.

Which suggests that one of the highest-value things you can do to improve renter satisfaction seems to be increasing rental opportunities of single-family homes. Unfortunately, this is precisely what the ROAD to Housing Act seeks to prevent. Warren and Moreno aren’t just wrong that all renters yearn for a mortgage, they’re actively trying to ban the type of rental housing renters want most.

The apartments I grew up in were real homes. So were the rooms, apartments, and row houses I’ve rented over the course of the past decade.
