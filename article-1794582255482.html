Andrew Potter: “Serious Guy” Carney needs to tell the truth on immigration

Does he think we won’t like the real number? Or has he actually lost control, just like Trudeau did?

Andrew Potter
Nov 13
 
READ IN APP
 

By: Andrew Potter

In late September, Prime Minister Mark Carney told the New York Times that, with the immigration system back under control, the question was when to “resume some controlled increase in the population.”

In light of last week’s federal budget it would appear that the answer he landed on was, “immediately,” though how under control it is, and what led to his decision to open it up, is an open question. But it matters enormously, because of this graph:

It is from the latest Environics report on public attitudes towards immigration and refugees, and it tells a complicated narrative about the collision of national identity, economic need, and social cohesion. It may be the most important graph in Canadian domestic public policy right now.

It helps to start by appreciating just how recent our national comfort with high levels of immigration actually is. When Brian Mulroney more than doubled the targets in the late 1980s, the public backlash led to the creation of the Reform Party and sparked a decade of anxiety about “hyphenated Canadians” and cultural balkanization. It was only at the end of the 1990s that opinion flipped from predominantly anti-immigration to generally in favour.

For most of the next quarter century, a solid multi-partisan majority rejected the idea that we were taking in too many people. That consensus did not just happen — it was a hard-won achievement driven by smart policy and even smarter public messaging. But the Liberals under Justin Trudeau didn’t seem to grasp that. Instead, they treated it as something close to a baked-in fact about Canada, a fundamental part of our supposed diversity-is-our-strength post-modern identity. And so when they opened the immigration floodgates after the COVID-19 pandemic, the government seemed completely shocked to see that the share of Canadians who said there was too much immigration more than doubled virtually overnight, from 27 per cent in 2022 to 58 per cent in 2024.

What matters here is precisely why people changed their minds. And while there was no doubt some unease about the changing face of our cities, the truth is Canadians didn’t turn on immigrants so much as they turned on the people in charge. In its surveys, Environics asks those in the “too much immigration” camp to explain their views. Over the past three years, the fastest-growing and now number one answer is that the government is doing a lousy job of managing the system.

When the public finally realized how badly Ottawa had lost control of immigration in late 2024, the Trudeau government responded to rising public anger by belatedly started cutting back on newcomers. When he took over, Mark Carney declared that immigration had been “far too high” and that the country had “lost control” of its temporary resident programs. He made finishing the job one of his signature promises.

Mission accomplished, according to the budget. It boasts that Ottawa has “taken back control of immigration,” and set targets of 380,000 new permanent residents a year through 2029, and 370,000 new temporary arrivals. But that’s not the end of it: alongside the official top-line number sits a “time-limited measure” to give permanent residency to up to 200,000 temporary residents already in the country, none of whom count toward the 380,000. Neither do the roughly 82,000 spots left over from last year’s two “one-time initiatives,” which were also kept off the books. Add it up and Canada could add as many as 662,000 new permanent residents in 2027. That is well past the Trudeau-era record of 484,000 that broke the consensus in the first place. It is also about 1.6 per cent of the population, substantially above the one per cent ceiling the Liberal platform promised to respect.

One may try to argue, as Andrew Coyne does, that this is actually just the government being extremely transparent about its targets. But transparent governments don’t make a habit of dropping the real news late on a Friday afternoon the day after a budget. Yet that’s what the Liberals did, putting out a notice right before the weekend announcing that low-wage temporary foreign worker approvals can now run two years instead of one. Early childhood educators are exempt from the freeze on low-wage hiring in high-unemployment cities. And provinces can let employers fill 15 per cent of their workforce with low-wage foreign workers, up from 10. None of it was in the budget, which had described Canada’s labour supply as “resilient.”

Some of this appears to be the government caving in the face of heavy lobbying by business associations, who have been complaining that since immigration rules were tightened two years ago they have struggled to find workers. But that is hard to square with the facts. As BMO chief economist Doug Porter pointed out this week, in a note explicitly about the new immigration targets, employment has fallen for three straight months and is up a grim 0.1 per cent year over year. Unemployment has risen two months running, to 6.7 per cent, and there are now almost three unemployed Canadians for every vacant job. According to a new report from the C.D. Howe institute, young Canadians are facing historically long bouts of unemployment.

So if the case for raising immigration targets now on labour market grounds is increasingly weak, what is motivating Carney’s rather sudden decision to “resume some controlled increase in the population?” What justifies the actual numbers? No one in the government seems interested in explaining what is going on. Instead, what we’re seeing is the return of a familiar Liberal tic of what could charitably be called fudging the facts. Justin Trudeau governed this way for the better part of a decade, communicating by half truths, fibs, and empty slogans until he ended up reflexively lying to Canadians whenever he couldn’t be bothered trying to explain the truth.

It’s a worse habit in Carney than it ever was in Trudeau, for the simple reason that Carney’s entire political brand is that he’s the Serious Guy. Canadians didn’t elect him for his last name or his good hair or emo rizz; they elected him because they believed he was a man who could be trusted to tell them the hard truths, whether it is about the economy, the federation, or the Americans. Snowing the country on immigration blows that credibility on the one file where the public has made it clear that trust is the whole game.

The issue isn’t whether 662,000 new permanent residents is too many, too few, or just right — reasonable people can disagree about that. The question is why the government felt it had to pretend the number was 380,000. One possibility is they think the real number is the appropriate target, but they don’t think they can make a convincing case for it to Canadians. The other possibility is that they know the real number is too high, that they don’t really have things under control, and have decided it’s not worth trying. And certainly not something they’re willing to admit.

Either way, this is not how you rebuild what remains a very shaky consensus on immigration. The glue that binds our increasingly diverse and multicultural society together, which serves as the catalyst for cooperation, stability, and social cohesion, is trust. But trust is hard won and easily lost, and a government that doesn’t trust Canadians enough to tell them what it is up to on immigration is playing a very dangerous game.
