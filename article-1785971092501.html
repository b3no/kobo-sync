They’ve had it up to here with the NIMBYs

From Massachusetts to California to Texas, officials and investors are increasingly overcoming local resistance to build more housing.

Halina Bennet
Aug 5
∙
Paid
 
READ IN APP
 

Last week gave us examples of everyone from private developers and investors to members of state governments and city councils taking steps to accelerate housing production, often by working around traditional local barriers. At the same time, communities continued experimenting with ways to add homes through accessory dwelling units, modular construction, and the redevelopment of underused land.

One financial giant is getting directly involved. JPMorgan Chase, the nation’s largest bank, on Monday pledged $750 billion through 2035 to promote housing production and homeownership. The commitment, which the bank says is a 40 percent increase in its housing capital deployment compared to the past decade, includes lending, financing, and investments to increase supply.

“An affordable and resilient housing market is essential to driving economic growth and increasing opportunity,” Michelle Herrick, head of commercial real estate for JPMorgan, wrote in the company’s statement.

No more NIMBYism

In Massachusetts, a city council defies its mayor and A.D.U.s make progress
Waltham, a city about nine miles from Boston with approximately 65,000 people, has been toying with a plan to allow a 135-acre office complex, which has been left mostly vacant in recent years, to become housing. That is, until Mayor Jeannette McCarthy vetoed the special zoning district that could have resulted in roughly 1,200 new housing units.

But on Monday night, the Waltham City Council voted near-unanimously to overrule the mayor, allowing the landowner BXP to move forward with a project slated to become Waltham’s “largest housing development in recent memory,” the Boston Globe reports. The project is one element of a broader vision to transform a congested corridor of Waltham that has become something of a no-man’s-land between the city’s residential neighborhoods and Boston proper. The city council’s eagerness to approve new housing despite executive opposition is a sign that the Boston metro area, and Massachusetts more broadly, has a real eye on development.

One way the state is trying to alleviate its housing squeeze, Canary Media reports, is by making it easier to build accessory dwelling units. In 2024, Governor Maura Healey signed a bill making the structures (which include granny flats, garage apartments, and small freestanding backyard homes) legal in all Massachusetts cities and towns except Boston. Since then, the state has pursued additional efforts to promote adoption and implementation through financing and incentive programs.

Texas stuck it to the NIMBYs. It’s working.
An editorial in the Dallas Morning News hailed early results from Senate Bill 840 — a zoning preemption law that passed last year and allows multifamily and mixed-use projects in areas zoned for commercial use — as evidence that the state’s effort to limit local barriers to housing is already generating significant development. Despite plenty of pushback, writes the Dallas Morning News Editorial Board, the bill has “directly facilitated more than 1,250 housing units and indirectly supported at least 7,235 units.”

California cracks down on defiant cities
California Attorney General Rob Bonta has had it with cities that find workarounds for the state’s housing development laws. In a letter to Menlo Park — one of the most expensive cities in the Bay Area (and therefore the country) — Bonta said the city violated California’s housing laws by changing its reasons for rejecting a proposed 665-unit project. He argued the city had improperly denied development protections outlined in the state’s builder’s remedy law and wrongfully required the developer to pay the city’s legal costs. The two-year-old dispute is ongoing, but Bonta’s letter surely strengthens the developer’s position as the developer continues to fight in court, the San Francisco Chronicle reports.

The proposal to develop the former Sunset magazine headquarters into a mixed-use community has faced opposition from Menlo Park residents over its height, density, traffic implications, and compatibility with the neighborhood. The case is shaping up to be a significant test of the state’s rapidly expanding housing laws, and the dispute’s outcome could have implications far beyond Menlo Park. Many other high-profile projects, including San Francisco’s proposed Marina Safeway redevelopment, are hoping to move forward under similar state housing laws. For now, Bonta has put his foot down against NIMBYism, signaling how the state will likely approach similar disputes.

Meanwhile, also in Northern California, a landlord-tenant battle at a rent-controlled apartment building has stretched into one of San Francisco’s longest rent strikes in decades. After residents said the building’s new owner cut services and neglected repairs, attempting to push them out, they formed a tenant union and have, over the past 32 months, withheld more than $400,000 while the case is in court. Read the San Francisco Chronicle’s report of the battle.

Production and development

Modular housing on Long Island faces a familiar obstacle
New York is hoping that modular and manufactured housing can help address Long Island’s affordability crisis by delivering homes more quickly and at a lower cost than traditional construction. The state recently gave East Hampton — one of the most expensive parts of Long Island — $7 million to build 16 below-market-rate manufactured starter homes as part of an effort to expand missing middle housing, Newsday reports.

According to 2023 research from Harvard University’s Joint Center for Housing Studies, modular construction can reduce building costs by about 27 percent compared to site-built homes, but savings shrink significantly where land prices are high, like on Long Island. Local builders cited by Newsday estimate that actual savings range from 5 to 30 percent, with the biggest advantage typically coming from cutting construction timelines in half rather than lowering construction costs per se.

Despite the benefits of modular homes (including greater energy efficiency, lower building costs, and factory-controlled construction that can improve quality and speed), high land prices, demand for customization, and skepticism of factory-built housing have curbed widespread adoption, particularly for single-family homes. Still, modular construction has seen success in multifamily and affordable housing projects. It’s just those starter-home and missing-middle markets that have yet to pick up.

Golf courses could become thousands of homes in Nevada — and millions in the U.S.
One new approach to Nevada’s housing shortage is to convert its underused golf courses into housing. The state’s most significant example is Desert Pines Golf Club in Las Vegas, where officials last year approved a plan to build 1,500 units of below-market-rate housing — the largest affordable housing project in Nevada’s history, Vox reports. In addition to housing, the development will include a soccer field, walking trails, a job training center, and expanded green space, all of which together will use less water than the golf course requires.

The project and others like it are a direct result of Nevada’s housing and land shortages. The state needs an estimated 120,000 additional affordable homes but has little land available for development because about 85 percent of it is federally owned and set aside for other purposes like recreation. It is estimated that the state will run out of land to develop by 2032. Redeveloping golf courses is a cost-effective solution since the parcels are already connected to city infrastructure through both roads and utilities.

If all 16,000 golf courses in the U.S. were redeveloped at a similar density, Vox reports, they would theoretically be able to accommodate 22 million homes. Even just converting municipally owned courses could create 4.3 million units, making a significant dent in the country’s estimated shortage of 7.2 million affordable homes. That’s of course not going to happen, but it is certainly true that there are golf courses all over the country that are integrated into urban and suburban areas but that I reckon are used by far fewer people than housing would be.

It is, of course, impossible to simply redevelop this kind of land without facing pushback. One such attempt to redevelop Las Vegas’s Badlands Golf Club became the center of a decade-long legal battle after the city blocked plans due to pressure from neighbors. The dispute cost taxpayers $286 million in legal settlements and delayed other ongoing projects.
