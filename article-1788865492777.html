There’s an awful lot we don’t know about data centers

An un-splainer about the issue of the moment

Matthew Yglesias
Sep 8
∙
Paid
 
READ IN APP
 

I had an idea for a post about data centers, and one thing I needed to know was how many data centers were built in 2025 compared with previous years. Are we on pace in 2026 to build more data centers than in recent years or fewer? We know that there are lots of new laws designed to curtail data center development, but is that because there are, in fact, way more projects?

I didn’t write that article, though, because I could not find an answer to this seemingly basic question.

I think one of journalists’ worst failings is that we don’t like to tell the audience what we don’t know, so we often end up writing around basic factual questions that articles should really address. I’ve read dozens of pieces about the data center backlash, and none of them are clear about the pace of data center construction because it seems like nobody’s really sure.

The whole “data” situation around data centers, in other words, is quite bad.

For example, on the left is a Pew chart showing the top states for existing and planned data centers, and on the right there’s a chart of the same thing from the Manhattan Institute. The two charts do not agree.

This is in part because Pew and Manhattan are relying on different underlying sets of data. It’s in part because the question of what it means for a data center to be “planned” is a bit ambiguous, and Manhattan is taking a much more generous definition of a plan. It’s also partly because Shawn Regan, in the piece that I took the Manhattan Institute chart from, is trying to distinguish Greg Abbott’s data center order from Josh Shapiro’s. He notes that Abbott’s “audit is intended to help distinguish projects with real development potential from more speculative ones,” in which case it’s important to note that many proposals exist in only vague form.

Still, that doesn’t fully account for the gap: Manhattan says there are more than twice as many data centers in Virginia as in Texas, while Pew sees a much narrower lead.

Pew and Manhattan are also counting different things — Pew is focused on the number of data centers, but Manhattan is counting the gigawatts of capacity. Texas, I guess, has smaller data centers on average than Virginia.

One underlying issue is that a “data center” is not really a regulatory category as far as the federal government is concerned. Via Google Maps, here’s a swath of Loudoun County in Virginia that features both NTT Global Data Centers Americas’ VA-1 data center and NTT Global Data Centers Americas’ VA-2 data center. They are separated by a Greek restaurant and a business that does concrete coatings.

Does it matter in any relevant way that these are two distinct smallish data centers rather than a single large one? Probably not. The gigantic Amazon/Anthropic data center in New Carlisle, Ind., is several distinct buildings, some of which are further apart than NTT’s two data centers in that Loudoun strip mall.

But there’s an even more fundamental issue in trying to get a handle on the actual rate of data center construction.

I asked someone who works on data center deals for a major tech company and was told that some of the information you’d need to determine this is, on some level, actually treated as commercial trade secrets.

That person’s team is only approximately aware of what its competitors are up to and would like to obscure its own activities from the competition as well. Everyone knows that Amazon Web Services’ US-East-1 data center cluster is in Northern Virginia somewhere, but for security reasons the company won’t specify the exact addresses or which facilities precisely they’re using.

All of which is to say that instead of the article I’d originally planned, today I’m sharing an explainer about data centers that is also a little bit of an un-splainer, underscoring the extent to which this issue has leapt onto the public agenda in a somewhat undigested way.

Data center projects are changing

A data center is more or less what used to be called a “server farm,” which is to say it’s a bunch of computer equipment in a big room.

To the extent that I.T. professionals have tried to draw a distinction here, a server farm could be something as modest as a little room in an office, while a data center implies a dedicated building. And the term “data center” is meant to encompass not just the computer equipment inside the building but the entire structure designed to house that equipment — notably, the infrastructure needed to keep it cool.

The idea of a big room full of computer equipment has nothing in particular to do with artificial intelligence, and the construction of data centers long predates the boom set off by the release of GPT-3 several years ago.

One common form of data center is the colocation data center — a big facility designed to host computer equipment. There, you can rent space and put servers, cables, networking devices, and other hardware. The idea is that a company that’s too small to invest in a dedicated facility with full-time staff might still need to maintain computer equipment, so it can rent space at a colocation data center for its own hardware. Alternatively, a larger company with a lot of computer needs might maintain its own enterprise data center, where the company both owns the servers and runs the facility. A further evolution of the concept is that really big computer companies like Amazon and Microsoft offer extensive cloud computing services — this way, instead of owning a bunch of computer equipment and renting space for it, a company can just purchase computing services from a giant company that itself will maintain data centers around the world.

An important aspect of all this is that despite the apparently weightless nature of the internet, the physical location of these data centers matters a fair amount because it does actually take non-zero amounts of time for bits to travel.

Northern Virginia became a hub for data centers precisely because an important piece of the early physical infrastructure for the internet was located there.

Similarly, the data centers for New York City’s major financial markets are located in northern New Jersey, which spawned additional Garden State data centers for financial services firms that need the fastest possible access to trades and data. The general trend for data centers has historically been that you want them to be near customers — meaning mostly near cities — and you’re just calculating a tradeoff between proximity and land costs, much as you would with warehouses or ports or other logistics infrastructure.

The rise of A.I. has done two things:

  • When people use A.I., that use has to run on computer equipment somewhere, so increased A.I. use creates more demand for data centers.

  • When frontier A.I. labs train new models, they need tons of computer equipment, so there’s new demand for large data centers for this training.

A lot of people talk about “A.I. data centers” as a category distinct from other kinds of data centers.

I think this is probably a mistake. The data centers for using A.I. are in a lot of respects similar to the data centers we’ve been building for years; it’s the training hubs that are distinct in a way that is most relevant to the general public and to politics.

The key is that the hyperscale data centers being built to train new frontier A.I. models are both physically large and relatively location-agnostic. For a data center meant to host a website or run a consumer-facing chatbot, latency — the delay caused by things like distance from users and network congestion — really matters. For a data center meant to be used to build GPT-17 or whatever, that stuff doesn’t matter so much. But that second kind of data center does have to be really big. So the balance of considerations shifts in the direction of “build it in the middle of nowhere” rather than “build it in a strip mall in the suburbs.”

Data centers use a lot of electricity

The earliest wave of the data center backlash focused largely on water, and the water use issue is mostly a red herring. America is a water-rich country, data centers don’t use very much water compared to other industrial or commercial applications, and water scarcity (to the extent that it’s real) has a trivially easy policy fix. But beyond that, it’s simple on a technical level to switch data centers to “closed loop” designs that consume basically no water. If water is what people are worried about, we can require the closed loops and move on.

Data center critics, I think, are implicitly conceding the point on water by switching to the idea that the “indirect” water consumption is actually very high, basically because power plants use a lot of water.

A more straightforward way of making this argument is just to say that data centers use a lot of electricity, which is unambiguously true. There’s a reason the industry has started quantifying data centers in terms of their megawatts of capacity — electricity use is a defining characteristic of the facilities.

The high levels of electricity consumption associated with data centers raise the fear that they are responsible for recent increases in utility bills and/or that they will be in the future.

Is this actually true?

The industry and its allies point out that the statistical relationship is ambiguous and often negative. But the key to this is that while generating electricity costs money, it’s a minority of utilities’ total costs. California, for example, has been hammered recently by utility costs related to wildfires. Maine in recent years had huge costs related to poles being knocked down in winter storms. Bringing new large users onto the grid spreads those kinds of fixed costs across more users and drives household prices down.

The complication for this argument is that whether new loads will bring consumer prices up or down is entirely a function of the specific circumstances and the details of regulatory implementation.

Most of us are not particularly interested in delving into these details in a rigorous way or capable of doing the relevant modeling. You end up on some level with the question of whether you trust the utilities, their regulators, the companies, and the relevant public officials to be making good deals or not.

And one of my main takeaways from Jasmine Sun’s extensive reporting on this is that we’re looking at a basic breakdown in trust.

I think this also makes the data center polling hard to parse. To be a bit flip, everything I see on this is something like “90 percent of people want to destroy every data center with a hydrogen bomb but it flips to 111 percent support if you promise them the data center will lower their bills and renovate a nearby playground.” It’s obviously possible to structure the tax and electricity rules concerning data centers to make them win-win. But is that actually happening? And if it is happening, will anyone believe it?

What makes this issue even thornier is that even if increased electricity consumption doesn’t raise consumer bills, it definitely impacts the world.

More electricity means either burning more natural gas or building more utility-scale wind and solar projects — most likely both, and the transmission infrastructure to support it all.

This has implications for air quality and climate change and local land use. All of these options are going to be controversial with someone. Optimists like Ted Nordhaus hope we can leverage data centers into a source of capital for advanced nuclear and geothermal plants, helping to bring new technologies to scale and unleashing a new era of clean energy abundance.

I hope that’s right! But the basic trust and polarization issues here are difficult to navigate.

One big faction of American politics pretends that global warming is fake and another is fundamentally opposed to increasing energy use at all. The whole space is swarming with lobbyists who just care about their narrow financial interests. It’s a tough political problem, made tougher because it intersects with other anxieties.

Is this an effective way to regulate A.I?

The biggest known unknown of all is whether public pushback to data center construction is a useful step toward regulating A.I. risk writ large or a dangerous distraction that’s going to leave everyone chasing shiny objects.

One reason I’m unsure about this is that many of the people who are interested in this topic aren’t debating it squarely. Instead, they’re writing pieces that try to explore the question of what the backlash is “really” about. Andy Masley, citing extensive polling data, argues that the backlash really is NIMBY opposition to data centers and that we should see it for what it is rather than project some larger ideological agenda onto it. David Wallace-Wells retorts that it’s hard to explain opposition to data centers as pure NIMBYism because they’re so much less popular than anything else — less popular than coal or nuclear plants, for example — so you can’t understand it detached from larger concerns about A.I. and democratic control of the future of our country.

Both sides in this debate raise good points. But what I think they really mean to be arguing about is whether, normatively, it makes sense for people worried about untrammeled A.I. development to lean on data center activism.

The skeptics note that it’s simply not the case that blocking data center construction halts A.I. progress or regulates the conduct of the A.I. industry. In the absence of strong export controls on chips, preventing data centers from being built in the United States mostly means the data centers will get built abroad instead. Some of those will be foreign data centers working for American A.I. companies, and others will be foreign data centers working for foreign (mostly Chinese) A.I. companies.

Similarly, raising a huge public stink about A.I. as a utility price issue raises the risk that the companies find a way to credibly address these concerns and the purported leverage evaporates.

This all reminds me of the debates over the Keystone XL pipeline during Barack Obama’s second term.

Literal-minded people like me felt it was perverse of the climate movement to pivot so heavily to trying to block a project that would have essentially no impact on the global emissions trajectory. Proponents of the anti-pipeline campaign saw it as a valuable organizing moment to build political power for the future. In retrospect, it looks to me like a politically costly waste of time and energy that was totally unrelated to what became the meaningful climate achievements of the Inflation Reduction Act. But mainstream environmentalists say that’s wrong and that the I.R.A. reflects their successful organizing and power-building.

I think I’m right about Keystone. I’m all for a little data center politicking as a “win the midterms” gambit, but I’m skeptical that the road to safe and secure artificial intelligence for the benefit of all humanity runs through NIMBY campaigns about physical infrastructure.

But I will admit to some uncertainty here.

People continue to debate the Keystone case. It makes me think, though, that the question the policy-minded are really asking about the data center backlash is going to be best answered by looking at the history of political organizing efforts, not by delving even deeper into the specific data center polling.
