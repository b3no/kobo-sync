The Vertiginous Revelations of “You Can See Everything”

Nathan Fielder and Lance Oppenheim’s interrogation of Elizabeth Holmes, the disgraced Theranos founder, is a compelling spectacle of manipulation, suspicion, and apparent sincerity.

By Richard Brody

Nathan Fielder may well have the best deadpan shtick since Buster Keaton. But, whereas Keaton offers stoic innocence, Fielder clinically probes other people’s misconceptions and delusions, which makes him a perfect match for Elizabeth Holmes. Holmes, the founder of Theranos, a Silicon Valley startup that purported to revolutionize blood testing, was convicted of four counts of fraud in 2022 and sentenced to more than eleven years in prison. In 2023, less than two months before the start of her sentence, she and her partner, Billy Evans, summoned Fielder to their beachfront house in Southern California and invited him to move in and film them in their domestic environment. The result is Fielder’s new documentary, “You Can See Everything,” which he co-directed with Lance Oppenheim, an experienced documentarian who also directed the recent drama “Primetime.”

One of my pet peeves with observational documentaries (including Oppenheim’s 2020 film, “Some Kind of Heaven”) is the elision of their transactional side: What do the movie’s on-camera participants expect to get from being filmed? There’s no such danger with “You Can See Everything.” Holmes states up front that she wants to correct the public narrative; she hopes that having the filmmakers in her house will enable her to be seen, as Evans puts it, with “radical transparency.” She says that she wants to present her story as “a human story”: “It starts with who I am and what my intent was and still is.” She claims to be innocent and appears bewildered by her fate. Fielder asks, with astonishment, whether she really thinks she committed no crime, and she responds, “I know I didn’t commit any crime.”

Fielder, of course, is no ordinary observational filmmaker; he has forged a TV career from drastic interventionism. His onscreen manner is based on affectlessly posing intrusive questions, and his shows involve real-life simulations akin to social-science experiments. (In his series “Nathan for You,” he forms faux businesses; in “The Rehearsal,” he directs participants in elaborate stagings of their real-life situations.) These features lend his work an air of cruelty, making people seem like specimens in the hands of a researcher bound by no scientific ethics or norms. The relationships are mainly ones of power, and he doesn’t let his subjects forget it.

In “You Can See Everything,” he doesn’t let Holmes forget it, either. He’s sarcastic to her and even embarrasses her in front of her parents, evidently assuming that, as her story is in his hands, she won’t dare push back. But Holmes’s preëxisting notoriety has its own power. Nothing Fielder discovers can top what’s already widely known, so his observations inevitably temper her public image. Fielder’s portrait of Holmes and his display of his methods turn his questioning to self-questioning, forcing him to assess, and even revise, basic assumptions about her and about life in general. The result is a movie of dazzling conundrums and vertiginous paradoxes, leading Fielder ever deeper into emotional and intellectual wilds. For all the unpleasantness of its backstory, its confrontations, its outcomes, and even, at times, Fielder’s own behavior, the film is more than fascinating and ingenious; it’s strangely, poetically, poignantly beautiful.
