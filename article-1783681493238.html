It really is about Israel

Gaza as the new Vietnam

There are a few books that I think everyone should read. One of them is The Nature and Origins of Mass Opinion by John Zaller.

It’s a fascinating, if a bit dense, text that develops a comprehensive theory of how people form and express political opinions. Zaller argued that most of public opinion is downstream of elite discourse, and his theories provide a good framework for understanding how Gaza became the new Vietnam.

Share

The book’s big idea is that most people don’t actually hold political preferences or structured belief systems from which they can derive policy preferences. Instead, Zaller argued that public opinion is heavily influenced by elite political discourse. He described the precise mechanism of elite influence as the “Receive-Accept-Sample” (or RAS) model.

Briefly, people have varying propensities to receive political information. If you subscribe to this magazine, you probably have a high propensity to receive political information; if you’re someone who rarely consumes news, you’re probably low-propensity.

Once you receive a piece of political information, you can choose to either accept or reject it. How likely you are to accept a given bit of information has a lot to do with your existing attitudes; for example, a registered Democrat is much more likely to accept a fact if it comes from Fareed Zakaria than if she hears it from Tucker Carlson.

Finally, people sample from their bank of existing ideas when they are asked to express a political preference — whether that’s in a conversation, in a poll, or at the ballot box, with different circumstances leading to different types of sampling.

In Chapter 9, Zaller turned to Vietnam as a case study of how opinion changes when people receive two different flows of elite messaging. Specifically, liberal voters received reasons to support the war from the Democratic president, Lyndon Johnson, as well as reasons to oppose the war from a growing group of journalists, intellectuals, and young activists.

Zaller measured the intensity of pro- and anti-war reporting using newspaper coverage. Discussion of the war increased over the period, with the number of pro-war messages peaking in 1966 and falling afterward, while anti-war messages steadily increased over time. He classified respondents as foreign policy doves and hawks based on how they responded to a series of questions on how the United States should conduct foreign policy in general, and he measured their level of political awareness using a series of factual questions.

In 1964, there was little difference in support for the war between doves and hawks, with more politically aware people being more supportive of the war. But by 1966, this pattern began to break down — highly informed hawks remained pro-war, but highly informed doves began to turn against it. By 1970, support for the Vietnam War had become polarized.

What caused this pattern? Zaller noted that “the biggest antiwar shift in liberal opinion occurred after Richard Nixon took over the presidency in 1969. With the war effort being led by a Republican rather than a Democrat, many doves found it easier to oppose the war.”

It wasn’t just partisanship. Zaller argued that liberals — particularly older and highly politically engaged ones — “who moved in a prowar direction between 1964 and 1966 may have thought, if they stopped to reflect on it, that they were moving in a liberal direction. After all, internationalism had been a core element of the liberal belief package for some thirty years, and the principal advocate for the prowar policy was the liberal Democratic president, Lyndon Johnson.”

But over the course of the war, liberal opinion leaders became increasingly anti-war.

If ideology is defined as knowing “what goes with what,” then one might say that opposing the Vietnam War was made into a part of the broader package of American liberal ideology. Younger liberal voters had less crystallized belief systems, so they turned against the war first in response to anti-war messages from liberal elites; once Johnson left office, older liberal voters did too.

This same type of opinion formation was observed by political scientist Hans Noel in his study of how views on civil rights within the two parties switched in the 20th century. The shift toward associating pro-civil rights views with anti-business economic views (rather than pro-business views) began among pundits and opinion leaders in the 1930s and 1940s. It was followed by shifts in congressional voting behavior in the 1960s and, finally, the exodus of white southerners from the Democratic Party after Lyndon Johnson pushed civil rights legislation through Congress.¹

We have seen exactly this pattern play out with opinions on Israel.

Below is a chart showing changes in the share of Americans who have an unfavorable opinion of Israel from 2022 to 2025, broken out by party affiliation and age. Unsurprisingly, Democrats have soured the most on Israel. But the shift in views among Democrats over the age of 50 is much larger than the shift for Democrats under 50.

Older Democrats have swung the most against Israel
Change in the percentage who have an unfavorable view of Israel

Partly, that is because younger Democrats started out less favorable to Israel. But this data fits the same pattern of two-sided information flows that Zaller applied to opinions on the Vietnam War.

For older Democrats, being pro-Israel is more closely bound up with being a Democrat or being a political liberal. To them, Israel is seen as a democracy that faces serious security threats from its neighbors and deserves our support in guarding against them. Older voters are also more likely to remember the Holocaust or know someone who does, so they are more likely to view Israel as a safe haven for Jewish people.

But for younger Democrats, the information flows are more complicated. For one, they’ve almost never known an Israel not governed by Benjamin Netanyahu, arguably the most right-wing prime minister in Israeli history and someone who does not seem at all concerned with Palestinian human rights. Over the last several decades, left-leaning intellectuals and opinion leaders have become increasingly critical of Israel. Even before Oct. 7, Israel’s treatment of Palestinians was coming under increasing scrutiny.

And so, while older Democrats like Joe Biden remained vocally pro-Israel, the tides of public opinion within the party were shifting. The war in Gaza brought things to the fore: Institutions, thinkers, and politicians that Democrats trusted were highly critical of Israel’s conduct in Gaza as the death toll mounted, while Biden remained steadfast in his support for Israel.

When Trump came back into office, it was like a pressure valve was released: Older Democrats no longer felt conflicted between loyalty to their party’s leader and the arguments of elites they trusted.

Pro-Israel Democrats are a dying breed

An important thing to understand is that Democratic voters — especially the types who turn out in primaries — are extraordinarily anti-Israel these days.

Let’s start with the obvious stuff: Among Harris voters, 59% want to either decrease or entirely end military aid to Israel. Among the most liberal voters, a majority want to end military aid to Israel entirely.

Democrats are more anti-Israel than the rest of the nation — in recent polling from AP-NORC, 52% of Democrats said that Israel has committed genocide against Palestinians. For context, among all Americans, 31% said Israel’s actions amounted to genocide, though a plurality (49%) didn’t know enough to say.

I want to stress that it’s not merely that left-wing voters are critical of the war in Gaza. They’re increasingly skeptical of the Israeli national project itself. A majority — 54% — of Harris 2024 voters agree with the statement “Israel is an apartheid state, engaged in racist oppression against Palestinians.” Among the most liberal voters, that share rises to 77%.

Liberal voters think that Israel is an apartheid state
To what extent do you agree or disagree with these statements? Israel is an apartheid state, engaged in racist oppression against Palestinians.

The most left-wing voters also have a radically different view of Zionism as a concept. When presented with six different definitions of Zionism, the most commonly selected response among all voters was “a movement for self-determination and statehood for the Jewish people in their ancestral homeland,” followed by “I’m not familiar with the term ‘Zionism.’”

But among extremely liberal voters, the most commonly chosen definitions of Zionism were:

  • “Establishing and maintaining a Jewish demographic majority in Palestine by driving out the native Palestinian population”

  • “A form of racism and apartheid against Palestinians”

  • “The creation of a nation-state where Jews get more rights than others on the land”

Left-wing voters have a very negative perception of "Zionism"

The typical retort to all of these data points from pro-Israel Democrats is that while primary voters may have very left-wing views on Israel-Palestine, it’s not a top voting issue for most people, so it can’t really explain primary outcomes. In an interview with Semafor, Rep. Susie Lee of Nevada pushed back on the notion that most Democrats need to shift their positions on Israel in response to the outcomes of safe-seat primaries. “I think it’s district by district, and I think the way we approach Israel, we need to continue to show that they are an ally and we’re supportive of the alliance,” Lee said.

This analysis is partially true.

In the Yale Youth Poll’s spring 2026 survey, Israel-Palestine was ranked 25th out of 30 issues when respondents were asked what was most important when determining their vote. But that’s among all voters.

Among the most left-wing voters — those who self-identify as “extremely liberal” — Israel-Palestine is much more important as a voting issue: They selected it 45% of the time, compared with 28% among voters overall. In fact, the gap in prioritization was larger than for most of the 30 issues tested.

Left-wing voters care much more about Israel-Palestine

Since very left-wing voters are disproportionately likely to turn out in primaries — especially in safe seats — Israel-Palestine can be a decisive issue in Democratic primaries, even if it’s not a top issue for the electorate overall.

You might think that this is all a recent trend. And, indeed, if you look at Gallup’s time series, you can see that Democrats’ sympathies in the Israel-Palestine conflict have changed dramatically since 2023.

Democrats have been shifting against Israel for a decade
In the Middle East situation, are your sympathies more with the Israelis or more with the Palestinians?

But the shift has actually been going on for about a decade. In fact, the pattern of opinion change on Israel within the Democratic Party is a near-perfect mirror of the pattern of opinion change on Vietnam in the 1960s that Zaller describes.

Israel is an albatross for moderate Democrats (in primaries, at least)

Israel does not explain every primary outcome. Phil Weiser and Michael Bennet have virtually identical stances on the topic; so do Antonio Reynoso and Claire Valdez.²

But being pro-Israel is an albatross around the necks of moderate Democrats in their factional battles with the left. The clearest example is arguably the primary in New York’s 10th Congressional District, where the position on Israel was the only high-profile difference between Brad Lander and Rep. Dan Goldman.³

Abdul El-Sayed⁴ and Melat Kiros have both used Israel as an effective wedge issue in their primaries. A large part of Graham Platner’s outsider brand was his harsh criticism of the Israel lobby: He called Sen. Susan Collins “bought and paid for by Benjamin Netanyahu” and Sen. John Fetterman “a stooge for AIPAC.”

If you look at what candidates talk about in their fundraising emails, you can see that the insurgent left-wing candidates talk about Israel and the pro-Israel lobby a lot, while candidates running in swing seats don’t talk about it at all.

Insurgent Democrats run on Israel and AIPAC; frontliners never talk about them
Share of each candidate's fundraising emails mentioning Israel, Palestine, Gaza, or the pro-Israel lobby (AIPAC/DMFI), from January 2025 through July 2026.

This goes deeper than a few primary races. Consider the Promise to America (PTA), the centrist pledge that was unveiled at this year’s WelcomeFest. There’s nothing about Israel in this very short document.

But the online left’s reaction has been to insinuate that if PTA signatories have pro-Israel positions because of donor influence, then their positions on a range of other issues might be suspect too.

This line of attack is, on one level, sort of silly. The economic plank of the pledge doesn’t really say anything of substance.⁵ The reason that Democrats running in frontline House districts are eager to signal that they are more moderate than leftists running in safe New York seats is not because of corporate donors; it’s because they want to win their races.

But, on another level, it’s a serious problem for the party’s moderate wing. As the data in this piece has shown, Democratic voters — especially the types who turn out in primaries — have very negative views about Israel. To the extent that one faction of the party becomes associated with pro-Israel politics, it will have an increasingly difficult time winning intraparty fights.
