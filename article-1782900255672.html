Women aren't born wanting to earn less money

The myth of women's "preferences"

Do women choose to earn less money? There’s a persistent effort to naturalize the gender wage gap; that is, to show that women earn less than men not because of discrimination or bad policy design but simply because they choose to.

A 2022 paper titled “Why Do Women Earn Less than Men? Evidence from Bus and Train Operators” began with the premise that, in the United States, the gender pay gap is around 18%. The economists zoomed in on the Massachusetts Bay Transportation Authority, or MBTA, which runs transit in the Boston metropolitan area, to illuminate the drivers of the gender pay gap.

Here they found a much lower gap: around 11%. Since these are the same workplaces (so career choice does not matter), all workers have similar minimal training (so there are no education decisions), they do the same work, and all workers are unionized (so a lot of discretionary pay decisions cannot legally happen), why is there still a wage gap?

The main innovation of the paper, like most contemporary economics research, is in the data: MBTA granted the authors confidential administrative data used by their HR department, merging time card data (that is, when someone starts and stops working) with particular worker characteristics (age, gender, prior experience, marital status, etc.). So the paper is more an accomplishment in quilting than in economic theory.

That way, considering MBTA has very few discretionary decisions on worker pay and promotions, the authors can isolate a very small source of variability: scheduled and unscheduled overtime and paid and unpaid time off (family leave, known as FMLA for the Family and Medical Leave Act). The authors found that the 11% gap in earnings between male and female bus and train operators comes from the fact that women take less unscheduled overtime and take more unpaid time off.

Regardless of seniority, men take about twice as much overtime as women when it is scheduled on short notice, and women take about twice as much unpaid family leave. Given that the number of hours worked is one of the very few decisions workers have over their earnings at MBTA, this explains basically all of the gap away.

The authors present four possible explanations:

The first is the value of time: Women just like spending less time at work than men.

The second is schedule predictability: Women don’t like time off more than men, but can’t take time off at short notice.

The third is schedule conventionality (preferring a 9-to-5 more strongly), which is just the value of time with extra steps.

The fourth is a stronger response by men than women to undesirable schedules, which, again, is just the value of time.

So, really, there are two explanations: Women just like spending time at work less or that women can’t take short-notice overtime.

The biggest gap in short-notice overtime is between unmarried women and men with children: Men take overtime 40% of the time, and women 34%. However, this happens because the gap in pre-planned overtime is very small (around 7%), while the gap in short-notice overtime is around 45%, and closer to 60% for unmarried men and women with dependents.

At MBTA, men recover the pay they lose to family leave by working extra hours, but women don’t, and that’s most pronounced for unmarried mothers. That’s sometimes taken as evidence that women just value time off more.

But when MBTA tightened its leave and overtime rules to stop people from using leave to skip the least desirable shifts, the pay gap fell to about 6%. That’s probably the most you could honestly blame on free individual choice — roughly half of MBTA’s gap, which is itself about half of the U.S. average. And, of course, people do not make choices in a vacuum.

The right-wing Mises Institute argued that the wage gap is mainly about women’s choices. In another Mises paper about the gender wage gap, the author argued that “female preferences for caregiving and stability align with evolutionary roles in child-rearing, implying that gender roles are deeply rooted in biology rather than social constructs.” It attributed much of the wage gap to choices “shaped by hormonal and biological influences.”

An opinion column on FoxNews.com argued that “women are doing just fine” and “by most measures, it’s our boys and men who are struggling.” The author claimed that “to the extent that the gap exists, it can be largely attributed to deliberate and meaningful choices made by women in the workforce that prioritize home and family over career.”

This is a common refrain among the right: That our choices can be structured by our institutional and cultural environment — which conservatives usually understand well — suddenly reads like liberal gobbledygook when applied to the question of what shapes women’s preferences.

So tell me what you want, what you really, really want

The MBTA paper revealed a small wage gap that corresponds to “preferences,” whatever that means. But that only explains about half of a gap at MBTA, which itself is merely half of the U.S. average (which is already low by global standards).

The data is very limited when it comes to factors like elderly parents or other infirm relatives, obligations of spouses for married people, ages of children, or area of residence. But it’s a bit silly to say that, even without those factors, “women are just choosing to be paid less” based on this paper. Not only did it find a gap that’s around a third of the U.S. average, it did so in a work setting that is institutionally unique by any standards.

Let’s take a step back.

How representative is the 11% gap of MBTA (before scheduling policy changes that reduced it to 6%)? Well, according to the Economic Policy Institute, in 2024, the average pay gap was 18%. Workers in the 10th percentile of the income distribution saw about a 9% pay gap, whereas those in the 90th percentile had a gap of roughly 19%.

Women who had at most a high school diploma made around 20% less than men in 2024; for women with at least a college degree, the difference was 24%. The gender wage gap has decreased moderately over the last decade, with the biggest decreases happening between the 1980s and the 2000s.

This suggests the MBTA example is particularly uninformative because blue collar, highly structured workplaces are just very rare: On average, blue collar occupations have smaller, not larger, pay gaps than white collar ones, as do workers who have lower levels of formal education.

Unionization is another big difference: Only around 10% of American workers are members of a labor union, and unionization is an important driver of a lower gender pay gap. Research in Norway showed that differences in earnings shrink as more employees join the union. EPI estimates that women represented by unions earn 94 cents for every dollar earned by unionized men, compared with 78 cents among nonunion workers.

Here we have a major narrative violation: The gender wage gap is primarily a problem for educated, wealthy women, such that the MBTA study is pretty uninformative in explaining their decisions.

Are girlbosses choosing to earn less cash?

To really answer “Do women actually choose to make less money?” we have to ask ourselves how, exactly, we measure the wage gap — which is the same as asking how women make career decisions in the first place.

The first step is to differentiate between the parts that are “explained” (say, by factors including education, hours worked, field of employment, and age) and “unexplained” (that cannot be directly measured — think discrimination or, well, preferences).

The “unexplained” portion of the wage gap was responsible for between 21% and 29% of the difference between men’s and women’s wages in the 1980s, and it has flattened out at between 8% and 18%. It’s worth noting that just like the unexplained share of the gender pay gap is smaller, the gender pay gap is slightly lower as well, meaning that “unexplained” factors have a lower absolute contribution than in the 1980s or other times past.

This is where the question of “innate differences” comes into play, as does discrimination: There is not much evidence for the former and limited evidence for the latter, but I would say that there is a very small share that consists of women simply preferring to earn less.

In contrast, the explained part, which makes up the vast majority of the gap, is usually attributed to “career choices”: women simply choosing fields that pay less.

This is partially true: A (very good) 2024 paper using evidence from 47 countries finds that women place relatively more weight on meaning, social impact, and flexibility when evaluating work. However, income was rated important by large and similar shares of both men and women. The paper also looked at a sample of MBAs and found that a near-majority of men were motivated primarily by earnings, compared with around one-third of women, while 35% of women were primarily motivated by “impact,” compared with one-fifth of men.

Gender differences in choice of major account for a substantial, but not overwhelming, part of the gap in postgraduation earnings.

However, just because something is a choice does not mean it is completely voluntary. LGBT people, for instance, tend to stick to certain professions in order to avoid discrimination; in addition, being on the receiving end of discriminatory behavior can raise awareness of social dynamics that is applicable in the workplace, perversely developing certain “skills” after being bullied or harassed in childhood.

“Gay and lesbian workers probably developed some of these skills as a result of social adaptation to discrimination. As societies become more tolerant of same-sex relationships, however, the need to conceal one’s sexual orientation in everyday social interactions might fade over time. As a result, gay men and lesbians may lose their skill for social perceptiveness, which may make them less distinct in the labor market,” researchers wrote.

In the same way, social norms play a substantial role in women’s career choices. Women tend to leave male-dominated workplaces at substantially higher rates than men do. And, in one qualitative study of San Francisco tech workers, white and Asian LGBT women who didn’t adhere to “feminine” stereotypes were perceived as more competent by their male colleagues compared with other women.

The influence of gender norms on even highly educated women is notable: Female MBA students tend to downplay their career ambition in the presence of men they think are single, an effect not observable in any other gender and relationship status subset. And single women are also less assertive during class discussions.

But another factor plays a significant role: flexible schedules. Women seem to value choosing their own hours about as much as making a difference.

That is why professions like pharmacist are now female-dominated. Women’s entry into the profession happened recently because of changes to the structure of the profession. This reduced the role of small practitioner-owned pharmacies that required long work hours.

Similarly, a 2003 policy capped the number of hours medical residents could work (to prevent physician exhaustion; good news for the cast of The Pitt). The number of women entering a given specialty rose proportionally to the reduction in hours, an increase driven by more interest among female physicians and not by more interest in hiring women.

Call your mother

Why do women consistently prioritize working predictable, flexible schedules? At the start of their careers, MBA students had basically no wage gap. Ten to 16 years later, a large gap between women and men emerged.

Even accounting for prior educational choices, the bulk of the pay gap comes down to differences in career interruptions and differences in hours worked (just like what was going on with MBTA). Women are more likely to spend six months or more away from work and less likely to work overtime.

This comes almost entirely from women with children: In the first 15 years after receiving an MBA, they have accumulated an average of eight months less post-MBA experience than the average man of the same graduating year, often due to time off for FMLA. They also work 24% fewer hours than men.

Women without children, meanwhile, have 1.5 months less post-MBA experience on average in the first 15 years and work only 3% fewer hours. This allows them to work jobs that are “family unfriendly” (or “greedy”), that is, demand very long hours.

This points out some major factors complicating the “preferences” theory: For instance, take the gender pay gap itself. The vast majority of people are aware that it exists. They are also aware of how much their spouse earns. Ipso facto, a woman will build her career around the world as it actually exists and not a kumbaya fantasyland where money doesn’t matter.

If someone needs to work 20% less, and one of two people in a marriage makes 20% less than the other, the math is very simple, preferences be damned. Moreover, research from Sweden and Germany shows that when couples are faced with a choice between moving for a man’s career or staying for the woman’s, they typically choose the man’s career, in some cases even if the woman makes more money.

Married couples are also likely to fudge the survey in their tax returns to show that the man is the breadwinner even if the wife objectively earns the most. And couples in which the wife out-earns the husband are associated with roughly a 50% higher likelihood of separating or divorcing.

Partnered gay men make marginally less money than straight men individually, while lesbians make slightly more money than straight women.

A perfect example of how this impacts parenthood is purely geographic: commutes. Commuting longer to work means, implicitly, accepting less of a role in certain childcare options. Straight men have much longer commutes than straight women; lesbians work farther away from home than straight women, and gay men have shorter commutes than straight men.

Moms reduce their hours the most (both MBAs and women in MBTA, I know it’s starting to get confusing).

Some of this may be preferences after all; women (and men) both spend and want to spend more time with their children than ever before. But if it were just a decision to spend time with kids, we would see a penalty to both mothers and fathers, and we don’t: Fathers make more money after having children, while mothers make less.

The motherhood penalty flattens significantly as children grow up because they need less supervision (a 13-year-old can stay home on their own, and a 16-year-old can make their own dinner). But the fatherhood premium compounds: More hours at the office means a better relationship with coworkers or clients, for example.

After having kids, women’s participation in the workforce (both in terms of working at all and in terms of how many hours they work) takes a downward plunge, a pattern that is seen in other countries as well. In fact, the expectation of future domestic labor is strong enough that traditional norm women sometimes game out their entire career in expectation of taking the smallest earnings hit possible from having to take time off and no overtime.

Fixing the wage gap

One of the major problems in discourse about gender and “choice” is that there isn’t a clear distinction between norms and preferences. Women who grew up right inside East Berlin (in the more gender egalitarian German Democratic Republic, its other merits notwithstanding) have a smaller motherhood penalty than women who grew up right inside the west side; women whose mothers-in-law worked during World War II have higher labor force participation rates, as do women whose fathers had female coworkers during World War I; the same is true for women whose childhood friends’ mothers had jobs, and for women who have sisters instead of brothers, or whose neighborhood friends have uncles instead of aunts.

These results tend to carry between generations, particularly from mothers to sons. The fact that these are all basically random calls into question the notion that there’s a deeper, inner truth among women causing them to care less about earning money.

Policymakers cannot control the gender and employment status of a mother’s friend’s cousin’s roommate during World War I; they can, however, ensure a level institutional context for parents to make career decisions.

Family policies like parental leave and childcare have yielded extremely limited results. Policymakers should spend more effort on enhancing and improving education outcomes (in both high school and college), broadening access to public services, notably childcare and early education, to allow families basic convenience when planning their finances and careers, and encouraging flexible work.

Women who were more likely to work remotely during the pandemic were more likely to continue working after having children, and suffered a less severe motherhood penalty.
