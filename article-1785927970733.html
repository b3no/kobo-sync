America's endless home makeover

How renovation is eating the housing market

Joseph Politano
Aug 5
∙

∙
Guest post
 
READ IN APP
 

Compared with 20th-century norms, the U.S. is building astonishingly few homes, even as its population continues to grow. The result is a persistent housing shortage and record prices.

U.S. homebuilding per capita
Adjusted for population growth, U.S. homebuilding is well below historic norms

The country has only built 44 new homes per 1,000 people across the last decade, down from 57 in the 2000s and 63 in the 1990s. In fact, the U.S. currently builds at a more sluggish pace than it did even during the recessions of the 1970s and 1980s. Thanks to this persistent failure of supply to meet demand, more and more Americans are now convinced they will never be able to buy a home.

Decades of underbuilding have left the U.S. with an aging housing stock that is only poised to get grayer: Today, the average American home is 37 years old, a record high. That number has shot up from less than 30 years old in just two decades.

Share

In a healthy housing market, the age of the housing stock would be kept down by a steady supply of new construction to meet the needs of new families, a growing population, and rising incomes. Stringent land-use restrictions prevent much of this new construction from happening, particularly in the metro areas where demand is the highest.

America's aging housing stock
America's housing stock is the oldest it's ever been and still getting older

Yet Americans’ growing housing needs don’t vanish just because homebuilders are turned away. In 2025, Americans spent more money on home improvements and renovations than on building new single-family homes — a historical first, excepting a brief period during the Great Recession. Total renovation spending now exceeds $430 billion per year, an increase of 36% in inflation-adjusted dollars over just the past decade.

Real U.S. residential investment by type
U.S. investment in renovating existing homes is near record highs

Higher housing costs mean lower mobility. That pushes homeowners to renovate rather than move into more appropriate new homes when they experience major life events like getting married, having kids, or becoming empty nesters. Restrictive permitting processes make replacing many old homes a nonstarter, so an entire cottage industry has erupted around renovating and flipping existing homes to meet the needs of new buyers.

Thanks to dynamics like these, what initially looks like American homeowners’ insatiable appetite for renovation may actually be a structural shift in construction activity that is directly caused by — or is perhaps a kind of relief valve for — the country’s chronic housing shortage.

If that’s the case — that a large share of housing investment has been redirected from building new homes to fixing old ones — then we may already be doing more to address the housing shortage than pure homebuilding and permitting data indicate. After all, converting a two-bedroom home to a three-bedroom does expand the housing supply (though it doesn’t directly address the problem of depressed household formation). And the booming business of energy-efficiency upgrades, while not increasing housing supply, can nevertheless make a big dent in the broader cost-of-living crisis.

Yet the housing market’s increasing dependence on renovations has inherent drawbacks:

  • Constantly reworking around ancient frames can be more expensive than wholesale replacement of old housing stock.

  • Renovation simply cannot provide the same type of opportunities to build safer, larger, more efficient, and better-designed buildings that new developments can.

  • Depressed construction limits the kinds of households people can form at different stages of life: Relying on old housing stock forces young adults, for instance, to take on roommates in multi-bedroom homes for families rather than allowing them access to the new one-bedroom apartments they would often prefer.

More fundamentally, America’s overreliance on renovations is reinforcing generational and wealth inequality in the housing market. Older, high-income homeowners are the most able to renovate their houses — such endeavors are typically cash-funded — while middle- and working-class Americans are left with more limited options.

Those same legacy homeowners get to sit on their home equity for decades, safe in the knowledge that any new family rich enough to become a homeowner will have to buy their home, rather than build something new for themselves. And they are increasingly bending the U.S. construction market to suit their needs, pulling workers and expertise away from building new homes and funneling them into more kitchen redesigns.

In short, we cannot renovate our way to affordability. Restoring a healthy housing market requires a world in which America gets back to building new homes and stops needing to constantly renovate old ones.

Renovation nation

So what kind of renovations are Americans doing? The single largest category is interior renovations, especially upgrades or replacements of HVAC systems. Exterior renovations — dominated by $47 billion in roofing expenditures — come in a close second. Those two categories reveal how the lion’s share of home renovations is merely intended to bring home infrastructure up to date. More than 25% of homeowners undertaking home renovations made efficiency upgrades.

U.S. annual home renovation spending by category
One cost of aging housing stock

Kitchen and bathroom remodels are the next major categories; $42 billion is spent on the former every year and $34 billion on the latter. Next come yard renovations, where spending is split rather evenly among driveways, fences, pools, sheds, and sprinklers.

The end result is that relatively few renovations involve directly expanding a home’s size: Room additions or expansions total just $25 billion annually, a figure only marginally larger than the amount spent on disaster repairs.

Renovations are primarily funded by cash. A bit under two-thirds of total renovation spending comes from personal savings, while less than one-fifth is debt-funded. (The remainder is funded mostly from home insurance settlements.) That means renovations are primarily the territory of older, richer Americans.

Today, more than half of home renovation spending is done by owners older than 55. Just under a third of renovation spending is done by the 15% of households with incomes above $200,000 a year, and a full half of spending comes from the 29% of households making more than $140,000. This divide has only become more stark since 2008, when renovation spending was still tilted toward cash spending from the rich, but debt-financed spending among the middle class was significantly more common.

All of this spending on home renovations has reshaped America’s construction labor market. For the first time ever, more people are employed by remodeling companies than by single-family and multifamily developers combined. More than 150,000 residential remodeling jobs have been added over the last decade, inching up to a record high in early 2026 even as hiring in new single- and multifamily construction slowly declined.

Residential construction employment
A record number of people are now working for professional remodelers

Of course, many construction workers are not employed directly by homebuilders or remodelers, but rather by contractors who focus on subcomponents like roofs or electrical connections for both new and old homes. Those contractors tend to specialize in the work most common for home renovations — especially electrical, HVAC, and plumbing. Indeed, renovations are a large part of the reason why total residential construction employment remains near its record high despite homebuilding rates that have been depressed since the 2008 housing crisis.

Residential specialty contractor employment
The types of contractors most common in remodels, especially equipment upgrades, are gaining jobs

Escaping the renovation doom loop

Overall, a thriving renovation sector has not come anywhere close to fully offsetting a national decline in total construction activity. Total residential construction is still a smaller share of the U.S. economy than it was at almost any point in modern American history before 2008. The situation is growing worse; relative spending has been falling for the past five years. Any increase in home renovation activity is at most a Band-Aid rather than a structural solution to America’s housing shortage.

Residential investment as a percentage of GDP
Despite the growth of improvements, residential investment as a percent of GDP remains low

There is no shortcut: Ending the housing shortage will require more homes to be built. In cities like New York or San Francisco, no amount of renovation can compensate for the fact that there are too few homes available for all the people who wish to live there. And exclusionary zoning laws prevent the construction of any significant amount of new housing.

In the short term, the renovation boom looks here to stay. But the longer America stays in this paradigm, the more difficult it will be to escape. A shift of hundreds of thousands of jobs from homebuilders to remodelers suggests we may be losing the skills and institutional capacity necessary for a building boom. Now is the time to change course.
