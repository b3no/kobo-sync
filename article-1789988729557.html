The scumbag style in American politics

Bad people make bad allies

Jeremiah Johnson
Sep 21
∙
Paid
 
READ IN APP
 

Earlier this month, the Effective Altruist (EA) funding platform Manifund made a shocking announcement: It had hired convicted fraudster Caroline Ellison.

Ellison is the former CEO of Alameda Research, a sister company of the disgraced crypto firm FTX, which was founded by Sam Bankman-Fried. Reporting suggests that she was fully aware of the fraud that precipitated the companies’ multibillion-dollar implosion while it was happening, and the FBI called her a willing participant in the scheme. Ultimately, Ellison pleaded guilty to two counts of wire fraud, two counts of conspiracy to commit wire fraud, conspiracy to commit commodities fraud, conspiracy to commit securities fraud, and conspiracy to commit money laundering and was sentenced to two years in prison.

Nevertheless, Manifund cheerily announced Ellison’s hiring, with CEO Austin Chen saying that he “believe[s]in redemption.”¹ And the company didn’t hire her for just any role, but one where she will be involved in researching how philanthropic money should be allocated. Making matters worse, Manifund was launched with funding from FTX’s charitable arm and has refused to return all of the money. Making matters even worse, Chen explicitly noted the “keen debt” he feels toward the FTX Future Fund for said funding, which gives this whole episode the appearance of a quid pro quo. The move set off a firestorm of controversy in EA circles, with the decision being savaged on the EA forum and in Manifund’s own comment section.

In seemingly unrelated news, Hunter Biden recently released a memecoin, which immediately crashed and lost nearly 99% of its value from its launch-day peak. One mystery trader reportedly made off with more than $900,000 in profit, while basically every other dope who put money into the coin lost their money. Why was Hunter able to pull this move? Because of his newfound popularity among some Democrats online who like the way he insults Republicans on social media.

These stories share a common thread. Both controversies are happening because we’ve lost sight of a very important, very basic principle: You shouldn’t associate with scumbags.

What goes around scums around

Both of the above stories center on a person who has demonstrated low moral character. And in both cases, third parties got involved with the scumbag despite clear foreknowledge that said person was, in fact, a scumbag.

Hunter Biden is a drug addict, philanderer, tax cheat, and failson who openly sold access to his famous father. All of this was public knowledge before some in the media decided it would be a good idea to promote his edgy new persona. Caroline Ellison’s financial crimes were extensively covered in the mainstream media — and admitted in court — yet Manifund hired her anyway.

Why do people make these choices? Sometimes it’s because they’re ideologically aligned with the scumbags in question (Ellison is, nominally at least, an Effective Altruist) or because they share a common enemy (as with Hunter and his libbed out fandom). Often, they’ll perceive some short-term benefits from cozying up to bad actors.

But getting friendly with sleazeballs is a mistake. Beyond the moral stain, it’s also just a bad strategy. Even if it seems like a good idea in the moment, you’ll live to regret it because aligning with scumbags will come back to bite you in the ass. And while that may not be a huge deal when you’re talking about memecoins and podcasts, it’s a very big deal in politics.

Unfortunately, contemporary politics appears to be losing any effective resistance to scumbags. There are many reasons for this development: Increased partisan polarization has turned much of our politics into a pure friend vs. enemy battle; political parties are so weak now that they can no longer effectively police themselves; and our fragmented media system is no longer capable of shutting controversial voices out of the national conversation.

To get a sense of the consequences of tolerating this development, just look at examples such as Sen. John Fetterman and Graham Platner. Both flashed very obvious warning signs before they won their primaries. Well before his stroke, Fetterman was delinquent on his taxes. He had a history of no-showing his jobs as mayor of Braddock and lieutenant governor of Pennsylvania, and he practically kidnapped a Black jogger at gunpoint. Platner won his primary despite multiple scandals involving sordid Reddit comments, a Nazi tattoo, cheating on his wife, and high levels of senior staff turnover.

Based on what we already knew before Fetterman was elected, how could anyone be surprised that he’s one of the worst Democratic senators in generations? He shows little interest in the basic work of being a senator: He skips committee meetings, prioritizes Fox News interviews over constituents, and infamously has sometimes had to vote by ducking his head in from the cloakroom because he can’t be bothered to put on a suit and thus isn’t allowed on the Senate floor.

And it’s hardly shocking that Platner, already weighed down by numerous scandals, was later accused of sexual assault and forced to drop out. Did his backers really think that no larger scandal was lurking behind that many red flags? In both cases, the signs were there to be seen. But because they were perceived as ideological allies, many Democrats chose to ignore those signs.

There’s a common complaint I’ve heard from the left when I’ve made this argument before: Why are you holding Democrats to such a high standard? Donald Trump is the biggest scumbag in America! And even beyond Trump, Republicans get away with this stuff all the time. If they can do it, why can’t we?

First, bad behavior by one’s ideological opponent is not a good reason to act badly. But more importantly, scummy, immoral behavior does hurt Republicans. If anything, the scumbag problem is much worse in the GOP: Republicans are constantly losing elections they should win because they run scumbags.

  • Fetterman’s opponent in 2022 provides a good example. Mehmet Oz (aka “Dr. Oz”) won the Republican primary for Senate in Pennsylvania despite a history of promoting miracle cures that ended up being quack nonsense. He had also lived in New Jersey for more than 30 years and moved to Pennsylvania only months before his run. He was an obvious political opportunist who publicly changed his views on a wide variety of issues for political expediency. It’s no surprise Oz underperformed, losing to Fetterman by five points in a state Trump lost by just one in 2020.

  • Republicans knew Herschel Walker was allegedly a violent psychopath years before he won his 2022 Senate primary in Georgia. Then, after he won the primary, additional scandals surfaced, and he ended up losing to Raphael Warnock.

  • Roy Moore was a scandal-prone judge who had twice been removed from his job as chief justice of the Alabama Supreme Court for failing to uphold basic ethical norms and court orders. He had already been in hot water for collecting more than $1 million from a “charitable organization” he ran. After it was too late to replace him as the GOP’s Senate candidate for the state in 2017, Moore was credibly accused of pedophilia. He then lost a narrow general election contest to Democrat Doug Jones — the first Democrat to win a Senate seat in the state in 25 years.

  • Ken Paxton, the current GOP Senate nominee in Texas, has long been embroiled in scandals. He violated securities law, was indicted in a separate case of felony securities fraud, cheated on his wife, and was impeached by a Republican-controlled state house on 20 articles involving allegations of bribery, abuse of office, obstruction, and misuse of public resources. We’re still waiting to see whether Paxton will lose, but it’s because of his many scandals that the Split Ticket/The Argument election model has Democrat James Talarico as a 63% favorite to win in ruby-red Texas.²

The fact that Democrats managed to win a Senate race in Alabama is proof enough that scandals and scummy behavior do in fact hurt politicians of both parties.³ But even if they do manage to win their races, the scumbags in your tent are likely to be some of the least loyal, most problematic members of your coalition: John Fetterman now routinely boosts Republicans, and former Rep. Marjorie Taylor Greene has gone from one of Trump’s biggest boosters to one of his biggest critics. Scumbags care about themselves more than they care about you or your cause. They aren’t loyal and won’t hesitate for a second to stab you in the back.

Scum in orbit

“Don’t nominate scumbags” is fairly obvious advice, even if there are far too many political actors who ignore it. But the principle applies more broadly than that: Don’t hire obvious scumbags, don’t host them on your podcasts, don’t campaign with them, and don’t allow them inside your tent.

Genuine, demonstrable repentance and rehabilitation might, in some cases, permit someone who has behaved badly in the past to reassume a position of influence. For many of today’s political actors, however, that seems to be too much to ask. Over the last few weeks, Michigan Senate candidate Abdul El-Sayed has become more and more annoyed by how often interviewers are asking him about controversial livestreamer Hasan Piker. El-Sayed campaigned with Piker in April but now seems upset that people keep wanting to talk about it.

“I’m less interested in going to the tabloid version of our politics,” he said in Ann Arbor. “This campaign is not about a streamer in California,” he told MS Now’s Katy Tur. “With all due respect, this is the third time you’ve asked about a Twitch streamer in California,” he testily responded to CBS’s Weijia Jiang.

I don’t think he has a leg to stand on. Hasan Piker’s record of promoting violence, terrorism, and authoritarian regimes was common knowledge when El-Sayed decided to campaign with him. When you choose to associate with scumbags, you can’t be upset when people ask you about it. And predictably, Piker has kept himself in the news by declaring that America is worse than al Qaeda, “pound for pound.” When you hang out with scumbags, you can’t be surprised that they keep dragging you down!

None of this is rocket science. If someone has displayed bad judgment in the past, they’re likely to do so in the future. That principle is true for tax cheats, sex pests, supporters of extremism and violence, people with a poor work ethic, and those with nearly any sign of poor moral character. Subjectively, it feels like we used to be better at this as a society. We didn’t tolerate dishonorable scoundrels then nearly as much as we do now, and scandal-plagued politicians would often resign in shame rather than keep fighting.

Far too many people in politics today seem to ignore the wisdom that promoting scumbags is a poor idea. We are, as a nation, failing the very basic Scumbag Test. This has consequences far beyond losing any individual race. Voters, in the long run, can tell when you’ve sacrificed your principles for a short-term political benefit. And filling your movement with scumbags makes it a less effective political force. This is how you end up with Robert F. Kennedy Jr. overseeing measles outbreaks across the country or Pete Hegseth bungling a war with Iran.

This shouldn’t be hard: Just don’t associate yourself with scumbags. Don’t run them as candidates. Don’t promote them as public voices, don’t campaign with them, and don’t make them part of your team. If you do, you’ll almost certainly end up regretting it.
