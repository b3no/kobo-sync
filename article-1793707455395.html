Does anything we do in schools actually matter?

Mad Libs: Kelsey Piper vs. Freddie deBoer

Kelsey Piper and Freddie deBoer
Nov 3

Welcome to Mad Libs. This is an irregular debate column where our columnists, contributors, and staff writers (or even you, dear reader) can duke it out over the big ideas we’re discussing in the metaphorical pages of this magazine.

Today’s Mad Libs was inspired by The Argument staff writer Kelsey Piper’s education coverage, which has pointed out, again and again, that American schools are failing kids in ways we already know how to fix. Piper has argued that illiteracy is a policy choice, that Mississippi’s much-ballyhooed reading gains are real, and, most recently, that we have known since the 1960s that direct instruction works.

Freddie deBoer, a committed education pessimist, is a frequent critic of our coverage. He thinks education “miracles” have a long history of turning out to be frauds, that no reform has ever reliably moved kids from one spot in the academic distribution to another, and that American schools were never the world-beaters we imagine they were.

When deBoer first responded to Piper, he wondered whether a magazine premised on the idea that “liberal good vibes can overcome every inconvenient fact and complication” would ever engage with his criticism. So, I invited him to debate Piper directly. You can read their exchange below!

Freddie deBoer:

The Sold a Story podcast is only the latest vehicle advancing the notion that we could fix our schools if we only choose to.

Sold a Story is not the whole story

Kelsey Piper
·
Oct 27
Read full story

This contention has been a constant in American educational debates for decades, exemplified by the 2010 documentary Waiting for “Superman” and its triumphalist narrative about charter schools. Unfortunately, this attitude runs into a stark reality: Schools and teachers just don’t have that much impact on relative student outcomes.

I’m afraid that the evidence shows that student academic talent is more or less static. Nothing is more predictive of a student’s future success than their past success. In large majorities, individual students gravitate to a given level of academic performance at an early age and tend to stay there throughout their academic life, as this 2025 meta-analysis demonstrates.

There are many other studies we can pull from. As far back as the 1960s, pioneering large-scale federal studies (like Project Talent, the Coleman report, and Project Follow Through) supported the notion that most students stay in the same performance band over time. Data collected the summer after kindergarten provides useful predictive information about how students will perform in high school; a student’s third-grade reading level is an effective indicator of who will go to college and who won’t. Standardized tests administered at age 12 or 13 tell us not only which students will go to college but which will earn a doctorate, hold a patent, or get tenure. The basic reality is that our rank in the academic hierarchy is largely fixed.

Douglas Detterman has summarized the research record by saying “only about 10% of school achievement can be attributed to schools and teachers.”

Interventions that are often treated as transformative just don’t appear to matter. Promising early results inevitably fade out in educational research, and I use the word “inevitably” carefully.

Phonics-based reading instruction is currently undergoing a period of immense hype, but the advantages students see from phonics at age 5 are dramatically reduced by age 7 and nonexistent by age 11. For years, liberals championed pre-K schooling as a magic bullet, but study after study shows little or no positive effects as children age.

Past hype is no predictor of future performance. Class size reductions were ballyhooed, then over time their effects were revealed to be inconsistent at best. Many high-quality studies have shown that coveted “exam” high schools in large cities make no difference for student outcomes.

The result is a very bare cupboard when it comes to finding educational interventions that “work.” As Rimfeld et al. put it in a 2018 paper, “Individual differences in educational achievement are highly stable across the years of compulsory schooling from primary through secondary school. Children who do well at the beginning of primary school also tend to do well at the end of compulsory education for much the same reasons.”

You can change the school or type of school, and you can change the teacher. But what stays the same is the student. None of this depends on any wild theories or contrarian reading of the available data; perfectly mainstream educational research has provided evidence for these conclusions for decades.

Consider Harvard economist Raj Chetty. He’s been immensely influential among reformers, in large measure because his work gives people the optimistic read on schooling that they’re looking for — a better teacher leads to lifelong higher earnings for students.

I’ve written about the fundamental methodological problems with Chetty’s work in the past. But setting aside those issues, I think it’s essential to understand: Chetty’s work implies that teachers control very little of the variance in student outcomes!

Taking Chetty et al. at face value, a much better teacher (a full standard deviation better) raises a student’s test scores by about one-tenth of a standard deviation, which implies that the difference between teachers explains just 1% to 2% of the variation in that year’s scores.

Chetty’s work is known for its use of future student earnings as a dependent variable. It suggests that, by age 20, a student with a one-standard-deviation-better teacher is less than 1 percentage point more likely to attend college. By age 28, they earn only 1.3% more.

This is… educational optimism?

We could play the same game with CREDO, the influential Stanford University-based research project. CREDO has long been cited as the gold standard when it comes to making the evidence-based case for charter schools; politicians, think tanks, and commentators regularly refer to CREDO’s research to back up the notion that the average charter school is significantly better than the average public school.

Setting aside very real methodological concerns, once we translate from researchers’ absurd, invented “days of learning” metric, we find that charter schools make hardly any difference at all. Again, we don’t have to dispute their numbers; we simply need to express them in the terms that are used in almost all credible education research.

And that’s from a research entity that was hosted by a conservative think tank and funded by pro-charter money! This is a funny basis for arguing the importance of schools and teachers in student outcomes.

This is what frustrates me about the educational debate in our media: There’s no acknowledgment of the profound limitations of what schools can do. We insist on having a conversation that takes as given that schools and teachers have a major impact on student outcomes — an assumption the data just doesn’t support.

Kelsey Piper:

Education indeed doesn’t affect relative student outcomes much. I don’t really care.

I care about absolute student outcomes.

A society where everybody can read and 10% of students are extremely well-read is vastly preferable, in my view, to one where only the top 10% in innate ability learn to read at all. A society where everyone understands mathematics well enough to make informed decisions about taking out a loan — or switching jobs from an hourly to a salaried role or buying insurance — is vastly preferable to a society where only 5% of people understand these things. This is preferable even if the rank ordering of everybody by mathematical fluency is exactly the same.

So the only part of your argument I am interested in is the discussion of whether absolute student outcomes change if we do a good job of educating students. Several of the studies you cite still create challenges for this view. If we can improve outcomes in absolute terms, shouldn’t that be expected to improve literacy 10 years down the line? Shouldn’t it be expected to improve employment and income as an adult?

Indeed, it’s very hard to measure any long-term life trajectory effects from education (or parenting, for that matter). But I think this is mostly just a feature of how measurement works and not grounds to adopt a sort of secular Calvinism. Almost all improvements look trivial if you zoom out enough and look at a sufficiently distant downstream variable.

Take, say, measles vaccination: My best estimate is that going from no vaccination to full elimination only improved average U.S. life expectancy by 20 days!¹

Imagine a world where we couldn’t tell what killed people when they died and still had to figure out how public health works. Economists would be struggling with extremely large-scale, high-powered difference-in-differences studies to discern whether measles vaccination did anything at all. You would probably find a null for polio (five days) or whooping cough (seven days).²

It is simply very hard to find large downstream effects even for interventions that we agree are definitely valuable. You need very large sample sizes and good methodological approaches to do it.

On the whole, of course, 20th-century innovation in preventive medicine radically reshaped the entire human condition, with 20 years in overall life expectancy gains in less than a century. If you were taking a broader view, it would be easy to detect that some countries have way fewer deaths from infectious disease than other countries — and that all countries have way fewer deaths from infectious disease than they did in the past.

Education is harder to study than public health. In both fields, the benefits of marginal interventions are way smaller than the benefits of all interventions (and are often zero). Nonetheless I think that “education doesn’t do anything” is about as reasonable as “public health doesn’t do anything.”

It is easy to detect short-run effects from good interventions: If you teach students to read, they will be better at reading. If you teach them math, they’ll know more math.

It is much harder to detect long-term effects like “higher adult income” or “higher adult employment.” You would expect this difficulty even if phonics is as good for income as the measles vaccine is for health; dozens of different factors contribute to those outcomes, and even an intervention that is decisively worthwhile will be hard to detect downstream.

But we are not in a state of complete hopeless ignorance. Just like you can get the overall picture of the importance of preventive medicine from the fact that U.S. infant mortality fell from 10% to 0.6% in the last century, you can notice that American adult illiteracy fell from nearly 11% in 1900 to 1% by 1969. And you can notice that some states seem to be doing a way better job than other states.

This might seem like I’m belaboring a point that’s not controversial, but I can never quite tell whether you understand yourself to be saying, “Within the range of education interventions being contemplated in current American schools, effect sizes are pretty small, and even if you work really hard you’ll only get moderate gains,” (I agree) or, “Education simply doesn’t affect life outcomes at all and if we just got rid of schools entirely, life outcomes would stay basically the same,” (I emphatically disagree).

In 2024 in Mississippi, 53% of black eighth-graders read at an NAEP “basic” level or above. (Their fourth-grade numbers are much more impressive, but you raised concern about fade-out and reversion to the mean, so let’s look at the eighth-grade numbers.) In Wisconsin, it’s 37%.

The effect of any individual specific intervention might be quite small. But, somehow, 16 out of every 100 Black students don’t learn to read in Wisconsin but would if they lived in Mississippi. The Mississippi kids are poorer than the Wisconsin kids, so it isn’t that. If you think it cannot possibly be better classroom instruction, what do you think explains it?

Freddie deBoer:

I’m a big believer in education for its own sake, so I’m amenable to looking beyond relative performance. But this is an attitude that’s conspicuously incompatible with basic school reform philosophy: If what you care about is getting more poor kids into Harvard and from there into the upper-middle class, you have to care about relative learning because you have to move such kids higher relative to the rest of the distribution. I respect your focus on absolute learning, but I think your enemy in that regard is not me but the school reformers themselves. It’s their basic definition of the purpose of reform that locks us into a relative framework.

Another distinction is too often missed in these debates: the difference between the value of teaching and the relative value of different teachers.

Consider, for example, this research out of New Zealand. Like much of the literature on reading, it finds that the test score advantages seen by students who receive structured reading instruction from ages 5 to 7 faded over time. Indeed, the advantage disappeared entirely by age 11.

This is classic educational fade-out in action. But what’s telling is that the comparison isn’t, as many would prefer, between students who got phonics instruction and those who got whole language instruction.³ No, this research compared students who received the instruction with students who did not receive formal reading instruction at all until two years later.

Now, this suits my priors about the inability of policy or pedagogy to meaningfully change relative student position. But set my obsessions aside for now. This research also tells us that teaching matters! Students who didn’t get reading instruction were badly behind those who did.

Teaching clearly has value; you can have the most naturally talented students in the world, and they’ll never learn algebra if no one ever attempts to teach them. What this result does not tell us — and what, I would argue, the available research writ large does not tell us — is that different individual teachers have a big advantage over other individual teachers.

This is a very important distinction. Our modern educational debates are fixated on the notion that the difference between one teacher and the next is deeply important for student outcomes, that our problems ultimately stem from too many low-quality teachers. I and a few other vagabonds have been protesting against this notion for some time, arguing that the data simply doesn’t support it.

Share

These days, even many dyed-in-the-wool “accountability” types will concede that merit pay and value-added models for evaluating teachers have been a disaster, frequently failing basic quantitative evaluations and providing administrators with incoherent results. (One infamous finding showed that teachers who scored in the bottom 20% of value-added rankings one year were more likely to score in the top 40% of those rankings the next year than to be back on the bottom.) And questions of basic fairness and labor rights are paramount here, particularly given that we’ve consistently failed to make teaching an attractive-enough profession to prevent widespread attrition.

Perhaps one place where you and I could find common ground, then, is in advancing the notion that teaching certainly matters, and even that we should find out which methods appear to have better outcomes, however temporary, as has happened with phonics and whole language theory. But we have neither any evidence that any one teacher is particularly better than any other nor any way to consistently and fairly tell the difference.

Kelsey Piper:

I am surprised, but delighted, to learn that we agree that teaching matters, that students who receive an education are ahead of those who don’t, and that absolute performance can improve.

I agree that there’s a contingent of education reformers who disregard any absolute performance improvement if it doesn’t close the gap between disadvantaged and advantaged students. But I think that the grouchy fatalism you sometimes bring to education debates is the wrong antidote to this.

My reply to the “achievement gap” crowd is something like: “Look, I’d love to see struggling students become top students, but I haven’t seen a single intervention do it. If you do a better job of teaching, both the bottom of the class and the top of the class benefit. That’s still good! So we should do it!”

When I say this, most people agree!

Whereas, when you say “put more simply and sadly, nothing in education works,” you run the risk people will believe you. And if they do believe you, Freddie, they might cease to invest in ensuring we educate everybody.

Nonetheless, I think our differences here are primarily rhetorical. It’s true that no one has found a way to make the weakest students into the strongest students — just to make them literate, numerate, and equipped to navigate the world. That’s still worth it.

Common ground on teacher performance might be harder to find. Just like “it’s hard to tell whether educational interventions matter,” the statement “we have no evidence that any one teacher is particularly better than any other” only seems true to me if you zoom out a lot.

I had a math teacher in high school who was told to teach calculus but didn’t know calculus; he found the smartest boy in the class and told him to teach the rest of us. This worked reasonably well because the smartest boy in the class was really good at math. The following year, I had a fantastic math teacher with, if I recall correctly, a mathematics Ph.D., who delivered compelling, well-designed lectures every day.

It would be a little hard to scientifically measure which was the better teacher, both because students compensated for the bad one and because the sample sizes are inherently pretty small. But that’s very different from claiming that there’s no real way to say who was the better teacher.

It’s very challenging for any organization to set incentives for performance in a way that doesn’t reward gaming those metrics, but we shouldn’t confuse that with all teachers being approximately equally good.

If all teachers were about equally good, it wouldn’t be worthwhile to try to hire, train, and retain good teachers. If some teachers are much better than others, but it’s hard to identify them at scale, then it is worthwhile but challenging. Those are different worlds, and I think we live in the second.

Ultimately, when I look at education, I see a situation where teacher quality, instruction strategies, and good curricula do matter even when they are not easy to measure — and even when no single intervention alone will change a student’s life trajectory.
