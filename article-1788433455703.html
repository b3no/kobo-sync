Stop making up reasons to be mad at your adversaries

A harmless old A.E.S. tweet, a paranoid anti-A.E.S. conspiracy theory, and the case for a mind of winter

Abdul El-Sayed is in hot water in certain corners of the internet over a tweet from five years ago in which he said that on 9/11 itself he would mourn the victims of the terrorist attack but that the next day he would mourn the tragic ways in which the American response went awry.

My sincere advice to anyone considering a run for elected office at any point in their life would be to never, under any circumstances, try to say anything thoughtful or provocative around a national day of mourning.

That said, as someone in the line of work where you are supposed to be thoughtful and provocative, this statement seems completely reasonable.

The view that the American military response to 9/11 ended up being unproductive is very widely held. Many innocent people died as a result of the wars in Afghanistan and Iraq. An enormous amount of money was spent, money which could’ve been used in many other, better ways. Lots of American soldiers died or were injured. I used to be on the same workout schedule as a guy who lost his left foot to an I.E.D. in Iraq. I respect his service and the service of others I know who fought in those wars, but unlike my grandfather who fought the Nazis, I can’t really say that their sacrifices were worth it.

It’s a pretty fucked up situation, and El-Sayed is right about that.

Unless, of course, he’s actually a hardcore anti-American Islamo-leftist who hates this country and is leaping at the opportunity to besmirch its name on a sacred day of mourning.

But is he?

There’s certainly no evidence within the text of that tweet that he is. It’s true that if you want to look at the most outré things Hasan Piker has ever said and then assert both that those things reflect Piker’s true feelings about the world and that El-Sayed’s association with Piker during the Michigan Senate primary campaign reflects his uncritical endorsement of that version of Piker’s worldview, you can read the tweet that way. But that’s a fully circular argument. If El-Sayed had never tweeted about 9/11, you could still be mad about the other stuff. Being mad about the tweet doesn’t add anything.

And he has specifically disavowed Piker’s remarks about 9/11. What’s more, El-Sayed supports Western aid to Ukraine — he is not part of the campist anti-American left, while Piker seems to be.

Not to do a whole take about 9/11, and I don’t want to get started on Piker, but this is an illustration of a problematic internet trend where you decide first that someone is your enemy, then read everything he says through the presumption that he’s a bad guy, and then treat this purely circular reasoning as if it’s new evidence of new wrongdoing.

I thought both El-Sayed and Haley Stevens had kind of bad takes on Israel and that all things considered, El-Sayed was a riskier electability choice who creates unnecessary complications for Democrats’ effort to take the Senate. But he is, in fact, the Democratic nominee, and there’s no need to make that into something bigger than it is.

A mind of winter

My favorite poem is Wallace Stevens’s “The Snow Man” from 1921.

Stevens is obsessed with the relationships between sense and perception. In another of his poems, the longer and less snappy “The Man With the Blue Guitar,” the titular man is challenged by an unknown audience who complain “You have a blue guitar / You do not play things as they are.” The guitarist retorts that “Things as they are / Are changed upon the blue guitar.”

“The Snow Man” is about the blue guitar of our minds.

It’s tempting to look upon the bleak emptiness of a snowy New England field in January and read it as a kind of metaphor. And it’s true that a writer setting a story against the backdrop of a bleak New England winter who took the time to dwell on this imagery would be making some kind of metaphorical or thematic point. The ice storm in the 1997 Ang Lee movie is a point about the weather, but it’s also symbolism.

Like the characters in the movie, Stevens is a guy who lived in Connecticut. And if you live in Connecticut, you sometimes get bleak January weather. That’s just a thing that happens; it’s genuinely what the weather is like. It doesn’t actually mean anything. The short daylight in the New England winter can make you feel sad. The decline in the pace of social activity can make you feel sad. But it’s not the case that God has chosen the weather to symbolize something or make you feel a certain way.

Stevens calls on us to reject the fog of metaphor and to “have a mind of winter,” which will allow us to see “nothing that is not there and the nothing that is.”

This is, of course, a terrible engagement strategy. If you want to gain clout and clicks on social media, you have to imbue everything and everyone your audience already dislikes with secret meaning and nefarious portent.

People who don’t like El-Sayed frequently do this, and it’s a big vibe in general on Islamophobic Twitter. But when you see a headline like “Hochul wears hijab at N.Y.C. Islamic center ahead of 9/11 anniversary, sparking backlash,” you have to ask yourself what kind of sense this makes. It’s totally normal to dress in line with religious norms when you’re visiting a place that’s not of your faith, and why shouldn’t she go there two weeks before the 9/11 commemoration?

The negative responses are clearly people who don’t like Kathy Hochul looking for stuff to get mad at. And stuff like this is totally endemic to contemporary internet culture.

Hypothetical anger

You see this on all sides, of course.

Abdul El-Sayed’s primary victory doesn’t change the fact that pro-Israel Democrats disagree with him about Israel, some of them quite strongly. This is categorically different from leftists who might be disappointed about being asked to support Mary Peltola, even though she’s moderate. Moderate as she may be, Peltola is still more progressive than Dan Sullivan. Pro-Israel Democrats in Michigan, on the other hand, are being asked to vote for El-Sayed, even if they’re closer to Mike Rogers’ views on the issue. It’s a real ask.

Still, Chuck Schumer and Gretchen Whitmer — who both pulled out all the stops for Haley Stevens — have endorsed El-Sayed. So did Elissa Slotkin. So did Stevens herself. That’s normal politics! But it’s become dogma on the left to seize on every bit of friction as evidence not of policy disagreements but of a secret plan to sabotage his election.

There is just zero evidence of this. In fact, while Schumer has endorsed El-Sayed, El-Sayed says he doesn’t support Schumer for majority leader. Under the circumstances, they are getting along pretty well!

David Sirota is making stuff up here, though.

Or, rather, based on his longstanding dislike of the bogeyman “corporate Democrats” he has decided that it’s clearly the case that in a purely hypothetical situation in which those Democrats could be assured of a Senate majority while El-Sayed loses, they would sabotage him. From that he infers that in the actual, non-hypothetical situation these unnamed corporate Dems are “willing to risk losing Congress to try to get what they want.”

I would say that in the completely non-hypothetical context of the Michigan primary, left-wing Democrats were willing to risk losing Congress by backing the weaker electability choice to try to get what they want. But they did get what they want, and while I think El-Sayed will almost certainly underperform the fundamentals, he’ll probably win. And everyone is endorsing him.

It’s not helpful for the pro-Israel faction to make up things about him beyond the scope of that disagreement, and it’s equally unhelpful for his fans to invent conspiracies.

Choosing to be angry

I think one of the real tells of this rhetorical style is its obsession with hypothetical scenarios. Earlier this week, a woman carrying knives stabbed multiple people in Times Square before being killed by police officers. Because crime is a lot lower in New York City than it used to be and because events in Times Square are inherently noteworthy, this was the subject of a high-profile press conference featuring the mayor and the police commissioner, during which the mayor commended the officers for their swift action in saving lives.

A number of people got upset not at what Zohran Mamdani said or did but at the idea that had this same set of facts played out at an earlier time in Mamdani’s career, he would have had a different reaction.

As a fan of counterfactual histories, I find this speculation plausible but … so what?

I’m glad that Mamdani has come closer to my views on these issues, and I’m glad that many of his supporters acknowledge the wisdom of his new approach, and I hope it brings us all toward a more sensible view of crime and law enforcement.

Now, again, if you just know deep in your heart that Mamdani is secretly burning with rage at the N.Y.P.D. and itching at the chance to go totally soft on crime, I guess you can find random splotches of data to “confirm” that.

But everything he’s actually saying and doing suggests that he understands the political imperative of low crime and that he broadly believes deference to the expert judgment of the existing set of police officials is a good way to secure that. And crime is, in fact, continuing to fall under this policy mix. Take the win!

Some of this is, obviously, opportunism:

  • If you don’t agree with Mamdani’s overall approach to being mayor, it’s objectively inconvenient for you that he’s done a good job of handling crime issues. So it might be better to just pretend he hasn’t.

  • If you want El-Sayed to lose, and you know his views on Israel are probably something the voters are reasonably comfortable with, it’s convenient to attribute to him a much farther-reaching anti-American view of foreign policy that he hasn’t actually articulated.

  • If you’re worried that El-Sayed running much weaker than Michigan gubernatorial candidate Jocelyn Benson debunks your larger thesis about the electorate’s hunger for leftist candidates, it’s convenient to explain this away with a conspiracy theory.

But I also think a lot of people are just addicted to a kind of internet rage.

Hyper-negative coverage of the Mamdani administration clearly sells copies of the New York Post. If your whole brand is selling people an anti-establishment insurgency, you can’t really say “the primary is over, we didn’t get all our guys but we got some of them and now it’s time to shift into cooperative mode.” Negativity does well on the internet, in general.

It would be boring for people who disagree with El-Sayed’s actual positions to just say that again every day; it’s more engaging to make stuff up and pretend this old 9/11 tweet said something it didn’t say. But beyond the immorality of the opportunism, there’s something soul-deadening and misery-inducing about behaving this way. Do you really want yourself and your audience to be sad and angry all the time? It’s a lot better to try to keep a calm head and not read maximum nefariousness into everything you see.
