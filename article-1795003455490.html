The Stephen Harper interview

Canada’s 22nd prime minister on his new book, getting and using power, and regrets

Paul Wells
Nov 18
 
READ IN APP
 

I admit a particular interest in the work of this week’s guest. I wrote two books about Stephen Harper. The second in particular took everything I had. But Harper is a cautious man, and while we usually chat briefly when our paths cross, I hadn’t interviewed him in many years. There’s an element of comparing notes in the conversation that resulted.

The occasion was the publication of Harper’s memoir, Here for Canada: a big book and, among memoirs from recent Canadian prime ministers, topped only by Brian Mulroney’s for length and detail. Nor is Harper’s book devoid of introspection, confession, and surprising revelation.

He wonders whether prime ministers should get out of the business of naming governors general. He decided early that he’d been wrong to support George W. Bush’s invasion of Iraq, but he also soured early on the Afghanistan war. It began as a righteous war but by the time he became PM in 2006, it just looked bloody and counterproductive. “I really spent eight years managing the exit,” he says. Harper is plainly still frustrated by his team’s execution of his final campaign, against Justin Trudeau in 2015. (Yes, he discusses the “barbaric cultural practices” hotline.) And there are glimpses of a private, more emotional Harper, at times overwhelmed by the job, unable to share the highs and lows of the day with colleagues because he was supposed to lead them.

As a man with a book to sell, Harper made it clear he didn’t want to talk about the headlines of 2026, which was mostly fine by me because I remain fascinated by the headlines of 1993 to 2015. But near the end of the interview, he admits he’s tickled to see a government pursuing so many of the priorities he pursued. That’s Mark Carney’s, in case you hadn’t guessed.
