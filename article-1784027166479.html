Trump hasn’t even tried to pass an immigration bill

You can’t change the Constitution with executive orders, but Trump might be able to destroy it.

Matthew Yglesias
Jul 14
∙
Paid

American immigration law is complicated, and almost every provision of this vast corpus reflects some political compromise reached long ago, often under different circumstances. I think it’s fair to say that almost none of it is optimal.

Lenin said of the bureaucracy, “better fewer, but better.” When it comes to immigration, I don’t see it that way. We should try to be more selective, but we should also want more immigrants to come to the United States than ever. So while I agree that many of the current visa programs aren’t ideal, unlike Stephen Miller or Elon Musk, I don’t have my hair on fire about it.

What’s crazy, though, is that for how much conservatives rant and rave about immigration, the Trump administration isn’t even seeking legislative changes to American immigration law.

They’ve enacted many consequential temporary changes, but the policy pendulum is just going to keep swinging, and they seem to genuinely believe the current situation is pretty bad. It’s possible that this is just a tactical error, but I think if you pay attention to the contrast between the apocalyptic rhetoric that rightists use on immigration and their lackadaisical attitude toward the legislative process, you also see the seeds of something more insidious: immigration as a pretext for abandoning democracy.

The power of legislation

Both fans and foes of Donald Trump are sort of grimly fascinated with the ways in which he’s wielded executive power, so much so that I think the significance of actual legislation he’s signed has become underrated.

But consider this — 21st-century Democrats have been extraordinarily unwilling to raise taxes on anyone other than the super rich.

There’s a one-way tax ratchet, and once a G.O.P. tax cut passes, Democrats are only willing to partially reverse it. As a result, even though almost no Democrats backed George W. Bush’s 2001 or 2003 tax cuts, almost all Democrats ended up supporting the extension of a majority of those tax cuts in 2010 and 2013.

The same thing happened with Trump’s 2017 tax cuts — the bill got zero votes from Democrats, but they were only willing to partially roll the cuts back. The 2025 Trump tax cuts were full of gimmicks like “no tax on tips” that Democrats didn’t vote for and wouldn’t have affirmatively enacted but aren’t going to want to repeal.

This isn’t a special feature of Trump or even of tax policy. No Republicans backed the insurance-market regulatory reforms that Democrats passed in 2010, but when Republicans tried to repeal them in 2017 it was hideously unpopular. Now they’ve landed where Democrats are on taxes: not even trying to repeal one of the main features of a bill they once unanimously opposed.

All kinds of legislation tend to be sticky.

On immigration, by contrast, Trump has just been governing through executive action. He really clamped down on irregular arrivals at the southern border. He also shut down Biden administration “parole” programs, whereby thousands of Haitians, Cubans, Nicaraguans, and Venezuelans were able to enter the country legally if they had qualifying sponsors in the United States. Trump has not made any new grants of Temporary Protected Status and has been yanking said status from groups that received it under previous administrations. Refugee resettlement has been limited to white South Africans.

These are all meaningful policy changes, but a lot of them will be reversed the next time a Democrat is in office. The next Democratic administration will, I think (and hope), act with more decisiveness than Joe Biden on basic border security. But especially if they succeed at that, and thus don’t have a public opinion millstone around their neck, they’re almost certainly going to reverse course on these other Trump immigration actions. And when people come on Temporary Protected Status or parole and have kids on American soil, those kids will be American citizens. Even if the next Republican administration kicks those families out, the kids — as citizens — will have the right to return as adults. And because the American green-card system is heavily weighted toward family unification, those American citizens will be able to sponsor relatives for further immigration.

The only thing Trump has even tried to do to address conservative grievances about all this is issue an executive order purporting to end birthright citizenship. When the Supreme Court smacked that down, a bunch of conservatives threw a demagogic hissy fit over absurd scenarios to which the legal doctrine doesn’t even apply.

But there is a real policy issue here. Because of birthright citizenship, temporary admissions to the United States can lead to permanent demographic shifts. I am not personally losing my shit over this because I am not Stephen Miller or Donald Trump or soaked in right-wing ethnonationalism. I’m just observing that on a topic many conservatives claim to see as existential, they’re not making any durable policy change.

By passing laws that cut SNAP and Medicaid and reduce the federal revenue base, Republicans have made it very difficult for the next Democratic administration to do anything on the welfare state other than roll back Trump’s cuts. But it will be incredibly easy to bring back Haitians to eat everyone’s pets.

An offer Democrats can easily refuse

Of course, if Trump did try to change immigration law, he would find it quite difficult. The Tax Cuts and Jobs Act of 2017 and the One Big Beautiful Bill Act of 2025 passed because they were budget reconciliation bills. The 2025 law contained a ton of money for immigration enforcement because you can do that in a reconciliation bill. It did not contain any substantive changes to immigration law because you cannot do that in a reconciliation bill — which is also why the Biden administration was not able to deliver on its promise of a path to citizenship for millions of long-resident illegal immigrants.

So that’s an impasse.

One potential solution, of course, would be to change the filibuster rule so that immigration-policy changes can be made by majority vote. I’ve been an advocate for filibuster abolition since at least 2005. Instead, successive Congresses keep chipping away at it in incremental and creative ways.

I can’t predict where this will go, except to say that neither party seems particularly eager to change the filibuster specifically for immigration purposes.

This leaves the possibility of a bipartisan bill. When Joe Biden was president, he maintained for a while that only legislation could clean up the asylum mess. Republicans then demanded an asylum bill as a condition for delivering aid to Ukraine. Chris Murphy and James Lankford negotiated such a bill, but Donald Trump got wind of it and decided it would serve his interests for no bill to pass, so he pulled the plug. Then Republicans let Ukraine aid go through without the asylum bill attached. But then Democrats, who initially were only willing to do the bill because it was paired with Ukraine aid, realized it would be a good idea to do it.

By that time, though, Republicans were unwilling. Biden then addressed asylum through executive action, and Trump has done the same.

Still, the old loosey-goosey asylum rules are on the books if any later administration wants to go back to them.

The Murphy-Lankford bill would have tightened those screws and also limited executive discretion on parole somewhat. I understand that restrictionists did not love this legislation because they thought it didn’t tighten the screws enough and they didn’t like the idea of letting Biden take a victory lap over a bill they felt was pretty lax. Still, they had all Democrats willing to do a restrictionist bill in exchange for nothing!

Trump could try to get something similar done if he wanted to. Or he could push for a bill that has tougher forward-looking measures but also offers Democrats more. Trump, for example, seems to have given up on trying to cancel the Obama-era Deferred Action for Childhood Arrivals (DACA) program that gives de facto legal status to so-called Dreamers who came to the country illegally when they were underage. Democrats would like to give them permanent legal status and a path to citizenship. Perhaps in exchange for something like that, Democrats would be willing to make restrictionist changes in other areas. A bunch of Republicans now say they want to expand H-2A guest worker visas for farmworkers, and they’re willing to make concessions around a path to citizenship for some illegal immigrants currently working in agriculture. That latter idea is also a priority for many Democrats and could be part of a larger compromise.

I’ve tried over the past few years to ask Democrats what they actually want out of immigration policy and what their red lines are, and honestly they mostly seem very confused about it.

I think it’s possible that if Trump engaged in a serious legislative effort, he’d get something done. It’s also possible that he wouldn’t and Democrats would tie themselves to the mast of unreasonable demands.

By declining to put forward a plan, though, he’s made it very easy for Democrats to say no to any durable policy changes. But we’re not going to find out whether a deal was possible, because Trump isn’t trying and he’s not taking criticism from anyone on the right for leaving a future Democratic administration free to reverse course. This is a bit weird as long as you accept that the parties are going to alternate in power. But perhaps some influential figures on the right don’t accept that.

The immigration pretext

Part of what’s happening here is that just as I often complain about on the left, a lot of conservative politics is fundamentally expressive.

Taxes are an exception. The well-connected people who pull the strings in Republican Party tax-policy circles really want lower taxes and they fight for that. But on immigration, what a lot of conservatives seem to want is to get mad and complain rather than actually do anything. Having the president write a memo purporting to overturn a Supreme Court precedent that’s more than a century old and then becoming enraged when that doesn’t work is not an actual attempt to alter immigration policy — it’s just getting mad.

Or perhaps it’s something more insidious.

In late June, I saw an Elon Musk post endorsing a meme from the new right-wing movie “Citizen Vigilante.” The idea here is that you can’t just do immigration policy qua policy or treat disagreement about immigration policy as part of the normal give-and-take of politics. Instead, before tackling the immigrant menace, you need to destroy the domestic political power of the “traitors” who disagree with you.

The following week, Musk posted about how he’s now against both “classical liberalism” and “universal suffrage” as well.

If you reject both liberalism and democracy, that’s a pretty good reason to opt out of the legislative game and take over immigration! It’s also really bad.

I am well aware that there are swirling currents of illiberalism on both sides of contemporary American politics. But I find this particular anti-liberal push from the right remarkable for a number of reasons:

  • Elon Musk is way richer and more influential than any individual actor on the left.

  • Prominent leftists tend to at least pretend to adhere to basic democratic norms and don’t typically fantasize openly about murdering their political opponents.

  • The combination of high levels of expressed concern about immigration policy paired with total disengagement from the legislative process seems rampant on the right.

There just really is some choosing to be done here. If American democracy is going to continue to exist, then people who want to see large changes made in immigration law need to either change the filibuster rule and accept that the opposition party will take advantage of these changes when it’s their turn to govern, or else engage in a bipartisan negotiation.

The good news, if you’re a conservative, is that the terms of a potential bargain have shifted to the right. If you look at Ruben Gallego’s immigration proposals, for example, you’ll find a prominent Mexican-American politician willing to settle for less expansive legalization programs than the 2013 Obama deal plus a tighter approach to future flows.

Of course that was 2025, when Democrats were feeling underwater on the immigration issue. Trump’s actions since then have mostly served to generate public opinion backlash and make Democrats feel that the politics of the immigration status quo are survivable. That, again, is part of the reason why traditionally you try to pursue policy priorities through legislation to cement gains rather than just shove the pendulum around.

If all you want to do is delegitimize the constitutional order and entrench some kind of quasi-permanent kleptocratic oligarchy, though, the current approach is probably fine.
