The Compulsion of Optimizing your Hotness

When looksmaxxing isn’t about finding a partner, it becomes an outlet for anxiety.

I’m going to say something that might or might not surprise some of you: I kind of understand Clavicular.

Yes, I’ve made a couple Clavicular jokes because he’s just funny. His obsession with looking more attractive, or “looksmaxxing,” (something which we assume is done to attract partners) seems oddly non-sexual, a phenomenon rife for jokes. Even videos of him flirting with women look more like Lynchian performance art than eroticism.

But, funny as it may be, I don’t think it’s incongruent that his appearance obsession seems unrelated to women or dating, or that he’s beautifying himself to a point that would mean nothing to women, crossing the rubicon which I’ve previously called The Point of Diminishing Hotness. This does not mean that he’s “pandering to the male gaze,” or that he’s non-gender-conforming either. I firmly believe that for some people, looksmaxxing becomes more of an anxious compulsion, or in a best case scenario, solo special interest, than a mate-finding strategy.

I know this because I had my own phase of looksmaxxing, optimizing my appearance with zero tangible real-life upside, the way a car hobbyist might tweak a vintage car he never plans to drive. I was in a serious relationship (with my now-husband) so I was not looking for a mate. And while looking good for my boyfriend was important to me, my degree of looksmaxxing eventually devolved into more of an obsession or hobby than anything related to his preferences. (At times, as you’re about to see, he even found it annoying.) Looksmaxxing towards an unattainable goal became a safe place to put my anxiety, which could suddenly take the more appealing form of self-care and productive self-improvement, sparing me the indignity of feeling uncertainty or loss of control.

And I suspect this is the form it is taking for many modern-day young people.

I’m not going to claim my looksmaxxing was “for myself” in the sense that capital-M-Men had nothing to do with it. If I were alone on a desert island, I don’t think I’d have done any of this. We are living in a world where “How attractive would people think I am?” is more or less an omnipresent thought, even if you aren’t acknowledging it. This is especially true for women, whose worth (even in situations totally unrelated to sex, like being in the public eye for their work) always ties back to whether they are hot enough to justify existing. For this reason, I can’t really separate looksmaxxing from societal beauty standards, but in my case (and I believe, in many people’s cases) there was no concrete, dating-oriented goal to any of it.

I had always been a bit self-conscious about my appearance in the way that many teenage girls are, but in my early twenties I took it to a new level. In part, this was my boyfriend-now-husband’s “fault” for getting me into fitness, and for being so good-looking himself that I felt I had to step up my game. At times, I worried that if people saw us walking down the street, they’d think I won him in a charity date auction—a bragworthy experience for men, not so much for women. Before this point, I was one of those genetically skinny teenage girls with zero interest in fitness, although I occasionally did ab crunches during my Sims loading screen. My boyfriend was really into working out and wanted a partner who shared this interest—and presumably, that type of body. Eager to impress him, I used fitness as a way to bond with him, and asked him for tips. We spent a lot of time together in our college gym, where he basically acted as my free personal trainer.

I had always thought that fitness was about losing weight or becoming a bodybuilder, neither of which appealed to me. But getting into fitness culture (and fitness-oriented social media) got me to realize that fitness could actually help me tweak my body composition in ways that would still read as unambiguously feminine. Huge game changer for me. See, I had a particular insecurity about looking unfeminine, probably a holdover from the type of mockery I received in middle school when I had the gall to be flat-chested as an eleven-year-old. I had always wanted a smaller waist, and bigger everything else. I wasn’t even especially rectangular, but in my mind, only when my body reached a point of hyperfeminine parody could I be satisfied. During my high school years, I had no real images of what this would even look like—almost all the popular actresses had fairly typical body proportions, if slightly bigger boobs than average—but once the 2010s rolled around, I had Kim Kardashian, along with a million Instagram models who were using FaceTune to create impossible body proportions. Specifically, they had generated enormous butts with skinny legs and size 00 waists. And I, too, could have this body if I drank the right “cleanse” tea, lifted the right weights, and ate the right protein-heavy foods. No plastic surgery required!

At this point, my fitness obsession had spun off from the basic stuff I had been doing with my boyfriend and reached the “special interest” tier. I had read a great amount of evolutionary psychology literature, confirming my theory that an extreme waist-hip ratio was the singular most important trait a woman could have. Of course, I asked my boyfriend how important this was to him, and he maintained he just liked women who were fit, but I feared even he couldn’t truly understand his own lizard brain and he was merely “virtue signaling” as an upper middle class white guy. (Liking slim, fit women without a strong preference for cartoonishly-huge anything? He must be lying to seem high-status!) Confident that I knew what he didn’t, I embarked on an expedition to alter my waist-hip ratio as much as possible. Sure, he said this was a silly obsession, but he wouldn’t be laughing when I showed up with a 24 inch waist and 40 inch ass.

I was hamstrings deep in Bret Contretas’ Strong Curves. As I embarked on my looksmaxxing journey, I had a rule for myself: everything had to be DIY. It went without saying that there would be no BBLs. I could purchase products, but not services or procedures unless you included a cheapo membership to 24 Hour Fitness. This rule mostly came out of the fact that we had no money back then, but I also just felt like getting any kind of outside help would be “cheating.” This quest was less about obtaining a specific type of body and more about being able to prove I could do it all myself.

I had an intense gym routine that focused almost exclusively on my butt, three times a week. My boyfriend told me I shouldn’t neglect my arms and upper body, but I told him I did not want to look “muscular,” I wanted to look like I had a fake ass. I did zero ab work. I wanted a comically tapered waist, and I was afraid ab work would make my abs look boxy. Often, I’d ask him to help me lift the bar for certain workouts because I didn’t have enough upper body strength to lift the squat bar.

I took my measurements daily. I was trying to optimize around the perfect weight and muscle mass that would result in the most extreme waist-hip ratio, because I was not fixated on any particular size, only a particular shape. Although I was clearly getting way too obsessed with this stuff, I don’t think it read as “dangerous,” because I was eating so much food—just weird. I mostly gained weight in my lower body, so my goal was to eat lots of healthy calories which would ideally result in mostly muscle gain (specifically glutes) plus some fat, but not enough to reach my upper body. Every day after my workout, I made my own protein shake out of egg white powder, raw honey, cocoa powder and whole milk. I ate a lot, but I believed that eating any grains or gluten would cause me to spot-gain in my midsection. Why did I come to this bizarre conclusion? Well, I had been reading a lot of paleo blogs which asserted that a woman’s hormones could reach optimal balance with fewer simple carbs, and any woman could have an extreme hourglass figure if she stopped eating rice. (In case anyone is taking this “fact” seriously: no, it’s not true.) But believing it to be true, I tried my best to adhere to the paleo diet despite not actually wanting to lose weight (I say “tried” because realistically, I had a “cheat day” at least once a week where I dug into a bag of Ruffles Spicy Cheddar and Sour Cream chips.)

At a certain point, I realized that weightlifting and diet alone were never going to get me where I wanted to be. I became entranced by Instagram ads for “waist trainers,” hawked by the Kardashians, until I realized these garments were just stretchy shapewear, which were never going to alter the shape of a person’s waist. After doing a bit more research (probably while I was supposed to be working—during all of this I somehow had a job) I realized that steel-boned corsets were the only type of “waist trainer” that might actually work. I began reading the Lucy’s Corsetry blog and was amazed at the before and after images of women who supposedly reduced their waist size even while staying the same weight or gaining weight. I quickly ordered a steel-boned corset and resolved to wear it all day and night unless I was showering, just as the blog instructed.

So, how did my boyfriend feel about his girlfriend suddenly wearing a black satin corset to bed every night? You might be surprised to hear that he didn’t think this was especially sexy. At this point, he was used to me doing a variety of bizarre beauty rituals and correctly clocked this as my latest endeavor in Crazy Shit Studies. He reminds me there was a time when I used turmeric on my legs as a natural self-tanner and black tea on my face as a toner, and yet another time he came into the kitchen and caught me attempting to grate an avocado pit to make a hair growth serum. I think his main concern was whether or not I had a refund policy on the corset.

I even wore the corset to the gym (because of course) and to work. Whenever I took it off to shower, I noticed a dramatic change in my waist shape, which paired really well with all my gym gains. But one day, I stumbled upon a different corsetry blog from a woman who had proof that “waist training” simply didn’t work, even with a steel-boned corset. She had trained with a corset for years and could tighten her waist to just twenty inches, maintaining a relatively similar waistline right after removing the corset, just as I had noticed after removing mine to shower. But after the corset had been off for several hours, her normal waist size returned. Given that wearing a corset all day was extremely uncomfortable, I hated the idea that I was doing this with no real long-term upside. I got rid of the corset. And while I kept up my fitness regimen, that too eventually became far less dogmatic.

At times, my looksmaxxing had been “fun.” I experienced a jolt of energy when I realized there was a new lever I could pull, a new diet I could try, a new exercise I could do. Sometimes, I looked in the mirror and felt extremely proud of myself, confident I would soon reach my goal. But for every high, there was a low. I would have days when I felt my body was grotesquely masculine, where I cursed my genetics for not including a free FaceTune premium subscription. It didn’t even matter that I already had a decent waist-hip ratio to start with, or that I wasn’t exactly surrounded by women in real life with the extreme measurements about which I fantasized. Those women were everywhere online, I kept seeing them, and I was no longer living in reality.

It wasn’t just my body, by the way, although that felt the most within my control. I was experimenting with different skincare regimens. I created my own hair masque intended to spark more growth and stop breakage, since my goal length was down to my butt (I never quite got there.) I was putting castor oil on my eyelashes every night, which I hoped would lengthen them but just made my vision blurry and then I started freaking out that I was going blind. I tried to pull a few millimeters of armpit fat into my bra cups as aggressively as possible, convinced that I could grow my breasts be re-distributing breast tissue that had migrated elsewhere. But my waist and butt were my main focuses.

I don’t even know what my fear was when it came to my body not being good enough. It wasn’t really about my boyfriend—I did not think he was going to dump me or cheat on me because my proportions weren’t extreme enough. It also wasn’t about other men—I had already made my mind up that I wanted to marry my boyfriend, and it looked like it was going in that direction. Like Clavicular, it wasn’t really about dating or sex at all. It was, to some extent, about what I imagined “most men” were into, but with no desire to interact with most men, what was the point? It became a personal competition, a way for me to push myself, challenge myself and punish myself. Moments of self-hatred and penance were part of the whole experience. I would revel in the suffering as I scrolled Instagram to examine the bodies of women I would never be. How could I ever be motivated to really shoot for the stars if I didn’t have a voice in my head reminding me that I was not good enough? Loving my body as it was felt like complacency, not self-acceptance.

I had moments where I worried about other things which always distressed me far more than my body shape—diseases, international conflicts, car accidents—and in a way, I was relieved when my anxiety could return to my body. It was the only form of anxiety that, on some level, I enjoyed. Most of the time, my anxiety was strictly torturous. At least when it was focused on looksmaxxing, I had feelings of euphoria and accomplishment from time to time. Even though my goals were too outlandish to ever become reality, I had some control over how I looked, and I had a lot more control over that than I did over whether or not mad cow disease would go airborne.

But I don’t think I’m alone in this quest, or the fact that my looksmaxxing wasn’t really about sex at all. It’s not really surprising that looksmaxxing feels so non-sexual—it’s not sexual most of the time. It’s inspired by the sexual preferences of your desired gender (in my case, men, and in Clavicular’s case, women) but is often not really about finding a partner in any concrete way.

Take, for example, the Reddit subs, r/Vindicta (the original female looksmaxxing sub) and r/Splendida (the slightly more compassionate offshoot.) Differences aside, these subs are all about “objective beauty,” or in other words, looksmaxxing for women. Men are, interestingly, prohibited from both subs. And if you look at the top posts on r/Splendida, basically none of them mention men, or trying to look attractive for men. Obviously, their goals are directly in keeping with male sexual preferences, and I don’t think they try to deny that, but “finding a boyfriend” doesn’t seem very central to what they’re doing. Some mention noticing more male attention after making certain changes, but it sounds more like a scientist observing silverback gorillas and less like a woman trying to get a date.

In fact, I saw one post from a woman on r/Splendida who listed all the things she did to looksmaxx, and wanted ideas for new levers to pull because she was bored and wanted new things on which to improve. The top comment suggested she see a therapist. For many of these people, they are viewing themselves like a car on Pimp My Ride, not like a person who needs to be improved for the dating market.

It’s not not about men. These women are almost never lesbians, which is a worthwhile data point. But at the same time, it’s not really about men either.

To this day, I have mixed feelings on looksmaxxing as a compulsion. I have defended women openly trying to look attractive for men, but looksmaxxing is something else entirely. And yet, can I blanket condemn it? If a person’s looksmaxxing never pushes them toward anything physically dangerous, and it’s a better outlet for their anxiety and compulsive tendencies than whatever else they’d be doing, is it such a problem? But on the other hand, is it just reinforcing and enabling maladaptive thought patterns? I still can’t be so sure. But the one thing I learned from all this, of which I am 100% certain, is that a corset is only worth buying as a fashion accessory.
