What did the right win with Malfoid?

The unlikely role of Harry Potter fan fiction in the midterms

Halina Bennet
Nov 2
∙
Paid
 
READ IN APP
 

There is a significant body of Harry Potter fan fiction involving romance between the titular character and Draco Malfoy. Sometimes, that romance is gay. Other times, it is gender-swapped — one of the oldest moves in fandom. Archive of Our Own, one of the internet’s most popular fan fiction sites, has nearly 100,000 entries tagged with the romantic and/or sexual Harry and Draco ship “Drarry,” and other sites such as FanFiction.net and Wattpad are home to thousands more.

This robust, yet formerly niche, literary subgenre — or at least a particular subset of it — recently burst forth into the center of American politics with a post the U.S. Department of Education made on October 27 featuring a female Malfoy looking on longingly at Harry and his friends Ron and Hermione, with the caption “There’s Magic in Learning. Make Education Great Again.”

This gender-swapped Malfoy — known informally as Malfoid — has rapidly become an icon of the online right. It’s a way to take control of Harry Potter — and the L.G.B.T.Q. fan fiction tradition — from the left, using a sex swap that is decidedly cisgender and heterosexual to stick it to progressives using their own tool.

That provocation has been successful on its own terms, with right-wing memesters showing they can make fan fiction culture their own. But it also raises a question that its creators seem eager to avoid: What, exactly, was won?

Malfoid emerged in full force in September 2026, just before the midterms, as a symbol of the online right’s values — and its prowess at creating A.I.-generated videos. What it failed to do was actually transform the original Harry Potter story, where the characters who embody progressive tolerance eventually win against those who carry bigoted beliefs, into a conservative text.

The inversion

The current wave of gender-swapped Draco content started with a human-drawn post on X of “Dracaena Malfoy” that was largely devoid of political content. The Dracaena images, however, were adopted by users who renamed her “Malfoid” — “foid” being manosphere shorthand for “femoid,” meaning female and android or humanoid, originating in online incel communities aiming to describe women as subhuman.

Thus, given the decidedly based moniker, it wasn’t long before videos, images, and stories centering Malfoid spread across multiple internet platforms. Often drawn in an anime style, the silver-haired, curvaceous teen in a short skirt demeans a doting Harry Potter. Wired writes that while in the books Draco’s bigotry is a character flaw to overcome, Malfoid’s “bigotry is romanticized, transforming her into a right-wing aesthetic vehicle” to promote toxicity and, more specifically, ethnonationalism.

Ultimately, Malfoid is a more sexually explicit, confident version of Draco — a saturation of his existing character traits that, combined with the sex swap, is not itself right-wing. There exists plenty of this kind of fan fiction, primarily produced and replicated by the fandom’s female and marginalized members, who drive this kind of content for their favorite media. Anthony Auspice writes that gender-swapped characters “carry certain connotations regarding the sort of leftist gender goblins who would be engaged with the fandom,” which is what makes their popularity on the dissident right notable. His argument is that Malfoid is “restorative storytelling,” a reaction to two decades of narratives in which the good guy gets the girl and the villain is bad.

The tool, in this case fan fic, has been taken from the left to the right, its label shifted. The success is that the symbol now means something else to a different group of people and that the other group is annoyed. The right has taken a form typically associated with L.G.B.T.Q. fandom and the left and made it straight and at least nominally right-wing.

But in Malfoid’s wizarding world, little about the structure of the Harry Potter story has changed. While Malfoid content varies across its thousands of permutations, it is fundamentally about a good, tolerant, sometimes meek boy wizard who has a romantic relationship with this domineering, bigoted girl who, in the end, wants to be with him.

The swap leaves much intact

In the original Harry Potter story, Draco is a pure-blood supremacist, a bully, and the heir of a bigoted family. He uses a slur to describe muggle-born Hermione and harasses Harry and his friends. Draco never converts to their cause, but his arc is a mellowing out of his prior beliefs as he comes to know the wizards-in-training who lead the charge against evil. In the sixth book, Draco cannot bring himself to kill Dumbledore, and in the seventh, he is a far cry from the bumptious schoolyard bully as he retreats from the violence that his family and Voldemort promote. Draco never renounces his family’s beliefs outright and is never fully redeemed in the books or epilogue. But he is also not fully punished, and the character is beloved by the thousands of readers who have completed his story in fan fiction and in the Broadway play “Harry Potter and the Cursed Child.” The play, written in part by the series’s original author, J.K. Rowling, follows Scorpius Malfoy, Draco’s son and Harry’s son’s best friend, who is more sensitive and sweet than his father. He was raised by his father to reject the prejudices and pure-blood supremacy long held by the Malfoy family, giving Draco some final kind of redemption.

Fan fiction, once a place to further redeem Draco, is now where Malfoid lives.

One of the most popular Wattpad female-Malfoy fics is an A.I.-assisted slow-burn retelling in which a girl who was never told “no” is finally told so by Harry Potter, resulting in an enemies-to-lovers arc.

There are many similar storylines that follow a similar structure. Malfoid stories typically map, with some heightened yearning, onto Rowling’s original story — the scion of a bigoted family is moved, by contact with people who are good to her or demonstrate good toward others, toward something better. The character, whether named Draco or Draconia, has an ideology that, through the plot, they must escape. That plot remains largely intact throughout Malfoid fics, which rest on the charged dynamic between the dominant girl and the submissive boy.

One X user prompted an A.I. video maker to “Consume every piece of Malfoid content, from image to text, then create a cinematic live-action video of the character. Two minutes minimum.” That video is recognizable as Harry Potter with a more seductive female version of Draco. In the end, she defects from her father as she falls for Harry.

If that video is, indeed, the agglomeration of Malfoid content, then the gender swap changed little beyond the character’s sex and left the moral architecture of the story in place. A story whose central movement is the abandonment of inherited prejudice doesn’t become less progressive just because the prejudiced person is now a girl. The right has created a heterosexual love story in contrast to same-sex versions of the ship, but it has left intact a fundamentally liberal plot architecture. Of course, there is likely Malfoid content out there that does change the story. (I can’t possibly consume it all.) But that doesn’t change the fact that the cultural understanding of the character as a right-wing symbol rests entirely on her traits and not at all on the narrative.

Wired argues that the bigotry is romanticized and Harry is a “stand-in for disaffected young men who fantasize about a relationship with a beautiful, cruel silvery-blonde girl.” That says something about the kind of figure that users in incel channels want to lust after, which is an essay about young men for another time, but it still doesn’t resolve the fact that she goes for Harry and defects from her family rather than Harry defecting from his friends.

What Malfoid says about the midterms

There shouldn’t be much to say about the sex of a character in Harry Potter fan fiction, or about how the symbol resolves one way or another, but one week before Election Day, the Education Department posted Malfoid. The post received substantial attention on X, and when the original Malfoid artist was asked whether they ever imagined this from the federal government, they replied, “considering how unserious [the] current government is, yeah, it was in a realm of possibility.”

Communications scholar Leslie Hahner told Wired that either explanation for the post, that the department did not know the symbol’s baggage or that it did, would be significant. If the department misread the symbol, it cannot be trusted to steward its messages. If it understood the symbol, it chose to speak to and be recognized by a subculture and not be understood by everyone else. In both cases, the department made a statement without making an argument.

To that end, the post and Malfoid are proof that it is possible to build engagement and belonging around a meme whose symbol is strong but whose substance is negligible.

In other words, a culture war symbol is overshadowing actual ideological coherence.

Critics of the left have long argued that a politics of representation, casting, depiction, and symbolism overshadows material change and that fights over symbols are a cheap distraction from fights over policy. That is the same criticism this essay makes of the right and of people across the political spectrum who are thinking about how much a female Draco sticks it to ’em instead of the substance, which has been mostly left untouched.

Malfoid’s arrival in the final months of the midterms is a perfect ending note that encapsulates today’s politics. Instead of interrogating the fundamental framework of policy or narrative, we are quick to jump on culture war issues like trans women in sports or a misogynistic, cishet female version of a wizard.
