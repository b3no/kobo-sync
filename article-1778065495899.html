Don’t copy Donald Trump’s failed presidency

The Orange Man is bad at the job.

Matthew Yglesias
May 6
∙

In the early months of Donald Trump’s second administration, many Democrats seemed awestruck by the apparent pace of activity. They felt that Trump and his team were running circles around the opposition and grew green with envy, eager to plot how Democrats could copy what was working so well for him.

At the time, I was skeptical. Trump took office with a higher approval rating than he’d earned at any point during his first term, but his numbers started dropping almost immediately. I completely agree with the conventional wisdom that the counter-Trump messaging from Chuck Schumer and Hakeem Jeffries has been uninspired, and also with the conventional wisdom that they have an objectively difficult job of rallying the troops against Trump given the anti-establishment mood of their grassroots supporters.

But that just meant that Trump’s falling numbers reflected poorly on his own choices.

Now, heading into the second summer of his second term, his approval rating is lower than at any point between his first inauguration and Election Day 2020. Democrats have plenty of their own problems, but Trump has catastrophically bobbled a once-promising political situation. The fact that Democrats’ own dysfunctions may save the Senate for him is noteworthy, but it’s hardly a ringing endorsement of his approach.

Closer to my own factional alignment, the notion that Trump is worthy of emulation also seems to be the premise of things like Project 2029.

Despite my general skepticism of candidates releasing tons of plans, I am definitely in favor of planning. But why would you give your planning operation that specific name ahead of the election? The main way that Project 2025 played as a brand concept was as a fiasco that Trump spent most of the election disavowing.

Of course those disavowals were largely bullshit, and Trump has used much of what was in the Project 2025 document as a blueprint for governance. But has it been a successful blueprint? Or has it been such a fiasco that it’s allowed Democrats to get up off the mattress while doing very little to address their own weaknesses?

Why are we trying to copy failure?

Learning from past lessons learned

When I think about making plans for a new Democratic Party administration, my first consideration is what we can learn from what the Biden administration thought they had learned from the Obama administration.

I start with Democrats learning from Democrats because I am a Democrat, and most of the people I know are Democrats, and I have relationships with people who worked in the Obama administration and people who worked in the Biden administration and people who worked in both — I’m pretty sure I understand what the thought processes were.

Team Biden’s takeaway from the Obama years was that while Team Obama had titanic achievements, they were smaller than they should have been given the scale of the Democratic Party’s majorities. They felt that time had been wasted in ultimately fruitless bipartisan processes, and that Team Obama came in with an outdated view of a “mandate” that they wrongly thought would force Republicans to cooperate with them. A budget reconciliation bill ended up being critical to getting the Affordable Care Act over the line, but it was never at the center of the legislative strategy for the A.C.A. or for the Obama presidency in general.

So Biden and congressional Democrats came in and ran a playbook that was genuinely successful at getting big bills passed in a way that is especially impressive if you adjust for the slender majorities.

That included a much larger stimulus bill than Obama achieved (because it was written as a reconciliation bill), a separate major infrastructure-spending boost that passed on a bipartisan basis with largely fake pay-fors, a second reconciliation bill that invested hundreds of billions in progressive priorities, and then a lot of smaller bipartisan legislation, including a gun control bill and a marriage equality bill.

Somewhere along the way, though, the focus on bill-maxxing led them to forget that you don’t actually want the largest possible stimulus!

You want an appropriately sized stimulus, and that’s especially true if you’re going to follow it up with an infrastructure-spending bill with fake pay-fors. And while the Inflation Reduction Act was structured to be slightly disinflationary in its macroeconomic impact, they settled for very minor impacts in this regard in order to mostly focus on achieving clean energy goals.

In other words, Team Biden did an excellent job of avoiding the mistakes of the past and in exchange made some brand new mistakes.

Blitzkrieg bop

A signature aspect of the American political system is its many veto points.

Over the course of my career, growing recognition of these veto points has led to more emphasis among political elites on the idea that the core goal of politics is to find ways around the Maginot Line of procedural obstacles in order to deliver the change that the public demands.

When I was at Vox and we published our cases for the 2020 Democratic presidential nominees, Ezra Klein wrote the case for Elizabeth Warren, with the piece centering on the idea that she understood the detailed mechanics of executive power and thus would be able to change-max. When I wrote my case for Bernie Sanders, I had almost the opposite argument: that presidents are so constrained in their domestic authority that it doesn’t matter so much that his economic ideas are too left-wing, and it’s most important to have a dovish foreign policy.

Team Biden turned out to be more effective at legislating than most of us expected.

They also brought on board a lot of people from the Warrenverse, such as Rohit Chopra and Khan herself, who are and were well-acquainted with how to maximize their regulatory power.

Trump, I would say, has mostly governed as if the Biden approach is a template for success. The One Big Beautiful Bill Act has such a strange name because Trump’s legislative approach is so reconciliation-centric that all his administration’s different policy initiatives are just glommed together in one law. That includes a big increase in funding for immigration enforcement that traditionally would not have been considered an appropriate subject for a reconciliation bill.

DOGE fired people based on “Do we have the legal authority to fire this person?” rather than on any theory of civil service reform, because the whole idea was to achieve whatever is achievable at the fastest possible pace.

This sort of blitzkrieg approach of just moving with maximum velocity wherever the procedural barriers are lowest works to an extent, especially if (like Trump but unlike Biden) the Supreme Court is broadly sympathetic to your goals.

But it prompts massive political backlash because “pursue your party’s ideological goals with maximum ferocity” is exactly what voters don’t want.

They keep saying they want politicians to address the cost of living, which requires taking a break from ideological goals to actually focus on the specifics of the situation.

Aim for durable change

Despite the Obama administration’s considerable achievements, I think Ronald Reagan still ranks as the most consequential president of my lifetime.

Some of that is because he took steely ideological stands. But a lot is just that he managed his politics well. He got re-elected by a landslide in 1984 rather than a slim margin. He successfully passed the baton to a same-party successor who was viewed as more moderate, securing a rarely seen partisan three-peat that entrenched a judiciary makeover.

Obama takes a lot of criticism for Democrats losing ground in down-ballot offices, but the in-party power has lost ground in state legislatures under almost every president. The exception is Reagan, under whose leadership the Republicans gained enough ground in the South to compensate for non-Southern losses.

Of course, Reagan also made consequential policy changes, and some of these were subtle.

Before Reagan, for example, income tax brackets were not indexed to inflation. As a result, between World War II and Reagan, nobody ever really had to raise taxes. Revenue as a share of G.D.P. just went up naturally by default, and elected officials were always passing tax cuts. If you wanted revenue to rise, you just did smaller cuts. Nobody has changed it back because the old system sounds dumb. But that change pushed the entirety of fiscal politics onto a whole other trajectory.

Obama was not as consequential as Reagan. But his two main bills (the A.C.A. and Dodd-Frank) are mostly intact, and ending Don’t Ask Don’t Tell and establishing marriage equality are durable achievements.

I get why progressives feel that some of his work was undermined by slowness.

In contrast, Lina Khan took an impatient approach to policy change. To make a giant shift in antitrust doctrine, you could try to pass a bipartisan bill. Or you could try to remake the judiciary over time. Or at a minimum, you could try to build a bipartisan consensus on the Federal Trade Commission that might endure in modified form after partisan control switches.

What Khan opted for instead was maximum partisan action: lots of enforcement decisions and litigation taken by 3-2 vote rather than settling for more modest efforts that could secure bipartisan support. That indicated to a lot of people in the business world that if they want to do certain kinds of mergers, they’d need Republicans to win first. This motivated some of them to fight hard to get a Republican administration in town while ultimately having zero durable impact on antitrust policy, because the parties rotating in power is an inevitable aspect of life in a democracy.

Outside the realm of antitrust, one Khan-era action that I really agreed with strongly on the merits was an effort to ban non-compete agreements, which was just dropped after Trump won. The way to accomplish this goal, ultimately, is going to be to get the bipartisan Chris Murphy / Todd Young bill on the topic passed. It’s just not something you can accomplish with blitzkrieg tactics.

When Trump is gone, I think people are going to be left scratching their heads over the fact that he didn’t pass any substantial immigration legislation during his term.

There are all these structural features of American immigration policy that conservatives object to that haven’t been changed. The diversity visa lottery is still there, family reunification visas are still there, the president still has broad discretion to grant Temporary Protected Status and parole people into the country. Trump pretended he can end birthright citizenship with an executive order, but he’s going to lose on that.

I don’t want to exaggerate and say that Trump isn’t achieving anything that will endure. Like Biden, he is making a difference. But the blitzkrieg strategy is poorly suited to long-term policy change, especially because it engenders such severe public-opinion backlash that it makes it hard to politically entrench anything.

Project “make correct decisions”

I have never been president or worked in the White House, but from what I’ve observed in my years of covering five administrations, it’s a pretty difficult job.

You need to make a lot of decisions on a staggeringly wide range of issues, often with imperfect information, while also maintaining a hectic schedule of symbolic and political events. Nobody really thanks you for crises you nipped in the bud (like Biden with Silicon Valley Bank); they just remember stuff you fumbled (like indecision about how to lift Covid restrictions in the face of waning public compliance).

And one of the biggest problems every administration faces is that they make substantive policy-analysis errors in good faith.

Invading Iraq was a catastrophe and, with the benefit of hindsight, Bush’s handling of Afghanistan was also a disaster. This was not some kind of partisan ploy — they just fucked up.

Obama-era Democrats got the macroeconomic situation wrong. They sunk a lot of political capital into fights about a regulatory Clean Power Plan that got killed by the courts. More importantly, though, America ended up hitting those targets anyway because of fracking and the natural gas boom. If the administration had known that, they could have skipped the whole thing and achieved just as much at no cost.

Trump spent the entire pandemic flailing. Biden mishandled the border situation. Trump’s war in Iran is destroying an already weak presidency.

Which is all to say that the main thing I believe administrations need more help with is how to get better information and make better decisions. How do we avoid getting boxed in by promises made under different circumstances? How do we make sure we don’t end up in an epistemic bubble, only listening to ideologues and co-partisans?

After all, a dilemma for any administration is that its swing voters are not that invested in its coalition’s partisan agenda, but its team is dominated by coalition members who are wired in to that coalition’s institutions.

Those institutions will constantly work to reframe their ideas as the perfect solution to whatever problems are on the minds of swing voters. Sometimes they might be correct; oftentimes they won’t. The coalition as a whole will do better if the administration does a good job, retains down-ballot support, wins multiple election cycles, and makes durable change over time.

But it’s still the case that each individual element of the coalition will want to maximize the political capital sunk into its idiosyncratic causes.

The hard part of being president is figuring out which ideas to prioritize, understanding which arguments coming from your own camp make sense, and maintaining access to an accurate picture of the world.

Over the past year and a half, Trump’s team has arguably perfected the alternative style of governance, where you just blow everyone off and pursue a pre-cooked agenda at maximum velocity. And everyone hates it!

This administration is a cautionary tale, not a model for the future.
