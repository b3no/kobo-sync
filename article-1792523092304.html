Alberta: Recess is over

Danielle Smith has what she had in February, plus a deeply annoyed population. Time to get back to the normal debates of a normal country

Paul Wells
Oct 20
∙
Preview
 
READ IN APP
 

At this writing, before the full ballot count is in, the aptly-named Option 2 is holding a majority in two of Alberta’s 87 ridings. It takes real work to be less popular in Alberta than Justin Trudeau (that’s not my line, but I’m pleased to pass it along to you).

The worst campaigners on the face of the planet are threatening to keep campaigning, but their first objective is to turn the United Conservative Party into an implacable losing machine after their own image. Any party that would take that gang’s advice — yet again — would deserve what it gets, but that’s out of my hands.

The fate of the so-called Nine is more complex, and worth parsing. Referendum questions 1 through 9 were entirely Danielle Smith’s brainchild. She can’t even awkwardly pretend, as she insisted on doing with the secession pre-question, that outside forces made her ask these other questions.

The first five asked the population’s permission to introduce a range of legislation designed to single out and ostracize immigrants. The last four asked for a mandate to seek constitutional amendments. At this writing, the “no to the nine” campaign succeeded only in shaving several points off what I think would otherwise have been large majorities. The Yes side won on every question except 8, about opting out of federal programs with compensation. Three other questions were nearly dead heats.

Now what? Smith has, today, what she had in February: A government that commands a majority in the Legislature and can introduce any proposed legislation it wants. The threadbare mandates on Questions 1 to 5 will not be much help. In February I couldn’t see the point of putting those questions to a referendum. I still can’t. Any government can put any bill before any legislature. Politics happens around that, and before and after, and a mandate from the people confers nothing magical, especially when it’s a decidedly mixed mandate. In particular, civil society and the courts will still have a say, in roles that are overwhelmingly supported by the population, over whether any bill makes sense, is constitutional, and makes the government that introduces it more or less worth supporting.

One more thing on the first five. Four of them ask for a mandate to “introduce a law.” In the parliamentary system where Danielle Smith has spent most of the last 13 years of her life, most people understand that phrase to be meaningless. Governments introduce bills. The legislature and the Crown make them laws if they get enough MLAs’ votes. If a serious government had sent its proposed questions to a Legislature committee for even a few days’ study, that would have been on a long list of drafting problems that could have been fixed. But on these questions, that’s not the kind of government Alberta has.

All the closest results were on the constitutional amendment questions. The voters have sent the whole project back to Smith with no particular instructions. Failing to win a majority on opting-out doesn’t deprive her of the right to try her luck with other provinces on an amendment to that effect. Winning a bare majority on making provincial laws paramount over federal laws doesn’t particularly strengthen her hand. Manitoba’s sovereignty within the federation on these matters remains entire, as does Nova Scotia’s and all the rest. Knocking on those other provinces’ doors with the news that Albertans really want this stuff need not have persuaded them; the news that Albertans sort of want it will be even less persuasive.

So Premier Smith’s brief vacation in the land of magical realism is over and she is back in the world of politics. She has often been better at politics than her opponents, though the by-election returns from Calgary—Shaw are a reminder that even skilled politicians sometimes lose their touch. I hope she won’t pass the full suite of measures implied in Questions 1 to 5 into law. I expect she will get nowhere with the Question 6 to 9 amendments. But she sometimes does what I hope she won’t. She is responsible not to me but to the voters, the courts and the give-and-take of federalism. In those arenas yesterday’s events changed nothing. Alberta voters will weigh how they feel about this colossal waste of time.
