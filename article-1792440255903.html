The Q&A: “Trump loves to be adulated. But he despises the adulators.”

Ricardo Hausmann, the founder of Harvard’s Growth Lab, on his advice to Canada in a post-American world

Paul Wells
Oct 19
 
READ IN APP
 

Turns out there were two books published last week by former cabinet ministers with advice to Canada on navigating Trump times. One was by Chrystia Freeland, and we wish her all the best. The other was by Ricardo Hausmann. He’s the founder and director of Harvard University’s Growth Lab. Many years earlier, before Hugo Chávez came to power, Hausmann was Venezuela’s minister of planning. His new book, with Eric Protzer, is Canada After America. Its advice is very broadly Carneyesque — Canada should increase trade with the rest of the world, since it is certain to face continued trouble trading with the US — but there are intriguing differences. Hausmann and Protzer put more emphasis on developing high-technology industry in Canada, and he views housing shortages as a drag on the economy, not only a cost-of-living issue.

Hausmann spoke to me from Montreal, where he was delivering the first of three Max Bell Lectures on opportunities for Canadian growth. This conversation has been edited for length and clarity.

Paul Wells: How did you get interested in Canada’s prosperity?

Ricardo Hausmann: Well, the Harvard Growth Lab worries about prosperity across the world. I’m from Venezuela. I’ve always asked myself the question: how can I make a place prosper, right? So, I created the Growth Lab.

Public policy is to economics what medicine is to biology. Developments in biology are fantastic for medicine but you wouldn’t go and get treated by a biologist. The core of a medical school is the teaching hospital. You cannot teach without the hospital, and you cannot do research without the hospital.

The Growth Lab thinks of itself as a teaching hospital for economic growth problems across the world.

From the moment we started to look for partners abroad, I had several Canadians on my team who were lobbying internally for us to get involved in Canada. And then, when Jennifer [Welsh, of McGill] approached me about the Max Bell Lectures, it was obvious that Canada had a big shock to its economic strategy, which was previously based on a deeper integration with the US and North America. And suddenly, that no longer was a viable option. I thought it was a really interesting strategic problem to think about. It helped me now that Venezuela has to think about its relationship with the US also and its sources of strategic sovereignty and independence that are now deeply in question.

PW: So, what does it do to Canada that its main path to prosperity for 60 years, if not 150 years, has been through the United States and now that path is compromised?

Hausmann: Well, the fact that the path is compromised and that Canada used to be very close to the US, and now in some economic sense, it’s farther away from the US. I always thought that Canada and Australia were kind of sister countries, both British offshoots. The difference is that Canada sat right next to the biggest economy in the world, and Australia was out there in the middle of nowhere. Both are prosperous places; both are happy places. Australia was able to find a way forward, even though it was far. In the book, we call it “Canada’s New Australian Geography”.

The question is, what are the channels through which this matters? On the one hand, it doesn’t really matter that much for natural resources. It complicates the logistics a little bit, but the world is used to having natural resources travel long distances. But it complicates some value chains that tend to be very geographically tight. OEMs [Original Equipment Manufacturers], and assembly plants, like to be very close to their suppliers. That’s typical in the auto industry. So for the auto industry, it’s a big, big shock because that’s an industry where suppliers are very close to the OEM. The OEM can be far away from the consumer—so the car can travel a lot— but the parts don’t.

That’s not the case in other industries. And so we detected which are those other industries, and we have found four: pharma, medical devices, aerospace, and defense. We empirically find that those are more tolerant of distance. And that means that these are industries that would be less dependent on relying on the U.S. market.

We ask: why haven’t these industries developed more in Canada? The thing that struck our attention is that these are very R&D-intensive industries. When we looked into the R&D scene in Canada we found that Canada scores well in terms of the generosity of public support for R&D, but scores very poorly in terms of actual private investment in R&D.

We found different kinds of answers. The first set of answers has to do with R&D policy itself. The second has to do with the sectoral policies of the R&D industries themselves.

For example, you can have horizontal policies in general about what’s the tax treatment of R&D, but if you’re going to do pharma, it’s going to be very dependent on how efficient you are or how quick you are at certifying drugs, certifying clinical trials, at procuring new drugs, and so on. Canada scored fairly poorly in all of that.

So, maybe Canada doesn’t do more pharma, not because of the R&D policy, but because of the pharma and drug certification and procurement policies.

The R&D policies that struck our attention are that you have a certain “small is beautiful” bias in your R&D policy. You are generous with R&D for small firms and less so in big firms. Big firms get a smaller tax credit and they don’t get grants, they get tax deductions. And obviously, you need to have profits in order to have tax deductions. So, in some sense, it is backloaded.

If you want to make a new airplane, you’ll spend 10 years designing it. And then it’ll take you a month to build an aircraft. But you have to be spending a lot of money for a very long period of time before you have an aircraft to sell. If you want to maximize the net present value of the future tax cash flow, that may involve being more generous with your R&D treatment. We think that there are some tweaks to R&D policy, but there’s other tweaks to certification policies and to procurement policies that would help Canada play a bigger role in this space.

PW: Broadly speaking, Mark Carney’s policy is to hang on to as much of bilateral trade with the United States as possible while building up the internal Canadian market and seeking third international markets for Canadian products. How do you think he’s doing on those fronts?

Hausmann: I think this is very reasonable. First, I was in the audience when he spoke at Davos. I remember feeling that I’m attending a really, really historic speech. That this was a change moment. So I think he has it right.

Canada needs a strategy that increases its sovereignty, and the more it has those options, the better its negotiating capacity will be with the U.S. If you say, well, keep as much of the US market as you can…yes. But that implies becoming less exploitable by the US. And there are some industries that are more vulnerable and there are some industries that are less vulnerable. And then there are other things that you might do that increase your leverage, like, for example, in critical minerals.

There are other industries, for example oil, that right now maybe have fewer options outside the US market, because you designed the industry assuming access to light crudes that you can mix with your heavy crudes and not invest in upgraders, and you don’t have the pipelines to BC and so on. So obviously, that industry has to be rethought in some strategic context that was not relevant before, right?

But I think [Carney’s] right. Raw materials, the natural resources that you export, have a global market. And, you know, the U.S. is a small player in that global market. You have been selling them to the U.S. because they’re near, but that doesn’t mean that you cannot sell them elsewhere. That’s definitely part of your resilience.

But if you want your large cities to participate in the global economy, it won’t be through your natural resources. It will be through the kinds of talent and human capacity that those urban agglomerations enable. And that’s why we think that you need a more complex high-tech component to your growth strategy, and that’s why we emphasize these other industries.

PW: I got a little nervous when you listed those industries, pharma and aeronautics and defense. Canada’s been doing a terrible job at most of those things. There’s been a kind of an unacknowledged debate among political leaders in Canada over whether we want to have a high-skilled economy or a low-wage economy, and it’s been moving more towards low wages than to high skills.

Hausmann: Canada has very, very high human capital indicators. You are doing well there, and you’re doing well in your universities. We show in the book that if you compare Canadian cities with U.S. cities and you look at the population of the city and the scientific publications of the cities, Canadian cities punch well.

And you know, you invented the way to produce insulin. I think it’s a problem you shouldn’t walk away from. If I were to compare the elements you have with the elements China had 20 years ago or 10 years ago when they got into pharma, you’re way ahead. They focused on it, and now they’re kicking ass. It’s a question of, you know, do you want to understand what caused this poor performance, and is it fixable?

PW: One of the surprises of your book is how much emphasis you put on housing. It’s seen as an important ingredient in improving quality of life here and ensuring that individuals and families have capital, but you also see it as a driver of innovation and of productivity. How so?

Hausmann: Yes, and growth. Essentially, we try to understand why different industries locate in different city sizes. There are some industries that like small cities, and there are some industries that like big cities. We have a theory of why that is. Industries are characterized by how broad the talent pool is that they need to make what they’re going to make. And the broader the talent pool, the bigger the city you need to locate, because only in a big city will you have the scope and the market depth to get everything you want.

We note that both in Canada, the US, and the rich world in general, the growth of employment has favored the kinds of industries that locate in big cities. And that translates into the fact that in the 1970s in Canada, 50% of population growth happened in the 10 largest cities. That ratio has moved up to 80%.

Then, you have two possible worlds. One world in which housing supply is very elastic. If more people want to be there, you just build more and you get more housing at the same price. The other world is where housing supply is very inelastic. And then if you have more labor demand there, you cannot get more labor supply because they don’t have anywhere to live and you get an increase in real estate prices. And that kicks out of the city all the activities that cannot afford the higher real estate prices. Instead of getting a multiplier effect because these industries are growing, you get a gentrification effect which then reduces the other industries.

We think that that is what’s happening in Canada. It was because of good news in these large cities that you got the big increase in demand, but because your supply was so constrained you got just higher real estate prices, gentrification, and much, much less of a growth bang from these trends. We associate that with zoning and with urban land use.

A very large proportion of zones were allocated to single-family detached homes which occupy an enormous proportion of the metropolitan area of Montreal, Toronto and Vancouver. That low density makes it very expensive to urbanize land.

PW: These questions are kind of a big driver of our domestic politics here, because one slightly oversimplified way to view our Liberal and Conservative parties is that the Liberals are the party of homeowners, and the Conservatives are the party of renters who can’t hope to afford a home. I don’t know if you’ve got any thoughts on that dynamic.

Hausmann: That’s interesting, because the homeowners want to restrict supplies so that the homes that they already own go up in price. But that’s also a policy against future homeowners because it makes their access to homeownership less accessible, right? So, in the book, we think it’s partly because the politics of it is decided at the municipal level. And in the municipal level, the people who live there vote, but the people who wish they could live there don’t vote.

PW: I do want to ask about Venezuela…

Hausmann: I was there 34 years ago and when I was in government, Chavez organized these two military coup attempts.

PW: Now the Americans have come and kicked that political current out of office—or at least claim they have. They certainly went to collect Maduro. Do you think life has improved for Venezuelans as a result of that?

Hausmann: That’s what Venezuelans thought in January. That’s not what they think today. It’s been somewhat of a disappointment. If you go to the World War II Memorial in the National Mall in Washington, D.C., it has a slogan there, it says, “We came to liberate, not to conquer.” And Venezuelans don’t think these days that that was the intention in Venezuela. Trump said at the UN recently: “To the victor go the spoils.”

My take right now is that even though Marco Rubio likes to mention the word “democracy” every once in a while, what you have in practice is a coalition between two kleptocracies. A kleptocracy in the Palacio de Miraflores in Caracas, and a kleptocracy around Mar-a-Lago. And kleptocracies don’t like democracy or rule of law, checks and balances, requirements of accountability, and so on. They like it the way it is. And the recent oil deal is scandalous. It’s not in the interest of Venezuela, or in the interest of oil production. It’s not even in the national interest of the U.S. It’s more in the private interest of those getting oil concessions and so on.

PW: There are people in Canada who say that really the biggest problem is that Canadians are overreacting to the presence of Donald Trump in the White House. That we could have substantially the same commercial relationships we always had if we just get over an aesthetic distaste for the way Trump goes about his business. Do you buy that?

Hausmann: This is a debate among the Venezuelan opposition right now. There are some people who think you have to appease Trump, and that he loves to be adulated. And there are people who have played that game.

I think that they’re right in believing that Trump loves to be adulated. But he despises the adulators. He does not respect the adulators, so, you don’t earn anything from it. Trump understands power and leverage. And if he sees you with something you have that he needs, then he wants to negotiate with you for that which he wants from you.

This is not only a Canadian issue. I think the way to interpret the Liberation Day tariffs that the US imposed in April of last year is really an abandonment of a role that the US had played internationally. The US was the leader of an international coalition that had to oppose the Soviet Bloc. And even though the US was a humongous part of the global economy, say in 1945 or 1948, the US needed to create a coalition of countries and needed to create enough incentives to be part of that coalition.

As a consequence, the US kind of renounced a little bit its ability to exploit its bilateral supremacy over all countries in exchange for some general rules. And trade was an important component of it because the most favored nation principle said that you cannot discriminate across countries that are playing ball.

Trump thought that was a stupid idea for the US, and what the US should do is to try to monetize or exploit every single interdependence with every bilateral relationship. And that’s what they went on to do, to negotiate bilaterally. And in every bilateral relationship, to extract as much as they could. So they went to the Europeans, and they said, You know what? You bring the tariffs down to 0 and I bring the tariffs up to 15%, and if you don’t like it, I walk away from Ukraine. And the Europeans said, Okay.

Now, the question is whether this is a winning strategy for the US. And the optimist in me says that it isn’t. That it’s a mistake. And the reason why it will become a mistake is because it leads countries like Canada to adopt policies to de-risk from the US.

And the actions that countries take to de-risk from the US — we used to talk about de-risking from China—well, all the countries I worked with are trying to de-risk from the US now, because they understand that the relationship is…risky, it’s… unpredictable, it’s capricious.

If they don’t like an ad from Ontario, then there’s a policy reaction. Or if they prosecute somebody in Brazil, then there’s a tariff response. Suddenly the US becomes risky, and people have to diversify the risk. The world is finite, so, you have to get closer to other people. And, and you know, who buys a lot of natural resources? Well, China. So [the US’s behaviour] forces people to get closer to China.

If other countries don’t change their behavior, and if they say, “No, let’s just continue trying to play ball”, then that becomes a winning strategy for the US. You really have to—in some sense—de-risk. And those actions are actually going to lower US risk by changing US behavior. That’s at least the way I think of it strategically.
