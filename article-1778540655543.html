Democrats have, for now, lost the redistricting war

Can enthusiasm still let them win the House?

Halina Bennet
May 11
∙
Paid
 
READ IN APP
 

The dominant news story of the week was the Virginia Supreme Court’s 4-to-3 ruling on Friday, which struck down the Democratic-drawn congressional map that voters approved in a special election on April 21. The state’s highest court found that the legislature, which is led by Democrats, violated the Virginia constitution’s procedural requirements by placing the redistricting amendment on the ballot. It specifically argued that the first legislative vote came after early voting had already begun for a prior election, failing the required “intervening-election” standard.

This verdict is a serious hindrance for Democrats, who believed their map would give them an advantage in 10 of Virginia’s 11 U.S. House seats. The state will now return to its original map, which today sees Democrats holding only six seats out of 11.

The loss stung beyond the seat count. Democrats spent close to $70 million on their redistricting effort. Virginia House Speaker Don Scott said the fight was “always about more than one election,” PBS reports, while Democratic Congressional Campaign Committee Chair Suzan DelBene called it a “setback that sends a terrible message to Americans,” according to The Hill. Virginia Governor Abigail Spanberger said she was “disappointed,” but vowed to focus on voter turnout. Democrats are weighing their options as they try to regain their footing. State officials today asked the U.S. Supreme Court to overturn the decision.

The ruling follows a rapid-fire sequence of redistricting developments that accelerated after the U.S. Supreme Court’s April 29 decision in Louisiana v. Callais, which struck down Louisiana’s majority-Black congressional district and significantly weakened Section 2 of the Voting Rights Act. Justice Elena Kagan wrote in dissent that the decision renders Section 2 “all but a dead letter.”

Tennessee moved fastest last week, taking advantage of the newly weakened protections. On May 7, the Republican-controlled legislature — in a raucous session that drew chants, air horns, and Democratic lawmakers linking arms in protest on the chamber floor — passed a new map that carves up Memphis, the state’s only majority-Black congressional district and its sole Democratic-held seat. State Senator Charlane Oliver stood on her desk holding a banner reading “No Jim Crow 2.0.” Governor Bill Lee wasted no time signing it into law.

Alabama has also passed redistricting legislation pending court approval, and South Carolina is taking preliminary steps.

The redistricting math as it now stands: Republicans believe ongoing redistricting fights could net them as many as 14 House seats, while Democrats see possible gains of roughly six seats of their own. That translates to a potential net Republican advantage of around eight seats from redistricting alone — before accounting for the Virginia setback and pending changes in Alabama, Louisiana, and South Carolina. Cook Political Report analyst Erin Covey estimated the real net benefit for Republicans is likely closer to four or five seats in 2026, even accounting for Virginia.

The House currently sits at 217 Republicans to 212 Democrats.

The enthusiasm environment

Despite Republicans’ redistricting gains, the broader political environment remains challenging for the G.O.P.

The enthusiasm gap between the parties is widening. A CNN analysis published May 7 citing a Marquette University Law School poll found that just 28 percent of Republicans and G.O.P.-leaning independents say they are “very enthusiastic” about voting in the midterms — a 19-point gap behind Democrats and Democratic-leaning voters. That Democratic enthusiasm number, 47 percent, is comparable to 2018, a year when Democrats swept the House. An NPR / PBS / Marist poll from the same week, also cited by CNN, found 61 percent of Democrats say they’re “very enthusiastic” about voting, against 53 percent of Republicans — and that Democrats’ share in this poll is essentially unchanged from the 2018 wave election.

But structural problems for Democrats persist, despite the party’s relative enthusiasm and Donald Trump’s tanking support — which, according to The Economist, has fallen underwater in all but five states.

Still, six special House elections held in 2025 and 2026 have produced an average Democratic swing of about 15 points, and Democratic gubernatorial wins in New Jersey and Virginia last November came with similarly large overperformances. That suggests the generic ballot polling, which shows Democrats with a lead of roughly five or six points depending on the aggregator, may be understating the eventual wave.

The election integrity front

But a separate concern is developing: the state of local election administration. A Brennan Center for Justice survey published last month found that more than half of local election officials are worried about political interference in their work ahead of November. About 71 percent reported at least some apprehension that politics would interfere in their work, and 45 percent expressed concern about politically motivated investigations of themselves or their peers. Seventy-five percent said federal cuts to election security support have not been addressed with additional resources, leading 71 percent of officials to begin preparing independently for disruptive scenarios.

PBS NewsHour reported that Trump called the Virginia redistricting vote a “rigged election” and a “travesty of justice” — without evidence — after Democrats won it, exemplifying the environment of mistrust and hostility that election administrators are navigating. Experts and state officials are actively gaming out contingencies for potential federal interference on Election Day, including scenarios involving immigration enforcement agents near polling places.

A separate integrity concern surfaced this past week in an NPR investigation published May 7, which found that campaign staffers are using private internal polling data to profit on prediction markets like Kalshi and Polymarket. NPR spoke with a staffer working on a statewide campaign in the South who described receiving a tip about an unreleased outside poll showing their candidate up by a significant margin — better odds than the prediction markets were pricing in. The staffer and others placed bets before the poll was released, watched the odds shift in their favor when it came out, and pocketed thousands.

“Sure enough, as soon as that poll came out, the stock went up and everybody made money,” the staffer told NPR anonymously.

The practice exists in a legal gray zone. Election-betting has been permitted in some form since 2014, and for-profit platforms like Kalshi now host billions of dollars in political wagers, NPR reports. Government ethics rules have not kept pace: There is no prohibition on campaign staffers trading on nonpublic polling information, and Polymarket, where some of the trades occur, operates largely offshore and outside U.S. regulation. Kalshi has taken some enforcement action, including by suspending and fining three users in April after an internal probe found candidates had bet on their own races, but the platform’s own credibility is complicated by the fact that Donald Trump Jr. holds financial stakes in both Kalshi and Polymarket.

Several bipartisan bills to restrict political insider trading on prediction markets have been introduced, but none has advanced. Election law experts are calling the current landscape a “Wild West” for campaign insiders, adding a new dimension to concerns about how money and private information intersect with the integrity of the 2026 election.
