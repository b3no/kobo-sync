Nonsense and sensibility

Jane Austen doesn’t think your feelings are valid

A modern reader looks at the title of Jane Austen’s 1811 novel, Sense and Sensibility, and sees two words that mean pretty much the same thing. But to readers in Austen’s time, they were opposites. “Sense” meant judgment or reason, just as it does now. “Sensibility,” however, meant the capacity to feel (and to feel in the body specifically).

Austen came down firmly on the side of sense. But the director of the latest film adaptation of Sense and Sensibility doesn’t want to take a stand either way.

“It’s not one or the other,” Georgia Oakley said of Sense and Sensibility in an interview with the Australian newspaper The Nightly. “That’s what is so lovely about the story because neither one nor the other way of being is right or wrong.”

Austen would be surprised to hear her own novel described in this way.

Sense and Sensibility is the story of two sisters, Elinor and Marianne Dashwood, whose father’s death leaves their family financially vulnerable. As a result, the sisters’ prospects for marriage are severely constrained, a challenge they respond to very differently. The elder, Elinor, feels deeply but prizes restraint, judgment, and duty; Marianne, on the other hand, believes that true feeling should never be suppressed. She recklessly throws herself into a relationship with the man she falls in love with, Willoughby.

Austen’s last word on Marianne Dashwood — the sister who is a conscious embodiment of the cult of sensibility — is that she “was born to discover the falsehood of her own opinions, and to counteract, by her conduct, her most favourite maxims.”

Oakley’s new adaptation, which was released in October and began streaming yesterday, equivocates on the fundamental moral question of the book.

Maybe that’s because Oakley knows that the zeitgeist is on the side of sensibility: “In this day and age, sensibility has essentially won over.”

The cult of sensibility returns

Your feelings are valid.

This therapeutic escape hatch has become a fixture in contemporary vernacular. Valid does not just mean “real” in this usage; it means “well-grounded or justifiable,” “logically correct,” and “appropriate to the end in view.” We live at a time when the expected response to someone’s emotional outburst is not mere comfort but affirmation.

The implication is that feelings are not only to be experienced or named or investigated — they are definitive evidence of something true about the real world. In Oakley’s interview, she framed sensibility’s victory in therapeutic language: “We understand that talking about one’s emotions and being true to who we really are is healthy.”

But the problem Austen was probing is, well, what if your feelings are wrong?

When Sense and Sensibility was published, takes were already flying in a live moral, literary, and political debate over what critics now call the cult of sensibility. This cultural movement was “devoted to tear-demanding exhibitions of pathos and unqualified virtue,” according to Janet Todd.

The cult of sensibility is premised on the ideas of intellectuals like Scottish philosopher David Hume, who wrote that morality “is more properly felt than judged of.” (He and others were reacting to moral rationalists who believed that right and wrong could be worked out like a math problem.)

If virtue lived in feeling acutely, then being quickly and visibly moved was proof of good moral character. The novels of this movement were considered tear-jerkers in a much more literal sense than we would think of today: If you remained dry-eyed, the implication was not just that you didn’t get the art but that you lacked moral virtue. (An 1886 edition of Henry Mackenzie’s The Man of Feeling, the genre’s most famous novel, appended an “Index to Tears“ to keep track of the 47 weepings in the nearly 200-page book.)

In David M. Shapard’s introduction to the annotated edition of Sense and Sensibility, Shapard recounts that one woman “remembered reading The Man of Feeling when she was fourteen and dreading that she would not cry sufficiently ‘to gain the credit of proper sensibility.’”

Adherents to the cult of sensibility prized weeping or uncontrolled paroxysms of temper, and even Hume felt that “reason is, and ought only to be the slave of the passions.” But where he diverged was on the idea that virtue would naturally spring from extreme outbursts of emotion. For Hume, moral sentiments were calm, “so soft and gentle” that we mistake them for ideas. (I don’t know if anyone ever cried 47 times reading Hume.)

The cult’s real enemy was the veneration of composure, calculation, and polish. Jean-Jacques Rousseau, a favorite of the cult, railed against the “perfidious veil of politeness.” His novel Julie spurred readers to write to him in tears. His legacy was the “elevation of sincerity over truth, and feeling over reason,” according to Mary Ann Glendon.

Against that polite order, the cult argued that the unmediated heart is a better guide than the trained mind: The man who weeps, screams, wrenches his hair is good. The man who doesn’t is hiding something.

We see this exact sentiment taking root in contemporary politics.

The politician who gets visibly worked up is “authentic,” and the one who asks for evidence or tries to see both sides is automatically suspect. What kind of person would respond to suffering with a spreadsheet?

Former President Barack Obama discovered this during the BP oil spill, when critics on the left complained that he wasn’t angry enough. One reporter asked the White House press secretary Robert Gibbs whether the public had “really seen rage from the president.” (Gibbs pushed back by saying, “If jumping up and down and screaming were to fix a hole in the ocean, we’d have done that five or six weeks ago.”)

More recently, the abortive Democratic nominee for U.S. Senate in Maine, Graham Platner, was praised for his authenticity. He was angry, profane, blunt, and purported to speak in the language of ordinary people rather than political consultants. In our populist moment, almost no one was focused on whether a man who had never held elected office and had little government experience could either tolerate the Senate or do the difficult work of translating his anger into policy wins.

Being able to articulate what people are angry about is not the same as knowing what to do about it, or how to get it done.

Share

Populism’s defining rhetorical move is to treat anger, grievance, and expressiveness as evidence, moral authority, or proof of authenticity. This worldview has perhaps benefited no politician more than our current president.

Technocracy asks citizens to hand over authority to experts who can work out the general interest through rational analysis. Many of the West’s electorates are in full revolt, demanding that power back.

“The people of this country have had enough of experts,” Michael Gove, then the U.K. justice secretary, said three weeks before the Brexit vote.

Margaret Canovan, who wrote that populism “follows democracy like a shadow,” located its source in “a deep revulsion against institutions that come between the people and their actions”; populists proclaim “the vox populi unmediated.”

But sense, as valorized by Austen, is mediation — the pause between feeling and action, the promise that binds Elinor to silence even as it pains her in the moment, the rule that applies to everyone.

Sensibility experiences this mediation as a betrayal of true feeling — just as populism experiences mediating institutions like the courts, central banks, and the deep state as impositions that can only hinder its aims.

Look how they massacred my boy

In Oakley’s adaptation, neither sense nor sensibility gets its full due.

If Oakley had mounted a serious defense of sentimentality and subverted Austen’s book to do it, that would have been at least interesting. But she doesn’t seem to even engage with the questions Austen’s contemporaries were grappling with about virtue and what it meant to be a good person. Instead, we’re presented with a modern form of emotional indulgence totally divorced from any higher ideal.

The film strips Elinor of her sharp and careful mind, Marianne of her creed, Edward Ferrars of his unwavering ethical commitment, and Colonel Brandon of his even temper. Oakley treats sensibility as mere emotiveness, a matter of having feelings and showing them. For Austen’s Marianne, it was so much more: a moral code that demands an absolute commitment to sensation.

Though Austen’s Marianne is frustrating and overwrought, she clearly understands herself to be pursuing a higher truth or beauty through her indulgence of sentimentality.

After Willoughby leaves early in the novel, Austen writes: “Marianne would have thought herself very inexcusable had she been able to sleep at all the first night after parting from Willoughby. She would have been ashamed to look her family in the face the next morning, had she not risen from her bed in more need of repose than when she lay down in it.”

Marianne “courted the misery” in books and music, and “this nourishment of grief was every day applied.” This is not merely youthful exuberance but a tireless commitment to the discipline of luxuriating in grief.

Marianne doesn’t just treat her feelings as evidence of what she wants; the fact that something feels right is all the proof she needs to decide what is the right course of action — even when her actions diverge from societal norms and expectations.

Earlier in the novel, when Marianne is found out after spending time alone with Willoughby (scandalous!), Elinor gently questions the propriety of such an act.

Marianne responds that “if there had been any real impropriety in what I did, I should have been sensible of it at the time, for we always know when we are acting wrong, and with such a conviction I could have had no pleasure.”

Marianne’s feelings are valid, so she couldn’t have been doing anything wrong.

More or less the entirety of Austen’s novel is an attempt to refute this worldview. But in the film, we never even learn what Marianne believes. She has the temperament of sensibility — she is passionate, expressive, and quick to tears — but none of its creed.

In Austen, Elinor’s patience and commitment to reason are her two best qualities. She is worried about how certain everyone else is regarding Willoughby’s attachment to Marianne. But in the movie, Elinor feeds into Marianne’s excitement, leaving no one to play the role of sober-minded sister.

By stripping Elinor of her sense, Oakley offers a completely different account of her character.

While Austen’s Elinor keeps secrets from her sister because of a promise she made to someone else, Oakley’s Elinor seems to conceal for the sake of concealment. Near the end of the movie, Oakley diverges from the text in a fiery conversation between Elinor and Edward where Elinor lies directly about her feelings for him.

It’s not only Elinor who is denied the virtue of sense. In the book, Edward denies himself what he wants in order to be true to his word — but in Oakley’s version, he is willing instead to throw away his word if Elinor will only ask him.

But while Oakley erases sense from its pivotal role in Austen’s plot, she doesn’t side with sensibility either: When Willoughby abandons Marianne, Oakley gives us a tearful, rain-soaked breakdown but none of Marianne’s justifications for wallowing. Oakley wants the tears but not the creed that could make them meaningful. At the heart of the film is a vacuum.

The abuse of reason

While Sense and Sensibility is Austen’s most hit-you-over-the-head-with-the-point novel — forgive her, she was around 19 when she wrote the first draft — she isn’t ignorant of the limits of sense. Her characters often pretend to be reasonable without actually doing any of the necessary thinking.

The book opens with the death of Elinor and Marianne’s father. Their half-brother John is to inherit the family estate, having made a deathbed promise to his father to provide for his stepmother and half-sisters. He initially intends to give his sisters a thousand pounds each, but his wife slowly talks him out of it.

At every step, the couple finds perfectly reasonable-sounding justifications for their increasing selfishness. They have their own child to think of! Their sisters will probably get married anyway, and then they won’t even need the money! Surely their father never intended them to deprive their own family!

By the end of the conversation, John has reasoned himself into believing that his sisters are so comfortable that they might be better positioned to give him money.

This is the abuse of reason. The fact that you can construct a logical argument for something doesn’t mean that you’ve arrived at the right conclusion. Sometimes you have just found an elaborate way to justify doing exactly what you wanted to do in the first place.

Nor does Austen confuse a lack of emotional expression with good judgment. When Elinor meets the reserved Lady Middleton, she quickly perceives that “her reserve was a mere calmness of manner with which sense had nothing to do.” Lady Middleton isn’t especially thoughtful or perceptive. She’s just… boring.

And Colonel Brandon, arguably the novel’s most admirable example of a sober-minded man, loves Marianne for her capacity for deep feeling. He worries that when the idealism of youth gives way, it is often replaced by opinions that are conventional and dangerous.

Austen’s Elinor is no dour scold, either. When the absurd Robert Ferrars lectures her about something he clearly knows nothing about, she simply agrees with everything he says; she does not think he deserves “the compliment of rational opposition.”

What distinguishes Elinor from Marianne isn’t that she feels less. Austen tells us from the beginning that Elinor’s feelings are strong. It’s that Elinor understands her feelings don’t relieve her of her obligations to other people — or of the responsibility to think clearly about what is true.

Austen isn’t asking us to be less human or even less emotional. There is no virtue in being unmoved. What she’s asking is for us to reject the school of thought that treats every strong feeling as a moral verdict or evidence of a greater truth.

Your feelings are not always valid. They might point you the right way, and they might point you the wrong way. The only way to find out is to use your mind.
