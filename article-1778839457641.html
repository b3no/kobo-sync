How to know what politicians really think

Plus AOC vs. Mamdani, the DC mayor’s race, and plot vs. story

Had a few questions about the DC mayor’s race, so I just wanted to aggregate my answers together at the top and say that over and above my serious doubts about Janeese Lewis George’s positions on crime¹ and education,² there is a clear affirmative case for Kenyan McDuffie. I disagree with some of his takes on traffic cameras and historic preservation, but where I really agree with him is his correct diagnosis of the actual specific situation facing the city right now. We in DC were hit unusually hard by Covid because the federal government was much slower than the private sector to resume in-person work.

And then, just when the Trump administration curtailed remote work, it hammered the city’s economy with DOGE. This is having all kinds of downstream adverse consequences for the city’s restaurants and other businesses and taking a toll on working-class people. McDuffie is doing an excellent job of understanding that this is the problem we are facing and that the solution has to be a city government that is focused on reviving private-sector economic growth. These are issues specific to Washington, while George is essentially copy-and-pasting a progressive playbook out of New York or Boston, which are cities facing very different circumstances. I’ve also been disappointed to see many local YIMBY groups endorse as if we are replaying the New York mayor’s race, which featured a socialist who favored upzoning facing off against Andrew Cuomo, a “moderate” NIMBY backed by all the building trade unions.

The actual election that DC is having features two candidates, both of whom are proposing large changes to the comprehensive plan and the city’s land-use framework. George has offered a higher target number of new units of housing as part of a comprehensive pattern of saying yes to every interest group demand from every quarter, but is also proposing many different large regulatory-burden increases that will make new housing investment impossible. McDuffie’s pro-growth housing proposals are grounded in an overall pro-growth platform that I think might actually succeed because, again, he is paying attention to what the actual situation is — job losses, building closures, and capital flight — and thus is trying to solve real problems.

The Digital Entomologist: A couple weeks ago Matt said that politicians who need to BS their voters should let him know their true policy objectives so he can help them. I guess a sort of Irving Kristol’s 4 truths type of thing. Is there a way, in general, to discover a politician’s true policy objectives as just a regular voter who is interested in finding them? Or do you have be in the back rooms and have deep sources?

It is extremely hard to tell what politicians really think, not only because it is generally hard to truly know the mind and soul of another human being, but also because most politicians don’t really think that way. A good politician will have a couple of issues he cares a lot about and works hard on and is very knowledgeable about, but then mostly passes through life addressing things either on the basis of going along with his colleagues or else some specific consideration related to politics in his home state or home district.

I don’t think it would occur to the vast majority of members of Congress to seek out factual information about the climate impact of liquefied-natural-gas exports and develop an accurate opinion about it.

The only possible thing that could result from something like that is you might develop an opinion that gets you in trouble politically. This is why I spend so much more time talking about the groups and their funders than about elected officials. Most politicians most of the time are just triangulating a set of electoral and coalition-management concerns and do not have the disposition to peer deep into the merits. By the same token, this is why governors are systematically more sensible than senators: When you hold a responsible office that forces you to address fiscal tradeoffs and be held accountable for results, you need to start caring more about the merits. But very few political jobs are like this.

So how can you tell? I really wasn’t joking that talking to me and other takeslingers is a good way for politicians to try to convey information to high-information voters about what they really think. Off-the-record chats are good — it’s worth taking seriously the possibility that writers whose judgement you find generally reliable may have access to information you don’t and that you should trust writers like that to some extent when we evaluate politicians.

On-the-record discussion is good, too. Zohran Mamdani recording an interview with a skeptical Derek Thompson where he explained what he liked about the abundance message and name-checked the sewer socialists of early 20th-century Milwaukee was an intriguing choice. Once you start trying to cultivate a reputation as that sort of guy, then if you win you need to try to follow through on that. I think there are real structural limits to how much sewer socialism can be achieved in the context of a blue state with strong public-sector labor unions without engaging in a hard break with conventional progressive politics. But within those constraints, he really is trying to do sewer socialism. Building that kind of political brand creates pressure to deliver.

Contrast that, say, with Tom Steyer, a smart guy who is going out of his way to convey to everyone that he wants to be seen as a yes man for every progressive advocacy group in California.

Last but not least, it’s worth trying to think about different people’s teams to the extent that they have them. On the campaign trail in 2008, Barack Obama criticized NAFTA, which is a smart thing to do if you’re running in Midwestern primaries against Bill Clinton’s wife. But Obama always had a very conventional economic team. This was a specific case I had in mind when I said that if you want to sling some slopulism on the campaign trail, you should also find ways to directly convey to technocratically minded opinion-leaders that you’re a well-informed person. No president has ever governed like a 100 percent textbook free-trader, but Obama always made it clear that he knew what the textbooks said and was going to have an economic policy team that dispensed normal economic advice. Donald Trump, by contrast, has always been very interested in cranks like Peter Navarro.

Of course, you can’t always know a lot about which advisors a politician is relying on. But to me, something like Chris Murphy bashing airline deregulation is a red flag because there’s no particularly obvious reason why a senator from Connecticut would be addressing a policy controversy from the Carter administration.

Johnson: Why does AOC draw so much more hostility from internet leftists than Mamdani, despite broadly similar politics, and despite the fact that he has actually substantively moderated more these four months than she has over six years? Is there another explanation besides gender?

I think the biggest difference is that the one topic Mamdani has really done nothing to demonstrate moderation or pragmatism on is Israel, and Israel is very much the “current thing” on the left. Ocasio-Cortez, by contrast, keeps getting into hot water with elements of the left specifically over Israel. For years, for example, she kept voting in favor of funding the Iron Dome and other defensive weapons systems. She has reversed course on that, but the latest blowup came when she said that she’s not entirely comfortable with the Strange New Respect being accorded on the left to Marjorie Taylor Greene.

This will probably not help AOC out with her enemies, but I thought it was a good take on her part. A lot of Israel critics used to note that Taylor Greene tended to get an undeserved free pass on spreading antisemitic conspiracy theories because she didn’t criticize Israel, while politicians who advocated for Palestinian interests were constantly put under the microscope. Taylor Greene ended up eventually flipping and becoming stridently anti-Israel. But given the fact that she was talking about Jewish-weather-control conspiracies years before she developed concerns about American military aid to Israel, this seems like a pretty clear-cut case of someone who is hostile to Israel because she doesn’t like Jews rather than because of her belief in the universal value of human rights.

The fact that AOC sees this distinction while her critics like Ryan Grim do not I think shows that Israel just does not play that central of a role in AOC’s worldview, whereas to Grim and many contemporary leftists this is the critical moral issue of our time.

I see Mamdani as almost the exact opposite of AOC in this regard. He showed up on campus at Bowdoin and co-founded a Students for Justice in Palestine chapter. That’s the issue that got him interested in politics. And at the time he was in school and when he graduated, the only place on the American political spectrum where anti-Zionist views were welcome was on the far left. But that’s a little bit incidental. If you go to Turkey or Iraq or some other Muslim-majority country, it’s not as if every single person who has stridently anti-Israel views also has left-wing opinions on climate change or transgender issues or economics. So as Mamdani has had to focus more and more of his practical attention on public policy issues facing New York City, he’s found a good measure of pragmatism while staying core to his original views on Palestine.

The other (but related) problem for AOC is that fundamentally left-wing people are extremely interested in what you can achieve by winning primaries in safe blue areas. There have been about 1,000 times as many articles written by progressive journalists about Graham Platner than about Roy Cooper because progressives just sincerely do not find beating Republicans in red-leaning areas to be an interesting topic. The problem with this worldview is that you can’t actually achieve very much by winning primaries in safe blue areas. AOC knocking off a member of Democratic Party leadership in a 2018 primary bid was fascinating, but essentially nothing changed as a result of that. The correct thing for left-wing people to do would be to notice that if you care about things like progressive taxation and giving poor people health care, electing AOC types in safe districts doesn’t accomplish anything while electing people like Marie Gluesenkamp Perez and Vicente Gonzalez in red districts accomplishes a lot.

But rather than revisit the premise, people are getting restless with AOC. If she’d run for mayor in 2021 or challenged Schumer in a 2022 primary or was running for governor in 2026, then she’d be exciting to left-wing people who love blue-on-blue primaries. The longer she stays in the House, though, the more she’s just experiencing convergent evolution to every other House member — writing bills, hoping to retake the majority, gaining seniority — which I think is great but is fundamentally boring to progressives who only care about blue-on-blue primaries. If she spends the summer hinting about a presidential campaign and announces soon after the midterms, she’ll be the left’s factional champion and be exciting again. But if she takes a pass and dedicates her life to being an effective and responsible member of the House, people will keep coming up with reasons to get mad at her.

Brian: Recently, Obama said, “What I’m more interested in for Democrats is, do you know how to just talk to regular people like we’re not in a college seminar? Can you talk in plain English to folks?” Obama has made similar comments at various points in his post-presidency. Am I right to say that Obama’s comments chiding Democrats have generally fallen into the category of the “dog-whistle moderation” that you criticize? Am I right to think that Obama has appeased the Democratic Party too much and squandered his unique credibility with the Democratic Party in an effort to maintain his popularity?

I wouldn’t say he’s “appeased too much,” but I agree with your take that he’s engaged in too much dog-whistle moderation. I think this is clearly a take that is meant to have the upshot “Democrats should be more moderate” but, as with his various invocations against cancel culture, it lacks specificity. Beyond that, it even lacks a recognition of tradeoffs. Something Obama said to a group that I was part of once back before he won the 2008 nomination was that if Lyndon Johnson had run in 1964 on overturning miscegenation laws, he might not have been in a position to appoint the Supreme Court justice who helped overturn them. Obama flip-flopped on marriage equality before the Obergefell decision came down, but he nominated Justices Sotomayor and Kagan back when he was officially opposed to gay marriage. In both of those cases, the politically expedient position was also right on the merits.

In the case of Democrats’ post-Obama leftward pivot on teacher compensation, I think they’ve landed on a position that is both worse politically and also worse on the merits. Obama’s second-term executive actions on immigration were a calculated political risk that backfired and left immigrants worse off than they were before.

I don’t want to see Obama try to dictate the party’s policy positions on everything, but I think some honesty with donors and staffers and elected officials about the kind of tradeoffs he wrestled with would be more constructive than this kind of vagueness. I’m sure Obama was often frustrated by people like Mary Landrieu and Byron Dorgan, but his ability to achieve things was downstream of their presence in the Senate. And they didn’t win those races with “vibes” — they were genuinely more moderate (or shmoderate, if you prefer) than he was. And while he personally was a progressive factional favorite in the 2008 primary thanks to Iraq, by 2008 “invading Iraq was bad” was a mainstream consensus position. He was constantly attentive to the actual contours of public opinion. Not just in terms of using relatable language, but in terms of the substance of what he said.

John Freeman: A few weeks ago you wrote that American prisons are too violent and should be relatively calmer places. What would paths to achieving this look like?

Arnold Ventures and the state of Pennsylvania are funding an experimental Nordic-style prison to try to assess whether this more expensive, more humane mode of incarceration leads to less staff turnover (which would reduce the actual cost of implementation at scale) and better recidivism outcomes. If it performs on recidivism, then there will be a strong case for expanding the model and hopefully we will see elected officials willing to follow the evidence.

Ed: Since you got into reading novels, can you pass on a question to your father, Rafael Yglesias, about what’s wrong with contemporary fiction?

Last summer he posted an interesting comment in response to this substack:

“I’m tempted to say it’s because they don’t read my novels but self-pity is no substitute for analysis, although it’s often a starting point. My real belief is that the widespread study of literature in academia planted the seeds of its decline in a variety of ways, culturally and in the marketplace …. A beautifully written work that has no story isn’t a successful literary work and for a long time now they have been hailed as such.”

Henry Oliver responded:

“I agree with all of this but it ought to be possible for it to survive outside academic study. Lots of writers didn’t study Eng Lit.”

Your father responded:

“I think the impact on the gatekeepers and readers is more significant. People believe for a novel to be serious it shouldn’t have a plot, the language should be a struggle to read easily, and it should refer to other literary works, blow up genres and so on. They are challenging to read in every way but emotionally, and unlikely to sell. So yes literary writers can avoid academic approaches to writing by writing for TV and movies but that doesn’t help the novel thrive as a dramatic form that engages the reader with their own lives in a serious way … the main issue for me has been the separation of a belief in story from elegance of language. And also a lack of awareness that’s there’s a distinction between plot and story that is crucial to understanding what once made literary fiction popular, apart from the obvious lack of competition.”

Henry Oliver responded:

“can you say more?”

But your father hasn’t responded yet. It would be interesting to hear him flesh out these thoughts.

“Distinction between plot and story”? Interesting.

Rather than ask him, I’m going to just try to prove that I’ve listened to my dad over the years. Plot versus story I think is easiest to understand in terms of very lowbrow books that are heavy on plot and light on story. Every Jack Reacher adventure has a plot. He shows up in a town, something happens, he gets sucked into a caper, and a bunch of events occur. These books are designed to be exciting so there’s a lot of bang-bang-bang events.

Early Reacher novels also have the outline of a story. Reacher is discharged from the Army, wandering the country and trying to figure things out. He avenges his brother in the first book. In the third book, he reconnects with his mentor and his mentor’s now-adult daughter. In the fourth book, he’s dating Jodie Garber and is considering establishing a permanent place of residence as a civilian. If he’d done it and settled down with Garber and started a family, that would be a proper story with an arc. The problem is that it would either need to be the case that the story of Jack Reacher ends with him settling down, or else that it continues and becomes a very different kind of story about marriage and parenting rather than about beating up criminals with turkey-sized hands. The basic logic of commerce is for Jack Reacher to keep having Reacher-like adventures, so he and Garber break up, he never has another even slightly serious relationship, and nothing about the shifts in American society or the passage of time seem to impact Reacher at all.

Network-television police procedurals have plots, but most of them have very little in the way of story. Law & Order took this to an extreme, giving us detectives and assistant district attorneys who have no life outside the case of the week. The Sopranos is a multigenerational story.

Anyways, I think my dad’s take is that as literary fiction got more influenced by academia and more divorced from the commercial sphere, people not only learned that plot-centric books are lowbrow but also lost sight of the fact that classic works of literature have stories. Crime and Punishment is not a gritty thriller à la Michael Connelly, but there is a crime. There is punishment. Stuff is happening. It’s not a writing exercise. People debate the merits of different translations, but one’s appreciation of the book will ultimately hinge on one’s appreciation of the story. Not coincidentally, Dostoevsky worked at a time when even highbrow novels were meant to be commercially successful mass entertainments. It wasn’t a thing that had developed its own niche economy of prestige, grants, prizes, and workshops totally divorced from breadth of readership.

BK: Should Disney reboot the Star Wars timeline after Return of the Jedi?

To nerd out a bit, there used to be a Star Wars Expanded Universe composed mostly of books and comics that had a fairly coherent canon. The best-regarded of these properties was a trilogy of novels featuring Grand Admiral Thrawn as the main antagonist (he led what remained of the fallen empire’s forces) and set about five years after the conclusion of Return of the Jedi. These books had Luke and Han and Leia as central characters and also introduced a new hero, Mara Jade.

The most logical thing to do for a sequel trilogy would have been to adapt these books into films. But due to the chronology, that would have required recasting the central three characters from the original movies. Disney instead elected to re-use the original actors, which required a whole new plot set much further into the future. But they didn’t want to make a movie just about senior citizens (and I think Harrison Ford had limited interest in doing extensive work) so they also wanted to introduce three new characters. Thus they decided to de-canonize almost the entire Expanded Universe (now rebranded as “Legends”) with the exception of the Clone Wars animated series and make what became Episodes VII, VIII, and IX. Unfortunately, while Episode VII seemed like a promising-but-unoriginal launch to a new trilogy, the next two movies are a mess.

So I think a popular idea among old fans of the Thrawn books is to throw out the sequel trilogy, reboot the canon, recast the parts, and do the Thrawn story.

I am all for this with one significant caveat. Adapting novels into movies is a non-trivial task. And when I look at the whole mass of Star Wars content that’s been produced for film and television since Return of the Jedi, it is mostly bad. Even the stuff that’s kind of good like Rogue One and The Force Awakens and the first season of The Mandalorian is actually pretty bad. Andor has shown that it’s not impossible to make actually good content set in the Star Wars universe, but that show has no Jedi, no Sith, and no lightsabers — it’s this very deliberate effort to wield the commercial appeal of the Star Wars brand to tell a story that is really very disconnected from the main conceits of the Star Wars franchise.

This all just kind of makes me think that if they made a Thrawn series of movies, they would probably be bad just because the people calling the shots don’t seem to be very good at making things that are good. Even Andor, though I like it, is far from my favorite thing that Tony Gilroy has done, so I don’t particularly want them to “put him in charge and fix Star Wars” or whatever — he’s working on a movie called Behemoth and I’m looking forward to seeing it when it’s done.

bulb5: Does Adam Hamilton running for Senate as a Democrat in Kansas have a chance? I’ve been a fan of him for a long time and was inspired by seeing him run, but I wonder if he should have tried the Dan Osborn independent route instead. But Kansas does have a Democratic governor, so they’re not as deep red as you might think.

He absolutely has a chance — it’s just not a very high chance. But I am incredibly supportive of what he is trying to do.

I understand the independent play and I like the people who are trying to run it, but fundamentally I’m skeptical of the strategy. The problem with the independent strategy is that it requires a level of de facto cooperation from the Democratic Party that I think tends to undermine its effectiveness. Saying “I am a Democrat because of XYZ, but I understand that lots of people in this state don’t like Democrats because of ABC and I want to specifically reassure you that I don’t agree with ABC and intend to be an independent voice” is a harder path in some respects but in other ways, I think, an easier and more comprehensible one. If you’re going to do it, you should say you want to be a Democrat in the style of Laura Kelly or Joe Manchin. Say you want to form a cross-party caucus with Susan Collins and Lisa Murkowski and break down the leadership-dominated congressional rules that enforce partisan conformity. Say things that the median voter in Kansas agrees with.

John From FL: What are your thoughts prosecutorial discretion? (Note: not the “too few resources so have to pick the biggest crimes to go after”, but instead “I don’t like these laws so will not enforce them”).

On the one hand it is usually a choice made by an elected executive (District Attorney, President, Governor), so there is some link to an election. But on the other hand, it seems anti-democratic since it diminishes or eliminates the role of the legislature in crafting laws, expecting them to be faithfully executed?

I think this illustrates that trying to assess things based on how “democratic” they are is not that productive. The tradition in the United States is that each county has its own separately elected prosecutor, so prosecutorial divergence can diverge quite a bit from place to place even though the formal criminal code is the same. This doesn’t make a ton of sense to me as a system relative to having a state prosecutorial agency staffed by civil servants and headed by an attorney general who is appointed by the governor. But I think most people would say my proposal is “undemocratic” and that having more elected offices is “more democratic.” That “democratic” system just naturally has the consequence that discretion will be wielded in a non-uniform way, with some jurisdictions behaving in ways that the legislature does not favor.
