Before diving into this week’s Mailbag, let’s talk television. Against my better judgment, I’ve become a fan of the new HBO Max show “Lanterns,” and it’s reminding me why I kind of hate TV. Is the show good? It seems good to me. At least, the three episodes that have aired are good. At least, they seem good. But are they actually good? The truth is that I have no idea. When you read a novel or watch a movie and you decide that it’s good, part of the goodness of the scenes or chapters that come at the beginning is how they relate to the overall story. And more to the point, if someone else tells you that “Moby Dick” is great, then when you come across something in the beginning that seems intriguing, you have reason to believe that the intriguing vibe isn’t just a vibe.

Other people who have actually read the end of the book say that it’s good.

But it’s not just that I haven’t seen the end of “Lanterns” and you haven’t seen it and none of the critics or podcasters talking about it have seen it — there literally is no end for anyone to see. An ending hasn’t been filmed. It hasn’t been written. It doesn’t exist. All this intrigue and vibes and setup and flashback could mean nothing. I was really happy to sit out watching “The Americans” while a lot of people I knew were singing its praises, then fire up the first episode after the final one aired and people were still saying it was great. And it is indeed great! I can tell you that because I know how it ends and it really all hangs together.

Michael Tolhurst: I am sort of curious. Have you investigated the housing stock and regulatory framework for housing in the Austro Hungarian Empire? Could the challenges of the Empire been solved by more market rate housing in growing industrial urban zones for national minorities to improve their economic standing and have more commitment to a joint polity? (Or, another way to put it, does the housing theory of everything really generalize *to everything*?) Is there an exciting alternate history genre waiting to be launched based on counterfactual housing policy?

I am only slightly knowledgeable about this, but I have looked into it a little because there is an obsession throughout the worldwide left with the successful public housing program that was inaugurated in the “Red Vienna” days of the 1920s and continues today in Austria. I think there are a lot of problems with the way this is usually invoked, and part of that involves dropping the historical context.

Obviously Vienna in 1910 was the capital of a large empire while by 1920 it was the capital of a rather small country that was ravaged by war and whose whole economy was in tatters. This is not really a situation that has much in common with any contemporary city, and it meant there were a lot of weird dynamics in place.

If you flip back to the prewar years, though, Vienna was growing very rapidly. I don’t know exactly what their land use policies were, but this is similar to what we see in the industrializing boomtowns of the American Midwest. It’s a reminder that zoning-induced housing scarcity is, broadly speaking, a postwar phenomenon. All across the Western world we used to know how to let housing supply grow with demand.

In 1850, the municipal boundaries of Vienna were expanded to include suburban districts beyond what’s today known as the “inner city” that was bounded by the old city walls. Soon after, the walls and adjacent fortifications were torn down to make space for new buildings and the construction of what today is the Ringstrasse. By the 1890s, the precursor to what today is the Vienna S-Bahn was in operation. So, contrary to the presumption of this question, I don’t think we can attribute anything in particular to housing issues when we’re looking at the problems of the late-phase Habsburg Empire. They seemed to have a pretty reasonable housing situation.

Beyond Vienna, as far as I know other Habsburg cities were also growing. The 1910 census, for example, apparently showed that 43 percent of the residents of Trieste were born outside the municipality. Of those about a quarter were immigrants from Italy (Trieste was a majority Italian-speaking city), and the rest were from elsewhere in the empire.

Several years ago, when I made the case that the collapse of the Habsburg Empire was far from inevitable and the polyglot polity could have survived indefinitely if not for defeat in World War I, this internal migration was key to my argument. Austria was a relatively late industrializer compared with the leading countries of Western Europe, but by the 1910s the Industrial Revolution was beginning to take off. There were, however, marked regional differences inside the empire with the western parts considerably more advanced and prosperous than the eastern parts. In practice, this meant there was a lot of concrete economic value in the way that membership in the empire meant that people who spoke Romanian or Ruthenian or Slovene or Croatian or Polish had the ability to migrate to richer cities like Vienna and Prague. For much of the 19th century, lots of people were moving from Central Europe to the United States of America. But right around when World War I broke out, America was moving in the direction of restricting immigration, so the right to internal migration would have become more valuable.

Conversely, the aforementioned Trieste went into a spiral of decline when it switched from being the main port of the Austro-Hungarian Empire to one of dozens of mid-sized Italian coastal cities.

There would have been, in other words, pretty strong countervailing reasons for members of minority national groups to not want the empire to break up. But that internal migration would have, of course, also created tensions of its own. Big influxes of non-Germans into Vienna would have generated NIMBYism and what you can’t quite call “anti-immigration” sentiment but something politically similar. Again, though, this simply points to the need for the imperial government to act as an honest broker rather than as an instrument of German oppression, helping to manage a multi-lingual entity in a way that would facilitate peace and prosperity for everyone — just as today’s European Union has a lot of problems and controversies but is also broadly recognized as offering a lot of concrete advantages.

Matthew: Alternate history question of the week! Two American Civil War scenarios to consider:

1) The First Battle of Bull Run ends in a decisive Confederate defeat, destroying the Confederate army in northern Virginia and opening the road to Richmond. Is there an outcome where the American Civil War ends up being a few months of political violence followed by some kind of negotiated settlement for the Confederate states to return to the Union (with slavery likely intact)?

2) Rather than surrender and go home, Confederate forces in 1865 disperse into the hills and maintain a guerrilla war against Union forces. What does a prolonged campaign of insurgency/counterinsurgency in the South look like for post-war American politics? Would a violent Confederate insurgency result in a better outcome for the African-American population in the South, with U.S. Government forces reliant on them as valuable local allies (rather than as a victimized group that needed protecting and could ultimately be politically bargained away, as in actual Reconstruction)?

On (2), in terms of the perennial debates about what the war was really about, the fact that guerrilla fighting didn’t happen is very telling. Letting the Union take the cities and the railroad lines and fighting an endless insurgency from the countryside would, after all, be the normal way for a war of national liberation to proceed. But if you head to the hills to fight as a guerrilla, you can’t hang on to your estates and your slaves. And the goal of the war was to hang on to the estates and the slaves. Once it became impossible to win, the Southern elite surrendered on terms that would let them keep their estates even if they had to give up the slaves.

So I think the big difference in an insurgent war scenario is really that you would have ended up with land reform: rebel estates seized and allocated to freedmen (the fabled “40 acres and a mule”) or white collaborators.

For roughly the same reasons that the Democrats eventually won power and abandoned Reconstruction, I think northern whites would have lost patience with an endless guerrilla war in the South and backed down eventually. But as you suggest, it would have probably led to the creation of a more entrenched class of armed African American small farmers who would have been harder to displace than in the historical Redemption process where Southern whites regained political control of their states and disenfranchised African American voters. This is kind of wild speculation, but it’s possible that the ultimate political settlement of this longer-running and more violent version of the conflict would have been population transfers and a kind of de facto partition of the South.

In the 1880 census, considered a lot more reliable than the 1870 one, three Southern states — South Carolina, Mississippi, and Louisiana — had a Black majority and Georgia and Alabama were both at 47 percent. What if four or five of the 11 Confederate states had been Reconstructed permanently and the other six or seven Redeemed with population migrations between the two blocs? One could try to spin this out.

Separately, the Bull Run question is interesting. It seems like, yeah, the Confederacy might have collapsed fast. Then it would have turned out that when Southern senators resumed their seats in Washington, the Republicans would only have 31 seats out of 68. So it’s not just that moderate Republicans like Abraham Lincoln would have promised not to end slavery in the existing states; it’s not clear how the Republicans would have actually been able to enact any of their ideas about halting the spread of slavery into the territories. The whole secession concept was a panicky overreach that actually brought about the Radical Republican legislative hegemony that Southern whites were afraid of.

Dan Diamond: Do you see a realistic path to revitalizing downtown DC and local real estate markets to the city’s pre-covid, pre-DOGE days?

One thing is that I sincerely think that while every other place on earth zigs against data centers we in D.C. ought to zag. The District of Columbia is unusual in not offering a sales and use tax exemption for people buying computer equipment to put in a data center, and we ought to consider changing course on that and clarifying our industrial zoning to facilitate data center construction. Our property tax base is seriously ailing at the moment, and it seems like an opportune time to grab some investment that will create jobs short term during a localized recession in construction and that will support the tax base over the longer term.

Beyond that, what we have right now is a central business district where the office space is like 20 percent empty and as a result the ground-floor-retail ecology in the central business district is also ailing.

The medium-term solution for this is for the office district to shrink geographically. Right now, there are non-trivial numbers of office buildings in the West End, Navy Yard, and NoMa, and those buildings should mostly close and be replaced by apartments and condos. Then the current office tenants in those neighborhoods could relocate to downtown proper, at which point the vacancy rate would normalize and the ground-floor-retail situation downtown would normalize as well.

A big problem that the city is having at the moment is that not only is the general DOGE shock to the local economy hurting demand for residential real estate, but the D.C. Council and the court system have also done a bunch of stuff to make us less attractive as a place to build and invest. The new mayor is committed to zoning reform and secured the support of most of the city’s local YIMBY activists. Allowing accessory dwelling units and rowhouses in the most exclusionary neighborhoods should do something to juice construction, but unfortunately with current demand conditions and capital competition I don’t think zoning changes are really going to get new multifamily builds. Smaller projects might be financially viable because they are subject to lower regulatory burdens, but if the mayor wants to make her YIMBY dreams come true, she needs to look at the broader regulatory system. Some of that is eviction rules and some of it is inclusionary zoning (which, remember, is a tax on housing), but some of it honestly is just rhetoric and openness.

Investors have gotten spooked over the past several years by the political direction of the city and that’s shut investment down. Electing a democratic socialist mayor doesn’t help.

On the other hand, to some extent it just means the thing investors feared would happen did happen. If the worst-case-scenario mayor showed up before taking office with some business-friendly regulatory reforms and then started taking meetings and speaking publicly about how she understands the need to attract private capital, that could really turn sentiment around.

The point of all of this is that despite the currently sagging market, the absolute price of housing in D.C. remains high. With a sensible, pro-growth regulatory structure, new multifamily housing should make sense financially. In fact, new multifamily housing is getting built in Virginia as we speak, despite it being exposed to the same regional economy. If there is demand for new multifamily housing, then the office district could shrink but become more vibrant and there would be plenty of demand for the city’s stores and restaurants. But if investors remain spooked, things will just sit partially empty instead of being repurposed.
