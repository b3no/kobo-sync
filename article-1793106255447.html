From Here to Eternity

In Matthew Aldrich’s thriller, “The Natural Order,” a Silicon Valley visionary conquers aging — but the kids are not all right.

Ron Charles
Oct 27

The Tuesday Review

I started reading Matthew Aldrich’s The Natural Order just a few hours after I got a colonoscopy. In the end, the novel was better. But both experiences put me in mind of what we’re willing to endure to live longer.

Immortality has been a preoccupation since we climbed down from the trees. More than 4,000 years ago, Gilgamesh dove deep to snatch a prickly plant that could make him young again. Alas, a snake stole that plant while he was taking a bath — a good reminder of the risks of bathing — and ever since, we’ve been lucky to make it four score and seven years.

Indeed, just two weeks ago, the New York Times published a whole section on longevity, which you’ll need to get through it.

There was a time when nothing was certain but death and taxes, but having escaped taxes, the super-rich are now zeroing in on death. Various Silicon Valley entrepreneurs and modern-day Ponce de Leóns have been swallowing hundreds of pills a day, siphoning off their children’s blood, and freezing their brains in hopes of attaining the imperishability of canned beets.

A cheeky Oxford researcher named Saul Justin Newman recently published Morbid: Debunking Modern Longevity Science, which dismembers this life-extension quackery like a cadaver in gross anatomy. But Aldrich’s new novel cuts through the longevity movement by imagining its success.

The year is 2070. Paul Whitmore, the founder of Benetus, is one of the world’s wealthiest people and “the most polarizing cultural figure of our time.” Rather than build spaceships or cripple democracies, Paul has devoted his tech fortune to inventing the Method, a regimen of shots and algae shakes that stops cellular aging. His days, spent alone in a neo-brutalist mansion, consist of meditation, video calls, and jogging around his vast Bay Area estate purchased from the National Park Service. Although he still looks as youthful as Paul Rudd always will, this Paul, at 122, is about to break Jeanne Louise Calment’s record and become the oldest person who has ever lived. Countdown clocks have popped up everywhere. “The largest stood in Times Square,” Aldrich writes, “where, when tidewaters abated, thousands would pause to watch it tick inexorably to a post-death future.”

For Melania Trump, this may be a horror novel; for the rest of us, it’s a curious thought experiment. What would we pay to escape death? Would it be worth living like Paul in a state of semi-starvation and eating only disks of nutritious goo? How exactly would we fill all those endless days after catching up on The Simpsons episodes we missed in the 2050s?

Aldrich sets up the premise with countervailing winds of reverence and creepiness. Having outlived almost everyone he once knew, Paul is now more guru than CEO. His company — and especially its soaring stock price — depends upon his well-publicized vitality during the weeks leading up to the very limited public release of the Method. Wealthy customers and powerful politicians are wheedling their way to the front of the line, while governments around the world brace for the social disruption of a new class of immortals.

Of course, the treatment is not without detractors, who raise a lifetime’s worth of practical, economic, and moral objections. Two dozen countries have preemptively outlawed the Method. Like some monastic vampire, Paul has already spent years considering what to do in the vast expanse of eternity. Lots of other people, though, are starting to freak out. I’d be concerned about where to put all those New Yorker magazines I’m going to get to this weekend, but some people have even bigger worries.

Among the most violent opponents of the Method is a shadowy group called the Natural Order. Unable to penetrate Paul’s well-guarded estate, they target his estranged 85-year-old son, Max, who’s secretly fiddling away his final days in a retirement home. So now Paul must defend his angry, aging son against a band of longevity assassins while guiding his company toward the most momentous product launch since the Crucifixion.

Aldrich may be new to novel writing, but he’s not new to storytelling. He wrote the script for Spinning Man, starring Guy Pearce and Pierce Brosnan, and he cowrote the animated movie Coco, which won two Academy Awards. Tellingly, film rights for The Natural Order sold last year even before the book found a publisher. Glen Powell is set to star, giving Method acting a whole new meaning.

The Hollywood appeal is obvious. The Natural Order has the DNA of a Michael Crichton thriller, which should make it more successful on the screen than it is on the page, where it hangs itself with its own double helix.

I suspect the problem stems from the author’s overidentification with Paul. When it comes to this trillionaire, we want less bedazzled Walter Isaacson and more unsparing Alex Gibney. Paul’s stew of self-aggrandizement, self-pity, and self-delusion requires a degree of irony that’s missing here because, like Fitzgerald, Aldrich is caught between satirizing the rich and idolizing them. But unlike Fitzgerald, he does not write like Fitzgerald. We’re left stranded in far too many of Paul’s philosophical and emotional wonderings. An extended allusion to Hamlet is particularly embarrassing. Dull disquisitions on what running is or how to set a table produce a thriller that keeps choking on its own fumes.

There are two fantastically exciting gunfights that will make cinema gold, but the novel’s tension is sapped by the fogginess of Aldrich’s villain. The Natural Order never comes into focus with sufficiently menacing clarity. In crucial scenes, the text atrophies into a skeleton that some future director will have to meaten up.

For suspense’s sake, I can’t disclose the story’s astonishingly ludicrous ending, but the novel’s prose harbors its own absurdities. For instance, we’re told, “He stepped heel-toe so as not to make a sound,” which is the way everyone walks except ballerinas. And later, “the vehicle came to a stop before tumbling over the edge onto the rocky shore below,” which is not the kind of stop you want to come to.

Faced with an eternity of guilt, Paul struggles to redeem himself as a father, while these little distractions accumulate across the chapters like damaged proteins. But just hang on — surely Glen Powell has the elixir for all these ailments.◆

The Natural Order, by Matthew Aldrich (Atria Books, 340 pp., $29)
