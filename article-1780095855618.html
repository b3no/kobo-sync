Breaking down the Clarity Act

Everything you need to know about the single most significant piece of crypto legislation to pass the House

Halina Bennet
May 29
∙
Paid
 
READ IN APP
 

Since it was formed, the U.S. crypto industry operated under what could most generously be called regulatory ambiguity. The Securities and Exchange Commission under Gary Gensler preferred filing lawsuits over drafting new rules for cryptocurrency, and the Commodity Futures Trading Commission claimed overlapping jurisdiction on some assets. Courts were asked to resolve questions regulators had actively declined to manage, and crypto companies operating in the United States were working based on their own interpretations of the law, leading to expensive and time-consuming corrections.

The Digital Asset Market Clarity Act — also known just as the Clarity Act — is Congress’s attempt at using regulation to avoid that headache and other potential crypto chaos. The act passed the House last July with a 294–134 bipartisan vote, cleared the Senate Banking Committee 15–9 on May 14, and now faces a full Senate floor vote where it will need 60 votes to beat a filibuster. It will need at least nine Democratic votes to meet that threshold.

The bill is the most significant piece of crypto legislation ever to pass one chamber of Congress, let alone both if it were to succeed in the Senate.

What the bill does

Dividing up crypto oversight

The Clarity Act’s central feature is a jurisdictional split related to digital assets that the crypto industry has long lobbied for. Most digital assets, once the blockchain underlying them is sufficiently decentralized and “mature,” would be classified as commodities and regulated by the Commodity Futures Trading Commission (C.F.T.C.). The Securities and Exchange Commission (S.E.C.) would retain jurisdiction over the primary market, meaning initial token offerings. It would also oversee digital assets traded on S.E.C.-registered alternative trading systems.

Defining a commodity vs. a security

The bill defines “digital commodity” as a digital asset whose value is “intrinsically linked” to the blockchain system. The term excludes securities, derivatives, and stablecoins. This is the “mature blockchain” idea: If a network is sufficiently decentralized and functional, the token on it is a commodity (C.F.T.C. territory), not a security (S.E.C. territory). The bill exempts digital commodities on mature blockchains (and those expected to mature within certain timeframes) from S.E.C. registration requirements.

Registration of exchanges, brokers, and dealers

Crypto exchanges, brokers, and dealers handling digital commodities would register with the C.F.T.C. and comply with rules on customer fund segregation and recordkeeping. Anti-money laundering requirements under the Bank Secrecy Act would apply. A separate title would prohibit the Federal Reserve from issuing a retail central-bank digital currency, reflecting the Republican coalition’s deep hostility to any government-issued digital dollar.

Decentralized finance exclusion

Section 409 provides an exclusion for decentralized finance (DeFi) activities. It is one of the bill’s most contested provisions for its exemption of DeFi software developers and peer-to-peer activities (financial activity between users on a blockchain without a middleman like a bank or brokerage) from registration and licensing requirements.

Bank participation

The bill amends the Bank Holding Company Act to allow financial holding companies and qualifying banks to conduct digital commodity activities.

The framework’s hinge is the “mature blockchain” concept. If a network is genuinely decentralized — meaning no single entity controls it — the asset on it is a commodity, not a security. Bitcoin and Ethereum are both considered commodities under this definition. Newer tokens issued by companies still firmly in control of their networks would stay under S.E.C. oversight until the network matures into something more decentralized. In theory, this draws a line between speculative investments and distributed systems that function independently. In practice, the demarcation will be determined through years of rulemaking and litigation, as is the case with most regulations.

The sneaky housing piece

One small detail in the Senate’s Clarity Act text might be of particular interest for anyone following the federal housing debate. Section 904 — which is tucked into Title IX under “Other Matters” in the Senate Banking Committee’s draft published May 12 — revives the Build Now Act as a Community Development Block Grant pilot program. This incorporates a housing proposal that Senators John Kennedy and Elizabeth Warren co-authored for the Senate’s ROAD to Housing Act, which the House stripped out two weeks ago when it passed its version of that bill in a 396-to-13 vote.

Senator Tim Scott, the Republican from South Carolina, chairs the Banking Committee, so he is leading on both bills. When the House killed “Build Now” in the housing context, Scott and the committee dropped it into the crypto bill that was next down the pipeline. Whether the House will accept it there, having just rejected it in a bill actually about housing, is unclear. As Representative Josh Harder told me earlier this week, though, there is hope that the provision will make its way through on the coattails of this crypto bill.

The money and muscle behind the bill

The bill was introduced by Representative French Hill of Arkansas, who chairs the House Financial Services Committee, in May 2025, and the White House was quick to support it, calling the bill a “pro-growth, pro-freedom framework that gives digital asset businesses the certainty they need to build the future of finance right here at home.”

The lobbying muscle is strong. Fairshake had $193 million in cash on hand heading into the 2026 midterms, including recent eight-figure contributions from Coinbase, Ripple, and a16z, Axios reported. The Washington Examiner found that 12 of the bill’s 22 House sponsors were initially backed by the Fairshake network. The crypto industry spent heavily in 2024 to elect a more favorable Congress for their agenda, and they certainly got what they paid for.

The opposition, even with its more limited resources, is pushing back. Traditional banks spent $56.7 million on federal lobbying in 2025, much of it aimed at specific provisions, according to the Examiner. The particular tension is stablecoin yield.

“One of the most contentious aspects of the bill is whether stablecoins, a class of cryptocurrencies intended to maintain a stable value by pegging themselves to a non-volatile real-world asset, can pay their owners rewards for holding them,” the Examiner reports. Coinbase pays roughly 3.5–5 percent A.P.Y. on the balances of stablecoins such as USDC, and banks see this as deposit competition dressed up in regulatory ambiguity, Yahoo Finance reports. The Clarity Act doesn’t fully resolve this fight, which is ultimately between two financial industries, and the crypto industry is, at this time, winning due to political and financial incentive.

The case for Clarity

The strongest argument for the Clarity Act is that the status quo is bad and something has to change. “Regulation by enforcement” — the approach by which agencies establish the law through litigation rather than through rules — can be financially, legally, and innovatively harmful to start-ups trying to build a compliant business, according to a number of legal experts. A primary critique of this kind of regulation is that it favors incumbents who can afford prolonged legal battles, punishes smaller players who can’t, and pushes activity offshore to jurisdictions that have existing frameworks.

Other governments have created their own versions of the Clarity Act. The European Union has Crypto-Assets Regulation (MiCA), a legal framework designed to regulate crypto assets. Singapore has clear rules. Dubai has a highly structured crypto-friendly regulatory system. The United States, meanwhile, has years of S.E.C. litigation and a whole lot of paperwork.

There are also real consumer protections in the bill that represent reasonable improvements over the current nothing. The mandatory segregation of customer assets, qualified custodian requirements, anti-money laundering compliance, and disclosure obligations are all rules that supporters of the bill say could help safeguard against future complications in a new and ever-changing system, the lack of which was made evident in 2022 when FTX collapsed and customer funds were simply missing.

The case against Clarity

On the other hand, the strongest argument against the Clarity Act is that the C.F.T.C. is a smaller agency with fewer resources and a historically lighter touch than the S.E.C., and giving it jurisdiction over a large chunk of the crypto market is an incredibly risky liability.

“The understaffed C.F.T.C. will begin from scratch to oversee an arena that’s flypaper for scammers,” Bart Naylor, financial policy advocate at Public Citizen’s Congress Watch division, told Bloomberg after the bill passed the House last July.

Former S.E.C. Chief Accountant Lynn Turner testified before the Senate Banking Committee in January that the bill is “severely deficient” and risks enabling “another FTX-type fraud,” Thomson Reuters reports. On May 12, the North American Securities Administrators Association (N.A.S.A.A.) — the trade group for state securities regulators — wrote to Congress asking for a “no” vote, specifically citing the bill’s preemption of state-level fraud enforcement authority. The DeFi exemptions drew particular criticism from the N.A.S.A.A., which, in a separate letter, argued the exemptions create a large loophole that could be easily exploited if companies sufficiently decentralized their product.

Some Progressive Democrats, chief among them Elizabeth Warren, are vehemently opposed to the act. “This bill puts investors, our national security and our entire financial system at risk — and it will turbocharge Donald Trump’s crypto corruption,” Warren wrote in a statement after the Banking Committee Republican majority released its new text of the legislation earlier this month.

The ethics problem

The elephant in the room, which has little to do with the S.E.C. or the C.F.T.C. but which Warren identifies, is the ethics question.

President Trump and the first lady have both issued their own meme coins, and his family has billions of dollars in crypto holdings and business interests. The Trump administration is simultaneously the bill’s most enthusiastic advocate and its most obvious potential beneficiary. Democrats in the House, led by Representative Joe Neguse, proposed an amendment that would have barred members of Congress, presidents, and vice presidents from issuing or endorsing digital assets and would have required existing holdings to go into a blind trust. It was defeated in the Rules Committee on a party-line vote.

A similar ban called the Stable Genius Act has been introduced in the Senate, where the issue is similarly unresolved. Democrats who might otherwise support the bill’s market structure framework are using the ethics provision as their bargaining chip for their involvement in the 60-vote coalition.

Patrick Witt, executive director of the President’s Council of Advisors for Digital Assets, said earlier this month that targeted provisions won’t be tolerated as the White House allegedly prepares its own conflict-of-interest provision that will apply “across the board, from the president all the way down to the brand new intern on Capitol Hill,” CoinDesk reports.

Where things stand

The Senate Banking Committee’s vote included all 13 Republicans plus two Democrats — Ruben Gallego of Arizona and Angela Alsobrooks of Maryland. Seven more Democratic votes are needed on the floor, and though the White House set a July 4 aspirational signing deadline, a more realistic timeline, according to the Crypto Citizens Network, is late summer or fall due to a mix of ongoing negotiations, a tight summer schedule, and tightening opposition from the left.
