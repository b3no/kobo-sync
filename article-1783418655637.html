Baltimore’s incredible crime-fighting success story

“Focused deterrence,” explained

Matthew Yglesias
Jul 7
∙
Paid
 
READ IN APP
 

Baltimore has been a notoriously high-crime city for basically all of my life. It is, statistically, a very high-crime city, but its reputation also stems from the fact that the American public’s main exposure has been the television shows “Homicide” and “The Wire,” both of which are largely about crime in Baltimore.

Of course, if you watched the excellent-but-short-lived series “Hannibal,” you’ve seen a different, more upscale side of Baltimore, including some of the city’s genuinely great cultural amenities. But even that one is about a cannibalistic serial killer.

In more recent times, though, Baltimore is best known (at least among policy heads) for one of the most remarkable crime drops in America. Even in the context of national good news about homicides and shootings, Baltimore stands out as the champion among major cities.

I think in a sane world the public officials who spearheaded this would be famous national heroes, but Mayor Brandon Scott hasn’t really gotten the credit he deserves.

That reflects, to an extent, some of his communications decisions. He talks about “gun violence” rather than crime, and his signature anti-crime initiative is called the Group Violence Reduction Strategy, avoiding the word “gang.”

But the actual upshot of Scott-era policy has not been to replace group violence inflicted by firearms with classy, Hannibal Lecter-style solo non-gun killings. There’s just less crime. And while I wish Scott would communicate about this somewhat differently, the thing that really matters here is the results, which are very good, and I want more people to understand how they came about because I think they’re broadly replicable in other large cities.

Baltimore is also interesting for some of its not-so-replicable qualities, though.

There are lots of American cities where ideally we’d see less crime. But Charm City is a place that is uniquely hobbled by crime, not just in the sense that crime is high but in the sense that the basic fundamentals on housing, jobs, and the built environment are otherwise pretty strong. A sustained low-crime spell in Baltimore could easily set off a positive cycle of in-migration and investment that unlocks a ton of new appealing neighborhoods and generates huge gains.

The Baltimore context

On the politics, Scott is an interesting case because he was elected in 2020 after a series of corruption scandals rocked the city’s political establishment. Against the backdrop of the Great Awokening, he positioned himself as a young progressive rather than a tough-on-crime candidate, which is probably one reason he hasn’t gotten the national press attention he deserves. Another notable thing is that if you look at the chart, Baltimore just skipped the entire murder surge that afflicted most of America in 2020 and 2021.

What happened instead is that after enjoying roughly 20 years of crime falling from its 1993 peak, Baltimore saw a huge surge in violence after Freddie Gray’s death in April of 2015.

The police officers involved in this were charged in state court but acquitted at trial, which led to a follow-up federal investigation of the officers that ended with no charges. There was significant rioting in the city of Baltimore associated with Gray’s death and that was followed by a reduction in the intensity of police activity and a rise in crime.

This was an all-around odd time in the politics of American crime.

In Ferguson, Mo., we also saw the Obama Justice Department conclude that no charges were warranted against the officer who shot Michael Brown, along with substantial rioting and a localized increase in crime.

As the late Richard Rosenfeld rightly complained, official crime data is reported with far too long a lag for people to actually know with any confidence in 2016 or 2017 whether it was true that St. Louis and Baltimore were experiencing localized homicide surges due to reductions in the intensity of police effort.

I believe this is clearly true in retrospect (per the Devi and Fryer research), and American progressives should have known this by 2020 but were kind of in denial. At the time of the 2016 election, it was a bit of a hot take that lacked clear empirical support.¹

Back to Baltimore, though.

The city’s huge crime surge in 2015 undid 20 years’ worth of slow, steady progress against murder, and crime then plateaued at a high level.

One consequence was that Baltimore didn’t have major anti-police protests in 2020 or big new explicit (or implicit) policy changes. Scott was kind of a Great Awokening progressive, but he was also running in a city that was already four and a half years into a vicious crime surge, so being anti-crime was part of his politics from the beginning.

Meanwhile, in 2022 Baltimore did have a tough-on-crime backlash as Marilyn Mosby — the progressive prosecutor who charged but then failed to convict the Freddie Gray cops and who also had some unrelated scandals — was defeated by Ivan Bates, who offered a back-to-basics anti-crime message.

Bates and Scott seem to have a bad relationship in which their fighting each other for political credit is making it harder for either of them to get the credit that they want and deserve. They might want to take P.R. lessons from San Francisco, where it’s true that both Dan Lurie and Brooke Jenkins sort of lowkey jostle for credit but are also basically saying the same thing and are visibly cooperating in a way that makes it easier to tell a San Francisco success story.

My verdict on the evidence, though, is pretty clear: Bates is right that replacing Mosby with a competent prosecutor who has a normal working relationship with the police department was a necessary and important reset. He’s also right that Scott has not discovered some kind of magical alternative to the traditional work of police and prosecutors. At the same time, the Baltimore crime decline is much steeper than a “return to normal” and is much larger in extent than the national trend.

You can’t explain an extraordinary effect by citing a normal cause.

Normalcy (i.e., Bates) accounts for quantitatively the largest slice of the crime decline but what’s unusual (i.e., Scott) accounts for the part of the decline that’s most interesting. So I don’t mean to slight Bates, who I think is basically correct — I’ve written a bunch of articles (one, two, three) about the importance of conventional proactive policing.

But the rest of this article is about focused deterrence.

Focused deterrence in Baltimore

The strategy of focused deterrence comes out of work that was pioneered in Boston in the 1990s by criminologist David Kennedy; I learned about this from Paul Grogan’s 2001 book “Comeback Cities.”

The basic idea is that a large fraction of urban shootings are quasi-predictable. Not pre-crime exactly, but predictable in the sense that gangs participate in regular spirals of retaliatory violence. Somebody will get shot or at least shot at. That somebody is a person who himself has a long record of arrests and associates who’ve also been arrested multiple times in the past. Whether or not you can prove beyond a reasonable doubt who exactly shot him — and especially whether or not you can prove that the shooter had accomplices — it is knowable via intelligence gathering which crew it is that people believe the shooter belonged to and who, broadly speaking, is in that crew. You do not need psychic powers to understand that the most likely series of events going forward involves members of the victim’s crew taking shots at members of the perpetrator’s crew, and then members of that crew shooting back.

Jeremy Biddle and Ben Struhl in Vital City and Charles Lehman in the Free Press offer slightly different accounts of how this works, reflecting the fact that the former are academics and the latter is a conservative think tanker. But they’re really saying the same thing.

In the focused deterrence model, you attempt to (a) gather timely intelligence about these predictable cycles of violence and (b) move decisively to prevent them. Step (b) involves delivering somewhat individualized messages to the people who the authorities believe are likely to be victims or perpetrators of crime:

  1. We know you are mixed up in the life.

  2. We know your life may be at risk.

  3. We want you to know that we’re watching you, and if you do crime you will be caught and punished.

  4. We also want you to know that there’s an alternative to being a perpetrator or a victim: getting out of the life.

  5. We are prepared to help you with (4), if you want.

In terms of the debates over whether you reduce crime with services or enforcement, I think what’s important about the focused deterrence model is that from the point of view of the state, you’re doing both. The goal is to present “services or enforcement” as a choice to the potential shooter.

I have personally been on a journey with focused deterrence. I read Grogan’s book in college and was totally pilled on it. The late Mark Kleiman, who was a major early influence on my crime thinking, was also a proponent. But then Jennifer Doleac convinced me the evidence base for Operation Ceasefire in Boston was actually low-quality. There were also examples of other cities, including Baltimore, saying they were going to do focused deterrence and then not seeing strong results.

Something that Scott deserves a lot of credit for is that he not only spearheaded a program that has worked well, but he also worked with smart academics to build a rigorous research design into it, so there’s now much better evidence that it is working.

The other thing Scott deserves credit for (but that also serves as a cautionary note) is that doing focused deterrence at large scale is hard.

It’s easy to say you’re going to do this. But a lot of cities have made failed efforts where the quality of the intelligence isn’t very good, where the call-ins involve excessively large groups of people, and where as a result the deterrence methods aren’t credible.

This can easily collapse, in practice, to a kind of “call in the usual suspects” and have social service providers wander around uselessly. But while police don’t need conviction-quality evidence to say to a group of young men, “Hey, we basically know what’s going on here and we’ve got our eyes on you so don’t fuck around but do reach out and accept our help,” they do need to actually know what’s going on.

Part of the Baltimore success is that they’re able to do this because they started in just one part of the city; even now, it hasn’t scaled up to every police district.

Fundamentally, though, the point is that a very large share of homicides consists of criminals shooting at other criminals. Because the victims are not necessarily super-sympathetic and their friends aren’t interested in cooperating with police, these things can just proceed on a low boil for a long time. But plenty of innocent bystanders end up getting hurt, a much larger range of innocent bystanders is terrified, and each expansion of killing risks drawing more young men into violent gangs for protection and solidarity.

Baltimore’s bright future

The upshot of all of this is that I am quite bullish on the city of Baltimore.

Lots of high-crime cities are also enmeshed in much larger regional problems that are not necessarily amenable to solution by city government. Places like Detroit, St. Louis, and Cleveland are dealing with issues that are downstream of global economic forces. But Maryland is always contending with New Jersey and Massachusetts for the status of number one in median household income. The Baltimore metro area has a higher median household income than Dallas, Austin, or Atlanta.

The issue is that Baltimore City specifically is poor compared to nearby counties:

On the other hand, in the context of a metro area that is richer than the fastest-growing Sun Belt metro areas, housing is generally very expensive in Maryland but relatively cheap in Baltimore.

Maryland as a whole should of course adopt YIMBY reforms. But in the Maryland context, “why don’t you move to the cheap city instead” isn’t like suggesting that people live in Syracuse rather than Brooklyn.

Baltimore is not isolated from job opportunities. It’s literally in the center of an affluent metro area in a rich state! Every workday, about 85,000 Baltimoreans commute out of the city to jobs in the suburbs and about 200,000 people commute into the city from the suburbs.

Marginal population growth in Baltimore could occur entirely from people who already have jobs in the city and just want a cheaper place to live. They are currently deterred by high crime and bad schools. Schools are a tough problem, but there are tens of thousands of childless young people in the Baltimore metro area and also tens of thousands of vacant homes and vacant lots in Baltimore City.

If you can get 100 current commuters to live in the city instead, that creates additional jobs in the city providing locally facing services and that increases demand for city living. Ultimately, you have to address the schools and make the city more family-friendly. But from a fiscal standpoint, childless employed people are actually ideal because they consume fewer city services. Population growth would make it easy to reduce Baltimore’s property taxes, which would encourage rehabbing more of the city’s vacant lots and structures.

There’s a virtuous circle in which lower crime means more residents means more local jobs means lower taxes means more residents.

The 1990s crime decline saw some of that virtuous circle happening, but at that point Baltimore was actually something of a laggard relative to the general trend toward lower crime in American cities. This time, they are leaders. And because Baltimore has also been less impacted by the prior gentrification of other East Coast cities, there’s more low-hanging housing fruit to pick.

There will probably be some recognition lag before people find out the good news about Baltimore, but if public officials stay the course, I think the city has a bright future.
