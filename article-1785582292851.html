Welcome to The Weekly Scroll!

The Weekly Scroll is a once-a-week roundup of the funniest, most interesting, and strangest stories from around the social internet.

The internet is not just a strange, hyperselected web of people hiding from reality; in many ways, the internet is reality. What happens online shapes real life just as real life shapes what happens online.

Every week, I sift through social media so you don’t have to, collecting the most notable trends, memes, and online dramas in one place. Think of it as a guided tour through the more insane parts of the web, with commentary about what it all means.

If you enjoy internet nonsense but don’t have the time to doomscroll for hours every day, you’re in the right place.

This week, we’re diving into what’s driving anti-capitalist messaging, how youths are disguising themselves as Charlie Kirk, and the young men who found community on a porn message board.

A story about gooners that explains our antisocial century

I have no idea what sort of journalistic steroids they’re feeding the reporters over at The Cut, but it must be the good stuff. The most remarkable story I read anywhere this week was Ezra Marcus’ “Revenge of the Simps: How a community of porn addicts tried to rescue the teenage camgirls they worshipped from the clutches of a violent webcam pimp.” (In case it’s not obvious, the link is highly NSFW.)

First of all, yes, let’s savor that incredible title.

The story details how users on “SimpCity” — a message board largely devoted to discussing livestream porn performers — began to suspect that some of their favorite webcam girls were victims of human trafficking and abuse. They noticed that the girls they followed had unexplained bruises, and that at times they could hear crying and screaming from women not on screen.

This being the internet, a group of these gooners banded together to try to investigate what was going on. They called themselves The White Knights (hopefully with a touch of irony).

The White Knights’ sleuthing skills eventually led them to a Seattle-area man named Nikita Tyukalo. Tyukalo, a 21-year-old Andrew Tate admirer, was subsequently arrested and charged with four counts of human trafficking after the Bellevue Police Department received tips from the White Knights.

This story rides the edge between horrifying and absurd. The contrast between the seriousness of the alleged crimes being committed and the ridiculousness of The White Knights (these are, after all, men posting hundreds of pages of commentary on various porn stars) makes the entire thing sound farcical.

At one point, Tyukalo finds out that the simps are on to him and joins one of their group chats to mock them, leading to this astounding exchange:

The back-and-forth continued all night — the simps calling Tyukalo an abuser; Tyukalo mocking the simps as broke, misguided losers and bragging about his McLarens.

“Cars aren’t status in the real world. Just to little kids showing off overpriced Hot Wheels,” wrote Hal.

“Brother,” Tyukalo responded. “Your in a Chaturbate chat.”

Last year, The Argument columnist Derek Thompson wrote that we are living in the Anti-Social Century: Public life and institutions are deteriorating as we spend more time alone, isolated, and online, staring at our phones rather than engaging in real-world relationships and activities. Thompson is definitely on to something important, but the antisocial century is also spawning new forms of niche, online socializing — like, well, Chaturbate chats.

People never stopped wanting community. They’re just getting it in these incredibly strange places. And while it may not be great for society that people are now turning to message boards like SimpCity for social community, it’s certainly interesting.

The White Knights may have been isolated men sitting alone in separate rooms — and many of them exhibit an unhealthy relationship with pornography and women in general — but together they possessed a shared culture and built a common purpose. Some of them spent time tracking down government records. Others tried to build rapport with the performers. They archived streams, tracked addresses, and communicated with police. Eventually they scraped together enough information that they legitimately contributed to the arrest of an abusive criminal.

Part of what happens when mainstream institutions weaken is that people look for alternatives. The failure of reputable systems leads disreputable systems to take their place. As the antisocial century marches forward, I expect to see more stories that revolve around the actions of subreddits, fandoms, message boards, or TikTok audience mobs. These are the places where culture is being created, and whether we like it or not it’s where community happens. And I have a growing sense that every online subculture is a latent institution just waiting to be activated by the right event or discourse.

Trend reporting under late-stage capitalism

We’re playing the hits again!

Posters on X this week decided to do the 800th edition of “You’re expected to work to eat: an inherent feature of existing on Planet Earth, or uniquely caused by capitalism?”

It’s not a new discourse: Capitalism has been blamed for anything and everything under the sun. Capitalism is why you can’t sleep at night. Capitalism is why ugly beige designs are everywhere. Capitalism is why you are lonely, why you have hobbies, and why incels exist. Capitalism is why there’s no life on Mars. Capitalism is to blame for mental illness, for why we haven’t cured Alzheimer’s, and for the existence of fraud. There is no problem so minute or absurd that it hasn’t been blamed on capitalism. There are a nearly limitless number of these complaints which all use the same “ugh, capitalism” strategy.

The nature of the internet today is that we’re all in different algorithms. Even for someone like me, it’s nearly impossible to keep track of “what’s happening online.” It’s a million different things in a million different algorithmic subcultures.

That’s what makes it all the more remarkable how wide and deep the seemingly ubiquitous complaint against capitalism goes. The examples above sometimes come from anonymous posters, but they also come from reporters at elite journalistic publications, published authors, and credentialed academics. This type of lazy argument against capitalism began with left-leaning elites and filtered down to everyone else.

Young Democrats haven’t turned on capitalism because they have a nuanced view of the difference between capitalism and the market structures that are a perennial feature of human society. And complaints about capitalism haven’t increased recently because there’s been a marked increase in greedy behavior, or because of any specific policy. People have just been subjected to decades of messaging about how capitalism lost their luggage and shot their dog, and internalized that capitalism is when things are bad and evil — and the badder and eviler the situation, the more capitalistic it must be.

Let’s check in on how all economic activity is becoming gambling

Wisconsin officials are warning residents that using the prediction market Kalshi to bet on elections could land them in legal trouble. Per a state law that dates all the way back to 1849, it is illegal to bet on elections in which you later vote. A recent study from Pew Research found that dozens of other states have similar laws banning or restricting betting on elections.

Kalshi’s response was to accuse Wisconsin of “voter suppression” for asking residents not to bet on the elections they’re voting in. Kalshi’s adventurous attempt to create a new definition of “suppression” goes something like this: Gambling — excuse me, taking a position on a prediction market — will get nonvoters excited to vote! But then they’ll be bummed out by the law against betting on elections … which is voter suppression.

I’m not sure why Kalshi bothers to care about Wisconsin election bets. Their real business is as a sportsbook. The prediction market folks often talk a big game about all the theoretical reasons why prediction markets are a pro-social technology with all sorts of interesting applications. But I notice that 80% of Kalshi’s trading volume is in sports betting, and that over the past year it has expanded “combo” bets that mimic the notorious parlay bets popularized by sportsbooks. These are definitely not signs that the company is now just a glorified sports betting shop with no social value.

But hey, at least you can now bet that nothing will ever happen.
