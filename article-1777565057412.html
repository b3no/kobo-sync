You can't redistribute your way out of a housing shortage

Blue states are failing the poor

By all accounts, California offers a comparatively generous set of welfare state and labor market institutions. It offers a high statewide minimum wage, is friendly toward organized labor, and is one of only 11 states (plus the District of Columbia) with its own refundable CTC to lift the incomes of the state’s poorest families with children.

Poverty, however, is not merely a function of a family’s income level. The poverty threshold measures the income needed for a family to meet its basic needs in a particular place. That threshold is lower in Boise than in San Francisco due to higher housing costs in the latter.

In California’s tug-of-war between the state’s income support policies and the rising costs of basic necessities (housing, especially), the latter is winning. In fact, the state’s rising housing costs have effectively absorbed much of the income redistribution flowing through the state in recent decades.

Share

As I quantified in a report for the Niskanen Center, the $11.4 billion increase in SNAP (“food stamp”) benefits provided to low-income Californians between 1980 and 2023 has effectively been wiped out by the $13.3 billion rise in how far Californians fall short of the poverty line as a result of surging housing costs.

If it weren’t for its high housing costs, in fact, California’s poverty rate would fall from highest in the nation to mirroring the national average. This is because the Supplemental Poverty Measure (SPM), a Census-produced poverty measure that most researchers prefer, incorporates variation in rental costs across municipalities into its poverty thresholds.

For example, in 2023, the poverty threshold for a two-adult, two-child family renting in San Jose, California, was roughly $58,000, nearly 1.5 times as high as for an equivalent family renting in Minneapolis (almost $40,000). That’s almost entirely because of how expensive it is to rent a home in San Jose.

That California and New York have higher poverty rates than Alabama and Mississippi may turn heads, but the patterns align with some other measures of destitution. The states with the highest rates of homelessness include California and New York — not Alabama or Mississippi. The city where nearly 1 in 7 public school students experience homelessness each year is New York City — not Huntsville, Alabama, or Jackson, Mississippi.¹

“Build like Austin” is comparable to an expanded Child Tax Credit

California is not alone in suffering high poverty rates due to high housing costs: New York, New Jersey, Massachusetts, Connecticut, and Hawaii join it as being states where rental prices consistently outpaced the income growth of the 10th percentile (that is, low-income households) from 1985 to 2024. What these states share is a tradition of electing Democrats to statewide office and, more directly, a shortage of housing supply relative to demand.

These states are “progressive” in their espoused political values yet have neglected the affordability challenges that ensnare their most vulnerable residents. Their practices, meanwhile, mandate a shift in the way we typically think about poverty: Lowering rental costs in high-cost areas ought to be valued comparably to, say, redistributing more income through SNAP.

In high-cost cities like San Francisco or Los Angeles, specifically, I estimate that a 15% decline in rental costs relative to the national average would reduce poverty thresholds by an average $3,614 for families with children in high-cost California cities, a value that is nearly identical to the gains from the 2021 Child Tax Credit (CTC) expansion.

Reducing living costs at this magnitude could, in turn, reduce child poverty rates in high-cost California by 20% relative to baseline. “Reduce housing costs” may not be as salient an anti-poverty rallying call as “expand the CTC,” but it is no less important in high-cost cities.

What type of increase in housing supply might be necessary to achieve this type of poverty reduction? If we assume a relationship of housing supply growth and rental prices that is more than twice as generous (for instance, elasticity of -0.25) as one recent study finds, high-cost California cities would need to increase their stock by around 60% from current levels in order to achieve that 15% decline in relative rental costs that would allow it to meaningfully reduce poverty (an elasticity of -.25 assumes that rents are very responsive to increased supply; it’s possible that rents will be less responsive and therefore a lot more housing could be needed to achieve these rent declines). For context, San Francisco has managed only a 31% increase in housing stock over the 50-year period from 1970 to 2020, and Los Angeles only performs slightly better.

That’s not to say, however, that the type of growth that these cities need is without precedent. Austin, situated in Texas’ increasingly deregulated housing markets, saw a 61% increase in housing units between 2000 and 2020.

Yes, Austin’s impressive building boom came during favorable financial conditions for developers; we don’t know when that environment will strike again, which makes it all the more important to reform zoning codes in preparation for those key moments. Even within California, dozens of reasonably large cities — from Irvine to Bakersfield — have neared the 60% benchmark.

Comparing peninsular San Francisco to Irvine or the sprawled Austin may be topographically unfair, but San Franciscans have normalized a level of density that is unsupportable for a major city. Take Paris, a city with few skyscrapers that has maintained its cultural and historical architecture. Paris is roughly 40.7 square miles, smaller than San Francisco’s roughly 47, yet it is able to fit roughly 2 million residents into that land area compared to San Francisco’s 826,000. If San Francisco were able to match Parisian density, that would mean 2.8 times as many people could live in the city.

San Francisco’s stated goal of adding 82,000 housing units by 2031 is a good start and could be as important as any additional redistribution provided to the city’s residents. Even if much of the construction is high-end housing, recent research suggests that benefits still concentrate at the low end of the rental market and do so within a few years. In Austin, for instance, while Class A (luxury units) barely saw their rents decline, Class C (older, cheaper buildings that house low-income tenants) saw their rents fall 11% from 2023 to 2024.

The political limits of redistribution demand more efficient income transfers

Direct cash redistribution has come under fire as of late, so I want to be very clear regarding the core of my argument: Direct income transfers remain an essential part of any morally defensible welfare state, and expanding housing supply is, quite obviously, not a substitute for direct support. However, political constraints on the amount of redistribution that the American public will allow necessitate that existing redistribution should efficiently reduce poverty. Instead, the opposite is occurring in states like California, where poverty-reducing income transfers have failed to keep up with a poverty-increasing acceleration of living costs.

The 2021 Child Tax Credit expansion provides direct proof: Despite decoupling income support from employment, driving U.S. child poverty to historic lows, reducing food insecurity, and improving parents’ mental health, the policy was allowed to expire in 2022 with virtually no public outcry or electoral consequences. The country proved it could solve child poverty through redistribution, then dropped the tool that largely led to that success, and, to dramatize slightly: No one cared.

The lesson here is not to lean into policy perspectives that disparage the role of direct transfers. Predistributionists are right that market earnings are far too unequal, but as Matt Bruenig correctly noted, predistribution as anti-poverty strategy misses the fact that a large share of the most vulnerable Americans are structurally out of the labor market (they are too young, too old, or too ill).

Moreover, the takeaway is not to repurpose spending on cash for spending on services (I have argued that state governments should do the opposite). Instead, the takeaway is to acknowledge, as Ezra Klein and Derek Thompson put simply in Abundance, that “Redistribution is important. But it is not enough.”

It is not enough because, in high-cost regions, income transfers are increasingly inefficient at reducing poverty. Even this fact, though, understates the point: In California and New York, expanding housing supply is a form of redistribution, as the comparison to gains from SNAP and the CTC makes clear. “Abundance” is, itself, an anti-poverty strategy.

To be sure, a supply-side strategy for reducing poverty is less direct and rapid than simply redistributing income to poor families. Only a portion of new housing built directly benefits the lowest-income renters who most need it, whereas $3,600 in SNAP or CTC benefits go directly to the families in need. Plenty of traditional redistribution is needed to support the families left behind, but funding this type of redistribution is set to become even more challenging.

In fiscal year 2028, many state governments will be on the hook for co-funding SNAP benefits, which have typically been funded almost exclusively by the federal government; one estimate suggests that California will need to more than double its state spending on SNAP to maintain its current level of allocations.

California is better equipped to handle the funding increases than lower-revenue states. Still, the fact remains that without meaningful housing policy reform, the state’s SNAP benefits will continue to follow the same pattern as the last three decades: pouring resources into the income side of the poverty equation without constraining runaway growth in needs.

Without an aggressive plan to build more homes, put simply, there is no sustainable path to lower poverty for California and its high-cost peers.

One critique of the SPM’s adjustments for living costs is that it does not incorporate amenity values, such as access to the beach, warm weather, and an In-N-Out within driving distance. This is true, and many of us would opt for California over Mississippi despite higher prices for those reasons. For low-income residents growing up in either state, however, the fact remains that high and rising living costs in California directly hinder material well-being for lower-income residents. Poor families cannot eat the sunshine for lunch.
