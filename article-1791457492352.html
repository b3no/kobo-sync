Speaking at a fundraiser for Abdul El-Sayed in New York earlier this week, Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez reportedly said that the Michigan Senate race will “break open the entire political calculus in America” because “if Abdul can win in Michigan, anybody can win anywhere.”

I and other moderate Democrats have often been accused of secretly wanting to see El-Sayed lose. For my part, that is very much not the case. But I am extremely worried about precisely this scenario: an El-Sayed victory followed by stakeholders on the left arguing, as they have been signaling for months that they intend to, that electability concerns about their style of politics are misguided.

I don’t particularly mind A.O.C. saying something wild and untrue in the context of a fundraiser. But I do worry that every time I see this discourse shoot around, I don’t see any intellectuals or thought leaders on the left engaging in honest discourse policing around this subject.

The truth is that right now, El-Sayed’s campaign is struggling somewhat. He’s up in the polls but only narrowly. A tight Senate race in Michigan is what you’d expect in a neutral national political environment — it’s a classic swing state — but Democrats are up by more than eight points in the national generic ballot. Elissa Slotkin very narrowly beat Mike Rogers in a Michigan Senate race two years ago when Democrats lost the national popular vote for both the House and the presidency. A hypothetical generic Democrat (like, say, Mallory McMorrow) should be easily winning Michigan right now. A Democrat who’s as effective at vote-getting as Slotkin should be crushing Rogers in this environment.

I’m hung up on Michigan because that race plays a unique role in factional politics and also because Republicans are running the guy who lost last time, so we can be sure the difference between 2024 and 2026 is about the Democratic nominee rather than the Republican.

Beyond Michigan or the specifics of that race, though, it’s just a lot easier to run against the backdrop of an unpopular Trump presidency than an unpopular Biden presidency.

Nonetheless, if we’re disciplined about it, we can discern specific candidate effects — they are real and they’re important. Democrats have several nominees who are winning races that would be lost by a generic Democrat, and an A.E.S.-scale underperformance in Alaska or even North Carolina would turn victory to defeat even in a strong national environment.

Of course, there’s no way to know which candidates and which campaigns are actually doing well until we see the vote counts. But it makes sense to use the currently available polling to pre-register some theories about what would and would not count as an impressive performance.

On some level, a win is a win. On another level, though, winning narrowly in Michigan is more impressive than winning narrowly in New Hampshire but less impressive than winning narrowly in Ohio, which is in turn less impressive than winning narrowly in Kansas. If we think it through, we can try to find intellectually rigorous ways to assess these races once the votes are counted.

Start with a naive benchmark

I think a good sanity check on any claims that anyone makes about the strength or weakness of any given campaign is to do a pretty simple adjustment for the national political climate.

Basically, you take the change in the generic congressional ballot since 2024 and apply that adjustment to each state to see how you expect a hypothetical generic Democrat would do in that state. Then you look at the current polling and can see whether someone is doing better or worse than a generic Dem.

What you’ll see is that — if you assume that current polling of both the generic ballot and the Senate races is correct — Mary Peltola, James Talarico, Adam Hamilton, Sherrod Brown, Josh Turek, and Roy Cooper are all (in rank order) doing better than the baseline, while Angie Nixon and El-Sayed are doing worse and Troy Jackson is doing much worse.

This is (deliberately) a very simplistic look at performance.

To take the most obvious issue, opponent quality matters. Jackson needs to run against a popular incumbent with a proven track record of winning crossover votes. Talarico gets to run against Ken Paxton, who is plagued by scandal and is a very controversial figure inside the Texas Republican Party. Estimating who is responsible for what means engaging in gut-based takes rather than quantitative analysis, and I think we should all be honest about that. To my eye, Peltola’s polling is even more impressive when you consider that she’s running against an incumbent who hasn’t been rocked by any particularly earth-shattering scandal.

But my main point here is just to draw a distinction between stuff reasonable people can disagree about and bad-faith electoral illiteracy. If you want to argue that Nixon would be doing better in Florida if national Democrats hadn’t written this race off a year ago, you might have a point. At least we could talk about it.

What you can’t do is analyze these outcomes without paying any attention to the shifting national climate or to the fact that underlying state partisanship varies. We don’t yet know what the election results will be. But if the final vote totals match what we’re seeing in the polls, that will be a big underperformance by El-Sayed, and if similar candidates had been nominated in Ohio, Iowa, Texas, and Alaska, that would turn victories into defeats.

Adding some nuance

There are, however, two big problems with my benchmark exercise.

One, which is a little silly but also very real, is that in practice, people need to start cranking out takes on the evening of Election Day and the morning after. But we actually won’t know what the national generic ballot looks like until all the votes are counted in slow-counting California, which will take more than a week. We know those California votes will overwhelmingly be for Democrats, so an early count is going to underestimate the Democratic share of the overall national vote and mechanically make every single Democratic candidate not on the West Coast look artificially more impressive than they really are.

Cooper, for example, is on track to win by a healthy margin. Relative to my benchmark, though, his current polling is actually nothing special — he’s just running in the least-red of the red states and benefiting from a huge national swing toward Democrats. But if you toss out a take on his performance based on an incomplete national popular vote, you’ll end up overestimating him.

Another problem is that my benchmark exercise assumes a uniform national swing, which is a convenient simplification but almost certainly wrong. Current polling seems to indicate a larger-than-average swing toward Democrats among Hispanics and young people.

This means the political climate specifically in a state like Maine that is full of old people and has almost no Latino voters probably isn’t as friendly as my benchmarks indicate. The uniform swing assumption is probably unfair to Jackson and too kind to Talarico, in other words.

A handy (but in a large state more annoying) way to address both of these concerns is to compare Senate candidates to the House candidates running in their state.

To win statewide, Talarico probably needs to run nearly as strongly as Latino Democratic House candidates running in South Texas while also moderately overperforming hopeless Democratic House candidates running in Texas’s deep red districts.

Michigan’s demographics are not exactly Maine’s, but it is an older-than-average state without many Hispanics. The fairest way to assess El-Sayed is probably to compare his results to House candidates running in Michigan. Rashida Tlaib is normally the best electoral performer in the Squad. Can he match her performance in what should be a friendly district for him? How will he do compared to State Senator Jeremy Moss in his open race for the safe suburban 11th Congressional District seat that Haley Stevens is vacating? Will he do better or worse than Hillary Scholten or Kristen McDonald Rivet in their districts around Grand Rapids and Saginaw?

If he does well by those benchmarks, I’ll gladly say I underestimated the guy.

By the same token, I expect moderates will want to hold up Cooper as their hero, but the polling actually calls into question how effective his campaign has been. He should win pretty easily, but will he actually run stronger than quality House Democrats like Don Davis? It’s worth keeping an eye on.

Have Democrats actually fixed anything?

These differences between the candidates are large enough to be the difference between winning and losing in many states. At the same time, even the gap between Peltola and El-Sayed is small compared to the shift in the generic ballot between 2024 and 2026. The national political climate and the state of the party brands, in other words, are the most important things as American politics has moved in a quasi-parliamentary direction. Recruiting good candidates matters, but the thing that matters most is that, for now, voters like Democrats on the whole a lot better than they like Republicans.

Optimistically, this goes to show that all the hand-wringing about the state of the Democratic Party has been misguided and all the big problems are fixed.

But The Argument ran a very relevant poll recently that showed Democrats have achieved a large edge in favorability almost entirely through people hating Republicans rather than through rehabilitating their own image.

There’s an old joke about two guys in the woods getting charged by a bear. One guy starts lacing up his shoes and the other one says, “What are you doing? You can’t outrun a bear!” The other guy says, “I don’t need to outrun the bear. I just need to outrun you.”

By the same token, it’s totally acceptable for Democrats to achieve a favorable national environment purely through the Republicans making people hate them. And it’s acceptable to win Senate races while underperforming the fundamentals or by running a race that would lose in a neutral environment. But to avoid complacency in the wake of victory, it’s worth setting a high standard. Everything I’ve done here is based on polling rather than actual results. But what you want to see when the votes are in is either the Democratic Party brand improving or else individual candidates running far enough ahead of the national brand to actually win in a neutral environment.
