SCOOP: Ontario’s $800-million retreat on industrial carbon pricing

Two weeks after major power users warned Queen’s Park of a 147% rate spike, the province moved to cut carbon emissions charges. Industry says it’s nowhere near enough.

By: Alan Findlay

As provinces race to attract new industry (from data centres to EV battery plants) and modernize old ones (like the electrification of steel plants), a massive build out of electricity generation and transmission is in the works across the country — but Ontario is now saying it needs to drop its prized industrial carbon pricing in order to make it happen.

Last spring Ottawa unveiled its national strategy for electricity as part of a slate of ambitious nation-building strategic plans. It didn’t get a lot of attention, which seems surprising in hindsight given the fact that the plan will incur a $1-trillion price tag to meet projections of doubled electricity demand across Canada by 2050.

The ambitions for tomorrow, however, are creating real headaches for those footing the bills today. The funding for that capital investment has to come from multiple sources, after all. And governments across the nation are facing an increasingly urgent dilemma between the need for enormous expenditures on the one hand, and addressing the affordability concerns of residential, commercial and industrial consumers, on the other. Governments are looking everywhere for places to cut power costs. And one area the Ontario premier has called out is the cost of carbon pricing.

Last month, representatives of Ontario’s industrial power users (who consume roughly 10 per cent of the province’s electricity) met with officials in the ministry of energy to deliver their blunt message. “Spiking Electricity Prices: A threat to Industrial Competitiveness in Ontario,” their presentation title reads.

A copy of an Association of Major Power Consumers in Ontario (AMPCO) PowerPoint deck obtained by The Line states that industrial electricity prices have been on a steep upward slope for the past two years, and are projected to go even higher. Over a three-year stretch, AMPCO predicts its members will be hit with a total increase of 147%.

“Industry is now facing a double whammy,” says Brad Duguid, AMPCO’s president. “It’s like you’ve got the tariffs we’re dealing with, which is really challenging, obviously, especially for steel and auto and some others, but now you’re dealing with a huge increase in electricity costs.”

Duguid points out that ongoing work to refurbish Ontario’s nuclear plants has meant using more expensive natural gas-fired generation to fill the gap.

Two weeks after that meeting, the Ontario government posted a notice proposing reductions to industrial carbon emissions charges.

The proposal would reduce costs for heavy users like steel and automotive plants by a total of $800 million from 2027 to 2031, according to a Ministry of the Environment, Conservation and Parks notice on the Environmental Registry of Ontario website. The proposal is open for public comment until December 2.

“Ontario industries are facing substantial challenges from global economic uncertainty,” the notice states. “To reduce costs, protect jobs and help businesses remain competitive so that they can continue to invest and expand in Ontario, the government is proposing changes to the EPS (emissions performance standards) and GHG Reporting programs.”

The ERO posting points to the federal government’s announcement last May lowering the minimum national carbon price. This in turn allows Ontario, for example, to lower the carbon pricing for GHG emissions above and beyond a certain threshold (called excess emissions units).

Duguid says that the latest proposal from the government is welcome, but still not nearly enough as winter’s peak demand and prices loom large. AMPCO’s own proposal is for an estimated $2.8 billion in rate relief for industrial consumers over five years. They may get it: At a Toronto event alongside Alberta Premier Danielle Smith this week, Ontario Premier Doug Ford repeated a call he has been making since the summer for more exemptions from the federally mandated industrial carbon tax.

Meanwhile, energy prices are set to rise again across the board.

Ontario’s public generator, Ontario Power Generation, and Hydro One, the predominant transmission company in Ontario, both have applications before the Ontario Energy Board, totalling more than $70 billion over five years, for rate increases that could kick in next year. Those proposed costs would impact all customers. A recent, yet-to-be approved, proposed settlement between OPG and various interested parties, including ratepayer groups like AMPCO, would see the monthly bill for residential ratepayers, for example, hit with an average total increase of $13 over those five years.

“That’s only the beginning,” Duguid said.

They are going to have to find the money from somewhere. Last week, Premier Doug Ford promoted the province’s investment in a third major nuclear refurbishment project at OPG’s Pickering Nuclear Generating Station and plans for two new nuclear plants that would produce enough power for the equivalent of more than 16 million homes.

“When you see the investments coming into our province and someone’s building a one-million square foot facility and they need power, that’s where a lot of power is going,” Ford said at an Ontario Chamber of Commerce event in Toronto last week.

For conservationists and environmentalists, however, cutting the price on carbon emissions sends the wrong signal to the market.

“Not only is this bad for our health and our climate, but it will jeopardize our energy sovereignty by making us even more dependent on American energy imports (natural gas) to keep our lights on,” Jack Gibbons of the Ontario Clean Air Alliance stated in an email, speaking about the current government proposal on reduced industrial emissions charges.

The ERO notice states that the change would lead to increased use of natural gas-fired generation and increase greenhouse gas emissions by about 0.9 million tonnes in 2030. For context, Ontario’s Auditor General reported last year that the electricity sector overall produced 6.8 million tonnes of greenhouse gases in 2023.

“More than 70% of Ontario’s gas is imported from the U.S,” said Gibbons.

The government’s proposal could kick in as soon as January 2027..
