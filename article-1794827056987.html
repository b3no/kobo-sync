Why is progress so unpopular?

My trip to a gathering of techno-optimists left me wondering about progress’s PR problem

Jeremiah Johnson
Nov 16
∙
Paid
 
READ IN APP
 

To attend the Progress Conference, an annual confab hosted by the nonprofit Roots of Progress, is to be surrounded by people who believe in a better tomorrow, and who are personally determined to make it happen. It’s an inherently optimistic place, and one that makes me feel more hopeful about the future of society every time I go.

The “progress movement” that the conference is intended to represent is a bit amorphous— it overlaps with slightly more well-defined efforts like Abundance, YIMBYism, rationalism, and effective altruism — but at its core it’s filled with people who believe that economic growth, technological innovation, and human ingenuity can make the world a better place.

At this year’s Progress Conference, which was hosted in Berkeley in October, I talked with policy experts trying to build high-speed rail faster, solar technology entrepreneurs, philosophers trying to grapple with what humanity would look like if we ever conquered aging, and an eclectic mix of scientists, wonks, investors, and academics all trying to push society forward.

But no matter how exciting any of these efforts seemed, there was one thought I couldn’t shake: Progress doesn’t seem very popular.

Artificial intelligence and the data centers needed to power it are deeply unpopular. More than two-thirds of Americans say they’d be uncomfortable riding in a driverless car. Far more people say that lab-grown meat will be bad for society than good, and the public opposes even researching the idea of many geoengineering efforts. It seems like everywhere you look, there’s a new technology that the public either disapproves of or outright fears.

And even when the public at large doesn’t disapprove, technology often inspires fierce opposition from a dedicated minority of critics. Nuclear power and vaccines both draw intense criticism, and organized groups exist to stop advances in those areas. New housing and new infrastructure projects often draw huge protests and face years of bureaucratic blockage to get off the ground.

As someone who sympathizes with the progress movement, it’s hard for me to grapple with the idea that in many cases these smart, hardworking, idealistic people who want to make the world a better place are pushing ideas that the public will meet with shrugs at best — and lawsuits at worst.

I went to the 2026 Progress Conference to find out if the people inside the progress movement understand why.

Progress and the pie

Fear of and resistance to new ideas, new technologies, and societal progress are practically as old as progress itself. The German abbot Johannes Trithemius wrote In Praise of Scribes in 1492, arguing that printed books were inferior to their hand-copied forebears. (His worry was that the printing press would undermine the spiritual life of monks.)

Opponents of the smallpox vaccine believed that introducing cowpox to humans could lead to people developing bovine characteristics.

An 1889 cartoon in a popular magazine warned how electricity could easily kill you.

And of course the Luddites, fearing for their own livelihoods, famously smashed mechanized textile equipment in Regency England.

But despite these fears, society in the shadow of the Enlightenment and Industrial Revolution exhibited an appetite for progress unmatched in modern American society. The sense that progress is possible, progress is desirable, and continued progress is the natural direction of history has fallen out of vogue. We once celebrated scientific advancements in national civic spectacles, held world’s fairs that invited the public to marvel at our technological accomplishments, and assumed that our children would lead better lives than we did.

In 1851, more than 6 million people gathered in London to see locomotives, hydraulic presses, and an 8-meter glass fountain. In 1939 and 1940, 45 million visitors went to the New York World’s Fair to see AT&T’s telephone-and-telegraph exhibit and a model of America built by GM that included expressways, suburbs, and skyscraper-filled cities. After the war, New York held another world’s fair in 1964 and1965, and 51 million came to see IBM’s computer exhibits and Bell Telephone’s Picturephone.¹

The U.S. doesn’t even try to do anything of this scale now — perhaps it’s because TV and phones have made it easy to entertain yourself at home or because of negativity bias in news and social media.

But over the course of the last few generations, something in American society seems to have shifted. The turn against progress expresses itself in a wide variety of ways: Sometimes it’s widespread public skepticism of AI and data centers. Sometimes it’s a generalized NIMBYism that tries to stop new housing developments or infrastructure projects. Sometimes it’s groups who mobilize pseudoscience to try to stop GMOs or nuclear plants, elected officials who try to ban self-driving cars from their cities, or crunchy moms who scaremonger about vaccines on Facebook.

Behind all this is a sense that, not just despite technological progress but perhaps because of it, the future may well be worse than the past. In a 2024 Pew Research Center survey, only 26% of Americans believed that today’s young people will be better off financially than their parents — this despite the fact that science keeps advancing and American economic growth continues to churn forward.

So why has the public turned against the idea of progress?

No one remembers how bad it used to be

At Progress Con, the Cato Institute’s Chelsea Follett told me that she believes that people have lost historical perspective. Follett is the author of The Grim Old Days, a book about the horrors of preindustrial life, and she thinks that we’ve simply forgotten how unfathomably terrible life used to be across nearly every dimension: medicine, labor, hygiene, cuisine, and more.

Follett thinks we’ve eliminated so many problems that we can no longer appreciate how far we’ve come — after all, few people currently living actually remember what it was like to live with diseases like polio (or even more minor annoyances like constantly having fleas and lice). She argues that we need to re-anchor expectations so that people judge society not against an imagined utopia, but against the bare facts from the past. This could mean a change in educational curricula to emphasize the struggles of everyday life in the past, or a change in pop culture — perhaps more reminders like the scene in Netflix’s popular period drama Bridgerton (spoiler alert) in which the family patriarch dies after being stung by a bee.

Follett is correct that the past was much, much worse than the present in nearly every facet of life, and she’s correct in judging that idea to be an underrated truth. But I don’t think societal forgetting can explain the recent turn away from progress — people in the 1970s had just as little reason to contemplate medieval life expectancy as we have today.

An aging society is a careful society

Over a falafel lunch, some think tankers offered demographic explanations: As society gets older — the age of the average American increased from 28 in 1970 to 39 in 2025 — we desire less change and our risk aversion increases. Others believed that, as society gets richer, healthier, and safer, our inherent drive for progress decreases because continued increases in material well-being produce diminishing returns.

The NIMBY theory of everything

Some blamed entrenched interests for the NIMBYism and proceduralism that make it so hard to build anything or deploy new breakthrough technologies. One scholar speculated that the ways in which society has advanced are real but harder to see: We basically fixed the hole in the ozone layer, we solved acid rain, and we cleaned up the smog in most major American cities, but these are the dog that didn’t bark — we don’t notice the absence of a problem that we used to have in the same way we would notice a flying car.

Innovation is happening in all the wrong places

Around a firepit, several people speculated that technological progress for the past few decades has been in all the wrong areas — that advances in the digital sphere haven’t made people’s lives better in the same concrete ways that previous scientific advances have. The single most visible and influential new piece of technology of the last 25 years is the smartphone, and most people have, at best, an ambivalent relationship with it.

Alex Trembath, the executive director of the Breakthrough Institute (and contributor to The Argument), echoed this line of thought: “Consumers living through the Depression and World War II… were born in a society without electricity and running water, and died with a car, a washing machine, and a refrigerator. There’s no such rapid advance in technological availability for the past 40 years or so. Yes, computers are everywhere now, but consumers experience computers and smartphones as a mixed bag at best.”

The missing variable

An explanation that I didn’t hear a single attendee at Progress Con mention was the rise in inequality. From 1979 to 2023, the share of household income going to the top 1% nearly doubled, rising from 7% to 13% in the U.S. Ordinary Americans still became wealthier, but the gains were distributed in an increasingly uneven fashion. Research has also shown that higher intergenerational upward mobility is associated with less zero-sum reasoning. It seems like a blind spot for the progress movement, perhaps because people inside the movement are disproportionately highly educated professionals: scientists, policy nerds, investors, and tech workers. Their incomes are unlikely to have stagnated, and in doing the work that they do, they’re far more likely to see the gains from progress than the potential costs.

It’s not that attendees weren’t willing to consider material explanations: One of the most persuasive stories I heard was the idea that low-growth economies are naturally more oriented toward zero-sum thinking. Imagine you live in a country like Germany, where GDP growth has been effectively zero since 2019. Without economic growth, the economy really does become a zero-sum contest in many ways: The size of the proverbial pie is not growing.

In that environment, it’s understandable for the public to suspect that new economic projects might simply be taking someone else’s slice of the pie. By contrast, in a high-growth economy it’s much easier for the public to see living standards improving and to understand the positive-sum nature of new inventions and developments.

There’s significant evidence this is true. People in middle-income countries (which have higher average growth rates) are more likely than people in high-income countries to believe that children will be better off than their parents. Studies have also found a negative correlation between GDP per capita and technological optimism. Given that economic growth has slowed down significantly in America since the 1970s, perhaps it’s natural that previous generations who experienced high growth were more progress-positive and current generations are more skeptical.

But Jason Crawford, the founder and president of Roots of Progress, doesn’t buy this story. “The problem with that theory,” he told me, “is that the backlash against the growth came first.” In Crawford’s telling, the default techno-optimism that ruled for much of the 19th century was undermined in the 20th century by world wars, global economic depression, and the rise of totalitarian governments. Far from making life better, technology seemed to be allowing us to do worse and worse things to each other with ever more powerful weapons and systematized forms of state violence and repression.

And while growth was strong for a few decades following World War II, the decline in trust in institutions and elites changed the stories we told ourselves as a society.

Postwar optimism took a series of body blows in the 1960s and 1970s that it never truly recovered from. Failures and scandals like Watergate and Vietnam taught the public that the technocratic elites in society couldn’t necessarily be trusted. The environmental consequences of growth led to fears that progress is actually destructive — the defining works of nonfiction from this era were books like Rachel Carson’s Silent Spring and Paul Ehrlich’s The Population Bomb, which both predicted widespread harm from unchecked growth. And the oil crisis and stagflation led to lower economic growth and an increase in zero-sum economic thinking.

“A perfect storm of Watergate, the oil crisis, and mounting opposition to Vietnam really turned the tide against technocracy… and the idea that elites knew what they were doing and could be trusted,” Crawford told me. The hippies began to reject the importance of material progress, the environmental movement rightly pointed out the costs of unregulated development, and the threat of apocalyptic nuclear war hung over the whole world. Even science fiction shifted in this era, from Asimovian techno-optimism to dystopian tales like Soylent Green or THX 1138. It’s no surprise that the public at large became far more cynical about elites telling them to trust that progress was both inevitable and good.

Taking progress on the road

It’s hard to sell the abstract idea of progress to most people: “Line goes up” arguments work on policy nerds, but my sense is that ordinary folks care deeply about how the gains from growth are distributed. People need to feel that their families and communities will be the beneficiaries of new technologies, new ideas, and new development projects. When the transcontinental railroad was completed in 1869, there were public celebrations across the country. Railroads were so popular that the public repeatedly voted for them — legislatures in 29 states approved over 800 authorizations for local government aid to railroad projects just in the seven years from 1866 to 1873. It was easy to understand how the railroads would connect far-flung communities and make life better for regular people, and so the public was eager to support them.

My suspicion is that in previous eras with higher growth and higher public trust, it was easier to sell people on the idea they’d benefit from new technologies and large-scale projects. Today, with lower growth and less trust in elites, that sales pitch seems harder. If it’s not immediately clear how something like a nearby data center will make someone’s life better, why would they embrace it?

Of course, some projects really are popular, but a dedicated minority of critics manages to stop them from happening anyway. Clearing out this vetocratic system is an important prerequisite to making progress possible. But there are also areas where progress advocates need to win over the broader public, and a politics of progress should aim to do both.

What the progress movement needs is a better ability to sell the benefits of specific technologies to the public — and a better way to create positive-sum deals to get buy-in from local communities. I love that this movement of people is so laser-focused on making the pie bigger for everyone in the long run, but you do also need to convince people that the pie is worth eating (and take away the power of entrenched interests to override the public interest). Programs like the Roots of Progress writing fellowships and Stripe’s investment in Works in Progress magazine are valuable contributions to this process, but there’s so much more work to do.

Driverless cars, data centers, GMOs, nuclear power plants: These are all technologies that inspire heated opposition, but have real benefits. When the public was skeptical of smallpox vaccination, campaigners gave public demonstrations. They naturally got doctors to endorse the vaccine, but they also recruited religious authorities to promote vaccination. Polio vaccine advocates even got Elvis to receive the vaccine backstage before his appearance on The Ed Sullivan Show; they organized dances all around the country and required vaccination to attend. They were willing to think creatively, talk to the public in a variety of ways, and convince them of the benefits of vaccination. They understood that persuading the public is an inherent part of the job of inventing the future. That’s what the progress movement needs to do today.
