Self-Improvement is Bad, Only Pretty Women Exist, and Women Shouldn’t Care About Looks

All the terrible lessons we learned from Hitch

READ IN APP
 

Surprising to nobody: I am a sucker for romantic comedies. The romcom heyday was the 1990s to the 2000s, so I watched most of these films as a young teenager. As a result, I had no frame of reference for how realistic they were, and I never really thought about the themes they contained. Did I like the fashion? Were the characters fun to watch? In fact, I was such an easy-to-please movie watcher that my favorite movie throughout my teen years was Double Jeopardy (not a romcom, I know) which I later told my husband was a “cinematic masterpiece” only to look it up on Rotten Tomatoes and discover a 28% rating.

But now, per subscriber request, I’m revisiting the romcom greats as an adult woman who has dated, married, and most importantly, fought in the genderslop trenches for the past several years. Please comment with any other romcoms you want to see reviewed through the lens of 2020s-era genderslop, because this truly is a treat for me.

Perhaps my favorite romcom of the era is Hitch, starring Will Smith and Eva Mendes. And the reason I’m writing about Hitch today is that while it’s great movie, I fear that it communicates quite literally all the wrong lessons about dating and attraction.

For those of you who haven’t seen it (and shame on you) Hitch follows a titular character (Alex Hitchens, Hitch for short, played by Will Smith) who is a “date doctor,” ie: a guy who teachers other guys how to date. He keeps this job under wraps when he meets Sara Melas (Eva Mendes) a closed-off gossip columnist (why are they always writers…?) who informs her boss (who is, for some reason, urging her to get a boyfriend) that she doesn’t have time for dating.

Hitch, via his top-tier charm and charisma, is able to sweep Sara off her feet while secretly coaching a dumpy accountant named Albert Brennaman, who seeks to woo the modelesque, uber-rich Allegra Cole, a socialite for whom he works. Eventually, Sara finds out about Hitch’s secret job, via her gossip columnist sleuthing, and breaks up with him in a fury, but eventually they get back together. Meanwhile—surprise!—it turns out Albert never needed Hitch’s coaching because Allegra actually loves him more when he’s being dorky and weird, and he just should have “been himself” all along.

The movie opens with Hitch telling the viewer the rules of dating, and it ends with him at Albert and Allegra’s wedding, reassuring us that there are “no rules.” The ultimate “this you?” of romantic comedies. Except he was right the first time.

Hitch’s Job Was Literally Fine

Hitch’s job was kept a shameful secret, as if he were Stephen Miller’s foot masseuse. Why? Hitch was not selling roofies to frat brothers. Hitch was not helping men create revenge porn by prompting Grok to generate images of their ex-girlfriends with vanilla ice cream smeared on their faces. Hitch was teaching socially awkward men how to date women, and all his advice was perfectly reasonable and respectful: dress better, have confidence, ask her questions, listen to her, don’t stare at her. At worst, he orchestrates a couple of dishonest meet-cutes, including a regrettable one involving a dog, but who amongst us has not found some fake excuse to talk to someone? Re-watching this movie just has me wondering why Hitch felt the need to lie about his job at all. He was doing the Lord’s work! Would it have been so hard to say, “I’m a social skills coach?”

Of course, the reason Sara is so mad, at least initially, is because she believes Hitch has been giving pickup artist tips to misogynistic men, like the one who took advantage of her best friend. Once this misunderstanding is cleared up, she seems to get over it. But even then, we’re supposed to believe his job needs to be kept secret because he’s doing something shameful and dishonest. All the single women I know would be thrilled if Hitch descended upon their city and got the male populace in shape.

Alternative theory: most men in 2005 actually had social skills, so Hitch’s line of work was, even if morally sound, extremely weird. Which brings me to the fact that the issue was probably never that these men were “lying” (they were taking relatively generic advice from a more socially skilled guy—the horror!) but about the fact that…

People do not like aliens in skin suits.

Here I go again with my “alien in a skin suit” metaphor—often used to describe myself as a formerly socially inept person who learned social skills. In this case, I’m using it to describe the awkward and dorky men who hire Hitch to get girlfriends. My theory: we are supposed to subconsciously dislike these men (or understand why someone would dislike them and their tactics) because they weren’t supposed to imitate normal or attractive men.

The self-improvement of Hitch’s clients is not seen as a noble cause, but as trickery, even when they aren’t lying about anything, and are merely getting tips for conversational skills and fashion. Learning better social skills isn’t dishonest, but it’s framed that way, because nobody wants to be “tricked” into dating a loser, even if they like the end result. After Hitch is more or less doxxed, women start coming up to him on the street with their significantly-less-attractive boyfriends and asking if Hitch had anything to do with it. A funny scene, surely, but one might ask, “Don’t you like this guy? If so, why would it be such a problem that he learned how to flirt before he met you?”

And this is where Hitch inadvertently touches on a truth that applies to everyone, not just women: people feel uncomfortable when they realize the person they like isn’t as cool as they thought, and they’ve been bamboozled into hanging out with a weirdo. As I wrote in “Be Yourself” Only Works For People Who Aren’t Annoying, people are really uncomfortable when confronted with the reality that they are talking to a formerly-annoying person, even if nothing about the person is currently off-putting. People will, therefore, urge annoying people to “be themselves,” even when nobody wants to hang out with them as they are. Yes, being annoying is bad, but attempting to be anything else is apparently worse. They’re fine with annoying people being socially isolated and ignored, as long as they don’t run the risk of accidentally interacting with them.

When I’ve written about improving my own social skills (often in a platonic sense) there is always someone quick to inform me that it probably looks fake and nobody will ever like me no matter how hard I try so I should just “own” being weird and pray that at least a few other people on Earth can tolerate me, and if not, tough luck. This is just not true. Yes, as Hitch said, you can eventually show all the sides of your personality, and you shouldn’t pretend to be a totally different person, but learning social skills and holding back your weirder elements at first, is not the same thing as adopting a fake personality. I’m sure plenty of people in real life still don’t like me, but my social life has seen measurable improvements since I consciously worked on my social skills. Data point 1: I used to have no friends, and now I have a lot of friends.

But would those friends be freaked out if I told them about my Hitch-style journey? Maybe! That would suck, which is why I waited until I was a full year into a close friendship with a new woman to tell her about it, and even then, I dialed back some of the stories about what a loser I used to be. Of course, I don’t think that consciously learning social skills is wrong, and I don’t think it’s a trick, and I would happily befriend someone who followed the same path, but most people don’t want to think that the top-shelf coffee they’ve been drinking is actually instant coffee crystals, even if it tastes the same:

Toward the beginning of the movie, Hitch advises a client, “Be yourself…but not all at once.” This is great advice! If you’re a weird person, it’s often best to let that trickle out instead of getting right down to hoe-scaring on date one!

If I were in charge of writing Hitch, aside from including a bunch of product placement for American Girl Dolls, I probably would have explored this theme a bit more—why do the women feel so betrayed that they are dating former losers (or just, gasp, unconfident and shy men) who learned how to speak to women? Given that the loserdom is in the past, what exactly is dishonest about taking the initiative to invest in self-improvement?

It’s like the reverse of the romcom trope where the geek takes off her glasses and undoes her ponytail and suddenly she’s hot. Just imagine if she did that, and all the popular guys were like, “Aaaah, put the glasses back on! How dare you trick us with this witchcraft?!”

Hitch’s Advice Worked!

One of the big takeaways from the movie is that Hitch, despite being incredibly successful at what he does, was never needed because, at least in the case of Albert Brennaman, Allegra Cole fell in love with the dorky, weird things about him—the things Hitch told him to conceal. We are supposed to believe that Hitch was, parallel to Sara, closed off and ultra-calculated, too focused on doing things “right” to understand that love is unpredictable. Too bad it’s not true.

As Hitch correctly points out during an argument, Allegra never would have noticed Albert if he hadn’t made a big deal defending her at a work meeting. (The content of this declaration was Albert’s idea, but he was told by Hitch to “shock and awe.”) Moreover, Hitch taught him how to display confidence and composure around Allegra, and gave him tips for how to impress her best friend, who gave him the stamp of approval. Hitch taught Albert how to read non-verbal cues, and if it wasn’t for Hitch, Albert never would have guessed that Allegra was ready for a kiss.

If Albert had never hired Hitch, Allegra would have no idea he existed. The fact that she wasn’t put off by his inhaler or dorky dancing does not mean that Hitch did “nothing,” as he dramatically realized.

Moreover, Hitch’s advice worked for himself. His origin story was that he was an over-eager late bloomer who scared off a girl in college by getting way too attached (during a make out session, he declares, “I love you. I know you don’t love me as much as I love you, but I’m just glad you love me,” when it’s clear she was just saying “I love you” to be polite.) After she dumps him for another guy, he starts dressing better, develops social skills, and stops coming on so strong. And if “be yourself” was always the way to a woman’s heart, Hitch would not have seen a measurable improvement in his own love life after making these changes, but he did. When Hitch first approaches Sara at a bar, he plays it cool, knowing she probably has men bugging her all the time. After Sara turns down a more awkward guy, she responds well to Hitch’s suave advance. Are we supposed to believe that the 1980s Eager Beaver version of Hitch would have done better?

So while it’s heartwarming for Hitch to realize that “there are no rules,” this is really a luxury belief on his part. These “rules” helped him become a total Chad, they helped all the clients who paid him, but all of a sudden there are no rules? Come on!

Where are all the plain women?

This gripe of mine has nothing to do with the dismissal of Hitch’s dating advice, but I had to mention it. Hitch assumes that the dating world is made up of three types of people: unattractive men, attractive men, and attractive women. Something is, I reckon, missing. I know a lot of straight guys might be saying, “Well, of course! All women are attractive!” but this is just not true. While I know the manosphere likes to say that women only see attractive men, the same is true (if not truer) for men, where the “average” woman is a slim twenty-something who just isn’t terribly glam, not a fifty-five-year-old overweight cashier. Meanwhile, the “average” man, in their eyes, is usually visualized more accurately.

I totally support Hitch teaching men social skills, and I support these men taking the initiative to improve instead of sitting around whining about how foids won’t look at them. I’m even fine with them leveraging this self-improvement to bag hotter women! But I do find it odd that none of them are attracted to their looksmatch. Briefly, Hitch teases Albert about this when he confesses his crush on Allegra, and Albert acknowledges that Allegra is out of his league. Hitch initially balks at the request, in part because she’s so beautiful but also because she’s rich. However, he gladly facilitates the pairing of these two:

Granted, most of the couples Hitch facilitates are not as drastically different in age, but these men are behaving as if there are no average-looking, older or geeky women in all of New York City, and the only viable option is to score a Stacey. Some of the couples are slightly more looksmatched and believable, to the movie’s credit, but nobody, except for perhaps Albert, is honest with themselves about the fact that they’re engaging in a version of male hypergamy. In fact, they’re all acting like their only options are to punch way above their weight, or never find love, and Hitch (while offering a valuable service) is validating this ridiculous worldview.

Example: at one point, Hitch defends his work by saying he gives guys like Albert a “fighting chance.” But a fighting chance at what? Scoring a woman way out of his league? A fine pursuit, sure, but let’s not be dramatic. Albert is not doomed to be single forever—he could simply date a woman who doesn’t look like Allegra Cole! There are plenty of overweight asthmatic single female accountants out there, and Albert Brennaman has curiously not been interested in them.

It’s understandable that the movie would not even pick up on how absurd this is. We live in a society where women being, by default, out of their husband’s leagues is just assumed, even when both people in the pairing are perfectly normal-looking. Nobody wants to grapple with a woman not being beautiful, especially not if that woman is still a well-rounded person just as deserving of love and companionship as anyone else. So, to avoid this uncomfortable reality, we pretend all women are gorgeous. In the case of Hitch, unattractive women are simply not part of the equation at all, except for one used as a punchline (during a speed dating event, an older woman loses it and declares that she “hasn’t been laid in a year.” I don’t see anyone trying to give her a “fighting chance!”)

I don’t even object to Hitch’s mismatched pairings on the basis that they’re mismatched—who doesn’t enjoy an underdog story?—but I do object to the way Hitch characterizes women’s preferences. He doesn’t just describe himself as teaching men social skills so they can score attractive women. He carries a belief that these women should be in those men’s leagues and would totally be into them if only their eyes were “opened up.”

Hitch ignores the fact that women may have physical standards at all, and is convinced that any guy, with the right tools, can woo any woman. He says these women are “standing in their own way” and often can’t realize a guy is a catch until he makes himself visible to her. He has not considered the possibility that a twenty-five-year-old is just not interested in a balding fifty-year-old, no matter what game he’s spitting, or that a guy can do everything right and still not be someone’s type. Hitch regularly talks about how you need to dazzle in order to have a chance with “beautiful women,” but why are beautiful women the only ones worthy of dates? His business was built on his own resentment of being passed over for a more charismatic and attractive man, so why does he have no sympathy for the women who, in this world, seem not to exist at all?

This error, combined with the movie’s lesson of, “Actually, never mind on the social skills, just be yourself” indicates that women have zero standards or preferences, and any guy who introduces himself and acts nice is going to get a date. And not just a date, a date with a beautiful woman, which is his right. Basically, the neckbeard’s manifesto. Tips fedora.

Hitch was right that dating has rules, and social skills are good. He was wrong that the right game can override a woman’s physical preferences, or that a woman who isn’t physically attracted to a man is just “standing in her own way.” Game helps, but sometimes it’s just not going to happen, and that’s when you move on. And you aren’t standing in your way by not being attracted to someone, unless your physical standards are particularly unreasonable.

Perhaps it would have helped if, at one point, one of Hitch’s clients realized that while he was chasing a top-tier beauty, his more looksmatched female friend was into him the whole time, making the point that “you can’t always see what’s right in front of you” can be true of anyone. But instead, Hitch operates under the premise that, no matter whether a man is “himself” or a better version of himself, the only women worth dating are significantly above average. Men can be as blinded by beauty (and in Allegra’s case, status) as they want, but women must have their eyes opened to people’s souls. Why is the onus on women to see the soul? If Sara was going to get mad at Hitch for any aspect of his work, it should have been this!

Still a Good Movie

Hitch is still a good movie. I’ll admit that when I watched it as a teenager, I did not notice all these issues and I loved it. And even after realizing I disagree with the message and premise of the movie, I still like it. It did at one point give me a terrible idea to dye my hair blonde and cut it short (damn you Allegra Cole) but other than that, I’d still give it five stars.

Why? Well, it’s funny. It’s well-written. It’s formulaic, like many romantic comedies, but that’s why we like them. Will Smith and Eva Mendes have wonderful chemistry and are insanely entertaining to watch. Both are good-looking in ways that feel authentic. And most importantly, there are two amazing turtleneck outfits:

I know the black turtleneck under the cognac leather jacket doesn’t get the same play as the iconic white fisherman sweater worn by Billy Crystal in When Harry Met Sally, but it should.

Should you watch Hitch? Of course, assuming you’ve been living under a rock and haven’t watched it yet, and even if you’ve already watched it I recommend a second, third or even fourth viewing. But if you take away any advice from such a film, it should be to wear really great turtlenecks, nothing more and nothing less.
