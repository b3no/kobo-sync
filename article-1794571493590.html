Plus the trouble with working with Trump, Florida’s blue vibes and red politics, and more

There’s been a lot of talk of G.O.P. “dummymanders” in states like North Carolina, where Republicans drew very aggressive maps that spread their voters too thin and got swamped in the Dem tsunami. Far be it from me to defend election-rigging shenanigans, but I don’t think we should characterize this kind of map as “dumb.”

It’s certainly true that the North Carolina map was not optimal for maximizing G.O.P. seats in a D+13 national environment. But why would you optimize for that? There’s no saving a party that does that poorly and the exact margin in the House isn’t very important. What these nasty partisan gerrymanders do is maximize your seats in the face of a mildly hostile climate.

To take a Democratic example, the party retained a comfortable majority in 2024 with 46 percent of the vote in the lower house of the Nevada legislature. The way this works is that they won a ton of seats in the Vegas suburbs narrowly while the G.O.P. won a bunch of rural seats by Assad-like margins. The same logic applies nationally. If Dems had done 10 points worse, they’d have gotten crushed on the congressional map. These gerrymanders undermine political accountability by making politicians feel immune to normal-sized swings in opinion, and that’s bad.

Person with Internet Access: One of your many Twitter fights this week, was about whether James Talarico was a moderate or progressive, with both sides trying to claim him. Is this more a case of success has many fathers, or an Obama like charisma that , according to Milan’s earlier poll, leads Democratic factions to see what they want in him. And what’s your empirical case for Talarico moderation?

Skillful, charismatic politicians get different kinds of people to like them. But that’s not really what factional politics is about. Rank-and-file progressive Democrats loved Barack Obama, but that didn’t mean progressive movement leaders agreed with them — part of what those leaders hated about Obama was precisely that he was so well-liked by their base. The entire purpose of the Progressive Establishment organizing project is to prevent the emergence of another Obama-like figure who connects with the mass base of the Democratic Party and puts the groups back in the veal pen.

On Talarico specifically.

This guy is a lifelong Democrat. Kate used to work for Mark Strama, who at the time was a Democratic member of the Texas House of Representatives from Austin. Talarico was in high school at the time, interning in that operation, and was by all accounts a standout member of the Strama Campaign Academy program. He is more progressive than the average American and just got elected to the U.S. Senate from Texas, a state that is more conservative than the average state. Having a guy like that win an election there is obviously a triumph for progressive politics, and progressives should be thrilled about Talarico and celebrating his rise. So I don’t particularly want to yuck anyone’s yum about Talarico or Laken Riley Act supporter Jon Ossoff or anyone else. But I do want progressives themselves to think about how they behave in the real world. Adam Hamilton won an election in an even more conservative state in an even bigger triumph for progressive politics, but then he was asked about Medicare for All on television and said he was against it, and progressive media figures have been denouncing him for it.

Here are some positions Talarico has taken that I have been personally dragged for on social media:

  • All-of-the-above energy policy

  • “I oppose trans athletes playing in girls’ sports if it endangers safety or fairness.”

  • “I’m a big fan of SpaceX and Tesla. [Elon Musk] is a great business leader and entrepreneur and innovator. I want him to stick to those things.”

Now, again, I’m not asking progressives to stop liking Talarico. I want them to be happy. And I think that part of why they are happy is that Talarico ran a very good social-media campaign that made sure that a national audience of actual and potential small donors was being fed a steady diet of clips of Talarico taking progressive positions and saying progressive things. But the difference between Democrats who progressives like and Democrats who progressives hate isn’t that the hated ones never take progressive positions. Haley Stevens had a lot of progressive positions! You don’t become a Democratic Party member of Congress without having tons of views that are to the left of the policy status quo and to the left of the median voter.

If Talarico had faced a primary challenge from the left, there would be video clips of him disagreeing with his challenger about various things, his challenger and their allies would have circulated those videos widely, and progressive internet users would be marinated in Talarico-hate. But because none of that happened, they’re not.

We’ll see soon enough how Talarico and Hamilton and Josh Turek and all the rest vote and then we’ll see what happens. But I really would like progressive-minded people to try to do some cognitive behavioral therapy. If there’s someone who won and you’re happy that they won and then they say something in office that you don’t agree with, before you get mad check — is what they just said in line with their campaign promises? If they backtracked on a specific progressive commitment, you’re allowed to be mad. If it’s a topic they just managed to stay silent about all throughout the campaign, you can be a little disappointed, but you can hardly be mad. And if it’s something like Hamilton’s remarks on health care, you not only can’t be mad; you need to actually try to convince your fellow-travelers to chill out.

None of these people ran on Medicare for All. Maybe they should have, but they didn’t. So Democrats aren’t going to pass a Medicare for All message bill in 2027-28, and even if Ro Khanna is president in 2029, they’re not going to pass a Medicare for All bill then. It is what it is. Thanks to flipping governors’ mansions in Texas and Georgia, there’s a chance to enact a massive reduction in America’s uninsurance rate via Medicaid expansion. Congress might do health affordability measures related to premium tax credits, a public option, or prescription drug pricing. If Democrats can figure out how to win elections in Florida, they can do even more Medicaid expansion. If you care a lot about health care, I think that’s what you should be focusing on. If what you care a lot about is shit-testing politicians, then by all means keep hectoring newly elected members about Medicare for All and keep getting mad at them. But in that sense, Talarico is not a progressive — he didn’t get elected by taking the leftward side of various intra-party wedge issues.

Joshua: Is there an option for Democrats to triangulate with Trump against the Republican Party? For instance, give him the arch, the ballroom, the reflecting pool, and rename the Lincoln Memorial the Trump-Lincoln monument. Ask in return for statehood for DC and PR, revocation of the new rules and political interference in NIH and NSF, a national anti-gerrymandering bill, restriction on redistricting beyond the decadal pace, and a few other things. I don’t think Trump himself cares about these, and since his veto is the only thing keeping these from becoming law, it could be several major wins.

I am skeptical of this plan on several levels.

Politics is politics, and for a good enough offer there is always a deal to be made. But in terms of what I was saying above, deliberately giving Trump a pass on acts of corruption really would be a betrayal of explicit promises most Democrats made. If we’re just literally talking about the construction of an arch at a traffic circle in Virginia, then, sure, obviously Democrats should be willing to fund that in exchange for substantive policy concessions. But I think what we’re really talking about here is giving him a pass on lawless behavior in general in exchange for some policy wins, and those wins would have to be huge.

That’s where we get to my other layer of skepticism. This idea that Trump is like a naive child who can be persuaded to give away the store in exchange for a shiny object was kicking around all throughout his first term, and it never worked. At one point, Chuck Schumer and Nancy Pelosi thought they had a verbal agreement for some kind of deal to do the DREAM Act in exchange for funding a border wall, but it got scuttled on a staff level. Trump is not a detail-oriented person, he’s not a consistent person, and he’s not a man of his word or deeply committed to honor and integrity — a superficial agreement isn’t worth anything, and a real agreement is going to need to be approved by a very right-wing team.

Which isn’t to say that deals can’t be done.

But if you look at the major bipartisan legislation of the Trump era — ROAD to Housing, the BAAJA permitting bill, the Covid-era CARES Act, First Step criminal-justice reform — it all reflects hard staff work done by Democrats with detail-oriented G.O.P. stakeholders either on the Hill or somewhere in the executive branch. This is work that got done despite Trump’s mercurial nature, not because of it. He in fact nearly scuttled ROAD with some unexpected remarks about private equity and kept spooking people by saying weird stuff about BAAJA. There’s no example of successfully leveraging Trump’s weirdness into doing policy.

Cam Causey: Nate Silver recently remarked in passing that Florida is a weird state because it’s a Red State but has “Blue State Amenities.” I’m born and raised Floridian, so maybe this is a fish-in-water problem, but I have no idea what he was talking about.

I can’t read another person’s mind. But let’s say you’re someone who really enjoys living in New York City — i.e., you enjoy walkable cities and find the consumption amenities available in the city to be so valuable that you are happy to pay the exorbitant price of housing there. What’s more, let’s say you’re the kind of guy who’d strongly prefer living in Chicago or San Francisco to living in Los Angeles. This is a definite kind of guy. I know guys like this. I think I probably am a guy like this.

And I think to these kinds of guys, Miami is a high-tier city in a way that Atlanta and Dallas are not. There’s a reason that when New York finance guys threaten to move to a low-tax jurisdiction it’s almost always Miami and not somewhere in South Dakota.

I don’t know that thinking of these lifestyle amenities in terms of the political lean of whole states is particularly enlightening, though. You could imagine a state of South Florida composed of Miami-Dade, Broward, Palm Beach, Lee, Collier, Monroe, and Hendry counties. This “state” would have a larger population than Arizona, Tennessee, or Massachusetts. And politically, it would have voted for Clinton and Biden before flipping to Trump in 2024 but would have backed Democrats in statewide offices this cycle. It is true that Miami is unusually conservative for a city of its size and character, but what that amounts to in practice is “not super-duper left-wing” rather than “actually conservative.” State politics as a whole is conservative because of the voting behavior in places like the Tampa and Orlando suburbs that I don’t think particularly have amenities that Nate or I would find appealing.

John K: Now, that we know the results, how do you think your pre-scandal case for Platner holds up? Specifically, the idea that Collins would be so hard to beat that we needed to ramp up the variance.

Eyeballing the results, it looks like ME-Sen would’ve been neck and neck if we’d had the D+6 environment many people expected earlier this year, which makes me skeptical, but maybe Jackson was stronger than I think?

If I want to rescue the take, I would say that if Jackson had been running in the first place, we’d have said that he was the high-variance play relative to Janet Mills and that in ending up with the logger from Allagash Democrats basically got the thing that Platner was supposed to have been.

The problem with trying to rescue the take is Jackson didn’t attempt any ideological heterodoxy — not even Platner’s flirtation with being a strong gun-rights guy — and Jackson did terribly in his home region, losing his home town and the district he represented in the State Senate pretty badly. If you look at where Collins ran weaker in 2026 than she did in 2020, the biggest swings are in the Portland suburbs followed by Portland itself and Maine’s smaller cities like Lewiston and Bangor.

If anything, Jackson did worse than Sara Gideon in the more culturally conservative parts of the state.

Bryan T. Mathew: How should democrats in Texas govern? What did Republicans get right that Democrats should leave alone? What should be changed?

The three big ones are that Texas should restore abortion rights, curb the unregulated school voucher program, and expand Medicaid. I am also proud to say that I am much more left-wing on economics than populist Gina Hinojosa — in my opinion, she’s wrong and Texas should create a (low but nonzero) income tax. She’s promised not to do that, and it’s moot with a Republican State Senate anyway, but I think it’s worth saying.

The question becomes “how can you expand Medicaid without a new tax,” and I think the answer will probably be “cut some kind of deal with the data center industry after having campaigned so hard against them.”

In terms of what not to change, my education reform friends are very worried that Hinojosa will go beyond anti-vouchers by wrecking the state’s accountability system and crushing the charter school sector. She should consider not doing that! I think Texas has the most successful electricity transmission and interconnection system in the country and Democrats shouldn’t change that (I see no indication that they will). And I think they should try to double down on housing growth by adopting new pro-infill measures statewide. I would also consider trying to complement housing growth by creating more state parks and the like because Texas is pretty weak on public lands. Again, though, you would need some revenue.

Hank Smathers: Matt, you were concerned that Trump was signaling pre-election a dramatic escalation of the war in Iran after the midterms. In light of Vance’s and other Repubs’ cynical efforts to now blame the war as the sole cause for their defeat at the polls, how do you think this plays out with the war going forward?

I’m not going to claim secret knowledge of the Pentagon’s planning here, but a basic problem facing every president who has ever decided that a war is a political millstone around his neck and he wants to end it is that “just surrender” is not very appealing. The only president who’s ever really attempted “give up” as a strategy, Joe Biden, got hammered for it politically.

And the war in Iran is worse than Afghanistan or Vietnam from the “give up” standpoint because the thing people are angry about isn’t the direct expenditures on the war but the downstream economic cost of the disruption to shipping. Trump does not have a “reopen the strait” button that he can just push. He would need to reach a diplomatic agreement with Iran that would probably involve making a lot of concessions to them. One set of possible concessions would be acquiescing to some kind of Iranian tolling of the strait, but that wouldn’t address voter concerns about energy costs. So if you’re going to do a deal with Iran that secures safe untaxed passage through the strait, you’re going to have to make some other kind of concessions to Iran that could get pretty politically dicey.

So if you’re sitting in the Oval Office thinking, “I need to end this war,” there’s a good chance you end up where L.B.J. was on Vietnam or where George Bush was on Iraq in 2007 or where Barack Obama was on Afghanistan in his first term and saying, “I need to do a big escalatory push to create a more favorable terrain for a diplomatic agreement.”
