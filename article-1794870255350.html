The YIMBY mayor trying to prepare Cincinnati for a wave of climate migration

Mayor Aftab Pureval is building transit and housing for today’s residents. He says it’s also how the city is preparing for climate migrants.

Halina Bennet
Nov 16

When Aftab Pureval took office as mayor in January of 2022, he inherited a different kind of housing crisis. Cincinnati, Ohio’s third-largest city, with a population of just over 300,000, is not San Francisco or New York. By national standards, in fact, it’s downright cheap in absolute terms. But that year, rents there rose more quickly than any other of the 50 most populous U.S. metropolitan areas, according to Redfin analysis.

“Even though on the national scale we were quite affordable, we were just pricing people out,” Pureval told me in an interview last week.

Cincinnati is one of the country’s fastest-growing metros, according to data from Bank of America, but as it has grown over the last decade, its housing stock has shrunk.

“Because we’re such an old city with old housing stock, we actually lost housing in that time, so there were more people living in Cincinnati and fewer units for them to live in,” Pureval said. “Of course rents and property taxes and mortgages all went through the roof.”

Nearly five years on, Pureval has overseen the passage of comprehensive zoning reform that went into effect in July 2024, tripled the pace of affordable housing, built a program to quell evictions that has kept around 2,000 families in their homes, and is designing the largest transit investment in the city’s history. Rents, accordingly, have begun to decline.

Pureval is also candid about how far the city still has to go, especially when considering the topic at the front of his mind: preparing Cincinnati to receive climate migrants.

Changing city, shifting desires

Cincinnati offers all the amenities of a true Solid B city. Its housing costs are lower than the national average; it has plenty of job opportunities; it has a symphony orchestra, a ballet, an opera, museums, and, as Pureval puts it, is “generationally investing in public art.”

In the mid-19th century, Cincinnati was middle America’s boomtown, the “Queen City of the West.” It was the fastest-growing and most important city west of the Appalachian Mountains, a hub for steamboat trade along the Ohio River that exported pork, whiskey, and grain. Its population peaked at 500,000 in 1950, but like many American cities, Cincinnati went through a period of decline during the second half of the 20th century amid suburbanization and deindustrialization.

But its spirit of enterprise is alive. One of the largest consumer-goods manufacturers in the world, Procter & Gamble, is headquartered in the Cincinnati area; so are the grocery store Kroger, the aviation and defense manufacturer GE Aerospace, and multiple major financial institutions such as Western & Southern Financial Group and Fifth Third Bank. This year, Valvoline — the oil change company — and the payroll and H.R. services company Paychex both opened headquarters in the city. It is also home to Cincinnati Children’s, one of the top-ranked pediatric hospitals in the country.

“We were bringing in talent from all around the world, and the challenge was — that was always true, but our city was not providing the services or the neighborhoods that they were looking for,” Pureval said. “And so, what we really doubled down on was, particularly young professionals and people of a younger generation were looking for dense, diverse, walkable neighborhoods with good public transportation and good access to public art.”

This shift in desire among young people is Pureval’s “north star,” driving the logic behind the city’s agenda for housing, office-to-residential conversion, and a bus rapid transit (B.R.T.) system now in design.

The B.R.T. system, named MetroRapid and budgeted for about $350 million, is funded primarily through Hamilton County’s sales tax levy that was passed in 2020 and is slated to break ground in 2027, with two lines operational by 2029.

There is also a fiscal logic to the mayor’s approach. He said that Ohio cities rely on income taxes, and remote work moved that revenue to the suburbs when workers stopped commuting. A city that wants to keep its tax base must give people a reason to live inside it.

“Build the damn housing”

The zoning changes shepherded by Pureval legalize “missing middle” housing such as duplexes, triplexes, quadplexes, row homes, and townhomes, allowing up to four housing units in areas previously zoned for single-family homes near major transit corridors and business districts. The plan, called “Connected Communities,” also eliminates or reduces parking minimums along transit lines in business districts to encourage business development through lowering costs and increasing walkability.

The reforms apply across all 52 of the city’s neighborhoods, each of which has a neighborhood business district that Pureval sees as a place to steer growth.

“We want people to live along transit routes,” he said. “We want people to live in neighborhood downtowns because that’s where the jobs are. That’s where the small businesses are, and it also starts to wean us off of our overreliance on cars, particularly for a Midwestern city.”

Since 2020, the Cincinnati Business Courier reports, residential conversions have resulted in more than 1,600 new housing units downtown, nearly all of which are above 90 percent occupancy. As for new buildings, Pureval said that permits are way up. Data analyzed by transparent.city shows that through October, new residential construction permits are up 33 percent from the same period in 2025. Permit approval times have fallen 25 percent.

Passing this kind of reform is hard, and Pureval said that the city did two and a half years of community engagement to get residents on board with change, the fear of which, he said, was driving opposition. Pureval explained that while there were plenty of objections, there was “nothing cogent.”

“The opposition was, ‘this is just going to gentrify my neighborhood,’” he said. “The other side was saying, ‘I don’t want new people in my neighborhood,’ so that intellectually was inconsistent.”

On the objection that the reform would bring renters, Pureval noted that more than 60 percent of the city is renters.

“At the end of the day, it really is just about the irrational fear that I’m going to put a 20-story condo building on their residential cul-de-sac, which obviously is never going to happen,” he said.

His diagnosis of the underlying politics is familiar. It is politically easier for the mayor to allow neighborhoods to vote on whether they want housing, but that process, he said, will never fix the crisis. “The only way to get out of it to stabilize prices is to build the damn housing.”

“Everyone will say they want housing until you try to build it in their neighborhood, so at the end of the day, it’s just about the courage of your convictions.” The public pressure, he said, comes disproportionately from wealthy homeowners who bought decades ago and now benefit from rules that keep others out. “I don’t begrudge them for that investment. That’s great,” Pureval said. “But maintaining rules that prevent other people from moving to that neighborhood or other people from accessing that kind of wealth, that’s what I have a problem with.”

I asked why he didn’t go as far as to abolish single-family zoning. As a leader who must govern, Pureval gave an answer rooted in political triage. He said he is open to going further but that there is room for single-family homes in a dense city and that ultimately, he sees a collective action problem and a risk of “picking fights unnecessarily.”

“If we’re in a place 10 years from now where we’re still struggling to build housing, particularly because of our zoning, then yeah, we should go further,” he said. “But I want to see this opportunity and this iteration work.”

The city’s limits

Pureval is clear on the limitations of a city government’s impact. His administration’s focus is housing for people at 30 to 60 percent of area median income (A.M.I.). Below 30 percent, he argued, is the province of federal housing programs, since supporting their needs requires significantly more investment than what private developers and city incentives can contribute to close the gap.

For the 30-to-60 band, the city layers its own grants and incentives onto other programs, including low-income housing tax credits, Community Development Block Grant funds, and new markets and historic tax credits (the last of which are particularly important in a city with a significant stock of older buildings). He said that the approach has tripled the rate of affordable development in 2026 compared with the output before he took office.

Pureval said that Cincinnati’s housing market is not strong enough to support true inclusionary zoning, so to make financing work, most projects that have been or are being built mix units targeting 80 percent A.M.I. and above with units at 30 to 60 percent.

For residents living below 30 percent A.M.I., the city collaborates with the Cincinnati Metropolitan Housing Authority (C.M.H.A.) through capital funding to renovate aging buildings rather than to add units.

“The worst landlord in Cincinnati is C.M.H.A., bar none.” He said that the agency is responsible for the most building inspection violations and evictions. “We provide an extraordinary amount of support, specifically for infrastructure maintenance.”

Indeed, the agency has faced intense scrutiny and legal issues over mismanagement allegations, including recent ones involving rent theft. When I called for comment, C.M.H.A.’s phone extensions each had a busy signal, and when I emailed them at their listed address, mail@email.com (yes, really), for comment, I received a mail delivery error: “Address not found.”

The city approaches tenant protections through a program that pairs a right to counsel in eviction court with emergency rental assistance. Pureval said about 90 percent of tenants represent themselves in eviction proceedings, while about 90 percent of landlords have attorneys. Usually, with that kind of imbalance, tenants lose. With counsel and rental assistance, he said, the city has kept more than 2,000 families in their homes over the two years the program has been in place, that number disproportionately representing Black women. The city has contributed property tax and general fund dollars to the program, called Access to Counsel, after emergency rental assistance from the pandemic era largely ended.

Room to grow

Pureval, a Democrat who is less than a year into his second four-year term as mayor, has learned a lot through his concentrated and fast-moving approach to reform and development. Zoning reform, he said, “in and of itself is not a silver bullet.” The market has to notice that Cincinnati is an easy — and an attractive — place to build. Meanwhile, community pushback continues every time a project breaks ground, whether it is affordable or market-rate, a conversion or a new-build.

I asked where he feels he has fallen short.

“I haven’t done a good enough job of being persuasive to convince people that if you say no to a housing development on your block in your neighborhood, that has a massive ripple effect on all the rest of the neighborhood’s housing affordability and housing access,” he said. “I lay the responsibility at my feet that I have not been persuasive enough to get enough community buy-in to make that easier.”

He described inheriting a city in the throes of post-pandemic fragility, where development had stalled and where he had to revive it from scratch. His predecessors, he said, believed the city had an income problem, not a housing problem, so there was no existing housing effort to build on.

“I believe we have both,” he said.

The next wave

While Pureval is laser-focused on housing for current residents, he said that there is another reason behind his urgent approach to development. He is focused on preparing for climate migration, though he concedes that when he mentions it people roll their eyes.

This year, Cincinnati released a Climate Migration Readiness Plan, becoming the first city in the country to create a municipal strategy to prepare for potential population growth driven by climate change and other domestic migration trends.

He said that cost-of-living pressures from climate change will make people rethink where they live and that the Great Lakes region is relatively well placed. The area has plentiful fresh water, with the Great Lakes to the north and the Ohio River to the south, and a record of recent manufacturing investment in central Ohio. He said that shifting insurance markets in coastal states are early signs of stress that is already affecting costs in Cincinnati and forcing him to think about planning implications.

“We’re going to have very soon more hot weather days than we have cold weather days,” he said, which is causing the city to reevaluate the materials it uses for roads, bridges, sidewalks, and buildings.

“If we get that right, and if we’re the destination for climate migrants, both high skill and low skill, that is a huge opportunity,” he said. “But it’s also even more urgency to get these systems right that we’re working on right now.”

He knows that planning for a future redrawn by a changing climate carries political risk.

Residents want a mayor who is focused on the people who live there now, and nobody likes to think about the scary unknown realities of the future, he said. But Pureval sees the agenda as one consolidated project. Housing and transit for current residents are the same investments that make the city prepared for new arrivals. Even if climate change doesn’t bring newcomers to the city, he said that an alternative is the scenario that other blue cities have experienced in recent years, in which leaders of red border states bussed migrants to Democratic cities, which he said could put “30,000 people at your doorstep overnight” and overwhelm the city’s safety nets.

“In order to avoid it, we have to start planning right now.”

Building where people say “no”

The city still has a long way to go, and the next test is whether — now that the reform passed, permits are up, and the development pipeline is moving along — homes get built in the neighborhoods that resist them.

For other mayors of mid-sized cities facing sudden growth, Pureval’s advice is to be “intentional and bold” on housing and transit because both are expensive and require years of scaffolding, to do real outreach to renters and potential residents, and to accept that not every criticism is valid or argued in good faith.

“At the end of the day, you have to do what you think is right, regardless of whether it’s popular or not.”
